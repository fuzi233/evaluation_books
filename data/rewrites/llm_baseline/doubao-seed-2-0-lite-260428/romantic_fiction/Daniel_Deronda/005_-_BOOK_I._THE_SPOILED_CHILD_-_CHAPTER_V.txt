Gwendolen’s reception across the neighborhood unfolded exactly as her uncle had predicted. From the grand gilded halls of Brackenshaw Castle to the warm, generous home of banker Mr. Quallon at the Firs in Wanchester, she was met with unmistakeable, eager admiration. Even the ladies who privately decided she was a touch too sharp, a touch too unapologetically radiant, could not resist adding her to their guest lists: hostesses who entertain often curate their parties the way statesmen build cabinets, choosing guests not only out of personal fondness, but to make the whole affair sparkle. There was no tedious baggage that came with inviting Gwendolen, either: her mother Mrs. Davilow was a quiet, gently picturesque chaperon who never demanded fuss, and her uncle Mr. Gascoigne was welcome everywhere on his own merits.

One of the first houses to send an invitation was Quetcham Hall, where no one quite warmed to her but no one dared leave her off the list for their massive dinner party: an event that acted as a formal, broad introduction of the new arrivals to every visitable family in the district, with thirty carefully selected guests spanning every relevant generation. No figure that night outshone Gwendolen as she drifted through the long suite of rooms draped in garlands and lit by hundreds of soft candles. At first she was nothing but a slim, floating silhouette in layers of flowing white drapery, drawing closer through one wide carved doorway after another, growing brighter, sharper, more unmistakeable with every step. She had never before been the centre of that kind of slow, grand public promenade, and she exulted in the knowledge that it suited her. A stranger glancing over could have sworn she had been raised on long gilded galleries and liveried footmen, while her cousin Anna, who was far more familiar with such aristocratic households, fidgeted so nervously she might have been a rabbit dropped suddenly into a brightly lit ballroom.

“Who is that with Gascoigne?” the archdeacon cut off mid-conversation about military maneuvers, a topic every guest naturally deferred to him on as a clergyman. His son, the brilliant young classicist who had already published several elegant, ingenious new emendations of ancient Greek texts, spoke at almost the exact same second, muttering under his breath: “By God, who is that girl with the perfectly held head and that glorious figure?”

To anyone who wanted every guest to feel flattered and at ease, it was almost exasperating how thoroughly Gwendolen eclipsed everyone else. The famously handsome Miss Lawe, daughter of Lady Lawe, suddenly looked broad, heavy, and completely lifeless beside her. Poor Miss Arrowpoint, who had unfortunately also chosen a white gown that night, suddenly resembled a tiny cheap carte-de-visite portrait where only the skirt had been paid for. Since Catherine Arrowpoint was universally beloved for her gentle, unassuming warmth and her endless patience softening her mother’s more eccentric quirks, many guests thought it oddly unfit that this new stranger should carry herself like someone born to far higher social standing than their well-liked local heiress.

“She is not really nearly as handsome if you stop to examine her features,” Mrs. Arrowpoint confided to Mrs. Vulcany later that evening. “It’s just a certain bold bearing that stuns you at first, but she grows far less pleasant the longer you speak to her.”

Gwendolen had not meant to offend her hostess, though she had. The lady of Quetcham was not a spiteful woman, but she had her sensitive spots. Everyone in the neighborhood loved to connect all her little oddities to the fact that she was heiress to a fortune made in some vague city trade: people joked that her short squat figure, her sharp parrot-like squawk of a voice, her elaborate towering headdresses, and her fondness for writing literary treatises were all inevitable side effects of that mercantile fortune. (A little observation would have shown how little sense that made: aldermen’s daughters were often tall and graceful, pretty women often had harsh voices, and terrible poetry was written by every sort of person, man or woman, across every class.)

Gwendolen, who had a sharp eye for the absurdities of others, had been fully determined to win Mrs. Arrowpoint over, showering her with more interest than most other guests would bother to offer. But self-confidence often tricks people into assuming everyone less self-assured is also slow-witted: rich people coo patronizingly at the poor, vibrant young people raise their voices and speak in silly simplified tones to seniors, assuming they must be deaf and dim. Even clever, kind Gwendolen fell for that trap. She unthinkingly decided that because Mrs. Arrowpoint was a little ridiculous, she must also be easily fooled, and began performing a little act of wide-eyed, girlish naivete without ever suspecting her hostess was noticing every tiny shift in her behaviour.

“I hear you love books as well as music, riding, and archery,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, pulling her aside for a private chat in the drawing room after the men had finished their port. “Catherine will be so glad to have such a sympathetic new neighbour.” The words would have sounded perfectly graceful if they had been spoken in a soft, mellow voice, but with that sharp loud twang, Gwendolen found herself shifting into patronizing mode without thinking, replying sweetly: “It is I who am fortunate. Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what good music really is. I am a complete beginner, I hear she is a master of the craft.”

“Catherine has certainly had every advantage. We have a first-rate musician staying in the house right now, Herr Klesmer—you must know his compositions. Let me introduce you later. You sing, I believe? Catherine plays three instruments but she does not sing. I hope you will let us hear you, I understand you are an accomplished singer.”

“Oh no, ‘die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross’ as Mephistopheles says.”

“Ah, you read Goethe. Young ladies are so advanced these days. I suppose you have read every important book.”

“Truly no. I’ve been rummaging through the library at Offendene and there’s nothing readable at all. The pages all stick together, and the whole place smells musty. I wish I could write books to amuse myself the way you do! How delightful it must be to write exactly what you want, instead of reading other people’s ideas. Home-made books must be so nice.” For a split second Mrs. Arrowpoint’s glance sharpened, but before she could take offence, Gwendolen gushed that she would give anything to write a whole book of her own, and the comment read as nothing more than silly girlish enthusiasm.

“Why shouldn’t you?” Mrs. Arrowpoint encouraged her. “Pen, ink, paper are there for anyone to use, just as they were for me. I will send you all my own published work with pleasure.”

“Thank you, I shall be so glad to read them. It must be so special to know an author personally, you’d be able to tell for certain which bits are meant to be funny and which are serious. I always laugh in the wrong places, I swear.” Gwendolen noticed she was treading on dangerous ground, and quickly added: “With Shakespeare and all the other great writers we can never meet, you know. I always want to know more than what is written on the page.”

“If you are interested in any of my subjects, I can lend you piles of unpublished manuscripts,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, as Gwendolen suddenly felt like the girl who had foolishly announced she was wildly fond of potted sprats. “I will publish most of them eventually, so many friends have urged me to, and one does not like to be stubborn. My work on Tasso, for example— I could easily have made it twice the size.”

“I adore Tasso,” Gwendolen said.

“Perfect, you shall have all my papers. Everyone who has ever written about Tasso has got it all wrong: the nature of his madness, his feelings for Leonora, the real reason he was imprisoned, what Leonora was really like— I think she was cold-hearted, otherwise she would have married him even against her brother’s wishes. No one else sees it that way.”

“How fascinating!” Gwendolen said. “I hate agreeing with everyone else. It’s so boring to just parrot other people’s opinions. That’s the worst part about publishing your work: you make everyone start agreeing with you.” That line made Mrs. Arrowpoint suspicious again, her gaze probing for a trace of sarcasm, but Gwendolen looked so wide-eyed and innocent, leaning in like an eager student, that she let it pass. “I know nothing of Tasso except *Gerusalemme Liberata*, which we had to memorize at school.”

“His life is far more interesting than his poetry. I have written the early part of his story as a full romance, accounting for his father Bernardo and all the rest, all the parts that must be true.”

“Imagination is often truer than fact,” Gwendolen said decisively, though she could no more explain that glib phrase than she could read Coptic. “I can’t wait to learn everything about him, especially his madness. Poets are always a little mad, aren’t they?”

“Of course—‘the poet’s eye in a fine frenzy rolling,’ and someone wrote of Marlowe: ‘For that fine madness still he did maintain, Which always should possess the poet’s brain.’”

“But no one always noticed it, right?” Gwendolen said innocently. “I expect some of them rolled their eyes in private. Mad people are often very cunning.” A flicker of irritation crossed Mrs. Arrowpoint’s face, but the doors swung open then and the rest of the guests filed in from the dining room, stopping any potential argument dead.

“Ah, there is Herr Klesmer,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, standing to lead him over to Gwendolen. The brilliant musician was a wonderful, rare mix of German, Slav, and Jewish heritage, with grand striking features, wavy brown hair styled in soft artistic disarray, and dark eyes behind round spectacles. His English was nearly flawless, no thick foreign accent to be heard, and his intimidating genius was softened just then by the foolish, gentle eagerness any great artist feels when he wants to charm a beautiful woman.

They soon moved to the pianos, where Miss Arrowpoint and Herr Klesmer played a four-handed duet that went on so long the whole general audience was quietly grateful when it finished. Gwendolen, in particular, walked away from the performance realizing that quiet, placid Catherine Arrowpoint had a mastery of the piano that would put all her own casual practice to shame—though she did not lose confidence in her much-praised singing voice.

Soon every guest was begging Gwendolen to sing, especially Mr. Arrowpoint the host, a perfect gentleman who was beloved by all for nothing more noteworthy than marrying Miss Cuttler and importing the very best cigars. He led her politely to the piano, where Herr Klesmer opened the lid for her, smiling as he stepped back a few feet to stand where he could see her clearly as she performed.

Gwendolen was not nervous. She never trembled when she set out to do something she knew she was good at, and singing was pure joy to her. She had a moderately powerful soprano that people often said sounded just like Jenny Lind’s, a perfect ear for pitch, and she had never known anything but unreserved applause for her performances. She had the rare gift of looking even prettier when she sang than she did at rest, and having the great Herr Klesmer watching her did not feel unpleasant at all. She sang her carefully pre-planned favorite Bellini aria, sure of every note.

“Charming!” Mr. Arrowpoint exclaimed from where he stood nearby, and the praise echoed around the room, the usual small amount of harmless social insincerity no one bothers to call out. But Herr Klesmer stood as still and silent as a statue in spectacles, not a word, not a clap. The guests begged her to stay seated and sing a second song, and she was not inclined to refuse—until she turned to Klesmer with a soft, smiling appeal. “It would be far too cruel to make a great musician listen to poor amateur singing.”

“No, that means nothing,” he said, and suddenly his voice shifted into a sharp, staccato thick German accent he had hidden until that point, the kind of brogue a man slips into only when he is angry, passionate, or fiercely sincere. “That makes nothing. It is always acceptable to see you sing.”

It was the most unexpected assertion of superiority she had ever encountered. Gwendolen flushed bright crimson, but with her usual quick presence of mind, she did not storm off in ungraceful resentment. Catherine Arrowpoint, who had stood close enough to overhear, and had noticed Klesmer’s admiring gaze on Gwendolen was far more focused on her than polite society permitted, stepped in to smooth things over with perfect kindness. “Imagine what I have to put up with from this professor! He can barely tolerate any of the English attempts at music. We all just put up with his severity, because it is almost a relief to know exactly the worst thing anyone could ever say about your playing, when everyone else is fawning over you.”

“I would be so grateful to him if he told me the worst,” Gwendolen said, recovering her wits instantly. “I expect I had very bad teachers, and no real talent—only a great love for music.” It was a very graceful line, for a thought that had never once crossed her mind before that second.

“That is true, your training is terrible,” Herr Klesmer said quietly. He adored beauty, but music meant far more to him. “Still, you are not completely without gifts. You sing perfectly in tune, and you have a fair, pleasant voice. But you produce your notes incorrectly, and that music you just sang is beneath you. It is that sort of sappy, see-sawing melody made for people with no breadth of horizon, no deep passion, no sense of the universal. It makes everyone who listens to it smaller. Sing something larger, now, and I will see what you are really capable of.”

Gwendolen’s heart sank. She had spent the whole night being treated like the brightest, most special guest in the room, and now this genius had ripped apart one of the things she was proudest of. “Not now, later,” she said, a little unsteadily. Catherine stepped in to save her again. “Yes, later. I always need half an hour to summon my courage after he criticizes my playing. Let us make him play for us now, he owes it to all of us to show what great music really sounds like.”

Klesmer agreed, and played one of his own compositions: the fantasia titled *Freudvoll, Leidvoll, Gedankenvoll*, a sprawling, richly layered piece that drew every possible thread of passion from the piano, his fingers working a kind of imperious magic that sent thrills up every listener’s spine, making the strings sing with a raw, quivering urgency few pianists could pull off. Even with her ego bruised, Gwendolen felt the full weight of that performance. Her mortification melted away into bright, restless excitement, until she was almost completely indifferent to how badly she had just been judged, determined to laugh off her own small failings like they belonged to some other girl. Her eyes sparkled, her cheeks flushed pink, and her tongue was sharp and ready for mischievous remarks.

“I do hope you will sing for us again, Miss Harleth,” young Clintock, the archdeacon’s classicist son who had walked her into dinner, said as soon as Klesmer finished. “That fancy complicated stuff means nothing to me. It’s like a jar of leeches, you can never tell where the beginning ends and the end starts. I could listen to your singing all day.”

“Yes, we would all love something popular now, another song from you would be such a nice relaxation,” Mrs. Arrowpoint added, moving over with polite intentions.

“That is because all of you are stuck in a puerile state of culture, with no breadth of horizon,” Gwendolen said, completely ignoring Mrs. Arrowpoint and grinning bright up at Clintock. “I just learned that. I’m having growing pains right now, and they are never pleasant.” Mrs. Arrowpoint noticed the intentional rudeness, but simply stepped back, saying no more.

“I’m so glad you like our little neighbourhood,” Clintock said, pleased that he had her full attention.

“It’s perfect. There is a little of everything, and not too much of anything.”

“That’s rather ambiguous praise.”

“Not to me. I love having a little of everything. A little absurdity, for example, is so much fun. I am thankful for a few odd, quirky people, but too many of them get boring.” Mrs. Arrowpoint, who could still hear their conversation, realized she was right to be suspicious of Gwendolen’s earlier gushing about Tasso, and decided the girl was double-faced and far more sarcastic than she pretended.

“I think we need more croquet, for one thing,” Clintock went on. “I’m usually away studying, but if I was home more I would start a whole croquet club. You’re in the archery club, I think, but trust me, croquet is the game of the future. It just needs someone to write it up properly. One of my friends wrote a whole four-canto poem about it, as good as Pope. I keep begging him to publish it. You’ve never read anything better.”

“I will study croquet tomorrow,” Gwendolen said. “I will give up singing and take up croquet instead.”

“No no, don’t give up singing! But do take up croquet. I can send you Jenning’s poem if you like, I have a handwritten manuscript.”

“Is he a very close friend of yours?”

“Just about, yes.”

“Oh, if he’s only just about, I think I will decline. Unless, if you send it to me, you promise not to quiz me about it afterwards, asking which part I liked best. It’s far easier to pretend you know a sermon you never listened to than pretend you loved a poem you never read.”

But Quetcham Hall continued to send the Harleths warm, polite invitations all the same. It was not just that social calls are never based entirely on personal liking: gentle Catherine Arrowpoint, who managed all the household’s social arrangements while her mother was busy writing her manuscripts, had felt a soft, protective fondness for Gwendolen after that tense little scene at the piano, and was determined to make sure that dazzling, sharp, bright young girl who carried the whole world at her feet got all the kindness and chances she deserved.
