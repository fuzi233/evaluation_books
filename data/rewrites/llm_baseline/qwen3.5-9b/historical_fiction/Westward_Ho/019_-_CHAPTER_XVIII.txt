"O' horseback, ye cuckoo!" retorted Falstaff. "But a-foot, he will not budge a foot."
"Yes, Jack, upon instinct," insisted the Prince.
"I grant ye, upon instinct," admitted the fat knight.

By nightfall, they had slipped past the southern tip of Grenada and drifted into that fairy ring of islands where nature had concentrated all her beauty, and humanity all its sin. If Barbados held a strange glory in the eyes of these newcomers, how much more magnificent must be the seas they now entered—calm, smiling waters untouched by the hurricanes that roared far to the north. Sky, sea, and islands formed one vast rainbow. To the sturdy, practical sailors whose minds were fixed solely on Spanish gold and pearls, the view offered little poetic appeal. Nor did it stir much wonder in Amyas, accustomed to tropical scenery, who speculated inwardly on the feasibility of extirpating the Spaniards and annexing the West Indies to Queen Elizabeth's dominions. Yet, even their unpoetic eyes could not behold these famous yet newly discovered lands without a mixture of awe and excitement. All the wonder, pity, and greed of the age had concentrated itself here. It was an awful, yet inspiriting thought, to enter regions almost unknown to Englishmen, where the penalty of failure was worse than death—the torments of the Inquisition. Perhaps fewer than five times before had English ships ventured these mysterious seas. Yet, there were those on board who knew them too well. These were the veterans of Captain John Hawkins' expeditions, the first British mariners to attempt trade along these coasts. Interdicted by Spanish jealousy from the necessities of life, they had, in true English fashion, won their markets at the point of the sword, only to buy and sell thereafter with honest peace. Throughout the day, the old mariners of the *Pelican* and the *Minion* were questioned incessantly regarding every isle, cape, fish, and bird, while Frank stood by, listening seriously and silently.

A profound awe seemed to possess Frank's soul, yet it was not a sad one. His face appeared daily to drink in glory from the splendor surrounding him. Murmuring to himself at intervals, "This is the gate of heaven," he watched all day, careless of food or rest, as every forward plunge of the ship revealed fresh wonders. Islands and capes hung high in the air, mirrored by their inverted images below; long sandhills rolled and weltered in the mirage. Yellow flowerbeds and huge thorny cacti, resembling giant candelabra, clothed the glaring slopes. Twisted and tossed by the heat, they flickered until the entire scene resembled a blazing phantom-world, where everything was as unstable as it was fantastic—even the sun itself, distorted into strange oval and pear-shaped figures by beds of crimson mist through which it sank to rest.

While Frank marveled, Yeo rejoiced. To the south of the setting sun, a cluster of tall peaks rose from the sea. Unless his reckoning was wrong, they were the mountains of Macanao, at the western end of Margarita, the Isle of Pearls. Famous in all the cities of the Mediterranean and at the great German fairs, it was second only in richness to the pearl island in the Gulf of Panama, which fifteen years prior had cost John Oxenham his life.

The following day saw them running along the northern side of the island, having passed undiscovered the castle the Spaniards had built at the eastern end to protect their pearl fisheries. Finally, they opened a deep, still bight wooded to the water's edge. Lying in the roadstead was a caravel and three small boats. At the sight, every man sprang onto the deck instantly, voices rising with opinions on what to do. Some argued for sailing straight into the roadstead, as the breeze blew fresh toward the shore—a common occurrence in those islands during the afternoon. However, seeing the waves break intermittently near the bay's mouth, they deemed it safer to skirt the coast quietly before sending in the pinnace and a smaller boat. Yeo suggested hoisting Spanish colors to avoid alarming the caravel, but Amyas stoutly refused. "Counting it," he declared, "a mean thing to tell a lie in that way, unless in extreme danger or for great ends of state."

Holding their course until blocked by the next point, they turned. Cary took command of the largest boat with twenty men, while Amyas led the smaller one with fifteen more. Among them was John Brimblecombe, clad in his cassock and bands, wielding an old sword belonging to his uncle, which he prized immensely.

Approaching the mouth of the bight, they found, as expected, coral reefs and too many of them. Forced to hug the edge of the reef for a long distance, they searched for a passage. Those unfamiliar with the Indies gazed in admiration through the clear water at the living flowerbeds and subaquatic gardens of Nereus and Amphitrite. Suddenly, beneath them appeared what Yeo termed "a school of sharks"—some nearly as long as the boat themselves. They looked up wistfully, if not menacingly, from their wicked, scowling eyes.

"Jack," said Amyas, sitting beside him, "look how that big fellow eyes thee. He has surely taken a fancy to that plump hide of thine and thinks thou wouldst eat as tender as any sucking porker."

Jack turned very pale but remained silent.

Just then, the large shark, spotting a parrotfish emerging from a cleft in the coral, lunged upward. Two others joined the attack. The poor fish, finding no escape, leaped clean into the air, landing almost aboard the boat. Precisely where it had emerged, three or four great brown, shagreened snouts clashed together within two yards of Jack, displaying horrible rows of saw-like teeth before sinking sulkily back into the depths to await fresh bait. At this spectacle, Jack whispered softly, *"In manus tuas, Domine!"* and turned his gaze away from the sharks entirely.

Having navigated the reef, they entered the bight with a fair breeze. The caravel was no longer a musket-shot distant. Cary maneuvered her alongside before the Spaniards could reach their ordnance. Standing in the sternsheets, he shouted for them to surrender. The captain asked boldly in whose name they spoke. "In the name of common sense, ye dogs!" cried Will. "Do you not see that you are but fifty strong to our twenty?"

Scrambling up the side, the captain fired a pistol at him. Cary knocked him over, unwilling to shed needless blood. Upon this, the entire crew surrendered; some fell to their knees, others leaped overboard, and the prize was secured.

Meanwhile, Amyas had circled under the caravel's stern and boarded the second boat, which was moored closest to theirs, ensuring a sure capture. The Spaniards in her yielded without a blow, crying *"Misericordia,"* while the negroes leaped overboard and swam ashore like sea-dogs. The third boat, however, was pulling away. A notable adventure ensued: John Brimblecombe, desiring to distinguish himself, grabbed a boat-hook and clawed onto her stern, shouting, "Stay, ye Papists! Stay, Spanish dogs!" As expected, outnumbered ten to one, he was immediately pulled overboard, falling nose-first into the sea with the hook still fastened to the boat's stern.

Seized by a panic born of his vivid imagination—which filled the sea with the sharks he had just witnessed—he began roaring like a town bull. In his confusion, he failed to turn and climb aboard, instead striking out lustily after the fleeing Spanish boat. Whether driven by hope of snagging the trailing hook or sheer madness of valor, no one could divine. He swam on, his cassock floating behind him, resembling a great black monk-fish. Howling and puffing with his mouth full of saltwater, he screamed, "Stay, ye Spanish dogs! Help, all good fellows! See you not that I am a dead man? They are nuzzling already at my toes! He hath hold of my leg! My right thigh is bitten clean off! Oh that I were preaching in Hartland pulpit! Stay, Spanish dogs! Yield, Papist cowards, lest I make mincemeat of you; and take me aboard! Yield, I say, or my blood be on your heads! I am no Jonah; if he swallow me, he will never cast me up again! Into your hands..."

And so forth, in a frenzy surpassing even Panurge's memorable seasickness. Expecting him to be snapped by sharks or brained by the Spaniard's oars at any moment, the English fired a volley into the fugitives. They all leaped overboard like their fellows. Jack scrambled into the boat, drew his sword with one hand, and wiped water from his eyes with the other, beginning to slash the empty air like a lion while crying, "Yield, idolaters! Yield, Spanish dogs!" Eventually recovering his senses and realizing there was no one left to strike, he sat down panting in the sternsheets and began stripping off his hose.
