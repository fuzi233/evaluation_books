Since that fateful, yet delightfully chaotic night in Grosvenor Place, the heart of Mr. Harry Foker had undergone a transformation so profound that one might struggle to reconcile it with the reputation of a man previously regarded as a philosopher of the highest order. Recall, if you will, the sagacious counsel Harry had once dispensed to Arthur Pendennis; recall the early maturity that marked this gifted youth. By all rights, a constant regimen of self-indulgence, befitting a gentleman of his station and prospects, should have accelerated his cynicism, rendering him increasingly indifferent to the human race—with the singular exception, naturally, of Mr. Harry Foker himself. Yet, against all logic and precedent, this "wise" man fell prey to the universal misfortune that strikes everyone once or twice in a lifetime: he fell in love. Like Achilles, Ajax, Lord Nelson, or Adam himself, Harry could not escape the common lot. Now that his hour had arrived, the All-Conqueror named Love claimed its due.

That evening, upon returning to the Back Kitchen after bidding farewell to Arthur Pendennis at the staircase door in Lamb Court, the usual delights held no sway. The gin-twist and deviled turkey tasted like ash; the jests of his companions fell on deaf ears. Even when Mr. Hodgen, the chronicler of 'The Body Snatcher,' launched into a new ballad even more terrifying and hilarious than his famous composition, Foker offered the requisite polite applause—"Bravo, Hodgen!"—bound by social obligation and his status as a club leader, yet he failed to comprehend a single syllable of the lyrics. Exhausted and weary, he retreated to his private quarters, seeking refuge in a downy pillow. But sleep proved elusive. The moment he awoke from his feverish dreams, the visage of Miss Amory materialized, declaring, *"Here I am, your princess and beauty. You have found me, and henceforth, nothing else shall matter."*

Suddenly, his former pursuits and friendships seemed dreadfully stale. Until this moment, Harry had rarely mingled with women of his own social standing. When discussing them, he referred to them as "modest women"—a term implying a virtue that, heaven forbid, they possessed, yet which failed to compensate for the lack of livelier qualities found among his relatives. These relatives included his mother, who was good but not amusing; his cousins, the daughters of the Earl of Rosherville, who bored him to tears. One was a blue-stocking geologist; another rode horses and smoked cigars; a third held heterodox religious views, while her sister turned her room into a High Church chapel and fasted Fridays. Their ancestral home, Drummington, was a place Harry preferred the treadmill to. To the household staff, he was unpopular. Lord Erith deemed him vulgar; Harry, in turn, labeled Erith a dull prig and the Speaker's shame. The memory of Harry poking his uncle in the side with a billiard cue and calling him "old cock" remained a sore point, causing the Earl to avoid him like the plague, though he dared not sever ties entirely due to financial entanglements involving bank cheques and IOUs signed in ink.

Furthermore, there was the matter of Lady Ann Milton, the fifth daughter of the Earl of Gravesend. From infancy, she had been earmarked for Harry. The arrangement was as natural to her as it was to Harry, who, despite being the dirtiest little boy with black eyes from school, readily accepted the decree. His father, the old gentleman, had explained the bargain clearly: *"She has no money, but she is good blood and looks fine. If you refuse, you'll have your mother's jointure and two hundred a year during my life."* Harry, trusting his sire implicitly, had acquiesced instantly, noting merely, *"She's not a bad-looking girl."* Consequently, Lady Ann led a sheltered life, rarely venturing out, her sole narrative being her engagement to Harry. It was a story devoid of drama, much like the rest of her existence.

However, the instant Harry awoke the morning following Lady Clavering's dinner, the reality of his situation crashed down. Blanche's image loomed large, her grey eyes piercing, her smile winning. Her tune echoed in his mind: *"Yet round about the spot, ofttimes I hover..."* He hummed it pitifully from his bed, surrounded by crimson silk. On the walls, French prints depicted Turkish ladies, Greek lovers, and opera nymphs in tight pantaloons, alongside autographed sketches of Miss Calverley and Miss Rougemont. These were the treasures of honest Harry, a jovial fast man about town richer than most. Beside him lay a letter from Miss Calverley, written in a loose, misspelled scrawl, addressing him as "dear Hokey-pokey-fokey," along with invitations to various sporting events and a reminder of a promised dinner at the Elephant and Castle.

Now, all these pleasures seemed faint and repulsive. Who cared for Conkey Sam or the Worcestershire Nobber? The French prints stared vacuously; Calverley's spelling was atrocious. The prospect of dining with a thirty-seven-year-old spinster filled him with disgust.

His mother, Lady Agnes, noticed the change immediately. "Why do you frequent that wicked Spratt's?" she asked, concerned over his pallor. "Those billiards will kill you."
"It isn't the billiards," Harry muttered gloomily.
"Then it's the Back Kitchen," she suggested, offering to intervene with the landlady regarding the strength of the negus.
"Do, ma'am. Mrs. Cutts is kind," Harry replied, adding with a ghastly sigh, "But it isn't the Back Kitchen, neither."

Lady Agnes, ever obliging, accepted his confession wholeheartedly. She spun tales of his adventures to her friends, painting Spratt's as a place of high culture where gentlemen played billiards as ancestors played hazard, and the Back Kitchen as a hub of literary enlightenment where Dr. Johnson and Mr. Sheridan once drank wine. Meanwhile, Harry practiced his diplomatic skills, praising the Claverings' furniture and Sir Francis's wit, carefully selecting a date in his mother's pocket-book for an invitation to Grosvenor Street.

Upon drafting the card, Harry spotted a critical error. "You haven't asked Miss Whatdyecallem—Miss Emery, Lady Clavering's daughter."
"Oh, that little creature!" Lady Agnes dismissed.
"We *must* ask Miss Amory," Harry insisted. "Pendennis is very sweet on her. Does she sing well, ma'am?"
"I thought her rather forward... but I will ask her if you wish," Lady Agnes conceded. Thus, Miss Amory's name was added to the list.

Triumphantly embracing his mother, Harry retired to his apartment. Dressed in a magnificent dressing gown and puffing his pipe in silence, he lay on his ottoman, brooding over the upcoming introduction. His polyglot valet, Anatole, watched with silent curiosity. When Harry emerged from his room, he was notoriously difficult to please, cursing his trousers, boots, and shirts. Finally, he summoned Anatole.
"I say, Anatole, when I engaged you, didn't you say you could dress hair?"
"Yes, sir," came the reply.
*"Cherchy alors une paire de tongs,—et—curly moi un peu,"* Harry commanded casually.
Anatole, confused by his master's sudden coquettishness, fetched tongs from the elderly butler and borrowed hair curlers from Lady Agnes. Within moments, Harry's head was transformed into a nest of curls resembling a negro's.

Dressed to the splendor, Harry prepared to leave.
"At what time shall I order the drag, sir, to Miss Calverley's door?" Anatole whispered.
"Confound her! Put the dinner off—I can't go!" Harry snapped. Then, reconsidering, "No, hang it—I must go. Poyntz and Rougemont are coming. The drag at Pelham Corner at six."

The hired drag was not his own; he commandeered his personal carriage instead. For what purpose? To drive down to Lamb Court, Temple, passing Grosvenor Place en route. There, he peered upward at Miss Amory's pink curtains, satisfied, before speeding to Pen's chambers. Why such urgency? Why this sudden longing for a friend he had barely visited in two years?

To any young lady reading this, let it be known: when this specific mishap befalls you, the world flips upside down. Those you ignored yesterday become fascinating; those you adored become insipid. Letters sent to distant cousins vanish; suddenly, you crave the company of his mother, his father, his sisters. You will send frantic notes to his unmarried sister, crying if prevented from visiting. You will despise his younger brother for intruding. You will undergo the hot and cold fits of this pretty fever. Mothers have walked this path before you; fathers are usually the object of such passions. Brothers, more selfish and headstrong, rush toward their destiny when the charmer appears.

So, why was Henry Foker, Esquire, in such a hurry to see Arthur Pendennis? Because Pen had suddenly become invaluable. Perhaps Pen wasn't the rose, but he had stood near that fragrant flower of love.
