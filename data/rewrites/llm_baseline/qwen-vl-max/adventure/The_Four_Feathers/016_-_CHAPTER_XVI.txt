Ethne had forgotten everything—Colonel Durrance, the world, even time itself. From the moment Captain Willoughby placed that small, stained feather into her hand—a once-white plume now turned yellow with age and journeying—her thoughts had been consumed by one name: Harry Feversham. She had brought Willoughby into the secluded garden enclosure, drawn in by his tale, and since then she had lived only through the echoes of it. As he spoke, she filled in the gaps with memories of Harry’s voice, his eyes, the way he moved—each detail a spark rekindling a flame long buried beneath five years of silence and strength.

She had left behind the sunlit August garden, its vibrant flowers and golden light, and slipped back into those weary years when she had carried herself with quiet dignity, smiling through pain and loss. But now, as she lifted her head and saw Durrance standing at the hedge’s entrance, the past rushed forward like a wave.

“Hush,” she whispered to Willoughby, her face draining of color, her eyes closing tightly as if to block out a sudden wound. Yet there was no time for sorrow. The brief respite was over. She must return to the life she had built—one defined by duty, restraint, and a marriage she could not abandon. If she could not forget Harry Feversham, she would not let anyone know she hadn’t. Durrance must believe her love was pure, unshaken, and whole.

He stood at the gate of the enclosure, then stepped inside. He was too sharp, too quick to read people—especially her. It would be dangerous if he met Willoughby or learned of his visit. Ethne scanned the space around them—no escape. A creek lay ahead; thick hedges rose on three sides. Only one path led in or out, and Durrance blocked it.

“Keep still,” she murmured.

“You know him?” Willoughby asked.

“Of course. We served together at Suakin. I heard he’d gone blind. I’m glad it’s not true.” He glanced at Durrance’s steady walk, his confident stride.

“Speak lower,” Ethne said sharply. “It *is* true. He is blind.”

“One would never guess it,” Willoughby replied. “What can I say to him?”

“Say nothing.”

Durrance remained near the edge of the clearing, facing them. His posture was calm, almost serene. He called out softly, “Ethne.”

The quietness of his voice made it seem as though he could see.

“It’s impossible—he sees us,” Willoughby murmured.

“He sees nothing.”

Again, Durrance called, louder this time—his voice tinged with doubt.

“He’s not sure,” Ethne whispered. “Don’t move. Don’t breathe.”

She gripped Willoughby’s arm, her fingers tight, her body rigid. She stared at Durrance, her face taut with fear, her breath held. Willoughby watched her, struck by her stillness, her intensity. Something in her dread infected him. He began to feel the same invisible danger, though he knew nothing of what she feared.

“He’s coming closer,” he breathed.

“Not a sound. Not a twitch.”

“Why?”

“Because he must not know you’re here.”

Durrance advanced, passing in front of their bench. He stopped, facing them. Willoughby’s heart pounded. Surely he sees? His eyes were fixed. His stance relaxed, as if about to speak. Even Ethne, who knew better, felt a flicker of doubt.

“Ethne!” he called again—softly, as before.

No reply.

He shrugged, turned, and walked toward the creek. His back was to them now. But Ethne, trained by years of watching a man whose face no longer revealed his mind, read the subtle signs—the tilt of his head, the way he swung his stick—everything told her he was listening. Listening intently.

Her grip tightened.

They sat frozen. A minute passed. Then Durrance turned abruptly, stepping quickly toward the bench.

But Ethne was ready. She did not flinch. No rustle. No movement. Her hold on Willoughby kept him still.

“I wonder where she can be,” Durrance muttered aloud. And then he walked away.

Only when he vanished from sight did Ethne release his arm.

“That was close,” Willoughby said, finally allowed to speak.

“What if he’d sat down right on top of us?”

“Then he would have,” Ethne answered calmly. “But he didn’t.”

“You’ve told me everything?”

“So far as I remember.”

“And all this happened last spring?”

“The spring of last year,” Willoughby confirmed.

“I want to ask you something. Why didn’t you bring the feather to me last summer?”

“My leave was short. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin.”

“I see,” Ethne said quietly. “I hope you had good sport.”

“It wasn’t bad.”

Last summer, she had been free. Had Willoughby come then, bearing news of Harry’s redemption, everything might have been different. A silent cry rose in her heart: *Why didn’t you come?* But she gave no sign. Her composure was forged in hardship, and she spoke so lightly that Willoughby believed her curiosity was mere politeness.

“You might have written,” she suggested.

“Feversham didn’t think it urgent. A letter couldn’t explain it properly. He asked me to tell you when I could. I didn’t get the chance until now. Honestly, I thought Feversham might already be back.”

“Oh, no,” Ethne said. “There’s no possibility of that. Two feathers remain unredeemed before he’ll ask me to take back mine.”

Willoughby shook his head. “Feversham can’t persuade Castleton or Trench to retract their accusations the way he did me.”

“Why not?”

“Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai.”

“Killed?” Ethne laughed—a short, sharp sound of satisfaction.

Willoughby stared, stunned.

But her face betrayed no surprise—only relief. Ethne was of Celtic blood, and death, to her, was not the end. She could hate fiercely, and she hated these men deeply. Though she had given Harry the fourth feather to show her agreement, she cared little for that act. She was glad Castleton was gone.

“And Colonel Trench?” she asked.

“No,” Willoughby said. “He was captured during a reconnaissance. He’s a prisoner in Omdurman.”

“Ah.”

“I don’t think you understand what captivity in Omdurman means,” Willoughby said, gravely. “If you did, even if you disliked him, you’d feel pity.”

“Not I,” Ethne replied firmly.

“I’ll tell you what it implies.”

“No,” she said. “I don’t wish to hear about Trench. But you must go. I need to ask you one thing first.” She rose.

“What became of Mr. Feversham after you gave him the feather?”

“I told him he’d done all that could be expected. He accepted my advice. He boarded the first steamer from Suakin bound for Suez and left the Sudan.”

“I must find him,” Ethne said. “He must come back. Did he need money?”

“No. He still drew his allowance. He said he had more than enough.”

“I’m glad,” she said.

She bid Willoughby wait while she checked the garden. The place was empty. Durrance was gone. The house, with its striped sunblinds, seemed asleep. The birds were quiet. Only bees hummed lazily among the blooms, and a boy’s voice drifted across the creek.

She returned.

“You may go now,” she said. “I cannot pretend friendship for you, Captain Willoughby—but thank you for coming. You’re returning to Kingsbridge? I hope so. I do not wish Colonel Durrance to know of your visit—or anything you’ve told me.”

“But Durrance was Feversham’s great friend,” Willoughby objected.

“He doesn’t know about the feathers,” Ethne said. “And I do not wish him to. He does not know why my engagement to Feversham ended. I will not let him learn it. Your story would reveal too much.”

“Why?”

She looked at him, calm and direct. “Because I am engaged to Colonel Durrance.”

It was her deepest fear—that he suspected she did not love him. If he learned Harry had redeemed himself, he would insist she choose between them. And she refused to give up the only thing left to him—her loyalty. She was bound by honor. To betray that now, after Harry’s sacrifice, would be a mockery.

Willoughby whistled softly. “Engaged to Jack Durrance?”

“Then I’ve wasted my time bringing you this feather,” he said, pointing to it in her hand.

She pulled her hand back sharply, as if afraid he might take it.

“I am most grateful,” she said.

“It’s a bit of a muddle, isn’t it?” Willoughby remarked. “Rough on Feversham, perhaps. Rough on Durrance, too—when you think about it.”

Then he looked at her—her careful handling of the feather, the glow in her eyes when he spoke of Harry, the eagerness in her questions.

“I shouldn’t wonder if it’s rough on you, too, Miss Eustace.”

She did not answer.

They walked to the water’s edge. She hurried him down, determined he sail unseen.

But Mrs. Adair had seen everything. From her window, she had watched Ethne meet Durrance, followed by the boat, the man disembarking, the meeting in the trees. She had noted the silence, the hesitation. And when Durrance returned alone, she guessed—*Could it be Harry Feversham?*

She saw Durrance enter the house, asking for Ethne.

“She’s not in the garden,” Mrs. Adair said.

“No,” he replied. “I went in and called. It was empty.”

“Indeed?”

“Are you going?”

“Yes, home.”

“Perhaps you’ll dine with us tonight. Eight o’clock.”

“Thank you. I’ll be pleased.”

He lingered by the window, idly swinging the blind’s tassel.

“I didn’t know until today that you planned for me to return home while Ethne stayed with you until I found out if a cure was possible,” he said. “Very kind of you, Mrs. Adair.”

“It was natural,” she replied.

“And when did you learn of my condition?”

“The day after Calder’s telegram reached her from Wadi Halfa, I suppose.”

Durrance smiled faintly. “Oh, so you knew of that telegram.”

“I thought you didn’t,” Mrs. Adair said. “Ethne asked me not to mention it.”

“Of course I knew,” he said, and walked out.

Mrs. Adair’s thoughts turned to the garden. She had seen Durrance call aloud—and yet someone had been there, hidden, silent. Her suspicion grew.

Then she saw Ethne and Willoughby emerge from the trees. Was it Feversham? She must know.

She ran down to the bank.

As Willoughby pushed off from the shore, he saw her—Mrs. Adair—standing there, eyes wide with disappointment.

She called to Ethne.

“I saw you cross the lawn from my window,” she said.

“Yes?” Ethne replied.

Mrs. Adair did not move.

Ethne sighed. “I was talking to Captain Willoughby. You don’t know him, I think?”

“No,” he said, raising his hat. “But I know Mrs. Adair by name. I know friends of yours—Durrance, for instance—and of course I knew—”

A glance from Ethne cut him short.

He turned to the boat.

“Of course, what?” Mrs. Adair asked, smiling.

“Of course I knew of you, Mrs. Adair.”

She understood—what he had nearly said was not meant to be spoken.

“Captain Willoughby,” she repeated to herself.

“You belong to Colonel Durrance’s regiment?”

“No,” he said. “North Surrey.”

“Ah! Mr. Feversham’s old regiment,” she said pleasantly.

Willoughby had walked straight into her trap.

But Ethne turned to her, calm and clear. “We’ve been talking of Mr. Feversham.”

She held out her hand. “Good-bye, Captain.”

Mrs. Adair interrupted. “Colonel Durrance has gone home, but he dines with us tonight. I came to tell you—and now I’m glad, because I can invite your friend to lunch.”

Willoughby, already half in the boat, stepped back. “That’s very kind—”

“It is,” Ethne said, “but Captain Willoughby’s leave is short. We mustn’t keep him.”

He looked at her, pleading.

She met his gaze—quiet, firm, unyielding.

He bowed, climbed into the boat, and pushed off.

“It’s a little rough on me, too, perhaps, Miss Eustace,” he said.

She laughed—a soft, clear laugh.

And as they walked back to the terrace, she opened her hand and showed Mrs. Adair the small white feather.

She laughed again.

But she said nothing.

It was her secret. And hers alone.
