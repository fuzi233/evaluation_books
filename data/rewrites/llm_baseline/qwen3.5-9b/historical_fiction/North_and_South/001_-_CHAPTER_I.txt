"Wooed and married and a'."

"Edith!" said Margaret, gently, "Edith!"

But as Margaret half suspected, Edith had already succumbed to sleep. She lay curled upon the sofa in the back drawing-room of Harley Street, looking exquisitely lovely in her white muslin and blue ribbons. If Titania had ever been attired in such simple finery and allowed to drift off upon a crimson damask sofa within a private parlour, Edith might easily have been mistaken for the fairy queen herself. Margaret was once again struck by her cousin's undeniable beauty. Having grown up together since infancy, everyone had remarked upon Edith's prettiness save for Margaret, who had remained largely indifferent to it until recently. Only now, with the impending loss of her constant companion looming on the horizon, did Margaret begin to truly appreciate every sweet quality and charm Edith possessed.

They had been discussing wedding dresses, ceremonial details, Captain Lennox, and his descriptions of life awaiting Edith at Corfu, where his regiment was stationed. They had also debated the formidable challenge of keeping a piano in tune during the Scottish travels that would inevitably follow their marriage. However, Margaret's voice had gradually softened into a drowse, and after a brief pause, she realized that despite the lively chatter spilling from the adjacent room, Edith had rolled herself into a soft sphere of muslin, ribbon, and silken curls, drifting peacefully into an afternoon nap.

Margaret had intended to confide in her cousin about her own visions for the future: a life in the country parsonage where her parents resided, a place where her brightest holidays had always been spent, even though for the last decade Aunt Shaw's house in London had served as her home. Yet, lacking a listener, she was forced to brood silently on the coming changes. It was a happy contemplation, albeit tinged with regret at being parted indefinitely from her gentle aunt and beloved cousin. As she imagined the joy of becoming the sole daughter in Helstone parsonage, fragments of conversation drifted from the next room.

Aunt Shaw was speaking with the five or six ladies who had dined there, whose husbands remained in the dining-room. These women were familiar acquaintances of the household; neighbors whom Mrs. Shaw termed "friends" simply because she dined with them more frequently than others, and because requests for favors between the households were exchanged casually before luncheon. These ladies and their husbands had been invited specifically to share a farewell dinner honoring Edith's upcoming nuptials. Edith had initially objected to the arrangement, noting that Captain Lennox was expected to arrive via a late train that very evening. However, though she was a spoilt child, she lacked a strong will of her own and yielded when she discovered her mother had strictly ordered the seasonal delicacies traditionally believed to alleviate excessive grief at farewell banquets. Content with this concession, Edith leaned back in her chair, idly fiddling with her food, appearing grave and absent-minded, while those around her delighted in the witty remarks of Mr. Grey, the gentleman who invariably sat at the foot of the table at Mrs. Shaw's gatherings and had requested that Edith provide music later in the drawing-room. Mr. Grey proved particularly agreeable tonight, causing the gentlemen to linger downstairs longer than usual—a decision that proved wise, judging by the snippets of conversation Margaret overheard.

"I suffered too much myself," one lady remarked. "Not that I wasn't extremely happy with the poor dear General, but disparity of age is a significant drawback—one I was resolved Edith should not encounter. Without any maternal bias, I foresaw the dear child was likely to marry early; indeed, I often said I was certain she would wed before nineteen. I had a prophetic feeling regarding Captain Lennox..." The voice dropped to a whisper, but Margaret could easily fill in the gap.

The path of true love in Edith's case had run remarkably smooth. Mrs. Shaw had acquiesced to this sentiment, expressing that she had actually encouraged the match, despite it falling short of the high expectations many held for a young, pretty heiress. Mrs. Shaw declared that her only child should marry for love—and sighed emphatically, implying that love had not been her primary motivation when she wed the General. Indeed, Mrs. Shaw seemed to derive greater enjoyment from the romance of the current engagement than her daughter did. While Edith was thoroughly and properly in love, she would undoubtedly have preferred a substantial residence in Belgravia to all the picturesque allure of life at Corfu. The very aspects that made Margaret glow with anticipation caused Edith to pretend to shiver and shudder; partly for the pleasure of being coaxed out of her reluctance by her devoted lover, and partly because anything resembling a gypsy or makeshift existence was genuinely distasteful to her. Nevertheless, had anyone offered a magnificent house, a vast estate, and a noble title, Edith would have clung to Captain Lennox as long as the temptation persisted. Once the novelty faded, she might well harbor regrets that her fiancé could not embody every desirable trait simultaneously. In this regard, she was merely following her mother's example, who, after deliberately marrying General Shaw out of mere respect for his character and status, quietly lamented her hard fate of being united to a man she could not love.

"I have spared no expense on her trousseau," another voice added. "She possesses all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarves the General gave me, though I shall never wear them again."

"She is a fortunate girl," replied another voice, which Margaret recognized as belonging to Mrs. Gibson, a lady taking a keen interest in the conversation due to her own daughter having married just weeks prior. "Helen had her heart set on an Indian shawl, but when I learned the exorbitant price, I was forced to decline. She will be quite envious to learn Edith has received such treasures. What kind are they? Delhi? With the lovely little borders?"

Margaret heard her aunt's voice again, seemingly rising from her half-reclining posture to gaze toward the dimly lit back drawing-room. "Edith! Edith!" she cried, then sank back as if weary by the effort.

Margaret stepped forward. "Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can do?"

All the ladies exclaimed, "Poor child!" upon hearing this distressing news, and the tiny lapdog in Mrs. Shaw's arms began to bark, agitated by the outburst of pity. "Hush, Tiny! You naughty little girl! You will wake your mistress. It was only to ask Edith if she would instruct Newton to bring down her shawls; perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?"

Margaret ascended to the old nursery at the very top of the house, where Newton was busy preparing laces required for the wedding. As Newton departed (accompanied by muttered complaints) to retrieve the shawls—which had already been displayed four or five times that day—Margaret surveyed the nursery. It was the first room in that house she had known intimately nine years ago, when she arrived, untamed from the New Forest, to share the home, play, and lessons of her cousin Edith. She recalled the dark, somber atmosphere of the London nursery, overseen by an austere and ceremonious nurse who was obsessively particular about clean hands and unblemished frocks. She remembered the first tea served there—served separately from her father and aunt, who dined far below, an infinite depth of stairs away; for unless she was soaring in the sky, the children reasoned, her family must be buried deep in the earth's bowels.

At home, before moving to Harley Street, her mother's dressing-room had served as her nursery. Since they maintained early hours in the country parsonage, Margaret had always eaten her meals with her parents. Oh, how vividly the tall, stately eighteen-year-old remembered the tears shed with such wild, passionate grief by the little girl of nine, hiding her face beneath the bedclothes on that first night! How she had been bid not to cry by the nurse lest it disturb Miss Edith, and how she had sobbed more quietly and bitterly until her newly encountered, grand, and pretty aunt arrived softly upstairs with Mr. Hale to introduce himself to his little sleeping daughter. Then, the little Margaret had hushed her sobs, striving to lie motionless as if asleep, fearing her grief might upset her father, which she deemed improper after the months of hopeful planning and preparation undertaken at home to accommodate her elevated circumstances and her father's temporary visit to London.

Now, the tall girl loved the old nursery, despite its dismantled state, casting a cat-like glance of regret at the idea of leaving it forever in three days.

"Ah, Newton!" she said. "I believe we shall all be sorry to depart from this dear old room."

"Indeed, miss, I shan't for one. My eyes are not as sharp as they used to be, and the light here is so poor I cannot see to mend laces except near the window, where there is a dreadful draft—enough to freeze one to death."

"Well, I daresay you'll have ample light and warmth in Naples. Keep as much of your darning as you can until then. Thank you, Newton; I can take them down yourself—you're busy."

Thus, Margaret descended, laden with shawls, inhaling their spicy Eastern fragrance. Her aunt requested she serve as a living mannequin to display the garments, as Edith remained asleep.
