Margaret had dubbed the stranger who’d carried an injured Marianne half a mile through the lashing autumn storm back to Barton Cottage “Marianne’s preserver,” a name far more warmhearted than precise. He called on them at dawn the very next day to check on her welfare, and Mrs. Dashwood greeted him with far more than formal politeness: her welcome brimmed with the unreserved kindness sparked by both Sir John’s glowing accounts of him and her own deep gratitude. Every moment of that first visit only confirmed for Willoughby that the family accident had introduced him to was full of sharp good sense, gentle elegance, fierce mutual affection, and the soft, unforced comfort of a home where love ruled over formality. He had not needed a second meeting to be convinced of the sisters’ personal charm, either.

Elinor Dashwood had a soft, clear complexion, neat regular features, and a quiet, graceful figure that drew admiring eyes wherever she went. But Marianne was still far handsomer. Her frame, less strictly symmetrical than her sister’s, was far more striking for its extra height, and her face was so bright and lovely that calling her simply “a beautiful girl” did far less violence to the truth than most empty compliments. Her skin was warm and sun-kissed, but so translucent that her whole complexion glowed; every line of her face was soft and pleasing, her smile sweet and infectious, and in her large dark eyes burned a fire, a spirit, a bright eagerness that no one could look upon without delight.

At first, Marianne held that light back from Willoughby, flustered and embarrassed remembering how he’d found her fallen helpless on the hillside, her ankle twisted too badly to walk. But that shyness faded fast. When her nerves settled, and she saw that beneath his perfect gentlemanly manners he was unreserved, lively, and quick to laugh—most of all, when he confessed he was passionately, wildly fond of both music and dancing—she gave him a look of such unreserved approval that he spent every remaining minute of his visit talking almost entirely to her.

It took no more than a mention of any favorite shared amusement to get her talking. She could not stay silent on subjects she loved, and there was no shyness, no performative reserve in the things she said. They quickly realized their tastes in music and dancing were perfectly aligned, rooted in identical judgments of what made each art form feel alive. Emboldened to dig deeper into his opinions, she began to ask him about books, bringing out her most beloved authors and gushing over their best lines with such rapturous delight that any man of twenty-five would have to be impossibly dull not to immediately embrace the beauty of those works, even if he had never cared for them before. Their tastes were shockingly, perfectly alike. They idolized the same books, the same exact passages; and on the rare occasion a tiny difference of opinion popped up, it vanished the second Marianne laid out her argument and those bright dark eyes lit up to make her case. Willoughby agreed with every one of her judgments, caught every spark of her enthusiasm, and long before his visit ended, they talked to each other like old, long-separated friends.

“Well, Marianne,” Elinor said the second he walked out the door, “I’d say you’ve had a very productive morning. You’ve already established his opinion on nearly every important topic under the sun. You know exactly what he thinks of Cowper and Scott, you’re certain he appreciates their beauty exactly as he ought, and you’ve got full assurance he doesn’t admire Pope more than is reasonable. But how on earth is your acquaintance going to last long at this pace? You’ll burn through every favorite topic before you know it. One more meeting and you’ll have covered his thoughts on picturesque scenery and second marriages, and then there will be nothing at all left to talk about.”

“Elinor!” Marianne cried, half-indignant, half-playful. “Is that fair? Is that just? Are my ideas really so shallow? I see what you mean, though. I was too at ease, too happy, too frank. I broke every silly little rule of decorum everyone else clings to. I was open and sincere when I should have been reserved, lifeless, dull, and deceitful. If I’d only talked of the weather and the roads, and only spoken once every ten minutes, you’d never have teased me for this.”

“My love,” their mother cut in gently, “you mustn’t be cross with Elinor. She was only jesting. I would scold her myself if she ever tried to smother the joy of your new friendship with this dear man.” Marianne’s irritation melted away instantly.

For his part, Willoughby gave every possible sign that he adored their company, and was determined to grow closer to them. He came by every single day. At first his excuse was checking on Marianne’s sprained ankle, but their welcome grew warmer and warmer with every visit, until the pretense was entirely unnecessary even before Marianne had fully recovered. She was stuck confined to the cottage for days, but no confinement had ever felt less tedious. Willoughby was sharp, quick-witted, vividly imaginative, full of life, and open and affectionate to everyone he met. He was perfectly made to win Marianne’s heart: on top of all that, he was strikingly handsome, and carried a natural fiery warmth that her own bright spirit only stoked higher, endearing him to her more than anything else ever could.

His company gradually became her greatest joy. They read poetry aloud, talked for hours on end, sang duets together; his musical talent was considerable, and he read every line of prose or verse with the full sensitivity and fire that poor Edward, to Elinor’s quiet regret, had never possessed. To Mrs. Dashwood, he was as faultless as he was to Marianne. Elinor could find nothing to criticize in him but one small flaw, a trait he shared with Marianne that made them all the more compatible: he spoke far too freely what he thought on every occasion, with no care for who might be listening, or what social niceties might dictate. He judged other people quickly and shared those judgments openly, set aside general politeness to give his full, undivided attention to anyone he cared for, and brushed off the trivial formalities of high society far too easily. Elinor could not approve of that lack of caution, no matter how fervently both Willoughby and Marianne tried to defend it.

Marianne soon realized her dark, desperate thought at sixteen and a half—that she would never meet a man who lived up to her idea of perfect goodness and passion—had been foolish and wrong. Willoughby was everything she had ever daydreamed of in that unhappy hour, and every brighter moment since: the kind of man who could win her whole heart, and his every action made it clear his affection was just as earnest, and his spirit just as strong. Her mother, who had not once let her mind drift to the thought of their marriage even when she learned Willoughby was set to inherit a large fortune, found herself hoping and expecting it before the week was out, secretly congratulating herself that she might soon have two such wonderful sons-in-law: Edward for Elinor, and Willoughby for Marianne.

Colonel Brandon’s quiet fondness for Marianne, which his friends had spotted long before, only became noticeable to Elinor when everyone else stopped paying attention to it entirely. Sir John, Lady Middleton, and Mrs. Jennings were all far too busy fawning over Willoughby, the far more fortunate rival, to notice. The silly teasing they had once aimed at the Colonel as a joke faded away now that his feelings were real, and would have only felt cruel directed at a man so clearly soft on the girl he’d grown to admire. Elinor was forced, against her will, to admit that the fondness Mrs. Jennings had once joked about for her own amusement was very real, and that even though Marianne and Willoughby were perfectly matched in their fiery, vivid tempers, the Colonel’s quiet, steady opposite nature had not stopped him from falling for her. She saw it with quiet concern. What could a quiet thirty-five-year-old gentleman hope to win, against a bright, lively twenty-five-year-old who matched Marianne’s every spark? She could not even wish for the Colonel to succeed, so she heartily wished he would stop caring for her sister altogether. She liked him, though: even with his quiet gravity and reserve, she found him a deeply interesting man. His manners were serious but gentle, and his reserve seemed to come from some old, lingering grief, not a naturally sullen temper. Sir John had dropped hints of old heartbreaks and disappointments long ago, that confirmed her suspicion he was a deeply unfortunate man, and she regarded him with genuine respect and compassion.

She might have pitied and esteemed him all the more because Willoughby and Marianne treated him like a trivial afterthought. Prejudiced against him for being neither young nor lively, they seemed determined to dismiss all his good qualities out of hand.

“Brandon is exactly the sort of man,” Willoughby said one day, when they were talking of him together, “that everyone says is perfectly nice, but nobody actually cares about. Everyone is happy to see him walk in a room, but no one ever remembers to actually talk to him.”

“That is exactly what I think of him!” Marianne cried at once.

“Don’t brag about that, though,” Elinor said. “You’re both being deeply unfair. He is highly regarded by everyone at the park, and I never see him without making a point of having a proper conversation with him.”

“That he’s your personal favorite is certainly a point in his favor,” Willoughby replied. “But as for everyone else’s approval? That’s an insult in itself. What man who could earn anyone else’s genuine interest would ever put up with the indignity of being called ‘nice’ by Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings?”

“But maybe your disdain and Marianne’s dismissal makes up for their praise,” Elinor shot back. “If their approval is barely an insult, your unfair judgment is far kinder. They are no more unthinking in their praise than you two are prejudiced and unjust.”

“You’re being sharp with me just to defend your little protégé,” Willoughby laughed.

“My protégé, as you call him, is a man of good sense—and good sense will always appeal to me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has traveled the world, been abroad, read widely, and has a thoughtful, sharp mind. He’s given me so much useful information on all kinds of subjects, and he always answers my questions with perfect good humor and politeness.”

“That means,” Marianne scoffed, “he told you the East Indies are very hot, and the mosquitoes are very annoying.”

“He would have told me that if I’d asked him,” Elinor said, “but I happened to already know those things.”

“Maybe his observations stretch even further,” Willoughby teased. “He might have mentioned nabobs, gold mohrs, and palanquins, too.”

“I daresay his experiences stretch far further than you are willing to fairly admit. But why do you dislike him so much?”

“I don’t dislike him, not really,” Willoughby said. “I think he’s a very respectable man, who everyone says hello to and nobody remembers. He’s got more money than he can spend, more time than he knows what to do with, and two new coats every year.”

“Add to that,” Marianne put in, “that he has no genius, no taste, no spirit. His mind has no sparkle, his feelings no fire, and his voice no warmth or expression.”

“You both judge his whole character all at once, based entirely on what you imagine about him, so my praise of him must sound very dull by comparison,” Elinor said. “All I can say is that he’s a sensible man, well-bred, well-informed, gentle in his manners, and I believe he has a very kind, good heart.”

“Miss Dashwood,” Willoughby protested, “you’re being unkind to me now. You’re trying to argue me into liking him against my will, and it won’t work. You’ll find I’m as stubborn as you are clever. I have three unanswerable, unchanging reasons to dislike Colonel Brandon: he told me it would rain the day I wanted perfect sunshine, he criticized the way I hung my curricle, and I cannot convince him to buy my beautiful brown mare. If it makes you happy, I will admit every other part of his character is completely irreproachable. But in return for that painful concession, you cannot deny me the right to dislike him just as much as I ever did.”
