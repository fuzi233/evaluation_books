The following day was Palm Sunday, known in the olive-rich valleys of the Valdarno simply as Olive Sunday. The morning sun broke with a crystalline clarity, washing away the lingering damp of the previous night's rain. Once more, Savonarola ascended the pulpit of San Marco, gazing upon a congregation whose faith remained unshaken. In a tone of calm, sorrowful sincerity, he proclaimed his readiness to die, seeing his own doom clearly before him, undisturbed by any other vision. He offered the benediction, and beneath his gaze rose the faces of men and women lifted in veneration and love. Then, having descended the steps, he turned his back upon that sight forever.

Before the sun had set, Florence erupted into chaos. The passions stirred the day prior had smoldered through the quiet morning, now bursting forth with a fury aided by design and sanctioned by official complicity. The disturbance began at the Duomo, where certain Compagnacci attempted to disrupt the evening sermon the Piagnoni had gathered to hear. But once blood was drawn and the gathering turned to affray, the cry rang out: "To San Marco! Fire to San Marco!"

Long before darkness fully claimed the sky, the church and convent were besieged by an enraged, swelling multitude. Resistance was fierce. The monks, long aware of the hostility brewing outside, kept arms within their walls; some fought with such vigor in their long white tunics that they resembled Knights Templar. Even Savonarola's command could not quell the instinct for self-defense that surged beneath the Dominican serge. Laymen who had refused to flee also fought desperately. From the high altar near the great crucifix came the crack of gunfire; from the convent roof, stones and burning embers rained down; within the cloisters, swords clashed in close combat. Despite the overwhelming force of the attackers, the assault raged until the depths of night.

The government's demonstrations had targeted the convent directly. Early in the attack, guards were summoned not to disperse the mob, but to order everyone inside to lay down arms, to command all laymen to depart, and to demand that Savonarola himself leave Florentine territory within twelve hours. Had Savonarola obeyed then, he likely would have been torn apart; he was willing to go, but his friends restrained him. It was deemed too perilous even for men of high standing to exit via the garden wall, yet among those who chose to flee was Francesco Valori, hoping to rally rescue forces from without.

Now, as the night grew deep—and the struggle could hardly endure much longer, with the Compagnacci poised to breach the library where Savonarola prayed with the brethren who had laid down their arms—a second body of guards arrived. Commissioned by the Signoria, they demanded the surrender of Fra Girolamo and his two coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

A roar of triumphant hate echoed as the lantern-light revealed the friar emerging from the convent door, escorted by a guard offering no safety save that of the prison. The struggle shifted instantly to a race: who should reach the stream rushing up the narrow street first to witness the Prophet carried back in ignominy to the Piazza, where he had dared them the day before? Who should stand closest to hurl insults, or worse, to strike and trample him? This was easy for some armed Compagnacci, who mingled freely with the guards.

As Savonarola felt himself dragged and shoved through the jeering crowd, as lanterns were raised to reveal mocking faces, and as he endured spittle, blows, and kicks accompanied by the grossest insults, it seemed to him that the bitterest cup of life had been drained. If men judged him guilty and thirsted for his blood, death alone awaited him. Yet the deepest bitterness cannot be wrung from the lips by external force; the lowest depth of resignation is not found in martyrdom, but only when one covers one's head in silence and feels, "I am not worthy to be a martyr; the Truth shall prosper, but not by me."

That brief, imperfect triumph of humiliating the friar, who soon vanished beneath the doorway of the Old Palace, was merely like the taste of blood to a tiger. Were there not the houses of the hypocrite's friends to be sacked? Already, half the armed multitude, too far in the rear to share heavily in the siege, had turned to the more profitable work of attacking wealthy homes. They did not plunder with planless greed, but with discriminating selection, striking only the residences of chief Piagnoni. This showed the riot was guided, the rabble with clubs and staves well officered by sword-girt Compagnacci. Next in rank of criminality stood the ambitious Francesco Valori, suspected of plotting with the Frate to make himself a Doge or Gonfaloniere for life. And there was the grey-haired man who, eight months prior, had raised his voice in ferocious demands for justice against five fellow citizens. He had barely escaped San Marco to experience what *others* called justice: his house surrounded by an angry, greedy throng, his wife struck dead by an arrow, and himself murdered on his way to answer a summons to the Palazzo by the swords of men named Ridolfi and Tornabuoni.

Thus, this Masque of the Furies, called Riot, was enacted in Florence through the hours of night and into the early morning.

Yet the chief director remained invisible, issuing orders from a private retreat, for his name was too high to let his red feather wave amidst the work done before dawn. The retreat was the same house and room in a quiet street between Santa Croce and San Marco where we had seen Tito pay a secret visit to Dolfo Spini. Here the Captain of the Compagnacci sat through this memorable night, receiving visitors who came and went, some disguised as armed Compagnacci, others dressed obscurely without visible arms. Abundant wine lay on the table, cups ready for chance comers. Though Spini guarded against excess, he drank enough to heighten the excitement produced by the ceaseless news brought to him.

Among the obscurely dressed visitors, Ser Ceccone was one of the most frequent. As the hours advanced toward the morning twilight, he remained Spini's constant companion, joined by Francesco Cei, who was then in careless hiding in Florence, expecting his banishment revoked once the Frate's fall was accomplished.

The tapers had burned themselves into low, shapeless masses, and holes in the shutters were marked by a sombre outward light, when Spini, having risen and paced with an angry flush on his face after some talk with those two unmilitary companions, burst out:

"The devil spit him! He shall pay for it, though. Ha, ha! The claws shall be down on him when he little thinks of them. So *he* was to be the great man after all! He has been pretending to chuck everything towards my cap, as if I were a blind beggar, all the while winking and filling his own *scarella*. I should like to hang skins about him and set my hounds on him! And he has that fine ruby of mine, which I was a fool enough to give him yesterday. Malediction! And he was laughing at me in his sleeve two years ago, spoiling the best plan that ever was laid. I was a fool for trusting myself with a rascal who had long-twisted contrivances that nobody could see to the end but himself."

"A Greek, too," said Francesco Cei, a slight smile of amusement on his face at Spini's fuming, "who dropped into Florence with gems packed about him. You did not choose your confidant very wisely, my Dolfo."

"He is a cursed deal cleverer than you, Francesco, and handsomer too," Spini retorted, turning on his associate with a general desire to worry anything that presented itself.

"I humbly conceive," said Ser Ceccone, "that Messer Francesco's poetic genius will outweigh—"

"Yes, yes, rub your hands! I hate that notary's trick of yours," interrupted Spini, whose patronage consisted largely in this sort of frankness. "But here comes Taddeo, or somebody: now is the time! What news, eh?" he continued as two Compagnacci entered with heated looks.

"Bad!" said one. "The people have made up their minds they were going to sack Soderini's house, and now that they have been balked, we shall have them turning on us if we do not take care. I suspect there are some Mediceans buzzing about among them, and we may see them attacking your palace over the bridge before long, unless we can find another bait for them."

"I have it!" cried Spini. Seizing Taddeo by the belt, he drew him aside to give directions while the other went on telling Cei how the Signoria had interfered regarding Soderini's house.

"Ecco!" exclaimed Spini, presently giving Taddeo a slight push toward the door. "Go, and make quick work."
