How long I slept against the cool stone of the subterranean larder, I cannot say. My senses had finally surrendered to the rich, encompassing olfactory landscape of the chamber—a vast culinary archive fragrant with the pungent tang of salt-cured zitidar, the earthy, fermented notes of subterranean moss-roots, and the delicate, powdery scent of desiccated spice-blooms. As a man who had long found profound meaning in the traditional agrarian rhythms of sustenance, I recognized this storeroom not merely as a utilitarian vault, but as a staggering record of Martian food history. 

But my reprieve was shattered by a brutal percussive violence that tore through the delicate sonic tapestry of the ancient chamber. A fusillade of shots rang out, a deafening, industrial dissonance of modern weaponry that battered the mind and entirely disrupted the acoustic ecology of our refuge. 

In an instant, I was upon my feet. From a grand doorway at the far end of the larder, a dozen lesser therns leveled their smoking rifles. Around me lay the bodies of my companions, their lives violently extinguished, their native dialects silenced forever. Only Thuvia and the great green elder Tars Tarkas—who, like myself, had been resting near the fragrant casks of preserved fungoids upon the floor—had escaped this jarring rain of death. 

As I rose, the therns lowered their weapons, their faces contorted in a dissonance of chagrin and alarm. It was then I weaponized the dominant lexicon. I understood the subtle power dynamics embedded in their oppressive tongue, and I borrowed its harsh, guttural imperatives to assert a false authority.

“What means this?” I roared in their own dialect, projecting the acoustic cadence of a master. “Is Sator Throg to be murdered by his own vassals?”

“Have mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” cried one of the guards, the syllables of his native tongue buckling under the weight of sudden terror. They edged toward the doorway, desperate to escape the resonant wrath of the mighty one.

“Ask them their mission here,” Thuvia murmured at my elbow, her voice a soft, rhythmic counterpoint to the harshness of the room.

“What do you here, fellows?” I demanded.

“Two from the outer world are at large within the dominions of the therns,” the guard stammered. “We sought them at the command of the Father of Therns. One was white with black hair, the other a huge green warrior.” He cast a suspicious glance toward Tars Tarkas, whose deep, weathered features carried the proud, agrarian history of a people who respected the harsh terroir of the sea bottoms.

“Here, then, is one of them,” Thuvia spoke flawlessly in the high thern vernacular, indicating the Thark. “And if you will look upon this dead man by the door, perhaps you will recognize the other. It was left for Sator Throg and his poor slaves to accomplish what the lesser therns of the guard could not—we have killed one and captured the other. And now, in your discordant stupidity, you have killed all but myself, and like to have killed the mighty Sator Throg himself.”

The guards looked deeply unsettled. 

“Had they not better throw these bodies to the plant men and return to their quarters, O Mighty One?” Thuvia asked me, weaving her words into the established hierarchy of their courtly language.

“Yes; do as Thuvia bids you,” I commanded. 

As the men gathered the fallen, the one who stooped to lift the body of the actual Sator Throg paused. I saw his eyes trace the dead man's features, and then he stole a furtive, searching glance toward me. Though I wielded the dominant lexicon with grammatical precision, the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience betrayed me; I could not perfectly emulate the soulless arrogance of a true holy thern. He suspected the truth. Yet, the paralyzing power dynamics of his own language bound him to silence. As he bore the body away, the final glimpse of his profile revealed a cunning, triumphant smirk. The cultural memory of our deception would soon be passed to his superiors. 

With the grim procession gone, Thuvia urged us to resume our flight. “It bodes no good for us, O Prince,” she whispered, her words returning to the intimate, fluid dialect of her ancestors. “Even though he dared not accuse you in error, Matai Shang will demand a closer scrutiny. That would prove fatal.”

I shrugged, the ache of my wounds a sharp reminder of our mortality. How I longed for the traditional, restorative broths and the aromatic, healing botanical salves crafted by the green Martian women—preparations that honored the body’s connection to the earth. In an hour, their profound knowledge of native ingredients would have made me whole. Now, despite the deep sleep, I felt the heavy despair of our predicament. 

“What will the fellow do first, Thuvia?” I asked. 

“He will go directly to the Father of Therns. Since he is a thorian among the lesser therns, Matai Shang will not keep him waiting. Within an hour, the courts and gardens will be filled with searchers.”

“What is the shortest way out of this subterranean Hades?”

“Straight to the top of the cliffs, Prince, and through the inner gardens. But it is hopeless. A million fighting-men guard the continuous fortifications of the Mountains of Otz. None could go a stone’s throw without detection.”

“Can we not better make the attempt after dark?” asked Tars Tarkas, his voice carrying the slow, measured rhythm of a tribal elder accustomed to charting the cyclical movements of the stars. 

“There would be a little better chance by night,” Thuvia agreed. 

To check the hour, Thuvia led us through winding passages smelling of damp limestone and ancient dust, until we reached an opening that overlooked the Valley Dor. The sun was setting—a huge, bleeding orb dipping below the western range, casting long, mythic shadows across the crimson sward. On Mars, there is no twilight. The transition is sudden, plunging the world from searing heat to the intense, bitter cold of night, a cyclical pacing as old as the planet itself. 

Beneath us on a balcony, a Holy Thern pulled his scarlet robe tight against the chill. Below, in the gorgeous forest, herds of plant men fed. Standing aloft upon their toes and mighty tails, their talons pruned every available leaf and twig. It was a revelation: the meticulous trimming of the trees, which I had earlier mistaken for the manicured playground of a cultured people, was in fact the grim, everyday domestic routine of these creatures—a brutal, agrarian harvest.

As we watched, a canoe emerged from the river Iss, bearing a dozen red Martians from the outer world. The herald on the balcony beneath us voiced a shrill, piercing wail that shattered the quiet valley, an artificial siren calling the demons to feed. 

The plant men poured toward the river’s bank. The doomed party fought briefly before the repulsive shapes covered them, scores of sucking mouths fastening to flesh. I turned away in profound disgust. This was not sustenance; it was a sterile, utilitarian consumption, devoid of the deep sensory appreciation and culinary reverence that elevated eating to a meaningful act. They viewed their victims solely as fuel. 

“Their part is soon over,” Thuvia said softly. “The great white apes get the flesh when the plant men have drained the arteries.”

I watched as a dozen white monsters loped across the valley to claim the remnants. Then, the sun vanished, and an oppressive, tactile darkness engulfed us.

Thuvia led us back into the winding corridors. Twice, massive banths blocked our path. But instead of drawing a weapon, Thuvia shifted the acoustic environment. She spoke to them in a low, resonant cadence—a native dialect entirely devoid of the therns' harsh syllables. The snarling beasts, sensitive to the sonic harmony of her voice, slunk sullenly away.

“If you can dissolve our obstacles as easily as you master these fierce brutes, I see no difficulties,” I said. “How do you do it?”

She shuddered. “When Sator Throg first threw me into the banth pits, I expected to be torn apart. But in my own country, I was accustomed to command. Something in my voice—the pitch, the memory of my homeland's tongue—cowed them. They fawned at my feet. The therns were so amused they kept me to handle them. I know them all by name. They are the scavengers here, solving the problem of sanitation by consuming those who die in chains.”

Her words sparked a thought. “Why not take a number of banths and set them loose before us above ground?” 

Thuvia laughed, a bright, musical sound in the dreary dark. “It would certainly distract them.”

She began to call out in a low, singsong purr, an acoustic balm that vibrated beautifully against the stone walls. It was a masterclass in acoustic ecology. From the diverging tunnels, soft, padded feet began to approach. The heavy, musky scent of wild predators mixed with the metallic tang of old blood as fifty great beasts emerged from the shadows. She spoke a word of her dying language to each one. Like well-schooled terriers, they paced with us, though their golden eyes lingered hungrily on Tars Tarkas and myself.

It was a staggering, mythic procession through the underbelly of the world: the noiseless passage of bare feet and padded paws; the golden walls splashing light over my stolen diadem; the towering green elder steeped in agrarian wisdom; and the beautiful girl whose linguistic mastery held our ferocious, maned scavengers at bay. 

We eventually reached a vast, brightly lit chamber. Thuvia motioned for caution as we threaded our way through a sprawling sea of prisoners. These were the hybrids and outcasts—deformed, sightless, stripped of their ancestral dialects and cultural memories. They sprawled in heaps, resembling the grotesque souls of an ancient purgatory. The air here was sterile, devoid of the rich aromas of life or cooking fires, reeking only of forgotten humanity and systemic decay. We navigated carefully, the banths sniffing at the helpless, tantalizing prey. 

“Why do we see no therns here?” I whispered.

“They fear the dark,” Thuvia replied. “By night, the banths prowl. The therns cannot abide the unpredictable sonic environment of the dark corridors. They fear the silent assassin in the shadows. By day, this place is alive with slaves passing to the granaries, but now, they leave it to the scavengers.”

We reached the foot of a steep ascent without detection. “Above us,” Thuvia whispered, “is the doorway to the inner gardens. From here, for four miles to the outer ramparts, we will be beset by countless dangers. Every inch is under a sentry’s eye.”

“Why such an enormous force for a place so hidden in superstition?” I asked.

“They fear the black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” she said, invoking a name that carried the weight of generational myth. “From whom may our first ancestors preserve us.”

Thuvia opened the heavy door. Instantly, the sterile, stale air of the tunnels was swept away by the profound, living breath of the gardens. My nostrils were greeted by the deep, loamy aroma of rich soil, the sweet exhalation of night-blooming flora, and the crisp, invigorating chill of the open sky. 

The banths, electrified by the sudden rush of unfamiliar, wildly evocative scents, broke past us with low growls, swarming out into the gardens beneath the lurid light of the nearer moon. 

Suddenly, the carefully maintained acoustic tranquility of the night was shattered. A great cry of alarm erupted from the temple roofs—an industrial, piercing warning that cascaded from point to point, echoing wildly to the east and west. Beside me, Tars Tarkas’s long-sword leaped from its scabbard with a sharp ring of steel, and Thuvia shrank shuddering to my side.
