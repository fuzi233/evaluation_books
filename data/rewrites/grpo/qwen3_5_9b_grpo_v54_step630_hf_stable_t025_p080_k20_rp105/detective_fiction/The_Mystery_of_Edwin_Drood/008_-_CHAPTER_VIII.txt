The two young men watched the damsels, their charges, vanish into the courtyard of the Nuns' House. Behind them, the brazen door-plate seemed to stare with insolent judgment, its glass eye reflecting the moonlight like a mocking glint. The pair exchanged a glance, looked down the perspective of the silent street, and turned slowly to walk away together.

"Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?" Neville asked, his voice steady but probing.

"Not this time," Edwin replied, his tone careless. "I leave for London tomorrow. But I shall be here, off and on, until next Midsummer; then I shall take my leave of Cloisterham, and England too; for many a long day, I expect."

"Are you going abroad?"

"Going to wake up Egypt a little," Edwin answered, with a condescending lift of his chin.

"Are you reading?"

"Reading?" Edwin repeated, a flicker of contempt crossing his features. "No. Doing. Working. Engineering. My small patrimony was left as a part of the capital of the Firm I am with by my father, a former partner; and I am a charge upon the Firm until I come of age. Then I step into my modest share in the concern. Jack—you met him at dinner—is, until then, my guardian and trustee."

"I heard from Mr. Crisparkle of your other good fortune."

"What do you mean by my other good fortune?"

Neville had spoken in a manner that was watchfully advancing yet furtively shy, embodying that peculiar air of being simultaneously hunter and hunted. Edwin's retort was abrupt, lacking all polish. They halted, exchanging a heated, silent look.

"I hope," Neville said, "there is no offence, Mr. Drood, in my innocently referring to your betrothal?"

"By George!" Edwin cried, quickening his pace. "Everybody in this chattering old Cloisterham refers to it. I wonder no public-house has been set up, with my portrait for the sign of *The Betrothed's Head*. Or *Pussy's* portrait. One or the other."

"I am not accountable for Mr. Crisparkle's mentioning the matter to me, quite openly," Neville began.

"No; that's true; you are not," Edwin assented.

"But," Neville resumed, "I am accountable for mentioning it to you. And I did so, on the supposition that you could not fail to be highly proud of it."

Two curious springs of human nature drove this dialogue. Neville Landless was already sufficiently impressed by Little Rosebud to feel indignant that Edwin Drood, far beneath her, should hold his prize so lightly. Conversely, Edwin Drood was sufficiently impressed by Helena to feel indignant that Helena's brother, far beneath her, should dispose of him so coolly and put him out of the way so entirely.

Edwin answered the last remark. "I don't know, Mr. Neville"—adopting the mode of address from Mr. Crisparkle—"that what people are proudest of, they usually talk most about; I don't know either that what they are proudest of, they most like other people to talk about. But I live a busy life, and I speak under correction by you readers, who ought to know everything, and I daresay do."

By now, both had become savage. Mr. Neville stood in the open; Edwin hid under the transparent cover of a popular tune, stopping occasionally to pretend to admire picturesque effects in the moonlight.

"It does not seem to me very civil in you," Neville remarked at length, "to reflect upon a stranger who comes here, not having had your advantages, to try to make up for lost time. But, to be sure, I was not brought up in 'busy life,' and my ideas of civility were formed among Heathens."

"Perhaps, the best civility, whatever kind of people we are brought up among," Edwin retorted, "is to mind our own business. If you will set me that example, I promise to follow it."

"Do you know that you take a great deal too much upon yourself?" came the angry rejoinder. "And that in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it?"

"By whom, for instance?" Edwin asked, coming to a halt and surveying the other with a look of disdain.

Suddenly, a startling right hand was laid upon Edwin's shoulder, and Jasper stepped between them. It appeared he too had strolled round by the Nuns' House, emerging from the shadowy side of the road.

"Ned, Ned, Ned!" Jasper said. "We must have no more of this. I don't like this. I have overheard high words between you two. Remember, my dear boy, you are almost in the position of host tonight. You belong, as it were, to the place, and in a manner represent it towards a stranger. Mr. Neville is a stranger, and you should respect the obligations of hospitality. And, Mr. Neville," laying his left hand on the inner shoulder of the young gentleman, walking on between them, hand to shoulder on either side: "you will pardon me; but I appeal to you to govern your temper too. Now, what is amiss? But why ask! Let there be nothing amiss, and the question is superfluous. We are all three on a good understanding, are we not?"

After a silent struggle between the two young men to see who would speak last, Edwin struck in: "So far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me."

"Nor in me," said Neville Landless, though not so freely; or perhaps so carelessly. "But if Mr. Drood knew all that lies behind me, far away from here, he might know better how it is that sharp-edged words have sharp edges to wound me."

"Perhaps," Jasper said in a soothing manner, "we had better not qualify our good understanding. We had better not say anything having the appearance of a remonstrance or condition; it might not seem generous. Frankly and freely, you see there is no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?"

"None at all, Mr. Jasper." Still, not quite so frankly or so freely; or, be it said once again, not quite so carelessly perhaps.

"All over then! Now, my bachelor gatehouse is a few yards from here, and the heater is on the fire, and the wine and glasses are on the table, and it is not a stone's throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you are up and away tomorrow. We will carry Mr. Neville in with us, to take a stirrup-cup."

"With all my heart, Jack."

"And with all mine, Mr. Jasper." Neville felt it impossible to say less, but would rather not go. He had an impression that he had lost hold of his temper; he felt that Edwin Drood's coolness, far from being infectious, made him red-hot.

Mr. Jasper, still walking in the centre, hand to shoulder on either side, beautifully turned the refrain of a drinking song, and they all went up to his rooms. There, the first object visible, when he added the light of a lamp to that of the fire, was the portrait over the chimneypiece. It was not an object calculated to improve the understanding between the two young men, as rather awkwardly reviving the subject of their difference. Accordingly, they both glanced at it consciously, but said nothing. Jasper, however—who would appear from his conduct to have gained but an imperfect clue to the cause of their late high words—directly called attention to it.

"You recognise that picture, Mr. Neville?" Jasper shaded the lamp to throw the light upon it.

"I recognise it, but it is far from flattering the original."

"Oh, you are hard upon it! It was done by Ned, who made me a present of it."

"I am sorry for that, Mr. Drood." Neville apologized, with a real intention to apologize; "if I had known I was in the artist's presence—"

"Oh, a joke, sir, a mere joke," Edwin cut in, with a provoking yawn. "A little humouring of Pussy's points! I'm going to paint her gravely, one of these days, if she's good."

The air of leisurely patronage and indifference with which this was said, as the speaker threw himself back in a chair and clasped his hands at the back of his head as a rest for it, was very exasperating to the excitable and excited Neville. Jasper looked observantly from one to the other, slightly smiled, and turned his back to mix a jug of mulled wine at the fire. It seemed to require much mixing and compounding.

"I suppose, Mr. Neville," said Edwin, quick to resent the indignant protest against himself in the face of young Landless, which was fully as visible as the portrait, or the fire, or the lamp: "I suppose that if you painted the picture of your lady love—"

"I can't paint," was the hasty interruption.

"That's your misfortune, and not your fault. You would if you could. But if you could, I suppose you would make her (no matter what she was in reality), Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus, all in one. Eh?"

"I have no lady love, and I can't say."

"If I were to try my hand," said Edwin, with a boyish boastfulness getting up in him, "on a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; in earnest—you should see what I could do!"

"My sister's consent to sit for it being first got, I suppose? As it never will be got, I am afraid I shall never see what you can do. I must bear the loss."

Jasper turned round from the fire, filled a large goblet glass for Neville, filled a large goblet glass for Edwin, and handed each his own; then filled for himself, saying:

"Come, Mr. Neville, we are to drink to my nephew, Ned. As it is his foot that is in the stirrup—metaphorically—our stirrup-cup is to be devoted to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!"

Jasper set the example of nearly emptying his glass, and Neville followed it. Edwin Drood said, "Thank you both very much," and followed the double example.

"Look at him," cried Jasper, stretching out his hand admiringly and tenderly, though rallyingly too. "See where he lounges so easily, Mr. Neville! The world is all before him where to choose. A life of stirring work and interest, a life of change and excitement, a life of domestic ease and love! Look at him!"

Edwin Drood's face had become quickly and remarkably flushed with the wine; so had the face of Neville Landless. Edwin still sat thrown back in his chair, making that rest of clasped hands for his head.

"See how little he heeds it all!" Jasper proceeded in a bantering vein. "It is hardly worth his while to pluck the golden fruit that hangs ripe on the tree for him. And yet consider the contrast, Mr. Neville. You and I have no prospect of stirring work and interest, or of change and excitement, or of domestic ease and love. You and I have no prospect (unless you are more fortunate than I am, which may easily be), but the tedious unchanging round of this dull place."

"Upon my soul, Jack," said Edwin, complacently, "I feel quite apologetic for having my way smoothed as you describe. But you know what I know, Jack, and it may not be so very easy as it seems, after all. May it, Pussy?" To the portrait, with a snap of his thumb and finger. "We have got to hit it off yet; haven't we, Pussy? You know what I mean, Jack."

His speech had become thick and indistinct. Jasper, quiet and self-possessed, looked to Neville, as expecting his answer or comment. When Neville spoke, *his* speech was also thick and indistinct.

"It might have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships," he said, defiantly.

"Pray," retorted Edwin, turning merely his eyes in that direction, "pray why might it have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships?"

"Ay," Jasper assented, with an air of interest; "let us know why?"

"Because they might have made him more sensible," said Neville, "of good fortune that is not by any means necessarily the result of his own merits."

Mr. Jasper quickly looked to his nephew for his rejoinder.

"Have *you* known hardships, may I ask?" said Edwin Drood, sitting upright.

Mr. Jasper quickly looked to the other for his retort.

"I have."

"And what have they made you sensible of?"

Mr. Jasper's play of eyes between the two held good throughout the dialogue, to the end.

"I have told you once before tonight."

"You have done nothing of the sort."

"I tell you I have. That you take a great deal too much upon yourself."

"You added something else to that, if I remember?"

"Yes, I did say something else."

"Say it again."

"I said that in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it."

"Only there?" cried Edwin Drood, with a contemptuous laugh. "A long way off, I believe? Yes; I see! That part of the world is at a safe distance."

"Say here, then," rejoined the other, rising in a fury. "Say anywhere! Your vanity is intolerable, your conceit is beyond endurance; you talk as if you were some rare and precious prize, instead of a common boaster. You are a common fellow, and a common boaster."
