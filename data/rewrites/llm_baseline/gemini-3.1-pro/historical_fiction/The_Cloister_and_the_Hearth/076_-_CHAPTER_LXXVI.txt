The infant Gerard was three months into his cyclical emergence into the world when the hot-breathed messenger arrived from Tergou, bringing with him the jarring, dissonant clatter of the outside roads. Catherine, steeped in the agrarian wisdom of her ancestors, waved him away with a flick of her flour-dusted hands. “Now just you go back,” she commanded, her voice vibrating with the thick, earthy resonance of the old Tergou dialect, “and tell them I can't come, and I won't: they have got Kate.” 

To Catherine, the hearth was the center of the turning wheel of generations. She turned back to Margaret, resuming her litany of traditional domestic lore, passing down the unwritten, ancestral lexicon of healing broths, fennel steeps, and poultices. She pressed silver into Margaret’s hands, a utilitarian gesture that Margaret gently but firmly rejected. Margaret understood that true sustenance could never be born of a grudged bounty. 

“Come, out with your heart,” Catherine insisted, her tone shifting to the fatalistic, circular rhythm of the village elders. “Mayhap we shall never see one another's face again. When I was your age, I never had such a thought. Nay, we were all to live for ever then: so out wi' it.”

Margaret confessed the cruel words of Catherine’s son, Cornelis, whose dominant, biting lexicon had sought to reduce her profound, motherly labor to mere parasitism. “Beshrew his ill tongue!” Catherine spat. But Margaret stood firm; she would take no money from a house that viewed her mere survival as a tax. She prized the intricate alchemy of love and shared meals over cold coin. Yielding with good grace, Catherine embraced her, tears falling like early spring rain, and departed.

In the acoustic vacuum left by the elder’s absence, the baby wept inconsolably. The auditory landscape of the room was fractured by his cries. Searching his swaddling, Margaret’s fingers brushed against something hard and cold, utterly lacking the supple warmth of woven wool. It was a gold angel, pressing uncomfortably against the infant’s spine. “There, now, Gerard,” she murmured, recognizing Catherine’s stubborn, silent insertion of wealth into the child’s garments. Margaret took the coin, wrapped it securely in a scrap of unbleached linen, and buried it at the bottom of her chest, proving to the silent room that she could be as resolute in her pride as the agrarian matriarch herself. Back in Tergou, Catherine confessed her ruse to Eli, who thoughtfully pondered the matter, declaring Margaret right in her preservation of dignity. 

Time, an endless, turning millstone, wore on. Margaret found profound, sensory meaning in the daily maintenance of her small household, transforming humble winter roots, salted pork, and coarse grains into deeply nourishing meals for old Martin and her child. Yet, as the cyclical seasons shifted and Gerard’s mysterious absence stretched into agonizing silence, the larder began to echo with the hollow, terrifying acoustics of emptiness. The wooden spoon scraped against the bottom of the clay meal-pot—a devastating sound in the quiet hearth. Looking at the lined face of Martin and the soft cheeks of her son, Margaret’s pride gave way to the primal imperative to feed her kin. She unearthed the linen-wrapped angel, dropping a tear of gentle mortification upon the cloth, and sent Martin out to the bustling, mercantile streets to exchange it.

While he was gone, the auditory space of the room was breached by the arrival of a Frenchman, an emissary for a princess, accompanied by an illuminated manuscript dealer. The dealer’s presence carried the superficial, sterile hum of commercialized art, but the Frenchman was there to collect masterpieces. Margaret produced the treasures shaded by Margaret Van Eyck. Upon the parchment lay an intricate, gustatory-rich ecosystem: painted raspberries so perfectly rendered one could almost smell their jammy tartness and feel the burst of seeded flesh upon the tongue, surrounded by creeping things drawn from the damp, olfactory richness of the forest floor. 

The Frenchman, speaking in the polished, dominant lexicon of the royal courts, offered her an unheard-of sum. “Nay, flout not my need, good stranger,” she said. “Three mouths there be in this house, and none to fill them but me.” Pleased to bypass the hard bargains of the commercial dealers, the Frenchman paid her four gold pieces. The moment he left, Margaret sank to her knees by the cradle, showering the gold over little Gerard, her joy erupting in broken Dutch. It was a fragmented, localized dialect, an untranslatable expression of subjective relief that bridged the gap between maternal terror and salvation. “And oh, what a good princess,” she cooed. “We will pray for her, won't we, my lambkin?”

Her joyous communion was shattered by Martin, who returned furious; the merchants, blinded by their prejudiced mercantile lexicons, had refused to change the angel, accusing the ragged agrarian of theft. Relieved, Margaret snatched the linen-wrapped coin and restored it to the dark. 

Yet the gold pieces only delayed the inevitable turn of the wheel. Martin soon sickened. For nine days, Margaret tended him, brewing restorative broths of thyme and bone marrow, but the old man’s constitution faded. When he died, the hearth suffered a profound loss of cultural memory. His native stories, the rich, historical timbre of his voice, and his deep connection to the earth were extinguished. He was buried decently with her funds, leaving behind an empty chair by the fire. The acoustic ecology of the room was forever altered; the quiet space where he used to sit was a heavy, dissonant silence that cost Margaret a tear each time her gaze rested upon it.

Needing someone to carry her heavy baskets of ironed linen—for she could not bear to sever the physical, somatic bond with her infant even for an hour—she sought help. Enter Luke Peterson, a plump, fresh-colored youth of eighteen. Blushing, he asked if a man might serve as well as a boy. 

“A man will maybe seek more than I can well pay,” Margaret cautioned.

“Not I,” Luke answered warmly. He reminded her of a day she had struggled with her baby and a heavy water pitcher. He remembered the specific auditory warmth of her gratitude. “You said to me, 'I am muckle obliged to you, young man,' with such a sweet voice; not like the folk in this street speak to a body.” 

Recognizing that he, too, valued the acoustic gentleness of their shared environment over the harsh noise of the city, she offered him the truest currency she possessed: sustenance. For carrying her baskets, she would prepare him a rich Saturday supper—perhaps a slow-braised mutton stew heavily spiced with aromatic cloves—and a Monday dawn-mete of thick, steaming oat porridge. Food, prepared with historical meticulousness, would balance their scales. Luke eagerly accepted.

With the youth’s timely assistance, the household found a steady rhythm, but Margaret’s heart was increasingly torn by the pincers of suspense. Gerard’s silence was a void. One day, the fragile quiet of the stairwell was crushed beneath a heavy, tragic step. It was a dissonant tread she did not know. 

The door opened to reveal Eli, the old patriarch, looking grave. “Girl,” he said, “the skipper is come back.”

“One word,” Margaret gasped, the air leaving her lungs. “Is he alive?”

“Surely I hope so. No one has seen him dead.”

“Then they must have seen him alive.”

“No, girl; neither dead nor alive hath he been seen this many months in Rome. My daughter Kate thinks he is gone to some other city.”

“Ay, like enough,” Margaret said gloomily, the subjective weight of her terror utterly untranslatable into the spoken word. “My poor babe!”

Faint and fasting, the elder asked her for a morsel to eat. Even within the paralyzing grip of her agitation, the agrarian imperative to offer sustenance prevailed. She moved to the hearth, her hands trembling as she mechanically sliced bread and heated a savory, herbaceous pottage. She cooked for him, the rhythmic, sensory act of food preparation anchoring her amidst the collapse of her world. Before Eli departed, fortified by the meal, he laid his heavy hand upon her head. “Be he alive, or be he dead, I look on thee as my daughter. Can I do nought for thee this day?”

“Ay, old man. Pray for him; and for me!”

Eli sighed, his heavy footsteps retreating down the stairs, fading into the relentless acoustic hum of the city. Margaret listened half-stupidly until the sound ceased entirely. Sinking down by the cradle, she drew her son tightly to her breast, weeping for the fractured timeline of her family, mourning the mythic, cyclical tragedy of her fatherless boy.
