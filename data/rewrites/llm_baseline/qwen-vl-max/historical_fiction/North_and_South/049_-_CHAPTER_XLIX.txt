“And down the sunny beach she paces slowly,
With many doubtful pauses by the way;
Grief hath an influence so hushed and holy.”

“Is not Margaret the heiress?” Edith whispered to her husband one evening, after they had returned from their sorrowful journey to Oxford. She had drawn his tall head down toward hers, standing on tiptoe, as if afraid of what she was about to say. “Don’t be shocked,” she pleaded, “but I must ask.”

Captain Lennox, however, was entirely in the dark. If he’d ever heard of such a thing, it had slipped from memory. A Fellow of a modest college could hardly leave much behind—certainly not enough to matter. And besides, he’d never expected her to pay for her board. Two hundred and fifty pounds a year seemed absurd, especially since she didn’t drink wine.

Edith stepped back, her spirits slightly dimmed, her romantic dreams shattered. A week later, she bounded into the room with a low, playful curtsey. “I was right, and you were wrong, noble Captain! Margaret has received a letter from a solicitor—she’s the residuary legatee. The specific legacies amount to about two thousand pounds, and the remainder, at current values in Milton, is close to forty thousand.”

“Indeed!” he replied, surprised. “And how does she bear this sudden fortune?”

“Oh, she says she knew she would inherit something—but not this much. She looks pale, almost frightened. But that’s only nerves, you know. It will pass. I left Mama pouring out congratulations like water, and slipped away to tell you.”

It was assumed, without question, that Mr. Henry Lennox should now become Margaret’s legal advisor. She knew nothing of business matters and relied upon him for nearly every decision. He selected her solicitor, brought documents for her signature, and took great delight in instructing her in the mysteries of law—explaining each clause, each phrase, as though unveiling sacred symbols.

One day, Edith said mischievously, “Henry, do you know what I hope and expect all these long talks with Margaret will end in?”

“No,” he answered, flushing. “And I wish you wouldn’t tell me.”

“Oh, very well,” she said, smiling. “Then I won’t warn Sholto to stop inviting Mr. Montagu so often.”

“Just as you please,” he replied, forcing calmness. “What you imagine may or may not come to pass. But before I commit myself, I want to see my ground clear. Ask whom you will—it may not be courteous, but if you interfere, you’ll ruin it. She’s been distant with me for a long time, only just beginning to thaw from her Zenobia-like pride. She has the makings of a Cleopatra—if only she were a little more pagan.”

“For my part,” Edith retorted, a little tartly, “I’m glad she’s Christian. I know so few!”

That autumn, there was no Spain for Margaret. Though she clung to the faint hope that Frederick might be summoned to Paris—where she could have joined him under safe escort—no such chance came. Instead of Cadiz, she found herself bound for Cromer. Her aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes were going there, and though they had always encouraged her to join them, they made only half-hearted efforts to support her own desire to travel separately. Yet perhaps Cromer was best suited to her needs. She required both rest and physical renewal. Among the vanished hopes was the belief that Mr. Bell would one day reveal to Mr. Thornton the true circumstances surrounding the tragic accident that led to Leonards’ death. She had longed for him to understand—not merely to forgive, but to grasp the full truth of what she had done and why. Such knowledge would have brought peace, a quiet certainty she feared she would carry through life unless she could resolve never to dwell on it again.

But time had passed too long. With Mr. Bell gone, the only way to explain those events had vanished with him. She could submit to being misunderstood, as so many others did. Reason told her this was common fate; yet her heart still ached with longing—that one day, years hence, before he died, he might know how deeply she had been tempted. She didn’t need to hear that everything was explained—only to know, with absolute certainty, that he understood.

But the wish remained unfulfilled, like so many others. When she accepted this, she turned fully to the present, determined to make the most of the life before her.

She spent long hours sitting on the beach, watching the waves crash endlessly against the pebbled shore. Sometimes she gazed beyond, at the distant swell where sea met sky, listening unconsciously to the ceaseless rhythm—the eternal psalm rising up from the ocean. She felt calmed, though she couldn’t say why.

She sat listlessly, hands clasped around her knees, while her aunt ran errands and Edith and Captain Lennox rode far and wide along the coast and into the countryside. Nurses passing with children would whisper among themselves, wondering what could possibly hold her attention so steadily, day after day.

At dinner, Margaret remained silent and withdrawn. Edith declared her moped, and welcomed her husband’s suggestion with relief: that Mr. Henry Lennox be invited to stay at Cromer for a week upon his return from Scotland in October.

Yet during these quiet hours, Margaret reorganized her thoughts, placing past events in their proper order—understanding their causes and meanings, both for her past and her future. Those solitary moments by the sea were not wasted. Anyone who could read her face would have seen the subtle transformation taking place.

Mr. Henry Lennox noticed it immediately. “The sea has done Miss Hale an immense deal of good,” he remarked when she first left the room after his arrival. “She looks ten years younger than she did in Harley Street.”

“That’s the bonnet I got her!” Edith exclaimed triumphantly. “I knew it would suit her the moment I saw it.”

“I beg your pardon,” Mr. Lennox replied, with the half-contemptuous, half-indulgent tone he often used toward Edith. “But I believe I can distinguish between the charm of a dress and the beauty of a woman. No mere bonnet could have made Miss Hale’s eyes so luminous and gentle, her lips so full and red, her face so radiant with peace and light. She is like—and yet more than—” he lowered his voice, “the Margaret Hale of Helstone.”

From that moment, the ambitious and clever man set his mind fully upon winning her. He admired her delicate grace, her intelligence—qualities he believed could be guided toward the goals he himself pursued. He saw her fortune not as separate from her worth, but as part of her complete character and position. Still, he was keenly aware of the rise it would bring him—a poor barrister suddenly elevated by her wealth. In time, he thought, he would achieve success and honors sufficient to repay her, with interest, for the advantage she had given him.

He had visited Milton on business related to her estate, returning from Scotland. With the sharp eye of a skilled lawyer, he observed how the value of her land and property increased yearly in the thriving town. He was pleased to see that the old awkwardness of their meeting at Helstone was fading, replaced by a natural relationship between client and adviser—one that allowed for greater intimacy.

Margaret was eager to listen whenever he spoke of Milton, even though he had not met the people she knew best. Her aunt and cousin had spoken of the town with disdain—just as Margaret had once felt, ashamed to recall. But Mr. Lennox admired Milton more than she did. He was captivated by its energy, its strength, its relentless drive. Its vivid, almost feverish existence fascinated him. He never tired of describing it. Only Margaret, in her quiet honesty, pointed out the flaw beneath the nobility—the materialism that often lay hidden behind their fierce industry.

Still, when other topics bored her and she gave brief answers, a question about some peculiar trait of Darkshire life would bring back the sparkle to her eyes, the warmth to her cheeks.

When they returned to London, Margaret carried out one of her seaside resolutions: she took charge of her own life. Before going to Cromer, she had obeyed her aunt’s rules as docilely as a child, remembering the night she wept herself to sleep in the Harley Street nursery. But the long hours of reflection had taught her that she must one day answer for her own choices. She began to grapple with the difficult question for any woman: how much of oneself should be surrendered to authority, and how much preserved for personal freedom?

Mrs. Shaw was kindly and good-natured; Edith inherited this quality. Margaret, perhaps, had the most volatile temper of the three—her sharp mind and vivid imagination making her quick to act, her early isolation fostering pride. Yet beneath it all lay an undeniable sweetness, a childlike tenderness that made even her rare moments of defiance endearing. Now, tempered by what the world called her good fortune, she charmed her reluctant aunt into accepting her independence.

She gained recognition of her right to define her own duties.

“Only don’t be strong-minded,” Edith begged. “Mamma wants you to have your own footman—and honestly, I think you’re welcome. They’re such nuisances. But please, darling, don’t go and be strong-minded. That’s the only thing I ask. Footman or no footman, just don’t be strong-minded.”

“Don’t worry, Edith,” Margaret replied. “I’ll faint in your arms at the servants’ dinner the very first chance I get. Then, with Sholto playing with the fire and the baby screaming, you’ll wish for a strong-minded woman—someone who can handle anything.”

“And you won't grow too serious? You’ll still joke and laugh?”

“Not I. I shall be merrier than ever—now that I’ve taken my own path.”

“And you won’t become a figure? Will you let me buy your dresses?”

“Indeed, I mean to buy them myself. You can come with me if you like—but no one else can choose for me.”

“Oh!” Edith laughed. “I was afraid you’d dress in brown and dust-color, so no dirt would show. I’m glad you’re keeping a few vanities—just to remind us of the old Adam.”

“I’ll be exactly the same, Edith—if you and Aunt could only believe it. Only now, since I have neither husband nor child to give me natural duties, I must create my own—besides choosing my own clothes.”

In private conversation, Edith, her mother, and her husband agreed that Margaret’s plans might only strengthen her bond with Henry Lennox. By distancing herself from other potential suitors, she limited her circle. And it was noted that she rarely showed pleasure in anyone’s company except Henry’s—outside their family.

Other admirers, drawn by her beauty or her fortune, were gently repelled by her unconscious air of disdain, drifting instead to other beauties less selective, or heiresses with larger dowries.

Henry and Margaret grew closer, slowly, naturally. Neither was one to allow their feelings to be openly discussed. They walked the fine line between friendship and something deeper—each careful, each guarded, yet unmistakably drawn together by the quiet currents of shared understanding.
