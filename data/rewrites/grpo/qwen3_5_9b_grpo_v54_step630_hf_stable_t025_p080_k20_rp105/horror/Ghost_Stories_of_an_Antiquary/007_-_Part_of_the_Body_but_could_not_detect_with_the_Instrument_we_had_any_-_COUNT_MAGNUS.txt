The documents from which I have woven this narrative arrived in my possession through circumstances that remain, for now, a sealed mystery. Before presenting the extracts themselves, it is imperative to establish the provenance of these papers. They constitute a hybrid archive: a portion comprises the typical travelogues of the mid-nineteenth century—chatty, illustrated volumes detailing hotel accommodations, carriage routes, and the rambling conversations of foreign peasants, reminiscent of Horace Marryat's *Journal of a Residence in Jutland*. However, as the author's journey progressed, these mundane observations curdled into a singular, harrowing record of personal terror, culminating on the eve of his flight.

The writer was Mr. Wraxall. From the scant evidence of his own trembling pen, I deduce a man past middle age, wealthy but profoundly isolated, drifting through hotels and boarding houses with the intention of settling down—a dream that never materialized. It is likely that the catastrophic fire at the Pantechnicon in the early seventies consumed much of his history, including property he once stored there. He had previously published an anonymous work on a holiday in Brittany, but his final obsession was a projected book on Scandinavia. Seeking to interweave travelogue with the history of great Swedish families, he secured introductions to the nobility and departed for Sweden in the summer of 1863.

His inquiries led him to the manor of Råbäck, a formidable red-brick structure dating to the early seventeenth century, built by a scion of the De la Gardie family. Though the family received him with polite hospitality, Wraxall preferred the independence of the village inn, accepting the daily trek to the manor. The estate was surrounded by ancient timber and overlooked a small lake, with a church perched atop a rocky knoll nearby. To the English eye, the church was peculiar: low, crowded, and dominated by a grotesque "Last Judgment" painting. But it was the addition to the church that haunted Wraxall—a mausoleum erected by the founder of the house. An eight-sided, domed structure of stark white walls and copper roof, it stood separate from the nave, accessible only by its own northern portal.

On his first night, Wraxall noted the mausoleum's keyhole, glimpsing marble effigies and copper sarcophagi within, sparking an insatiable curiosity. The family papers he examined revealed a dark history: the first Count Magnus de la Gardie was a tyrant who suppressed peasant uprisings with brutal efficiency, ordering executions and burning encroaching homesteads. The local innkeeper whispered of a "Black Pilgrimage" the Count had undertaken, returning with something—or someone—unnatural. When pressed, the landlord evaded the question, citing a summons to Skara, leaving Wraxall to piece together the truth from the archives.

Among the account books, Wraxall discovered a tract titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis* (Book of the Black Pilgrimage). The fragment read: "If any man desires to obtain a long life... it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince..." The text hinted at alchemy bordering on necromancy. This revelation transformed the Count in Wraxall's mind from a mere historical figure into a sorcerer of grim renown.

That evening, driven by a solitary compulsion, Wraxall found himself inexplicably walking straight to the churchyard gate, having missed his own dinner. He spoke aloud to the tomb, unaware that a cleaning woman's dropped metal object had startled him. The next day, he interviewed the parish deacon. When asked about Chorazin, the clergyman spoke of Antichrist being born there and recounted a chilling tale of two men who ventured into the Count's forbidden woods. They heard a scream that seemed to twist a soul from its body, followed by a laugh that was not human, and finally, the sound of a great door shutting. The men who investigated found one survivor, Hans Thorbjorn, frantically pushing at invisible forces, and the corpse of Anders Bjornsen, whose face had been sucked clean from his skull, his eyes staring upward even in death.

Armed with this lore, Wraxall returned to the mausoleum. Inside, the sarcophagi were magnificent, but the lid of the Count's tomb bore a disturbing engraving: a hunter pursued by a monstrous, tentacled form, and a cloaked figure watching from a hillock. As Wraxall copied the epitaphs, he noticed that two of the three massive steel padlocks securing the coffin were loose. He collected them, placing them on the window ledge, unable to unlock the third. He felt a strange, morbid fascination with the "ferocious and grim" nobleman, unaware that the silence of the building was about to be shattered.

On his final day, delayed by the De la Gardie family's insistence on dining with him, Wraxall lingered on the churchyard knoll. Determined to bid farewell to the Count, he approached the mausoleum. As he spoke his goodbyes, a blow struck his foot. A heavy object clattered onto the pavement—the third padlock. Before he could fully rise, the sound of creaking metal echoed, and the lid of the sarcophagus shifted upward. Terrified, Wraxall fled the building, discovering to his horror that he could not turn the key in the lock. He ran, leaving the sound of the hinges behind him, unsure if he had heard them close again.

Wraxall survived the journey to England, but he was broken. His journals became disjointed, filled with frantic attempts to describe passengers on canal boats who were never counted at meal times: a man in a long black cloak, a short figure in a hood. Upon landing at Harwich, he hired a closed fly to escape to Belchamp St Paul. There, under a moonlit sky, he encountered two shadowy figures at a crossroads—one tall with a hat, one short with a hood—who caused his horse to bolt in sheer panic. He had seen them before.

Trapped in a lodging house, Wraxall wrote of his despair, wondering what sin he had committed to invite such pursuit. He locked his doors and cried to God, unaware that his story had already ended. Years later, the house at Belchamp St Paul stood empty until it came into my possession as part of a legacy. I had it demolished, and in a forgotten cupboard beneath the window of the master bedroom, I found the papers of the antiquary, the final testament of a man who had stared into the abyss and found it staring back.
