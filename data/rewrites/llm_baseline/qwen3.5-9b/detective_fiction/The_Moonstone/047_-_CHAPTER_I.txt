Few words are required on my part to conclude the narrative already set forth in the *Journal of Ezra Jennings*. Of myself, I need only state that I awoke on the morning of the twenty-sixth, utterly unaware of anything I had said or done while under the drug's influence—from the moment the opium first claimed me until I opened my eyes in Rachel's sitting-room.

As for events following my awakening, I feel no compulsion to provide a detailed account. Limiting myself strictly to results, I must report that Rachel and I achieved a thorough mutual understanding before a single explanatory word was spoken by either of us. Neither of us feels called upon to explain the extraordinary speed of our reconciliation. Gentlemen and Ladies, recall the time when you were passionately devoted to one another; you will understand exactly what transpired after Ezra Jennings closed the door of the sitting-room, just as clearly as I do myself.

I have no objection, however, to noting that we would undoubtedly have been discovered by Mrs. Merridew had it not been for Rachel's presence of mind. Hearing the rustle of the old lady's dress in the corridor, she instantly rushed out to intercept her. I heard Mrs. Merridew ask, "What is the matter?" and Rachel reply, "The explosion!" Mrs. Merridew allowed herself to be gently guided by the arm and led into the garden, safely away from the impending shock. Upon returning inside, she encountered me in the hall and expressed herself as profoundly impressed by the advancements in science since her schoolgirl days. "Explosions, Mr. Blake, are infinitely milder than they used to be," she assured me. "I barely heard Mr. Jennings's blast from the garden, and there is no lingering smell now that we are back inside! I must apologize to your medical friend; he has managed it beautifully!"

Thus, after overcoming Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings also triumphed over Mrs. Merridew herself. There remains, after all, a great reservoir of undeveloped liberal sentiment in the world!

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff openly expressed his desire for me to accompany him to London via the morning train. The watch kept at the bank, and the potential outcome of the investigation, appealed so irresistibly to Rachel's curiosity that she immediately decided—to so long as Mrs. Merridew had no objection—to join us back in town, ensuring she remained within reach of the earliest news regarding our proceedings.

Mrs. Merridew proved to be remarkably pliable and indulgent, considering how benevolently the explosion had behaved; consequently, Betteredge was informed that we were all four traveling back together by the morning train. I fully anticipated that he would request permission to accompany us. However, Rachel wisely assigned her faithful old servant an occupation that deeply engaged him. He was tasked with completing the refurnishing of the house and was so absorbed in his domestic duties that he felt no trace of the "detective fever" he might have succumbed to under different circumstances.

Our sole regret in departing for London was the necessity of separating more abruptly than we desired from Ezra Jennings. It was impossible to persuade him to come with us. I could only promise to write to him, while Rachel insisted on inviting him to visit her upon her return to Yorkshire. Every prospect pointed toward a reunion within a few months; yet, there was something profoundly sad in watching our dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train departed the station.

Upon arriving in London, Mr. Bruff was intercepted at the terminus by a small boy dressed in threadbare black cloth, personally distinctive due to the extraordinary prominence of his eyes. They protruded so far and rolled so loosely that one wondered uneasily how they remained in their sockets. After listening to the boy, Mr. Bruff asked the ladies if they would excuse his accompanying them back to Portland Place. I had barely promised Rachel that I would return to tell her everything before Mr. Bruff seized my arm and hurried me into a cab. The boy with the ill-secured eyes took his place on the box beside the driver, who was instructed to proceed to Lombard Street.

"News from the bank?" I asked as we started off.

"News concerning Mr. Luker," Mr. Bruff replied. "An hour ago, he was seen leaving his house in Lambeth in a cab, accompanied by two men recognized by my men as police officers in plain clothes. If Mr. Luker's fear of the Indians motivates this caution, the inference is plain: he intends to remove the Diamond from the bank."

"And we are proceeding to the bank to witness the consequences?"

"Yes—or to learn what has already transpired, if it is concluded by this time. Did you notice my boy on the box?"

"I noted his eyes."

Mr. Bruff laughed. "They call the poor little wretch 'Gooseberry' at the office," he said. "I employ him for errands, and I wish those clerks who have nicknamed him were as reliable as he is. Gooseberry is one of the sharpest boys in London, Mr. Blake, despite his eyes."

Twenty minutes to five, we drew up before the bank in Lombard Street. Gooseberry looked longingly at his master as the cab door opened.

"Do you wish to come in as well?" Mr. Bruff asked kindly. "Then come along and stay close to my heels until further orders. He is quicker than lightning," Mr. Bruff continued, whispering to me. "Two words suffice with Gooseberry where twenty would be wasted on another boy."

We entered the bank. The outer office, featuring the long counter behind which the cashiers sat, was crowded with people awaiting their turn to withdraw or deposit funds before closing at five.

Two men approached Mr. Bruff immediately upon his appearance.

"Well," asked the lawyer, "have you seen him?"

"He passed us here half an hour ago, sir, and proceeded into the inner office."

"Has he not emerged again?"

"No, sir."

Mr. Bruff turned to me. "Let us wait," he said.

I scanned the surrounding crowd for the three Indians. Not a sign of them appeared anywhere. The only individual with a noticeably dark complexion was a tall man in a pilot coat and round hat, resembling a sailor. Could this be one of them in disguise? Impossible! The man was taller than any of the Indians, and his face, where not obscured by a bushy black beard, was at least twice as broad as theirs.

"They must have a spy stationed somewhere," Mr. Bruff observed, casting a glance at the dark sailor. "And he may be the man."

Before he could elaborate, his coat-tail was respectfully tugged by his attendant sprite with the gooseberry eyes. Mr. Bruff looked where the boy indicated. "Hush!" he commanded. "Here is Mr. Luker!"

The moneylender emerged from the inner sanctum of the bank, flanked by his two guardian policemen in plain clothes.

"Keep your eye on him," Mr. Bruff whispered. "Should he pass the Diamond to anyone, he will do so here."

Ignoring us both, Mr. Luker slowly navigated toward the door, weaving through the densest and thinnest parts of the crowd. I distinctly saw his hand move as he passed a short, stout man respectably attired in a sober grey suit. The man startled slightly and glanced after him. Mr. Luker continued his slow progress through the throng. At the exit, his guards positioned themselves on either side. All three were trailed by one of Mr. Bruff's men, and then they vanished from sight.

Turning to the lawyer, I looked significantly toward the man in the grey suit. "Yes!" Mr. Bruff whispered, "I saw it too!" He spun around, searching for his second man. The second man was nowhere to be found. He peered behind him for his attendant sprite. Gooseberry had disappeared.

"What the devil does this mean?" Mr. Bruff exclaimed angrily. "They have both deserted us precisely when we require them most."

It became the turn of the man in the grey suit to conduct his business at the counter. He deposited a cheque, received a receipt, and prepared to depart.

"What is to be done?" Mr. Bruff asked. "_We_ cannot stoop to following him."

"_I_ can!" I declared. "I would not lose sight of that man for ten thousand pounds!"

"In that case," Mr. Bruff rejoined, "I would not lose sight of _you_ for double that amount. A delightful occupation for a man in my position," he muttered to himself as we followed the stranger out of the bank. "For Heaven's sake, do not mention it. My ruin would be certain if it became known."

The man in the grey suit boarded an omnibus heading westward. We followed suit. There were latent reserves of youth remaining in Mr. Bruff. I assert it positively: when he took his seat in the omnibus, he blushed!

The man in the grey suit halted the omnibus and alighted in Oxford Street. We followed him again. He entered a chemist's shop.

Mr. Bruff started. "My chemist!" he cried. "I fear we have made a mistake."

We entered the shop. Mr. Bruff and the proprietor exchanged a few words privately. The lawyer rejoined me with a crestfallen expression.

"It is greatly to our credit," he said, taking my arm and leading me out—"that offers one comfort!"

"To our credit?" I queried.
