The news of Rosanna’s disappearance sifted through the estate’s out-door servants like fine-milled flour through a tamis. I was deep in the sanctuary of the kitchens, seeking the rhythmic, ancestral solace of preparing a traditional venison pie—a receipt passed down through four generations of Betteredges. The rich, earthy aroma of simmering marrow and bruised juniper berries usually anchored my soul, but today the domestic rhythm was irreparably broken. They had just laid hands on a quick little imp we nicknamed “Duffy,” who helped weed the kitchen gardens, and he testified to seeing Rosanna Spearman barely half an hour prior. She had passed him in the fir-plantation, not walking, but running toward the sea-shore.

“Does this boy know the coast hereabouts?” asked Sergeant Cuff. His voice cut through the warm, yeasty air of the kitchen with the grating, metallic dissonance of a factory loom, an industrial intrusion wholly devoid of the rich, rolling acoustic of our native Yorkshire. 

“He has been born and bred on the coast,” I answered.

“Duffy!” said the Sergeant, tossing a coin. “Do you want to earn a shilling? Come along with me. Keep the pony-chaise ready, Mr. Betteredge, till I come back.” He launched himself toward the Shivering Sand with an unnatural, clockwork gait that my own legs had no hope of matching. Little Duffy gave a feral howl, a sound of pure, untamed youth, and trotted off at the Sergeant’s heels.

I tried to return to the marrow-broth, but a stupefying restlessness seized me. The kitchen, usually a symphony of bubbling pots and the comforting thud of wooden spoons against earthenware, felt suddenly hollow. The acoustic ecology of the house was entirely out of tune. I chopped parsley with frantic, needless energy, the bright, peppery scent stinging my nose, yet providing no comfort. I don’t know how long it was before Duffy returned, breathless, bringing a note torn from Cuff’s pocket-book. *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman’s boots, and be quick about it.*

I dispatched a maid to Rosanna's room, but I resolved that I would not let Cuff handle the boot alone. I grasped the worn leather when it was brought to me—it smelled faintly of blacking, lye soap, and the damp, salty ozone of her isolated life. Taking it, I hurried toward the sea at the nearest approach to a run which a man turned seventy can reasonably hope to manage. 

As I neared the shore, the sky bruised purple and the storm broke. The civilized sounds of the estate were swallowed by the mythic, deafening percussion of the rising tide. Rain drifted in great white sheets, tasting of crushed shells and bitter salt. The air was a gustatory assault of brine and pulverized limestone. I passed the boy crouching for shelter under the lee of the sandhills, and then I saw the raging sea boiling like a massive cauldron of frothing albumen. Standing in the yellow wilderness of the beach was one solitary black figure—Sergeant Cuff.

He waved his hand toward the north. “Keep on that side!” he shouted over the roar. “And come on down here to me!”

I went down to him, choking for breath, the taste of copper sharp on my tongue. I was past speaking. I had a hundred questions, but they stalled in my throat; the dominant lexicon of the London detective was hopelessly inadequate to translate the raw, primal dread seizing my gut. His face frightened me. I saw a look of horror in his eyes. 

He snatched the boot out of my hand and pressed it into a footmark in the wet sand, bearing south from us and pointing straight toward the rocky ledge called the South Spit. The mark was not yet blurred by the rain—and the girl’s boot fitted it to a hair. Cuff pointed to the impression without a word. I caught at his arm, struggling to speak, but the poverty of the Queen's English failed to capture my terror. 

With an obstinate, clinical patience that felt profane against the mythic hunger of the landscape, Cuff tested the boot in footprint after footprint, following them down to where the rocks and the sand joined. The South Spit was awash with the incoming tide; the waters folded over the hidden face of the Shivering Sand like thick, dark molasses. Always, the prints pointed straight *to* the rocks. Hunt as he might, he could find no sign of footsteps walking *from* them. 

He gave it up at last. He looked at the waters heaving deeper and deeper over the quicksand, and I saw his thought form in his face. A dumb, crawling terror covered me. I fell upon my knees on the beach.

“She has been back at the hiding-place,” Cuff muttered to himself, his voice a flat, industrial hum against the roaring wind. “Some fatal accident has happened to her on those rocks.”

I tried to tell him that his sterile vocabulary had entirely misjudged the situation. I tried to express the untranslatable sorrow I had seen in her—the numbed, deadened way she had swept the corridor hours ago. Her own internal rhythm had fallen out of tune with the world, a dying language of the soul that no one had bothered to interpret. I tried to say, *The death she has died, Sergeant, was a death of her own seeking.* But the words wouldn't come. The dumb trembling held me. I couldn’t feel the driving rain or see the rising tide. I only saw the poor lost creature, remembering the morning I had fetched her into the house, hearing her say the Shivering Sand seemed to draw her to it. The horror of it struck through the thought of my own daughter. My girl, just her age, might have lived that miserable life and died this dreadful death.

The Sergeant kindly lifted me up, turning me away from the site of her perishing. Looking toward the sandhills, I saw the men-servants and Yolland, the old fisherman, running down to us. Yolland was an agrarian elder whose very bones understood the tidal rhythms and the ancient, briny recipes of the sea. 

In the fewest words, Cuff showed them the evidence of the footmarks and stated a fatal accident must have occurred. He then asked Yolland, “Could a boat have taken her off, in such weather as this, from those rocks?”

Yolland pointed to the rollers tumbling in, churning the sea-floor into a violent soup of sand and foam. “No boat that ever was built,” he bellowed, his voice resonant with the deep, chest-heavy timbre of the old coastal dialect, “could have got to her through *that*.”

Cuff considered the footmarks, now fast blurring out. “There is the evidence she can’t have left by land. And here,” he said, looking at Yolland, “is the evidence she can’t have got away by sea. She was seen running here an hour ago. How high would the water be, at that time, on the south side of the rocks?”

“As the tide makes today,” the fisherman answered, “there wouldn’t have been water enough to drown a kitten on that side of the Spit, an hour since.”

“How much on the side of the quicksand?” Cuff asked.

“Less still. The Shivering Sand would have been just awash, and no more.”

Cuff turned to me, asserting the accident must have happened on the quicksand's side. My tongue was finally loosened. 

“No accident!” I cried, the words tasting of bile and profound grief. “When she came to this place, she came weary of her life, to end it here.”

He started back from me. The others crowded round. Cuff, enforcing the power dynamics of his authoritative calm, put them back. “Let him alone a little,” he said, citing my age and the shock. Then he asked Yolland, “Is there any chance of finding her, when the tide ebbs again?”

“None,” Yolland answered. “What the Sand gets, the Sand keeps for ever. It digests what it takes.” Yolland stepped closer to me. “Mr. Betteredge, four foot out, broadwise, along the side of the Spit, there’s a shelf of rock, about half fathom down under the sand. My question is—why didn’t she strike that? If she slipped, by accident, she fell in where there’s foothold at the bottom, barely covering her to the waist. She must have waded out, or jumped out, into the Deeps beyond. No accident, sir! The Deeps of the Quicksand have got her, by her own act.”

After that testimony from a man whose traditional knowledge was absolute, Cuff was silent. We all held our peace. With one accord, we turned back up the slope of the beach. 

At the sandhillocks, we were met by the under-groom, running from the house. He handed me a little note. “Penelope sent me with this, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “She found it in Rosanna’s room.”

The paper carried the faint, intimate scent of her room, a final recipe of farewell to the old man who had done his best to befriend her. *You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in past times. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness.*

There was no more than that—a last scrap of her personal dialect fading into extinction. The sheer untranslatability of her lonely existence crushed me. Your tears come easy when you are young and the world's feast is just beginning. They come easy when you are an old man, clearing the plates at the end of the banquet. I burst out crying. 

Sergeant Cuff took a step nearer, meaning kindly, I don’t doubt. I shrank back from him. His very presence felt like the grinding gears of the metropolis that had alienated her. “Don’t touch me,” I said. “It’s the dread of you, that has driven her to it.”

“You are wrong, Mr. Betteredge,” he answered, quietly. “But there will be time enough to speak of it when we are indoors again.”

I followed the rest of them, with the help of the groom’s arm. Through the driving rain we went back—returning to the domestic hearth that now felt irreparably fractured by the mythic terror waiting for us at the house.
