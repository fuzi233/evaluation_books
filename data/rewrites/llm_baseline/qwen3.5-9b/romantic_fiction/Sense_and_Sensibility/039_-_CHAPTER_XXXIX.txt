More than two months had now passed since the Dashwood sisters arrived in London, and Marianne's longing to depart grew daily stronger. She sighed constantly for the fresh air, the freedom, and the peaceful solitude of the countryside, convinced that if any place could bring her true comfort, it must be Barton. Elinor shared her anxiety for their departure, though hers was tempered by a practical awareness of the immense difficulties involved in such a long journey—a reality Marianne refused to acknowledge. Yet, Elinor began to seriously consider the logistics of their return and had already voiced their desires to their generous hostess. Lady Middleton had resisted with all the warmth of her goodwill, until a new proposal emerged.

Mrs. Palmer intended to move to Cleveland near the end of March for the Easter holidays. She extended a very enthusiastic invitation to Mrs. Jennings, along with her two guests, to accompany them. While this alone might not have satisfied Elinor's refined sensibilities, the invitation was delivered with such genuine politeness by Mr. Palmer himself—and coupled with the marked improvement in his demeanor toward them since learning of his wife's unhappiness—it compelled Elinor to accept with pleasure.

When she revealed her decision to Marianne, however, the younger sister reacted with immediate distress. "Cleveland!" she cried, agitated. "No, I cannot go to Cleveland."

"You forget," Elinor said gently, "that its location is not..."

"But it is in Somersetshire," Marianne interrupted. "I simply cannot go into Somersetshire. There, where I once hoped to travel... No, Elinor, you cannot expect me to go there."

Elinor saw no point in arguing against the propriety of suppressing such strong feelings. Instead, she sought to counteract them by appealing to other sentiments. She framed the trip as a way to secure a timely and comfortable reunion with their beloved mother, whose presence they both cherished dearly. From Cleveland, merely a few miles from Bristol, the journey to Barton would take no more than a single day, albeit a long one. Their mother's servant could easily meet them halfway, and since they would likely stay at Cleveland for no more than a week, they could be back home in under three weeks. Given Marianne's deep love for her mother, Elinor reasoned, this practical benefit should outweigh her imagined fears.

Indeed, Marianne's affection proved powerful. Furthermore, Mrs. Jennings was far from tired of her guests; she pressed them earnestly to return with her from Cleveland. Though Elinor appreciated the kindness, her plans remained unchanged. With their mother's agreement quickly secured, arrangements for their departure were finalized. Finding solace in the countdown, Marianne drafted a list detailing exactly how many hours separated them from Barton.

"Ach, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings exclaimed as he visited shortly after settling their departure, "what shall you and I do without the Miss Dashwoods? They are absolutely determined to leave the Palmers' house. And how desolate we shall be when I return! Lord, we shall sit staring at one another as dull as two cats."

Perhaps Mrs. Jennings hoped this vivid description of their impending boredom would prompt the Colonel to make an offer, thereby saving himself from such loneliness. If so, she soon realized her hope was justified. As Elinor moved to the window to measure a print she intended to copy for a friend, the Colonel followed her, speaking with a distinct intensity. The effect on the ladies was unmistakable. Though Mrs. Jennings, bound by honor, shifted her chair specifically to avoid overhearing, she could not help noticing that Elinor flushed, her face betraying agitation, and became completely absorbed in the Colonel's words, abandoning her task.

Later, during a pause in Marianne's piano practice, fragments of the conversation drifted to Mrs. Jennings' ears. The Colonel seemed to be apologizing for the poor quality of his house. This confirmed her suspicions. She marveled that he would deem such an apology necessary, assuming it was mere social etiquette. Unable to hear Elinor's response clearly, she guessed from the movement of her lips that the Colonel's comment was not a significant obstacle. Mrs. Jennings silently praised Elinor for her honesty.

They continued talking for a few moments without further interception, until another break in Marianne's playing allowed the Colonel's calm voice to reach her: "I am afraid it cannot take place very soon."

Startled and shocked by what sounded like a rejection, Mrs. Jennings nearly shouted, "Good heavens, what could possibly prevent it?" but checked herself, offering only a silent gasp. "This is peculiar! Surely he doesn't need to wait until he is older."

However, this apparent hesitation did not seem to wound or dishearten his companion. Shortly after the conversation ended, as they parted ways, Mrs. Jennings distinctly heard Elinor say, with a tone revealing her own feelings, "I shall always feel deeply indebted to you."

Delighted by this expression of gratitude, Mrs. Jennings only wondered why the Colonel could then bid them farewell with such perfect composure (*sang-froid*), departing without a single reply. She had never suspected her old friend could be such an indifferent suitor.

What actually transpired between them was quite different.

"I have heard," the Colonel said with profound sympathy, "of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered at the hands of his family. If I understand correctly, he has been entirely rejected by them for persisting in his engagement to a truly deserving young woman. Am I correct? Is this true?"

Elinor confirmed it.

"The cruelty, the politically disastrous cruelty," the Colonel replied with deep emotion, "of separating, or attempting to separate, two young people deeply devoted to one another is terrible. Mrs. Ferrars does not realize what she is doing—or what she may be driving her son to. I have met Mr. Ferrars a couple of times in Harley Street and am greatly pleased with him. He is not a man with whom one becomes intimately acquainted quickly, but I have seen enough to wish him well for his own sake, and as a friend of yours, I wish it even more. I understand he intends to take holy orders. Would you be so kind as to inform him that the living of Delaford, which has just become vacant according to today's post, is offered to him, should he deem it worthy of acceptance? However, given his unfortunate circumstances, it might seem foolish to suggest it without reservation; I only wish it were more substantial. It is a rectory, but a small one—the late incumbent earned no more than £200 per annum. While it is certainly capable of improvement, I fear not to the extent required to provide a truly comfortable income. Nevertheless, my pleasure in presenting it to him is immense. Please assure him of this."

Elinor's astonishment at this commission was scarcely less than if the Colonel had proposed marriage. The position, which she had considered hopeless for Edward just two days prior, was suddenly provided to facilitate his marriage—and *she*, of all people, was chosen to deliver it! Her emotions were intense, though Mrs. Jennings interpreted them as arising from a different cause. Regardless of any lesser, less noble feelings that might have contributed to her reaction, her admiration for the Colonel's general benevolence and her gratitude for his specific friendship were profoundly felt and warmly expressed.

She thanked him wholeheartedly, spoke highly of Edward's character and principles knowing them to be deserving, and promised to undertake the mission with pleasure if he truly wished to delegate such a pleasant duty to someone else. At the same time, she couldn't help thinking that no one could perform it better than he. It was an errand from which she would gladly have been spared, unwilling to cause Edward pain by accepting an obligation from her personally. Yet, the Colonel, motivated by similar delicacy, declined to handle it directly, appearing so eager for her to convey it that she could not oppose him further.

Edward, she believed, was still in town, and fortunately, she had learned his address from Miss Steele. She could therefore inform him of the news later that day.

Once this was settled, the Colonel turned the conversation to his own gain in securing such a respectable and agreeable neighbor. It was then, as Mrs. Jennings had anticipated, that he expressed regret regarding the smallness and mediocrity of the house. Elinor, as expected, dismissed this concern lightly, particularly regarding its size.

"The smallness of the house," she remarked, "I cannot imagine any inconvenience for them, as it will suit their family size and income perfectly."

To the Colonel's surprise, this indicated that she viewed Mr. Ferrars's marriage as a certainty resulting from the presentation. He did not believe the Delaford living could generate an income sufficient for anyone of his station to consider marrying on, and he stated as much.

"This little rectory," he explained, "can do no more than make Mr. Ferrars comfortable as a bachelor; it cannot enable him to marry. I am sorry to say that my patronage ends here; my influence is hardly more extensive. Should an unforeseen opportunity arise in the future to assist him further, I must view him very differently from how I do now if I am not equally ready to be helpful then as I sincerely wish to be now. What I am doing today seems insignificant compared to what must be his primary, and only, goal: happiness. His marriage must remain a distant prospect; at least, I fear it cannot happen very soon."
