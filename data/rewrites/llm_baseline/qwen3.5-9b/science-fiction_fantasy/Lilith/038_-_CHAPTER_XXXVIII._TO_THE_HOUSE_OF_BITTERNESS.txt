Dawn broke gray and heavy as we pushed forward toward the forest, driven by urgency. I rode Lona's horse, carrying her lifeless form wrapped tightly in her cloak. My destination was her father's house; I intended to offer her a proper resting place within his chambers, a final dignity for the dead. If he refused—if he deemed her unworthy since she had not chosen death willingly—I would leave her in the desert to return to dust. But I trusted he would accept her. Surely, she had been dead for eons. And yet, the shame of presenting her corpse to her kin weighed heavily upon me. How lowly must I bow before him?

Beside me sat Lilith. I had no authority to force her repentance, no right to have slain her, and certainly no claim to remain her eternal jailer. Still, I bore her. Time and again during our march, I offered her food, but she met my gestures only with a gaze of starving hatred. Her eyes burned with fire, rolling restlessly, refusing to close until we crossed the scorching stream. Beyond that barrier, they remained sealed until we finally approached the House of Bitterness.

One evening, as we camped, a small child ventured toward Lilith. Instinctively, I lunged to intervene, but the child had already placed something at the princess's lips before I could reach them. A sharp cry erupted—a sound of agony. "Please, King!" the child whimpered, pulling back. "Suck the finger! The bad giantess made a hole in it!"

I leaned down and sucked the tiny wound clean. "Well now!" the child exclaimed triumphantly. A moment later, she held out a second piece of fruit to a mouth eager for sustenance. This time, however, Lilith snatched her hand away violently, dropping the offering to the earth. The child's name was Luva.

The following day, we crossed the hot stream. Back on their own territory, the Little Ones—my companions—were jubilant. Yet their nests lay far ahead, so we halted at the Ivy-Hall, drawn by the promise of its grapevines. Seeing the great clusters, the children rushed forward, devouring eagerly, and soon lay fast asleep on the green floor and scattered throughout the surrounding woods.

Hoping to witness the nightly dance, and assuming the children would sleep through it, I cleared a wide circle in the center of the hall. I settled down among them, placing Lona's body beside me, though sleep eluded me. Night descended, and suddenly, the dancers appeared. I found myself pondering whether this ritual would repeat eternally, and if my stubbornness would eventually compel me to join them in their endless motion. Then, the children's eyes snapped open. They leaped to their feet, alert and bright. Instantly, every child seized a dancer, and they bounded away, skipping and laughing.

The spectral figures seemed to welcome them; perhaps these spirits recognized the Little Ones, knowing well that they too had once walked the path back to childhood. Whatever the case, their innocent games brought a strange refreshment to weary souls stripped of their futures, leaving only the shadow of a vanished past. The children played many merry pranks, none rude. Even when their antics momentarily disrupted the dance's rhythm, the ghosts showed no annoyance; they possessed nothing left to smile with anyway.

Just as dawn began to bleed into the sky, I noticed the skeleton-princess standing in the doorway. Her eyes were open and glowing, a terrifying black mark staining her side. She paused, then glided inward as if intending to join the revelry. I sprang up. A cry of repugnant terror tore from the children, and the lights vanished. Only the pale moon remained, casting its glow upon the group huddled together in the dark. The ghosts were gone—or at least invisible. The princess had vanished too. I darted to where I had left her; she lay there, eyes closed, appearing undisturbed. Returning to the hall, I found the Little Ones settling onto the floor for sleep.

The next morning, as we departed, we spotted two skeletons moving through a nearby thicket. The Little Ones broke formation and ran to greet them. Though I walked freely now, free of splints and ligatures, I recognized the pair I had seen before. The children instantly befriended them, grasping their bony arms and stroking the knuckles of their long fingers. The skeletal creatures accepted the attention warmly. They seemed perfectly content with one another; their shared deprivation had forged a bond. The loss of everything had become the start of a new existence.

Seeing the skeletons gather handfuls of herbs—presumably to rub against their bones, for how else could such rudimentary systems find nourishment—the Little Ones carefully inspected the herbs, collected similar ones, and filled the empty hands the skeletons extended. They bid farewell, promising to visit again, and continued their journey, remarking that they had never known such kind people lived in the forest.

Upon reaching the nest-village, I stayed one night to oversee their resettlement. Lona still resembled the newly dead, and there was no rush. The princess had eaten nothing, her eyes remaining shut. Fearing she might perish before we reached our destination, I crept to her in the night and pressed my bare arm against her lips. She bit down with ferocious intensity, forcing a cry from my throat. I barely escaped her grasp, collapsing beyond her reach. By morning, I prepared to depart.

Selecting twelve of the Little Ones—not the largest or strongest, but the sweetest and merriest—I mounted them atop six elephants. I chose two of the wise *Clumsies* to carry the princess. Riding Lona's horse, I dragged her body, wrapped in her cloak, before me. Taking the most direct route across the left branch of the riverbed toward the House of Bitterness, I hoped to learn how to navigate the broader, rougher branch and avoid the monster-filled basin below. I dreaded the terrain for the elephants and the danger for the children.

Our journey included one terrible night—the third spent in the desert between the two branches of the dead river. We halted the elephants in a sheltered spot and allowed the princess to slide down between them, lying on the sand until morning. She appeared utterly dead, yet I suspected otherwise. I positioned myself a short distance away, keeping watch over both the deceased Lona and the sleeping princess. The moon hung halfway down the western sky, pale and thoughtful, mottling the desert with shifting shadows. Suddenly, it was eclipsed. Visible still, it emitted no light, veiled by a thick, diaphanous film that obscured its beauty, making it look troubled. The film swept aside slightly, revealing the jagged outline of a bat-like wing, torn and hooked.

A cold wind, stinging with heat, swept over us—and Lilith was upon me. Her hands were bound, but with her teeth, she ripped the cloak from my shoulder and drove her fangs into my flesh. I lay paralyzed as my life seemed to drain into her. Remembering my resolve, I struck her hand. She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, shivering violently. I flung her off and sprang to my feet. She knelt, rocking back and forth. A second blast of hot-cold air enveloped us; the moon cleared, revealing her face—gaunt, ghastly, smeared with blood.

"Down, devil!" I shouted.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked, her voice a dull echo from a tomb.

"To your first husband," I replied.

"He will kill me!" she moaned.

"At least he will take you off my hands!"

"Give me my daughter!" she screamed suddenly, grinding her teeth.

"Never! Your doom is upon you at last!"

"Loose my hands for pity's sake!" she groaned. "I am in torture. The cords are sunk in my flesh."

"I dare not. Lie down!" I commanded.

She collapsed onto the ground like a log. The remainder of the night passed in silence. By morning, she appeared dead once more.

Before evening, we came into view of the House of Bitterness. Immediately, one of the elephants drifted alongside my horse. "Please, King," whispered the Little One riding on its neck, "are you not going to that place?"

"Indeed I am! We are staying the night there," I answered.

"Oh, please, don't! That must be where the cat-woman lives!"

"If you had ever seen her, you would not use that name!"

"Nobody ever sees her! She has lost her face! Her head is back and side all round."

"She hides her face from dull, discontented people! Who taught you to call her the cat-woman?"

"I heard the bad giants call her so."

"What did they say about her?"

"That she had claws on her toes."

"It is untrue. I know the lady. I spent a night at her house."

"But she *may* have claws on her toes! You might see her feet, and her claws be folded inside their cushions!"

"Then perhaps you think *I* have claws on my toes?"

"Oh, no; that can't be! You are good!"

"The giants might have told you so!"

"We wouldn't believe them about you!"

"Are the giants good?"

"No; they love lying."

"Then why do you believe them about her? I know the lady is good; she cannot have claws."

"Please, how do you know she is good?"
