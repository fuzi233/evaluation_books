If the weight of a guilty conscience were measured strictly by the harm a person has done, human history would read very differently: the architects of greedy wars and the ravenous moguls of the money markets would march in the same self-lacerating line of penitents as the petty thief, the pickpocket, and the desperate murderer who kills for a paltry handful of coin. Wickedness doles out its glittering prizes, no doubt—but to claim the highest reward of unshaken, serene cruelty, a soul must be far baser, colder, and more brutal than most human beings born into this soft, messy world can manage. The vast majority of us carry some faint spark of conscience: a fear that is justice’s long shadow, a flicker of pity that is love’s own echo, enough to bar us from that icy peak of perfect, unfeeling vice.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda knew the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey—but he had not caught a single glimpse of them before he headed up to change for dinner. A heavy, glorious snowfall had blanketed the grounds that day, granting the children rare hours of loud, giddy joy: snowball fights that left their mittens soaked through, lopsided snow forts capped with sprigs of holly. The Mallinger girls would suffer no amusement at all unless their beloved “cousin” joined in and oversaw it, and after all that outdoor exertion he had drifted straight to the billiards room, his mind so full of small, loud, childish demands that he barely spared a thought for the new couple’s arrival. Even so, the prospect of seeing Gwendolen again hummed quietly at the edge of his attention, and when he slipped into his room before the half-hour dinner bell rang, a little flushed and tired from hours of playing at amusement, he found himself wondering how her marriage to Grandcourt had changed her. He knew there would be new shades to her manner, just as there had been between their first meeting at the Leubronn gaming tables and their later encounter at Diplow.

“Some natures you could watch shift, grow, or erode a little every single day, if you paid close enough attention,” he thought to himself. “I’m certain she holds tight, deep inside, to every impression that ever marked her. That little business of the necklace, the quiet knowledge that someone thought her gambling was wrong—you could tell it had bitten right into her. That kind of deep impressibility cuts two ways, though: it can push a person to desperate, reckless choices just as easily as it can lead them toward something good. And no matter what small charms Grandcourt might hold for capricious tastes, good heavens—who could imagine him drawing out soft, tender affection in the quiet, unforgiving hours of daily life? I could almost want to horsewhip him, just to force some flicker of real passion into that blank, perfect face and steady, unreadable voice. I’m afraid she married him out of pure ambition, to escape the grinding poverty that loomed over her family. But why did she run from him at first? The poverty came later, of course. Poor thing—she was almost certainly pressured into it. How could anyone feel anything but pity for a creature as bright as that, full of unused, unspent life, reckously rash, hanging all her blind, glowing hopes for the future on that empty, polished remnant of a man?”

His harsh judgment of Grandcourt came all the easier for the soft pity he wrapped around Gwendolen in his thoughts. His description of the bridegroom as a “remnant” was not rooted in any secret knowledge, merely the impression that every polite conversation with him left: Grandcourt had long since worn out every scrap of natural, healthy curiosity about the world around him.

It is a near-universal rule that when a prominent couple marries, male acquaintances will almost always pity the bride, female acquaintances the groom—each side convinced their beloved friend could have done far better. Especially when the bride is as brilliant and charming as Gwendolen, every young gentleman in the room is apt to assume she could never care for a man as uninteresting to them as her husband, and must have married him for status or money alone. Who ever pities the groom, in that situation? Even his female friends tend to think his position is retribution, that he ought to have chosen someone else. Deronda can hardly be blamed, then, for feeling no shred of pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck a single acquaintance as the sort of man who suffered more than he inflicted. For Gwendolen, though—young, headstrong, adored by flattery that had long fed her belief that she was born to rule—how quickly could life curdle from bright expectation to the bitter, suffocating realization that a choice could never be undone? After all he had seen of her, Deronda would have had to be made of stone not to look forward to her entrance with quiet, intense interest. With the honeymoon three weeks behind her, and Gwendolen already established first at Ryelands, then at Diplow, he expected she would have mastered the art of composing her face to hide whatever feelings churned beneath it—she was never the sort to give gawking onlookers the satisfaction of seeing her raw and unguarded.

The drawing room was filled with a crowd invited expressly to meet the newlyweds: old aristocracy in Lord and Lady Pentreath, old gentry in the young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam of the Worcestershire branch, a member of Parliament for West Orchards named Mr. Fenn who cared for nothing but cider, accompanied by his two daughters, Lady Mallinger’s brother Mr. Raymond and his wife, the eminent lawyer Mr. Sinker, and Mr. Vandernoodt, the Dutch-born bachelor Sir Hugo had found so pleasant at Leubronn that he had invited him to the Abbey.

Everyone had gathered in the drawing room before the new couple appeared. The guests passed the lingering minutes before dinner cooing over the children—Lady Mallinger’s three girls, and the horde of little Raymond nephews and nieces, all allowed to stay up for this pre-dinner hour. The room was soft and glowing: full-length portraits set deep into the cedar paneling, a ceiling crammed with bright, richly colored coats of arms, light pouring more warmly from the crackling oak-bough fire on the hearth than from the pale beeswax candles dotted across the mantel. The deep piled carpet swallowed sound, and the quiet, well-bred tone of the gathering softened every voice, while the mix of ages—from white-haired Lord and Lady Pentreath down to four-year-old Edgar Raymond—lent every group of guests a soft, varied charm. Lady Mallinger drifted through the room in her black velvet gown, a tiny white lapdog tucked against her arm like a little finishing detail to her costume, the children clustered around the ladies while most of the gentlemen stood apart, chatting with the very moderate, unhurried vivacity common to drawing rooms in the minutes before a dinner bell. Deronda found himself cornered by Mr. Vandernoodt, that useful fixture of society who knew every prominent person alive but cared nothing for their actual work: pale-eyed, nonchalant, neatly clipped, a perfect, unassuming foil to Deronda’s vivid gravity.

Vandernoodt was gossiping about the bride and groom they were all waiting for, gleaning every small personal detail he could: “Men who’ve lived as much life as Grandcourt don’t usually pick wives this well, you know. He’s got quite a history, deep into every sort of pleasure, lazy as he seems. But of course you know all about him, being Sir Hugo’s ward.”
“No, really,” Deronda replied, keeping his tone deliberately indifferent. “I know little more than that he is Sir Hugo’s nephew.”

Just then the drawing room door swung open, cutting off Vandernoodt’s eagerness to share all his collected secrets.
The room was perfectly set to show off a striking new arrival, and no one in the gathering could deny that Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt cut a perfectly distinguished figure. The bridegroom was exactly as he had always been: his costume faultlessly tailored, his face as impassible and unreadable as a statue’s, as if he would never settle for anything less than absolute perfection in his outward possessions, his wife included. The bride was exactly the sort of jewel he would have chosen. “By George, she’s even handsomer than she was at Leubronn,” Vandernoodt breathed. Deronda agreed, though he said nothing. She wore white silk, and diamonds glinted on her neck, her ears, tucked into the curls of her hair. That new, imposing sharpness to her beauty struck him immediately, more unquestionable than it had been the first time he saw her leaning over the roulette table. Back then, he had found a soft, tender, womanly charm in her when they met at Diplow—but he could not spot it now. She moved through the greetings with a cold, proud quietude, a thin, superficial smile on her lips, and he thought he could see that same sharp, almost feral force in her that had gripped her the day she had fixed him with that resolute glance, then turned away to walk off as a loser at the tables. There was no time to parse his impressions further, no time even to go over and greet her, before the gong sounded for dinner.

He sat not far opposite her at the table, and could overhear bits of her conversation with Sir Hugo, who was in especially high spirits chatting to the new bride. Though he looked toward her again and again, hoping to catch her eye and bow, she pointedly avoided his gaze for long minutes. At last Sir Hugo, who assumed they had already spoken, called across the table: “Deronda, you’ll like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt has to say about your favorite Klesmer.”
Gwendolen’s lashes had been lowered to her plate, and Deronda, already watching her, saw a faint, involuntary quiver run through her eyelids as she was forced to lift them. She met his unembarrassed, gentle bow and smile, but her own smile never reached past her lips, a thin, formal curve. It lasted only an instant, before Sir Hugo was talking again, unhurried: “The Arrowpoints have forgiven the marriage, you know. He’s spending Christmas with his new bride at Quetcham.”
“I expect he’s glad of it for his wife’s sake,” Deronda said. “Else I don’t imagine he’d have minded keeping far away.”
“It’s a proper troubadour story,” rumbled Lady Pentreath, the deep-voiced old dowager. “I’m glad there’s still a little romance left in the world. These young people nowadays are all far too worldly-wise for their own good.”
“Still, it shows good sense on the Arrowpoints’ part, taking the pair back after all that fuss in the papers,” Sir Hugo laughed. “Disowning your own child over a mésalliance is like disowning one of your eyes: everyone knows it’s yours, and you’ve no other to make a show with.”
“As for mésalliance, there’s no grand blood on either side,” Lady Pentreath scoffed. “Old Admiral Arrowpoint was one of Nelson’s men, a doctor’s son, for heaven’s sake. And we all know where his wife’s money came from.”
“If there was ever a mésalliance in this case,” Deronda put in, “I’d say it was Klesmer marrying up, not down.”
“Ah, you think the immortal genius married the mortal girl! What do you think of that, Mrs. Grandcourt?” Sir Hugo asked, turning to her.
“I’ve no doubt Herr Klesmer considers himself completely immortal,” Gwendolen said, her composure fully recovered now. “And I’ve no doubt his wife will burn as much incense before him as he could ever require.”
“Don’t you approve of a wife burning incense before her husband?” Sir Hugo teased, playful.
“Oh, yes,” Gwendolen replied. “If only to make everyone else believe in him. When Klesmer is too busy admiring his own genius to notice how absurd he sounds, it takes the edge off if his wife says Amen.”
“Klesmer’s no favorite of yours, I see,” Sir Hugo said.
“On the contrary, I think very highly of him,” she corrected quickly, suddenly serious, the sharp edge of her unspoken bitterness over his rejection of her singing career pricking through. “His genius is far above my poor judgment, and I know for a fact he is exceedingly generous.”

Deronda found himself wondering what he would have thought of her if he had met her for the first time that night: he would have guessed she was putting on a thin coating of hardness and defiance to hide some sharp, unspoken pain. But why was she so deliberately unfriendly to him?

To shift the subject, Sir Hugo gestured around the dining hall. “Isn’t this a beautiful room? It used to be the refectory of the old Abbey. Those pillars and three arches used to split the space in two, half as big again as it is now. Long rows of Benedictine monks used to sit right where we’re sitting. Imagine if the lights suddenly dimmed, and the ghosts of all those old monks rose up right behind every chair!”
“Please don’t say that!” Gwendolen laughed, with a playful little shudder. “It’s all very nice to come after ancestors and monks, but they ought to know their place and stay underground. I’d be terrified to wander this house all alone. I expect all those old generations are furious with us for altering their home so much.”
“Oh, I expect the ghosts are all political sorts,” Sir Hugo chuckled. “The ones who spent their whole lives wanting to change things and never got their way would certainly be on our side. But if you don’t want to tour the house alone, you’ll be happy to go in company, I hope? You and Grandcourt ought to see every inch of it. We’ll ask Deronda to lead the tour—he knows more about the place’s history than I do.”

Gwendolen stole a quick, furtive glance at Deronda, who was looking her way as he helped himself to the entree on his plate, his face as impassible as a painted portrait. The thought of him showing her and her husband the estate that, she had heard with a sharp, painful twist, might well have been his if circumstances had been different, sent a rush of tangled, unnameable thoughts through her head, and she knew her glance had come out awkward, furtive, obvious. To cover her betrayal, she laughed lightly. “You don’t know how afraid I am of Mr. Deronda. Ever since that first night at Leubronn. He came over to the roulette table to watch me play, and I started losing immediately. He put a curse on my luck! He didn’t approve of gambling, he told me so himself. Now whatever I do in front of him, I’m sure he’s judging me.”
“Gad, I’m a little afraid of him myself when he disapproves,” Sir Hugo said, grinning over at Deronda, then leaning in to add softly to Gwendolen: “I don’t think most ladies object to having his eyes on them, though.” The old baronet’s usual facetiousness stung Gwendolen, every bit as much as it often annoyed Deronda.
“I object to any eyes that are critical,” she replied coolly, lifting her chin. “Are there many old rooms left in the rest of the Abbey?”
“Not that many. There’s a beautiful cloistered courtyard, with a long gallery running over the top of it. The finest bit of the old place was turned into stables, though—part of the original medieval church. I restored every other part of the house as well as I could, but the stables were far too big a project, so the horses get the run of the old Benedictine choir. You absolutely must go see it.”
“I’d love to see the horses as much as the old building,” Gwendolen said.
“Oh, I’ve hardly any stud to speak of—Grandcourt will look at my horses with utter contempt, I’m sure,” Sir Hugo waved off. “I’ve given up hunting, gone at a slow, jog-trot pace that suits an old fellow with three daughters. We all lived out at Diplow for two years while the Abbey renovations were going on, you know. Do you like Diplow?”
“Not particularly,” Gwendolen said, with deliberate, effortless indifference, as if she had half a dozen family estates of her own to choose from.
“Ah, of course it can’t compare to Ryelands,” Sir Hugo said, delighted. “Grandcourt only took it for the hunting, of course. But he found something far better there,” he added, lowering his voice, “so he could well prefer it to any other house in the whole world.”
“It has one attraction for me,” Gwendolen said, brushing off the compliment with a thin, cold smile. “It’s close to Offendene.”
“Of course you miss your family,” Sir Hugo said, letting the subject drop.

Deronda had not heard most of that exchange, his attention turned elsewhere, but the glimpses he had caught of Gwendolen that night deepened his impression that every part of her manner was newly, deliberately artificial.

Later, back in the drawing room, Deronda sat down at the piano at someone’s request, and sang. When Mrs. Raymond took his place at the keys, he glanced over and saw that Gwendolen had left her seat, drifting to his end of the room as if to listen closer, now standing with her back turned to the whole party, staring at a beautiful carved ivory cowled monk’s head hung over a small side table. He longed to walk over and speak to her. Why not? He would have done the exact same for any other lady in the room. He hesitated for several long seconds, watching the soft graceful lines of her back, before his feet carried him forward.
It is a terrible mistake to stare long at the back of a woman you have an unspoken wish to speak to: the urge to see what face she hides only grows stronger. Deronda rounded the small table, stepping to stand at her side, and before he could utter a single word, she turned to him. There was no sharp, false smile on her lips, no teasing flicker in her eyes—only a raw, pleading look of deep, unguarded sadness, so different from the chill formality of her greeting at the dinner table that the words died in his throat. For a long, suspended beat, the rest of the drawing room faded away, and they looked only at each other: she sinking into the quiet, wordless rest of someone who no longer has to pretend, he answering her with a depth of soft sympathy that erased every other feeling from his mind.
“Will you not join in the music?” he said at last, to break the hush.
