I found my lady in her private sitting room. Her composure fractured instantly upon hearing that Sergeant Cuff wished to speak with her, replaced by a visible irritation.

"Must I see him?" she demanded, her voice tight. "Can you not represent me, Gabriel?"

I was at a loss to interpret this sudden defensiveness, and my confusion was evident on my face. She quickly clarified her distress.

"My nerves are shaken," she admitted, her tone dropping to a whisper. "There is something in that police officer from London that makes me recoil—I cannot name the reason. I have a presentiment that he carries trouble and misery into this house. It is foolish, and entirely unlike me, yet it is true."

I struggled for words. In my observations, I found Sergeant Cuff to be a man of considerable integrity, far removed from the caricature of the constable. My lady rallied somewhat after confiding in me; she possessed a natural courage that often surfaced when she felt safe.

"If I must see him, I must," she declared, though her resolve wavered. "But I cannot bear to face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain here until he leaves."

This was the first onset of the 'megrims' I had witnessed in my mistress since her youth. I returned to the boudoir where Mr. Franklin was strolling into the garden to join Mr. Godfrey, whose departure was imminent. Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded directly to my mistress's room.

Upon seeing the Sergeant, my lady paled perceptibly. She commanded herself, however, and immediately asked if he objected to my presence. She introduced me as her trusted adviser and old servant, suggesting that my counsel would be most profitable regarding household matters. The Sergeant graciously accepted, noting that he intended to discuss the servants generally and that my experience in that sphere had already proven useful. My lady indicated two chairs, and we seated ourselves for the conference.

"I have already formed an opinion on this case," Sergeant Cuff began, "which I beg your ladyship's permission to withhold for the present. My immediate purpose is to report what I discovered upstairs in Miss Verinder's sitting-room and to outline my proposed course of action."

He detailed the smear on the paint and the conclusions drawn from it, echoing his earlier report to Superintendent Seegrave but with greater decorum. "One fact is certain," he concluded. "The Diamond is missing from the drawer in the cabinet. Another is nearly so: the marks from the smear must belong to an article of dress worn by someone in this house. We must locate that garment before proceeding further."

"And that discovery," my mistress interjected, "presumably implies the identification of the thief?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon," the Sergeant corrected gently. "I do not claim the Diamond is stolen, only that it is missing. Finding the stained dress may lead us to its recovery."

She turned to me. "Do you understand this?"

"Sergeant Cuff understands it, my lady," I replied.

"How do you propose to find the stained dress?" she asked, addressing the Sergeant again. "My faithful servants, who have served me for years, have already had their boxes and rooms searched by the other officer. I cannot and will not permit them to be insulted in such a manner a second time!"

(The irony of a mistress demanding such deference! There was a woman in ten thousand, if you will.)

"That is precisely the point I was about to raise," the Sergeant said. "The other officer has severely hampered this inquiry by allowing the servants to believe they were specifically suspected. If I cause them to feel accused a second time, the obstacles they might erect are unpredictable—particularly among the women. Yet, their boxes *must* be searched again. The first search sought the Diamond; the second must seek the stained dress. I fully agree with you, my lady, that the servants' feelings deserve consideration. However, I am equally convinced that their wardrobes require examination."

This appeared to be a deadlock. My lady expressed her dismay in refined language.

"I have a plan to resolve this difficulty," Sergeant Cuff offered. "I propose explaining the case to the servants."

"The women will immediately feel suspected," I interrupted.

"The women will not, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant countered, "if I inform them I intend to examine the wardrobes of *everyone* who slept in the house on Wednesday night—from your ladyship downwards. It is a mere formality," he added, glancing sideways at my mistress, "but the servants will accept it as equitable treatment between themselves and their superiors. Instead of hindering the investigation, they will likely make a point of honor of assisting it."

I recognized the logic. My lady, once her initial shock subsided, saw the validity of his approach.

"Are you certain the investigation is necessary?" she asked.

"It is the most direct path to our objective, my lady."

My mistress rose to summon her maid. "You shall speak to the servants," she said, "holding the keys to my wardrobe."

Sergeant Cuff halted her with an unexpected query. "Had we not better ensure first that the other ladies and gentlemen in the house consent as well?"

"The only other lady is Miss Verinder," my mistress replied, surprised. "The only gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There is no fear of refusal from any of them."

I reminded her that Mr. Godfrey was departing. As I spoke, Mr. Godfrey knocked at the door to bid farewell, followed by Mr. Franklin, who was accompanying him to the station. My lady explained the situation. Mr. Godfrey resolved it instantly. He signaled to Samuel through the window to retrieve his portmanteau and then placed the key directly into Sergeant Cuff's hand. "My luggage may follow me to London once the inquiry concludes," he stated. The Sergeant accepted the key with a polite apology, noting that the example of the masters would greatly ease the servants' acceptance of the search. After leaving a sympathetic farewell message for Miss Rachel—one that clearly indicated he had not taken her previous hesitation as a final rejection—he departed. Mr. Franklin, following his cousin, assured the Sergeant that all his belongings were open to inspection and held no locks. The Sergeant expressed his gratitude. His strategy had been met with remarkable readiness by my lady, Mr. Godfrey, and Mr. Franklin. Only Miss Rachel remained to align herself with their cooperation before we could convene the servants to begin the search for the stained dress.

As soon as we were left alone, my lady's aversion to the Sergeant seemed to intensify, making the conference increasingly unpleasant. "If I send you Miss Verinder's keys," she said, "do you presume I have fulfilled all your requirements for the present?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon," Sergeant Cuff replied. "Before we commence, I should like, if possible, to inspect the washing-book. The stained article may be linen. Should the search yield nothing, I wish to account for every piece of linen in the house and every item sent to the wash. If an article is missing, there will be at least a presumption that it bears the paint stain and was deliberately destroyed yesterday or today by its owner. Furthermore, Superintendent Seegrave drew the women-servants' attention to the smear when they crowded into the room on Thursday morning. That," he added, turning to me, "may prove to be yet another error of Superintendent Seegrave's."

My lady instructed me to ring the bell for the washing-book. She remained seated, ready to address any further requests the Sergeant might have.

Rosanna Spearman brought the book in. The girl appeared miserably pale and haggard, though sufficiently recovered from her recent illness to perform her duties. Sergeant Cuff observed her attentively—her face as she entered, her crooked shoulder as she exited.

"Do you have anything further to say to me?" my lady asked, eager to escape the Sergeant's company.

The great Cuff opened the washing-book, comprehended it within thirty seconds, and closed it. "I venture to trouble your ladyship with one final question," he said. "Has the young woman who brought us this book been in your employ as long as the other servants?"

"Why do you ask?" my lady queried.

"The last time I saw her," the Sergeant answered, "she was in prison for theft."

At that, there was no recourse but honesty. My mistress emphasized Rosanna's impeccable conduct and the high regard she held at the Reformatory. "You do not suspect her, I hope?" she added earnestly.

"I have already told your ladyship that I suspect no one in this house of theft—at present."

With that assurance, my lady rose to go upstairs and request Miss Rachel's keys. The Sergeant preceded me in opening the door for her, bowing low. My lady shuddered as she passed him.

We waited, and waited, but the keys never arrived. Sergeant Cuff made no comment. He turned his melancholy gaze to the window, shoved his lanky hands into his pockets, and softly whistled "The Last Rose of Summer."

Finally, Samuel entered, not with the keys, but with a scrap of paper for me. I fumbled for my spectacles while the Sergeant's somber eyes fixed upon me. Two or three lines, written in pencil by my lady, informed me that Miss Rachel had flatly refused to allow her wardrobe to be examined. When asked for reasons, she had broken into tears. Pressed again, she had declared: "I won't, because I won't. I must yield to force if you use it, but I will yield to nothing else." I understood my lady's reluctance to confront the Sergeant with such a defiant answer from her daughter. Had I not been past the age of youthful sentimentality, I might have blushed at the thought of facing him myself.

"Any news of Miss Verinder's keys?" the Sergeant asked.

"My young lady refuses to have her wardrobe examined."

"Ah!" the Sergeant said.

His voice lacked the usual discipline of his countenance. The "Ah!" was delivered with the tone of a man hearing an expected outcome. He unsettled me, half-angering and half-frightening me, though I could not discern why.

"Must the search be abandoned?" I asked.

"Yes," the Sergeant replied. "The search must cease because your young lady refuses to submit like the others. We must examine all wardrobes or none. Send Mr. Ablewhite's portmanteau to London by the next train, and return the washing-book, with my compliments and thanks, to the girl who brought it."

He placed the book on the table and, withdrawing his penknife, began to trim his nails.

"You do not appear much disappointed," I observed.

"No," the Sergeant said. "I am not much disappointed."

I attempted to elicit an explanation.

"Why does Miss Rachel obstruct your path?" I inquired. "Is it not in her interest to assist you?"

"Wait a little, Mr. Betteredge—wait a little."

Cleverer minds than mine might have perceived his drift. A person less enamored with Miss Rachel might have seen it too. My lady's horror of him might, as I have since surmised, indicate that *she* had perceived his intent, albeit "in a glass darkly." I did not see it then—that is all I know.

"What is to be done next?" I asked.

Sergeant Cuff finished trimming the nail, inspected it with a melancholy interest, and capped his penknife.

"Come out into the garden," he said. "Let us have a look at the roses."
