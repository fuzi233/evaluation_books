The industrial heart of Milton hammered in a rhythm that defied the natural order, a ceaseless roar of steam and iron struggling perpetually against the sky. Chimneys belched black plumes, and the machinery whirred with a dazzling, senseless fury, driven by crowds whose movements were as mechanical as the gears they tended. Yet, unlike the wood and iron, these people possessed a terrifying vitality; they moved with a frantic, purposeless energy, driven by a hunger for news that tasted like ash in their mouths. There were no loiterers in the streets, no strollers seeking mere pleasure. Every face was etched with the sharp lines of anxiety or avarice, eyes darting between the Mart and the Exchange with the fierce avidity of predators sensing a shift in the wind.

A heavy gloom settled over the town, thicker than the coal dust coating the cobblestones. Trade had faltered; buyers were sparse, and the few who appeared were scrutinized with suspicion by the sellers. Credit was a fragile thing, easily shattered by the tremors rippling from the great neighboring ports, where shipping houses were collapsing like dominoes. Though Milton had hitherto remained untouched by failure, the shadow of American speculation hung low, casting long doubts over local businesses. Whispers circulated in the crowded thoroughfares, a dangerous currency traded in hushed tones. Men avoided naming the doomed, fearing that idle breath might drag the solvent down with the insolvent. *"Thornton is safe,"* they muttered, praising his vast enterprise and his prudent daring. But behind the praise, a darker current flowed. A man would draw another aside, leaning close, whispering that while Thornton's business was large, he had squandered his profits on expansion, leaving him with no capital reserve. His machinery, gleaming and new, was a liability costing a fortune, a gamble that could leave him exposed.

Mr. Harrison, a man of inherited wealth and fearful caution, watched these rumors with a sneer, grudging every penny earned by the bold. But the truth was far more precarious for Mr. Thornton himself. He felt the pressure acutely, not in his pocket, but in the vulnerable fortress of his pride. He viewed himself as the architect of his own destiny, believing that commerce granted every brave, honest man the power to rise above the mundane and command the great game of worldly success. From afar, his name was to be respected, his wishes fulfilled, his word passing like gold. This was the merchant's dream, the ideal his mother had once recited as a trumpet-call to battle: *"Her merchants be like princes."* He had sought to build a legacy, a firm known for generations, alive to distant horizons and dead to immediate needs. It had taken him years of silent toil to glimpse the reality of his station today, standing amidst his own people, in his own town.

For too long, he and his workers had lived parallel lives, close yet never touching, separated by the invisible wall of master and servant. That barrier had begun to crack only recently, sparked by the accidental intimacy of knowing Higgins. Face to face, stripped of titles, they had recognized the shared humanity beneath the skin, that single human heart beating in both chests. Until now, the threat of losing this fragile connection, of seeing his carefully nurtured plans for mutual understanding nipped in the bud, had filled him with a poignant terror. He had never realized how deeply he cared for his role as manufacturer, precisely because it afforded him such intimate contact with a race of people shrewd, ignorant, yet undeniably full of character and strong feeling.

Now, the tide had turned. Bad trade had swept through the market, halving the value of his stock. Orders vanished, locking his capital in idle machinery while expenses drained his reserves relentlessly. Bills for cotton piled up, forcing him to borrow at exorbitant rates, unable to liquidate his assets. Yet, he refused to despair. Day and night, he calculated, foresaw, and prepared, maintaining a calm gentleness at home and a stoic silence in the mill. The men sensed the weight pressing upon him; instead of hostility, they offered a sympathetic silence. Higgins noted the master's suppressed sighs and the way he sat, looking as pale and stiff as a piece of grey calico. Higgins and another worker stayed late, unseen, to finish a neglected task, ensuring the overseer credited the effort to himself.

One evening, as Higgins approached Mr. Thornton in Marlborough Street, he paused, his eyes scanning the employer's weary countenance. *"Eh! I reckon I know who'd ha' been sorry for to see our measter sitting so like a piece o' grey calico!"* he thought, recalling the woeful look on the master's face. He stepped forward, interrupting the hurried stride of his employer.

"Measter," he called out, causing Thornton to stop with a startled annoyance, as if pulled from a trance. "Have yo' heerd aught of Miss Marget lately?"

"Miss—who?" Thornton replied, his brow furrowing.

"Miss Marget—Miss Hale—th' oud parson's daughter," Higgins persisted, his tone respectful despite the familiarity. "Yo' known who I mean well enough—if yo'll only think a bit—"

"Oh yes!" Suddenly, the wintry frost that had bound Thornton's features melted, replaced by a soft, summer-like clarity. Anxiety evaporated, though his lips remained compressed; his eyes smiled benignly. "She's my landlord now, you know, Higgins. I hear of her through her agent here, every now and then. She's well and among friends—thank you, Higgins." The "thank you" lingered, warm and genuine, sending a ripple of hope through the astute observer.

"And she's not getten married, measter?"

"Not yet." The clouds returned instantly. "There is some talk of it, as I understand, with a connection of the family."

"Then she'll not be for coming to Milton again, I reckon."

"No!"

"Stop a minute, measter." Higgins stepped closer, lowering his voice conspiratorially. "Is th' young gentleman cleared?" He emphasized the query with a wink that added layers of mystery to the exchange.

"Th' young gentleman, I mean—Master Frederick, they ca'ed him—her brother as was over here, yo' known."

"Over here."

"Ay, to be sure, at th' missus's death. Yo' need na be feared of my telling; for Mary and me, we knowed it all along, only we held our peace, for we got it through Mary working in th' house."

"And he was over. It was her brother."

"Sure enough, and I reckoned yo' knowed it, or I'd never ha' let on. Yo' knowed she had a brother?"

"Yes, I know all about him. And he was over at Mrs. Hale's death?"

"Nay! I'm not going for to tell more. I've maybe getten them into mischief already, for they kept it very close. I nobbut wanted to know if they'd getten him cleared?"

"Not that I know of. I know nothing. I only hear of Miss Hale, now, as my landlord, and through her lawyer."

Thornton broke away, resuming his business, leaving Higgins baffled. Alone, Thornton reflected, *"It was her brother,"* he whispered to himself. *"I am glad. I may never see her again; but it is a comfort—a relief—to know that much. I knew she could not be unmaidenly; and yet I yearned for conviction. Now I am glad!"*

This small piece of knowledge was a golden thread weaving through the darkening web of his fortunes, which grew ever more ominous. His agent had trusted a failing American house, and the collapse had triggered a chain reaction of failures, toppling others like a house of cards. Could he stand?

Night after night, Thornton retreated to his private room, surrounded by books and papers, staying awake long after his family slept. He believed no one knew of his vigilance. One morning, as dawn filtered through the cracks of his shutters, he sat there, exhausted, resigned to skipping the necessary rest before the day's labor began. The door creaked open, and his mother stood there, dressed exactly as she had been the previous day. Like him, she had refused sleep.

"Mother! why are you not in bed?"

"Son John," she said softly, "do you think I can sleep with an easy mind, while you keep awake full of care? You have not told me what your trouble is; but sore trouble you have had these many days past."

"Trade is bad."

"And you dread——"

"I dread nothing," he replied, lifting his head with rigid determination. "I know that no man will suffer by me. That was my anxiety."

"But how do you stand? Shall you—will it be a failure?" Her voice trembled, breaking its usual steadiness.

"Not a failure. I must give up business, but I pay all men. I might redeem myself—I am sorely tempted—"

"How? Oh, John! keep up your name—try all risks for that. How redeem it?"

"By a speculation offered to me, but full of risk; but, if successful, placing me high above water-mark, so that no one need ever know the strait I am in. Still, if it fails—"

"And if it fails," she interrupted, stepping forward to lay her hand on his arm, her eyes blazing with urgent light. She held her breath, waiting for the conclusion.
