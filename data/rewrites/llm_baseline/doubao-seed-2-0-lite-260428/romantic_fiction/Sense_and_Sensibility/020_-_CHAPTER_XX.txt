The next afternoon, as the Miss Dashwoods stepped through one door of the Park’s drawing room, streaks of cold November rain smudging the windows behind them, Mrs. Palmer came skittering in through the opposite entry, her cheeks pink from the chill, her good humour and giddy sparkle as bright as ever. She squeezed all three of their hands in turn, warm and affectionate as if they were dearest long-lost friends, and gushed over how delighted she was to see them.
“I was so afraid this horrid rain would keep you home!” she exclaimed, sinking onto the sofa to tuck Elinor on one side and Marianne on the other. “It would have been such a shame, for we’re off again tomorrow, you know. We absolutely must go, the Westons are coming to stay with us next week. The whole trip was such a surprise! I had no idea we were bound for Barton until the carriage was already pulled up to our door, and Mr. Palmer asked out of nowhere if I’d like to come. He’s so droll! He never tells me a single thing ahead of time. I only wish we could stay longer – but I do hope we’ll all meet again in town very soon.”
The Dashwoods were forced to gently dash that expectation, as they had no funds for a London winter.
“Not go to town?” Mrs. Palmer laughed outright, scandalised. “I shall be so disappointed if you don’t! I could get you the loveliest little house imaginable, right next to ours in Hanover Square. You simply must come. I’ll happily chaperon you to every ball and dinner for as long as you like, right up until I’m confined, if Mrs. Dashwood doesn’t care to venture out into public herself.”
They thanked her warmly, but held firm against all her entreaties, even when her husband wandered into the room mid-plea.
“Oh my love!” she called, tugging at his sleeve. “You must help me persuade the Miss Dashwoods to come to town this winter!”
Her “love” said nothing at all. He offered the ladies only the faintest, most perfunctory bow before launching into a petulant complaint about the weather.
“How detestable all this rain is,” he grumbled. “This sort of weather makes every thing and every body unbearable. The dullness seeps indoors just as it does out of doors, turning every acquaintance tedious. What on earth is Sir John thinking, not having a billiard room in his house? So few people understand what comfort even is! Sir John is every bit as stupid and lifeless as this dreary weather.”
The rest of the party soon drifted into the room, and Sir John boomed out a greeting to Marianne before leaning in teasingly.
“I’m afraid, Miss Marianne, you couldn’t take your usual walk out to Allenham today, eh?”
Marianne’s face fell grave, and she said nothing, her fingers twisting tight in the fabric of her skirt.
“Oh, don’t be so sly with us!” Mrs. Palmer giggled. “We all know all about it, I assure you! And I admire your taste immensely – he’s so very handsome, that Mr. Willoughby. We don’t live all that far from him in Somerset, you know – not more than ten miles, I’d wager.”
“Nearer thirty,” her husband corrected dryly.
“Ah, well! Hardly a difference at all!” she waved off. “I’ve never been to his house, but everyone says it’s such a sweet, pretty place.”
“The vilest, most desolate spot I have ever laid eyes on in my life,” Mr. Palmer said.
Marianne stayed perfectly silent, but her wide, bright eyes gave away just how closely she was hanging on every word.
“Is it really that ugly?” Mrs. Palmer blinked. “Well then, I suppose the pretty house everyone gushes about must belong to someone else.”
When they gathered in the dining room, Sir John groaned at the sight of only eight place settings. “My dear,” he said to his wife, “it’s so provoking we’re so few today! Why ever didn’t you ask the Gilberts to join us?”
“Sir John, I told you before you asked,” Lady Middleton replied serenely, “that it was impossible. They dined with us just last week.”
“Now you and I, Sir John,” Mrs. Jennings cut in cheerfully, “would never stand on such silly ceremony!”
“Then you would both be very ill-bred,” Mr. Palmer pointed out.
“My love, you contradict absolutely everyone!” his wife laughed, as merry as ever. “Don’t you know you’re being perfectly rude?”
“I did not contradict anyone by calling your mother ill-bred,” he said.
“Go on, abuse me as much as you like!” the good-natured old lady cackled. “You took Charlotte off my hands, and you can’t give her back. So I have the whip hand over you forever.”
Charlotte laughed so hard she snickered, gushing that she didn’t care how cross he got with her, they had to live together after all, and she wouldn’t trade his droll moods for anything. No one could have been more unshakably good-natured, or more determined to be happy, than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband’s performative indifference, insolence, and general discontent never stung her – if anything, when he scolded or snapped, she found it delightfully diverting.
“Mr. Palmer is so droll!” she whispered to Elinor later. “He’s always out of sorts!”
Elinor, watching him for a while, did not believe he was genuinely as ill-natured or ill-bred as he pretended to be. She suspected his temper was a little soured, as many a man’s was, by the realisation that he had married a very silly woman thanks to some inexplicable bias toward a pretty face – but that was too common a mistake for any sensible man to be permanently broken by. No, it was far more likely a hunger for distinction that made him treat everyone with contempt, and rail against every small thing around him. He simply wanted to appear superior to everyone else. It was a common enough motive, not worth wondering at – though his methods, while they might establish he was far ruder than anyone else, were unlikely to endear him to anyone but his wife.
“Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood!” Mrs. Palmer said, a moment later. “I have such a favour to ask of you and your sister. Will you come spend Christmas at Cleveland with us? Do say yes, especially while the Westons are staying! You can’t imagine how happy it will make me – it will be perfectly delightful! My love,” she turned to her husband, prodding his arm, “don’t you long to have the Miss Dashwoods come stay at Cleveland?”
“Certainly,” he replied, with a cold little sneer. “I came all the way to Devonshire for no other reason at all.”
“There now!” his wife beamed. “You see Mr. Palmer expects you – you simply can’t refuse.”
Elinor and Marianne both politely but resolutely declined the invitation.
“But you simply must come!” Mrs. Palmer pressed on. “I know you’ll adore it. The Westons will be there, and we’re so gay just now! Mr. Palmer is forever gallivanting around the county canvassing for the election, so there are dozens of new people coming to dine every night that I’ve never met before – it’s perfectly charming! Though poor fellow, it is terribly fatiguing for him, he has to make every single person he meets like him.”
Elinor had to bite her lip to keep from laughing as she agreed how very hard that must be.
“Won’t it be so wonderful when he’s in Parliament?” Charlotte gushed. “I shall laugh so hard! It will be absurd to see all his letters addressed to him with an M.P. on the end. But do you know, he says he will never frank my post for me? He swears he won’t. Don’t you, Mr. Palmer?”
Mr. Palmer ignored her.
“He hates writing, you know,” she continued, unphased. “He says it’s a dreadful bore.”
“No,” he snapped. “I never said anything so irrational. Don’t go palming all your silly turns of phrase on me.”
“There now, you see how droll he is!” she giggled to Elinor. “He’s always like this! Sometimes he won’t speak to me for half a whole day, then out he pops with some absurd little remark about absolutely nothing at all.”
When they returned to the drawing room, Mrs. Palmer surprised Elinor by leaning over to whisper, “You do like Mr. Palmer excessively, don’t you?”
“Certainly,” Elinor said. “He seems very agreeable.”
“I’m so glad you do! I knew you would, he’s so pleasant. And Mr. Palmer is excessively fond of you and your sisters, I can tell you – he will be dreadfully disappointed if you don’t come to Cleveland. I can’t imagine why you keep objecting!”
Elinor quickly steered the conversation away, and asked if the Palmers saw much of Mr. Willoughby, back home in Somerset. She hoped to hear a more unbiased account of his character than the Middletons’ partial acquaintance could give, something that would set all her lingering worries about Marianne at rest.
“Oh dear, yes! I know him extremely well,” Mrs. Palmer replied. “Not that I ever actually spoke to him, you understand – but I saw him constantly in town. I just never happened to be staying at Barton when he was at Allenham, what bad luck! Mama met him once here before, but I was with my uncle at Weymouth. I dare say we would see a great deal of him at Cleveland if his estate wasn’t so far out of the way. He’s hardly ever at Combe Magna anyway, from what I hear – and even if he were, Mr. Palmer would never call on him, he’s in the opposition, you know, and the place is miles out of the way. But I know exactly why you’re asking about him! Everyone says your sister is going to marry him. I’m monstrously glad of it, that means she’ll be my neighbour!”
“Upon my word,” Elinor said, “you know far more about the matter than I do, if you have reason to expect such a match.”
“Oh don’t pretend to deny it! Everyone is talking about it!” Mrs. Palmer laughed. “I heard it in London, right before we left.”
“My dear Mrs. Palmer!”
“Upon my honour, I did! I ran into Colonel Brandon on Bond Street, Monday morning just before we left town, and he told me himself.”
Elinor was stunned. “Colonel Brandon told you that? That can’t be right. Even if it were true, he would never share such private news with someone who had no particular stake in it.”
“But I assure you it’s true, let me tell you how it happened!” Charlotte insisted. “When we met him, he turned around and walked with us, we started chattering about my brother and sister, and all sorts of things, and I said to him ‘Colonel, I hear there’s a new family settled at Barton Cottage, Mama writes that they’re all very pretty, and one of the girls is going to marry Mr. Willoughby of Combe Magna. Is it true? You must know, you’ve been in Devonshire so recently.’”
“And what did the Colonel say?” Elinor asked, her heart tight.
“Oh, he didn’t say much at all! But he looked like he knew it was perfectly true, so I set it down as fact right then. It will be so delightful! When is the wedding to be?”
“I hope Colonel Brandon was well?” Elinor asked, dodging the question.
“Oh, perfectly well! He sang your praises the whole walk, wouldn’t stop saying how wonderful you are.”
“I am very flattered by his good opinion. He seems an excellent, uncommonly pleasing man.”
“So do I! He’s such a charming man, it’s a terrible pity he’s so grave and dull all the time. Mama says he was in love with your sister too, you know – and that’s such a compliment, he almost never falls in love with anyone.”
“Is Mr. Willoughby well known in your part of Somerset?” Elinor asked.
“Oh, everyone knows him! Though I suppose not many are actually intimate with him, since Combe Magna is so far out of the way – but every last person thinks he’s extremely agreeable. No one is more liked than Mr. Willoughby wherever he goes, you can tell your sister that. She’s a monstrous lucky girl to get him, though he’s far luckier to get her – she’s so handsome and sweet, nothing could ever be good enough for her. Though I don’t think she’s all that much handsomer than you, you know – I think you’re both excessively pretty, and Mr. Palmer thinks so too, even if he wouldn’t admit it last night.”
Elinor was glad for even that small, unprompted testimony in Willoughby’s favour.
“I am so glad we’ve finally become friends!” Charlotte went on cheerfully. “Now I hope we shall always be great companions. You can’t imagine how long I’ve wanted to meet you! It’s perfect that you live at the cottage. And I’m so glad your sister is going to make such a good marriage! I hope you’ll visit them at Combe Magna all the time, everyone says it’s such a sweet place.”
“Have you known Colonel Brandon very long?” Elinor asked.
“Oh, ages! Ever since I married my Mr. Palmer. He’s a great particular friend of Sir John’s,” she added, lowering her voice to a giddy whisper. “I do believe he would have been very glad to marry me, if he could. Sir John and Lady Middleton wished for it desperately. But Mama didn’t think the match was good enough for me, otherwise Sir John would have asked him, and we would have been married straight away.”
“Had Colonel Brandon never spoken of his regard for you to you, himself, before Sir John proposed the match to your mother?”
“Oh no! But if Mama hadn’t objected, I’m sure he would have asked me, right away. He’d only seen me twice back then, I was still at school. But I’m far happier as I am now, anyway. Mr. Palmer is exactly the sort of droll, fun husband I like.”
