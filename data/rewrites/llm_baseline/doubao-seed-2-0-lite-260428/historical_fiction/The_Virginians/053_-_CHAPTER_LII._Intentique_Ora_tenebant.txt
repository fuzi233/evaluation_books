For months on end, our weary life dragged on within that rickety French frontier fort. Its commandant and I passed the days in a cycle of petty spats and quick, drink-mellowed reconciliations: greasy, smoke-hazed card games by tallow candlelight, discordant duets where he wheezed through his asthmatic old flute and I fumbled over chords on my cracked, warped guitar. The Seneca woman they called the Fawn took her lord’s blows and his jugs of raw liquor without complaint, tending her infant papoose, nursing the commandant through his worst gout flares, and tending me through bouts of chattering ague, and so the days drifted by until the start of last autumn, when a roving hunter rode in bearing news that set the tiny garrison buzzing with wild excitement.

The Marquis de Montcalm had dispatched a great body of troops to reinforce all French-held forts, and push new outposts deep into British territory. The regiments had left Quebec and Montreal, sailing up the St. Lawrence and inland across the lakes in bateaux, hauling artillery, rations, and piles of war stores. It meant our host Museau would be stripped of his independent command, superseded by a ranking officer who might choose to exchange me for a French prisoner, or hand me over to their native allies in reprisal for the cruelties British frontiersmen had visited on countless French soldiers captured on the border. The fort’s ragged rank and file were giddy at the prospect of reinforcements: they would march on Pennsylvania and New York, seize Albany and Philadelphia, drive the “Rosbif” redcoats into the sea, and claim all America for France from the Mississippi to Newfoundland.

For all that triumphant boasting, though, the coming of the reinforcements only soured old Museau’s mood.
“Eh, commandant!” I teased him. “Fort bien — but what of your farm in Normandy, your jugs of hard cider, and the tripes à la mode de Caen you rave about? Where will those be, stuck out here overseeing a garrison of a hundred rowdy regulars?”
“Ah, it all sounds very fine, my lad,” he grumbled back. “But where will you be, when honest old Museau is no longer here to watch out for you? Few men alive have my humanity. Once there’s a full garrison posted here, my successor will never grant you the leniencies I’ve let you enjoy. You’ll be locked in a sty like a pig fattened for slaughter. As sure as one of our officers falls into the hands of your frontier brigands and comes to harm, you will pay for it with your own skin. They’ll hand you over to our red allies — to the brethren of La Biche there behind you. Did you not see what they did to your countrymen we captured on Braddock’s field last year? Roasting was the mildest torment they meted, ma foi — was it not, La Biche?”

He went on to recount a string of gory, jocular descriptions of tortures: eyes burned out of their sockets, teeth and nails wrenched loose, limbs lacerated raw. I saw dear Miss Theo turn pale at the recital, and spared her the rest of the grisly details, out of pity. La Biche, for her part, was far less disturbed than the ladies in our parlor that night: she had witnessed such atrocities all her life, a Seneca woman whose home villages stood near the great cataract between Ontario and Erie, where tribes shifted their alliances between French and English as easily as the wind changed, and no man could say whether white or red skin bore the greater share of cruelty in that border war.
“They may chop me into cutlets and broil me for all I care, commandant,” I said coolly. “But you will never see that Normandy farm you dream of.”
“Go fetch the whisky bottle, La Biche,” he roared.
“And it is not too late, even now,” I pressed on. “I will pay any guide who sees me safe home a very large reward. On my honour as a gentleman, ten thousand livres — and I will give that sum to anyone who brings me a token to prove he has done it: my grandfather’s watch and seal, carved with our family arms, which I know sits locked away in a chest somewhere in this fort.”
“Ah, scoundrel!” he cackled, hoarse with laughter. “You have eyes everywhere, do you! All is prize in war, of course.”
“Think of the little house in your village,” I went on. “A fine field nearby, half a dozen cows grazing in it, an orchard heavy with fruit come harvest.”
“With Javotte at the door spinning at her wheel, and a pair of rascal children with cheeks as red as the apples! Oh, my country! Oh, my mother!” he whimpered, slamming his fist on the table. “Quick, La Biche, bring the whisky at once!”

That whole night the commandant sat turning the matter over in his head, and La Biche too sat silent and melancholy, huddled in a corner nursing her baby, her eyes fixed steadily on me every time I glanced her way. When the little papoose began to cry, Museau snarled a string of oaths and ordered her to take it off to her own hovel at the edge of the fort. Once she was gone, we spoke our minds freely, and I laid out every argument his greed could not resist.
“But how do I know that hunter you speak of will not betray us?” he asked.
“That is my secret,” I told him. And now, here among friends, I may confess it. The hunter Silverheels — that was his name — loved to linger in the fort for days at a time when he came in from the wilderness, drinking, gambling his furs against the garrison’s liquor, joining the rough concerts we held after tattoo, where I scraped away at my guitar and the men sang raucous chorus songs. At one of these gatherings, after we had run through all our French drinking songs, I announced I would sing an English ballad for them. To the tune of *Over the Hills and Far Away*, the same old air my grandfather used to hum in Marlborough’s camp, I strung together some rough verses:
“This long, long year, a prisoner drear;
Ah, me! I’m tired of lingering here:
I’ll give a hundred guineas gay,
To be over the hills and far away.”
The hunter pretended not to understand the words, but I caught the twinkle in his eye. The very next day, when no other men were within earshot, he slipped past me and hummed that exact refrain under his breath, in perfectly good English. “If you are ready,” he murmured, “I am ready. I know who your people are, and the way to reach them. Talk to the Fawn, and she will tell you what to do.” Then he switched to loud French as two soldiers rounded the corner, made a mocking bow, and wandered off to gamble elsewhere.

I knew then the escape was agreed, and the poor Fawn played her part with perfect, quiet deftness. No more words needed to pass between Museau and me. She had long been granted free access to my quarters, tending me through my wound and my fevers, bringing me food, helping keep my small log cabin tidy. She was permitted to leave the fort whenever she pleased, to gather vegetables from the garden plots by the riverside. Silverheels gathered up his store of flints, powder, and blankets once he had gambled away the last of his peltries, and rode off. Three days later, La Biche gave me the signal that the night of my escape had come.
My quarters were a small log cabin just behind the fort’s single small bastion gun, pointing west over the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela, where the French Fort Duquesne stood on its little island. “You see those trees on the far shore?” she said to me in her rough French jargon, the day before. “He waits for you behind them.” That same afternoon, she came back from the fields with a pick slung over her arm and a basket of soup herbs, sat it down just inside my door, and whispered “Hide it. You will want it soon.” The next day, she stormed into my room pretending to be angry, yelling “My lord! My lord! Why do you not come to commandant’s dinner? He is in a very bad mood! Entendez-vous?” — and as she spoke, she tossed a coiled length of rope at my feet.
I hobbled after her on my crutch to the commandant’s quarters, and she breathed “Pour ce soir,” as I passed, and I knew the hour had come.

Museau played the fool perfectly. He snapped that the soup was cold, chatted idly of this and that, and settled in to play piquet with me as we smoked our pipes, while La Biche huddled in her usual corner. His whisky bottle ran dry, and he gave me a knowing little grin as he said he would fetch another bottle for both of us, stumping off to lock it away in his inner store room, out of La Biche’s reach. The moment his back turned, La Biche sprang to her feet, was at my knees in an instant, kissing my hand, pressing it to her heart, bursting into quiet tears. I had never guessed the depth of the poor creature’s silent, tender attachment to me before, and I was still flustered and off balance when Museau returned. He noticed nothing amiss, and we sat back to our cards, my mind so full of the coming escape I scarcely saw the faces on the cards before me.
“I am winning every hand from you to-night, milor,” he said, grinning grimly. “We play on parole, eh?”
“And you may count on mine,” I said.
“Ah, that is all you have left!” he laughed.
“Monsieur,” I replied, “my word is good for ten thousand livres,” and we played on.
At last he complained of a headache, and said it was time for bed. “Good night, my little milor,” he said, then paused, his eyes twinkling with sarcasm. “Wait — you will fall over if you leave your crutch behind.” I had been so caught up in my nerves I had forgotten my old limp, and was walking away as spry as a grenadier. “What a villainous night out there!” he added, glancing through the door. A great tempest was roaring over the fort, rain lashing down in sheets. “Bring a lanthorn, La Tulipe, and lock my lord comfortably into his quarters!” I caught a glimpse of poor La Biche peeking out behind him in the dark of the doorway.

The rain was so heavy the sentries huddled in their boxes, and no one heard me at work. The cabin was built of heavy logs set deep in the ground, and I dug at the earth under the base for hours, long past tattoo, long past midnight, until I lifted my hand up and felt the cold rain falling on it from the outside. I crept out through the hole, climbed up to the bastion, and slung my rope over the barrel of that small cannon, my heart thundering in my chest for fear the sentry pacing above would spot me and fire a musket ball into my back. The wall was only twelve feet high, and I dropped soft and silent into the ditch below. The moon broke through the storm clouds soon after, and I saw the silver sheen of the Monongahela stretching before me, the dark clump of trees waiting on the far shore. I ran for them as fast as my still-weak legs would carry me, and oh, what a rush of relief flooded through me when I heard a low voice humming, just under the trees, the exact refrain of that old ballad: *Over the hills and far away*.

As George spoke those last words, Miss Theo, who had been sitting at the harpsichord, turned around and dashed out the melody on the keys, and every soul in that little parlor broke into the merry, familiar chorus.
“Our route,” George went on once the singing died down, “lay along the right bank of the Monongahela, through flat, wild forest my guide knew like the back of his hand. By dawn we had reached a clearing we knew all too well: it was the fatal field where Braddock had fallen, the very ground I had been rescued from a year before, the trees now just beginning to blaze with the fiery crimson and gold of American autumn.”
“Ah, brother!” Harry cried out, seizing George’s hand tight in his own. “I was off gambling and making a fool of myself at Bath’s Wells and in London, while you were flying for your life through this very wilderness! What a miserable, selfish spendthrift I have been!”
“I think you are not unworthy to be called your mother’s son, dear boy,” Mrs. Lambert said softly, her eyes glistening with tears. To see Harry’s repentance, his unselfish joy at his brother’s safe return, was proof enough of the goodness still in that humbled, kind-hearted young sinner.
“We crossed the river soon after,” George resumed, “striking out along the western slopes of the Alleghenies, through grand old forests of oak, maple, and towering poplars that grew a hundred feet high without a single lower branch. We kept a careful watch for wandering Indian bands — their loyalty has never been certain, and they have turned far more hostile to us ever since we cheated and mistreated them, and the French routed Braddock’s force two years prior — and for roving French patrols.
I was still weak from my long illness, and our trek through the wilds lasted more than a fortnight. The nights grew sharp with frost, so we lit small fires at our feet and slept wrapped in our blankets as best we could. We passed multiple camps of mountain families, out in the woods at maple sugaring time, who welcomed us to their fires and shared their venison with us. We crossed the two ranges of the Laurel Hills, then the main Alleghenies, before descending the wild, breathtaking pass of Will’s Creek, a valley hemmed in by thousand-foot white cliffs that looked like the towers of some giant forgotten fortress, with eagles circling the crag tops to guard their nests.
And then we came down into Cumberland, where we had marched out a year before, now garrisoned with a full regiment of our own countrymen. You cannot imagine what a welcome sight it was, when I rounded the last bend of the trail and saw the English colours flying bright over the banks of our own native Potomac.”
