I cannot say how many hours I passed unconscious on the cold stone of the underground storeroom, but it felt like an entire cycle of Barsoom’s faint, distant twin moons. I jolted awake to sharp, shrieking cries of alarm, my wits still fumbling to place me in the dark stone cell, when a roar of gunfire split the air. The blasts bounced off the subterranean walls in a cascade of deafening, overlapping echoes that rattled my teeth in my skull.

I was on my feet before the last echo died. A dozen lesser therns blocked the far storeroom doorway, their wicked rifles still smoking. My remaining companions from the escape party lay motionless on the floor—all save Thuvia of Ptarth and Tars Tarkas, who had been asleep out of the line of the first raking fire, same as me. The therns lowered their weapons then, their faces twisted with chagrin, confusion, and raw, unmasked fear, as they realized they had nearly gunned down the very high-ranking Holy Thern they served.

I seized the gambit instantly, roaring in tones of thunderous, feigned fury: “What treason is this? Do your vassal hands dare raise arms against Sator Throg?”

One of the men dropped to his knees, the others edging backward toward the exit like rats trying to slip from a lion’s den. “Mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” he babbled.

“Demand their purpose here,” Thuvia breathed at my elbow.

“What do you trespass in this chamber for?” I snarled.

“We hunt the two fugitives from the outer world, by order of the Father of Therns!” the man stammered. “One a pale man with raven hair, the other a giant green Thark warrior—” He darted a suspicious, wary glance at Tars Tarkas as he spoke.

Thuvia stepped forward then, her voice sharp and cutting. “Here is the Thark, and if you trouble to look at the corpse crumpled by the entry, you will find the black-haired man. It was Sator Throg and his small band of slaves that did what your useless guard ranks could not: we slew one fugitive, captured the other alive, in return for the liberty he promised us. Now your fool’s rush of gunfire has butchered all the prisoners save myself, and nearly murdered the Holy Thern himself!”

The lesser therns looked so sheepish and terrified I almost laughed. When Thuvia suggested they drag all the bodies down to the plant men to dispose of, then return to their quarters, they jumped to obey at once. As one of the men stooped to hoist the corpse of the real Sator Throg, he paused, his gaze locking on the dead man’s bald, polished scalp. He flicked a furtive, sneaking glance at me from the corner of his eye, and I knew for a certainty he had guessed the truth of our disguise. He dared not speak it aloud yet, not without proof, but as he carried Sator Throg’s body out the door, a thin, cunning smile of triumphant conspiracy curved his lips.

When the last of the therns vanished, only the three of us remained. The marksmanship of the guard had wiped out every last slim chance our dead companions had held of breaking free to the surface world above. Thuvia wasted no time pressing us to run for our lives: she, too, had seen that thern’s knowing smirk. “That bodes no good, Prince of Helium,” she said, her voice tight with urgency. “He would not dare accuse you outright without proof, but he will run straight to Matai Shang, the Father of Therns. He is a high-ranking thorian among the lesser ranks—his audience will not wait long. By the time he finishes telling his tale, every gallery, chamber, garden and temple courtyard within ten miles will be swarming with searchers, and we will be hunted down like trapped banths.”

I shrugged, weary, my old wounds throbbing so sharply I could taste blood where I bit my lip. I had lost enough blood in earlier fights that the ache would not fade, and there was no salve here to knit the flesh fast—nothing like the miracle-working unguents the green Martian women of the Thark hordes used that could put a warrior back on his feet in an hour. For the first time in a hundred battles against Tharks and Zodangan raiders, a wave of utter hopelessness washed over me. But then the long, flowing yellow silk of Sator Throg’s robes blew across my face, caught by a stray underground draught. That disguise might still buy us the time we needed to outrun the alarm.

“Will darkness aid us?” Tars Tarkas rumbled, his great tusks glinting in the dim radium light. “We stand small chance of slipping past sentries in the glare of day.”

Thuvia nodded, glancing toward a cliffside window cut high in the stone wall overlooking Valley Dor. “Night is far safer here. Few souls wander the gardens after dark, and the sentries’ watch grows lax. You freed me from my chains at midnight, we reached this storeroom two hours later, and you all slept for fourteen full hours. The red sun is about to set right now—we have only to see it for ourselves.”

She led us through winding, narrow corridors until we reached that open ledge. The huge, bloated red orb of Barsoom’s sun was dipping below the western peaks of the Otz range, painting the crimson sward of the valley below in fire. The thin, rarefied atmosphere of Mars traps almost no heat: we could already feel the sharp, bone-deep cold seeping in before the last sliver of sun vanished. There is no twilight on Barsoom, no soft fading of light: the sun drops below the horizon like a snuffed lamp, and the world plunges at once into total darkness.

Below us, we spotted a canoe drifting down the sacred River Iss, carrying a dozen red pilgrims from the outer world, sailing to their fated death in Dor. The thern sentry on the balcony beneath us spotted them at the same instant, and lifted his voice in a shrill, keening wail that called every man-eating plant man in the valley to the river bank. The plant men bounded across the sward on their overgrown toes and massive, prehensile tails, their talons outstretched. The pilgrims landed, put up a brief, futile fight, and then vanished under a heaving, writhing mass of the brutes, their blood drained in seconds. Thuvia pointed further across the valley: a dozen great white apes were loping toward the bank, to feast on the flesh the plant men left behind.

Then the sun vanished, and darkness so thick you could almost press your palm against it swallowed the valley whole.

We turned back to the ascent corridors, heading thousands of feet up the winding cliff passages toward the surface. Twice, giant banths—Barsoom’s ferocious tawny lion equivalents, maned, six-legged, the top predator of the underworld—blocked our path, snarling, green eyes glowing in the dark. But Thuvia spoke a single low word of command, and the great beasts dropped their heads, growling sullenly, and slunk away to let us pass. When I asked how she tamed them so easily, she laughed, then shuddered. Sator Throg had once thrown her into a banth pit for spurning his advances, she explained, and some odd quirk of her voice had calmed the beasts so thoroughly they fawned at her feet instead of tearing her to shreds. The therns had kept her ever since to train and control their scavenger guard of banths, who disposed of unclaimed corpses in the underground levels.

A wild, brilliant idea struck me. “Call every banth you can reach,” I said. “Unleash them ahead of us in the upper gardens. The chaos they sow will draw every guard’s eye away from us, buying us a clear run for the ramparts.”

Thuvia grinned, and began to hum a low, thrumming half-purr, the kind of call a Martian beast tamer uses to summon her charges. One by one, the banths crept out of side tunnels to join us, until fifty of the massive brutes padded around our little party, their soft, noiseless paws making no sound on the stone floors. It was a surreal procession: the gem-encrusted golden walls of the therns’ underworld glinting under the dim glow of distant radium bulbs, the great green warlord Tars Tarkas towering head and shoulders above even the largest banth, me clad in Sator Throg’s yellow robes and priceless diadem, and Thuvia, the stolen princess, leading the ravenous horde like some goddess of old Mars.

We crossed through chambers crammed full of the warped, blind, half-human hybrid offspring of prisoners trapped in the underworld for generations, their skin bleached pale from lack of sunlight, their limbs twisted by centuries of inbreeding, looking less like living people than the lost souls of Dante’s Inferno. The banths sniffed hungrily at the limp, defenseless forms strewn across the floors, but held fast to Thuvia’s unspoken command. She explained that no thern would dare wander these lower levels after dark: the banths hunt free, and chained prisoners regularly break their bonds to tear any wandering thern apart.

At last we reached the top of the ascent, and Thuvia paused at a heavy carved door. “Beyond here lies the inner gardens,” she said. “Four miles to the outer ramparts, and every inch of the way is patrolled by sentries. The therns fear no one in all Barsoom, for no soul dares step foot in the forbidden Valley Dor—save their only enemy, the Black Pirates of Barsoom.”

The door swung open. The sweet scent of blooming Martian blossoms washed over us, and the cool night air bit at my cheeks. The banths caught the wild, open scent of the gardens, and burst past us in a surging, roaring tide, disappearing across the sward under the lurid light of the low, hurtling nearer moon of Mars.

A second later, a sharp, high cry of alarm burst from the nearest temple rooftop. It was picked up by the next sentry, and the next, racing east and west along every roof, wall, and rampart, echoing until the whole forbidden kingdom rang with the warning call. Tars Tarkas’s long, double-edged Thark sword flew from its scabbard with a metallic shriek, and Thuvia pressed close to my side, trembling. The hunt was on.
