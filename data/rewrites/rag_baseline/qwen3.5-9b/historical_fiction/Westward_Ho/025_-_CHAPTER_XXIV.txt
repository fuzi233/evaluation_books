To truly comprehend the psychological landscape of Amyas and his companions, one must first deconstruct the epistemological frameworks governing their perception of the New World. Their worldview operates on a binary dialectic: the rigid, dogmatic certainty of scripture versus the fluid, empirical reality of indigenous culture. They reject the Enlightenment-era scientific consensus—that humanity evolved from primate ancestors—as a reductionist error that strips human agency of dignity. Instead, they cling to a theological narrative wherein humans are inherently fallen, susceptible to demonic influence, and perpetually engaged in a struggle against spiritual entropy. This cognitive dissonance creates a lens through which every interaction is filtered not as cultural exchange, but as a battle between civilization and chaos, order and the void.

Upon landing, the settlers encounter a settlement that defies their expectations of primitive savagery. The architecture, composed of orderly palm-leaf sheds with hammocks suspended between trees, suggests a society capable of organization and aesthetic refinement. Yet, the auditory assault immediately reasserts their preconceived notions. The cacophony of drums and trumpets, producing a discordant symphony of howls and thumps, serves as an auditory manifestation of the "evil spirit" they anticipate. In their minds, this noise is not merely sound; it is evidence of a ritualistic invocation of malevolent forces, a sonic barrier designed to repel the intruders. The settlers interpret the villagers' attire—the vibrant dyes of arnotto and indigo, the feathers, the jaguar skins—as costumes worn by actors in a grotesque drama, specifically the role of the Devil.

This interpretation finds immediate validation in the arrival of the Piache. Dressed in a costume explicitly designed to evoke the figure of a demon—complete with horns, a grinning mask, and a rattling instrument—he embodies the settlers' fears. When the Piache advances to deliver a harangue, the settlers perceive it not as diplomatic protocol, but as a theatrical performance of vice. The Piache's query regarding Amalivaca, the mythical civilizer, triggers a defensive reflex in Amyas. He responds by projecting the virtues of Queen Elizabeth onto the local context, a rhetorical strategy aimed at establishing a moral equivalence between the English crown and the indigenous leadership. However, the Piache's response reveals a deeper layer of syncretism; he conflates the English monarch with the daughters of Amalivaca, suggesting a shared lineage or divine mandate that transcends the settlers' rigid categorization of "us" versus "them."

The negotiation shifts from verbal posturing to a display of supernatural authority when the Piache enters the hut to consult the "Daughter of the Sun." The ensuing spectacle—a frenzied trance involving screaming, foaming, and convulsions—is interpreted by the Englishmen as a classic staging of dramatic irony, where the appearance of Vice necessitates the entrance of Virtue. As the Piache collapses, exhausted from his exertion, a voice emerges from the darkness. This voice possesses qualities that defy the settlers' expectations: it is round, clear, and rich, lacking the guttural timbre associated with indigenous speech. Its melodic range and emotional depth resonate with the musical traditions of Old England, creating a bridge of familiarity amidst the alien surroundings. The song, described as possessing the purity of a bird's call yet the power of a human choir, acts as a seductive force, momentarily suspending the settlers' hostility and inviting them into a realm of aesthetic transcendence.

The revelation of the maiden's identity further complicates the settlers' understanding of the local hierarchy. Emerging from the hut adorned in elaborate feather robes, she commands the attention of the prostrate villagers with an air of regal authority. Her gestures—pointing to the resources of the land, touching Amyas's hand to her brow—are universal symbols of sovereignty and submission. The settlers, initially scandalized by the uncouth dances and the offering of intoxicating drinks, find themselves drawn into a feast that challenges their moral certainties. The presence of the maiden, whom Amyas speculates is a descendant of the Inca Manco's wife, introduces a historical continuity that links the present indigenous population to a glorious, albeit destroyed, imperial past. This connection transforms the locals from mere "savages" into survivors of a fallen empire, victims of Spanish tyranny rather than agents of inherent depravity.

As the evening progresses, the boundary between the internal world of the English camp and the external jungle dissolves. The settlers gather for their customary religious observances, singing Psalms and catches under the moonlight. The eerie echo of their own melodies returning from the dense forest, carried by the same clear, ethereal voice, confirms the supernatural status of the maiden. This auditory phenomenon reinforces the settlers' belief in a hidden, mystical order governing the wilderness, one that mirrors their own spiritual practices yet remains distinct and untamed. The repetition of the song creates a feedback loop of recognition, suggesting that the "Daughter of the Sun" is not merely a local leader but a prophetic figure connected to the cosmic order.

Over the following days, the settlers' attempts to engage directly with the village reveal the limitations of their linguistic and cultural tools. The cacique's evasive demeanor and refusal to discuss the maiden underscore her sacred status, marking her as a taboo subject within the community. The settlers' attempt to leverage their position by threatening the destruction of cassava crops fails to elicit the desired information, highlighting the asymmetry of power and knowledge. Ultimately, the cacique retreats to a canoe, delivering a rhythmic account of the tribe's migration and the discovery of the maiden. This narrative arc traces the trajectory of the indigenous people from a mighty nation displaced by Spanish conquest to a marginalized group seeking refuge in the mountains. The maiden, found as a child and raised with royal honors, represents a synthesis of the divine and the human, a living testament to the resilience of the Inca legacy. Her abilities as a counselor, warrior, and prophetess elevate her beyond the status of a mere woman, positioning her as a guardian of ancestral wisdom and a beacon of hope for a people awaiting restoration. Through this intricate interplay of myth, history, and personal encounter, the settlers are forced to confront the complexity of the New World, challenging their initial assumptions and opening a pathway to a more nuanced understanding of the indigenous experience.
