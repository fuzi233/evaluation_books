INTRODUCTION

Before any man in this tale ever set eyes on a Spanish treasure-train, before oars struck time on a moonlit swell, before the Queen’s command or the Armada’s thunder rolled across the Western seas, there was—like the first smell of bread before it shows brown—a promise, quiet and domestic, sitting warm inside a list of names.

A book, like a great household, has its rooms. One opens first upon a long passage, and along it hang the labels of chambers yet unvisited: doors with salt-stained thresholds, doors that open on old courts and wind-worried combes, on Christmas boards laid with holly, on bridges that somehow contrive to dine like gentlemen, and on ships that go and come again as steadily as a tide and as strangely as a dream. Each title is a hook hung in the larder-beam: not meat itself, but the place where meat will be kept; not the song, but the throat that will sing it.

Listen, if you will, to how those doors sound even before they swing. There is the soft brush of a white bird’s wing—so slight a stirring that a man must learn to hear it beneath the common noises of a port town. There is the thicker, homelier sound of a son coming up the path the first time: boots on gravel, a latch lifted, a breath caught back at the smell of a familiar hearth. There is hounds’ music in Wales—horn, cry, and the many-voiced running of dogs—yet tangled with another running, more secret and more ancient, where men hunt with the hounds and yet run with the deer. Love, too, will cross men in two fashions, as wind crosses a chimney: sometimes whistling, sometimes choking it with smoke; and it will do so not in airy talk, but over tables and thresholds, with eyes that have watched the same pot boil for years and suddenly cannot bear the sight of it.

There will be Clovelly Court, not as a picture hung up for strangers’ coins, but as it stood when old words were still alive in the mouths that spoke them—words for field and cliff and fish, words whose very shape carried the taste of the place. The far-west combes will open their green mouths, and within them the sea will be heard always, even when it is not seen: a steady undertone like a great kettle beginning to sing, and above it the clear, small bells of birds and brook-water—an acoustic world that keeps the mind sane as surely as good food keeps the body. And against that, later, will come harsher sounds: iron, shouting, the manufactured din of crowds and commerce; for there is a kind of noise that is not music, only wear, and it will press in as surely as any enemy.

Mr. John Oxenham of Plymouth will have his true and tragical history, not as a printed moral, but as something that happened to a man with a name and a trade and a certain timbre in his voice when he swore. A noble brotherhood of the Rose will be founded—chivalry, yes, but also the close, workmanlike fellowship men make when they must share danger as they share bread. Amyas will keep a Christmas Day, and the very remembering of it should bring you the crackle of green wood, the scent of spice and ale, the knife’s steady rhythm through meat, and the blessed hush that falls when a room full of folk bows their heads at once. A Mayor of Bideford will bait his hook with his own flesh—so the saying will become more than a saying—and Eustace Leigh will meet the Pope’s Legate, where power speaks in careful phrases that do not fit the tongues of all who must obey them.

You shall see Bideford Bridge dine at Annery House—stone and mortar turned by story into a guest—and the Golden Hind come home again, bringing with her not only gold and gunpowder, but the sea’s smell ground into rope and tar, and the strange silence that sometimes falls on a quay when men count the missing before they count the gain. Salvation Yeo will slay the King of the Gubbings; Mr. John Brimblecombe will discover the nature of an oath; and there will be a most chivalrous adventure of the good ship Rose, where courage is not a painted word but the feel of wet deck underfoot and the salt burning in cracked lips.

They will come to Barbados and find no men therein, and the emptiness will have its own sound—a wind through palm-leaves without any answering human stir, like a kitchen after the feast when the last guest has gone and the embers tick down. They will take the pearls at Margarita, and what befalls at La Guayra will remind you that the same hands that pray can also bind; that mercy and cruelty often share the same language in different mouths. Spanish bloodhounds and English mastiffs will tear at each other; the Communion will be taken under the tree at Higuerote, where the leaves overhead will make a soft, living roof and the words of faith will sit strangely among mosquitoes and musket-smoke. The Inquisition in the Indies will show how dominion works not only by sword but by vocabulary—by who may name sin, who may name truth, and how quickly a man’s own speech can be made into evidence against him.

The banks of the Meta will appear, and Amyas will be tempted of the Devil—not as a mere allegory, but as temptation comes in life: with a voice that fits itself to your desire, speaking smoothly, as if it were only offering you what you have always deserved. They will take the gold-train and the great galleon; Salvation Yeo will find his little maid again; Amyas will come home the third time, and homecoming will not be a single moment but a circle, like the season’s turning—each return tasting of the last and yet different, because those who eat at the same board are not the same people year after year.

The Virginia fleet will be stopped by the Queen’s command; Admiral John Hawkins will testify against Croakers; the Great Armada will swell like a storm-line across the horizon, and the sea’s roar will become a history-book written in thunder. Amyas will throw his sword into the sea, and in that gesture you will hear the clean, final splash—metal leaving hand, pride leaving heart—and then, at the very end, the apple will fall westward.

So much, then, for the passage and its doors.

If you would go on, remember that every great voyage begins in a still room: in the scrape of a chair on flagstones, in the comforting bubble of broth, in the low talk that carries a dialect like a family heirloom—each local word a spice that cannot be replaced without changing the whole dish. And remember, too, how easily such things are drowned: not only by cannon and the sea, but by louder, cheaper noises—by the clatter of profit and the thin jingle of art sold as ornament. Yet as long as one person can still hear a white bird’s wing in the common air, and as long as a table can still be laid with care, the old sounds and old meanings are not entirely lost.

The doors stand ready.
