The ink of Drusilla Clack's pen hovered over the page, trembling with a mixture of sanctimonious indignation and theatrical despair. She addressed Mr. Franklin Blake with the formal deference of a servant addressing a lord, yet her tone crackled with the static of a storm about to break. "Miss Clack presents her compliments to Mr. Franklin Blake," she declared, her words rising like incense in a stifling chapel. "In sending him the fifth chapter of her humble narrative, she begs to say that she feels quite unequal to enlarge as she could wish on an event so awful, under the circumstances, as Lady Verinder's death." She paused, letting the gravity of the tragedy hang in the air, before pivoting to her solution. "She has, therefore, attached to her own manuscripts, copious Extracts from precious publications in her possession, all bearing on this terrible subject. And may those Extracts (Miss Clack fervently hopes) sound as the blast of a trumpet in the ears of her respected kinsman, Mr. Franklin Blake."

Across the table—or rather, across the chasm of their conflicting methodologies—Franklin Blake sat with the quiet, unyielding composure of a man who sees through the fog of others' delusions. His reply was a shield raised against her intrusion. "Mr. Franklin Blake presents his compliments to Miss Clack, and begs to thank her for the fifth chapter of her narrative." He moved swiftly to dismiss the attachments she had deemed essential. "In returning the extracts sent with it, he will refrain from mentioning any personal objection which he may entertain to this species of literature, and will merely say that the proposed additions to the manuscript are not necessary to the fulfilment of the purpose that he has in view." For Blake, the truth was not found in the dusty archives of other authors, but in the raw, unvarnished testimony of the living.

Miss Clack, however, was not easily dethroned. She acknowledged the return of her materials with a sigh that suggested martyrdom rather than defeat. "Miss Clack begs to acknowledge the return of her Extracts," she wrote, her fingers dancing across the paper. "She affectionately reminds Mr. Franklin Blake that she is a Christian, and that it is, therefore, quite impossible for him to offend her." This invocation of divine law served as her armor. "Miss C. persists in feeling the deepest interest in Mr. Blake, and pledges herself, on the first occasion when sickness may lay him low, to offer him the use of her Extracts for the second time." Yet, her primary grievance remained the mystery itself. "In the meanwhile she would be glad to know, before beginning the final chapters of her narrative, whether she may be permitted to make her humble contribution complete, by availing herself of the light which later discoveries have thrown on the mystery of the Moonstone."

Blake's response was a wall of ice, impenetrable and absolute. "Mr. Franklin Blake is sorry to disappoint Miss Clack," he stated, leaving no room for negotiation. "He can only repeat the instructions which he had the honour of giving her when she began her narrative. She is requested to limit herself to her own individual experience of persons and events, as recorded in her diary." He drew a hard line in the sand. "Later discoveries she will be good enough to leave to the pens of those persons who can write in the capacity of actual witnesses." To him, speculation dressed in borrowed authority was nothing more than noise obscuring the signal.

Undeterred, Miss Clack drafted another missive, her frustration boiling over into a performance of wounded dignity. "Miss Clack is extremely sorry to trouble Mr. Franklin Blake with another letter," she confessed, feigning humility while asserting her resolve. "Her Extracts have been returned, and the expression of her matured views on the subject of the Moonstone has been forbidden." She felt the sting of rejection keenly, the sensation of being pushed aside by a superior intellect. "But, no—Miss C. has learnt Perseverance in the School of Adversity." Her goal shifted slightly; she sought validation for her very presence in the story. "Her object in writing is to know whether Mr. Blake (who prohibits everything else) prohibits the appearance of the present correspondence in Miss Clack's narrative? Some explanation of the position in which Mr. Blake's interference has placed her as an authoress, seems due on the ground of common justice. And Miss Clack, on her side, is most anxious that her letters should be produced to speak for themselves."

Finally, Blake capitulated, but only to end the dance entirely. "Mr. Franklin Blake agrees to Miss Clack's proposal," he conceded, his tone devoid of warmth. "On the understanding that she will kindly consider this intimation of his consent as closing the correspondence between them." There would be no further debate, no more weaving of external theories into the tapestry of the tale.

Yet, even as the door closed, Miss Clack felt compelled to deliver one final blow, a parting shot wrapped in the guise of Christian charity. "Miss Clack feels it an act of Christian duty (before the correspondence closes) to inform Mr. Franklin Blake that his last letter—evidently intended to offend her—has not succeeded in accomplishing the object of the writer." She turned the tables, framing his refusal as an insult to her spirit. "She affectionately requests Mr. Blake to retire to the privacy of his own room, and to consider with himself whether the training which can thus elevate a poor weak woman above the reach of insult, be not worthy of greater admiration than he is now disposed to feel for it." With a flourish of self-righteousness, she concluded: "On being favoured with an intimation to that effect, Miss C. solemnly pledges herself to send back the complete series of her Extracts to Mr. Franklin Blake."

And then, silence. To this letter, no answer was received. Comment is needless. The exchange ended not with a resolution, but with the stark reality of two minds colliding—one demanding the weight of history, the other insisting on the purity of witness—and the ghost of the Moonstone lingering between them, waiting for the next turn of the plot.

(Signed) DRUSILLA CLACK.
