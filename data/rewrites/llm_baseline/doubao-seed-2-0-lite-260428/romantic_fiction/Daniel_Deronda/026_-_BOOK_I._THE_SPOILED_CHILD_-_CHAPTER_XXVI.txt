He brings white asses laden with the freight
Of Tyrian vessels, purple, gold and balm,
To bribe my will: I’ll bid them chase him forth,
Nor let him breathe the taint of his surmise
On my secure resolve. Ay, ’tis secure:
And therefore let him come to spread his freight.
For firmness hath its appetite and craves
The stronger lure, more strongly to resist;
Would know the touch of gold to fling it off;
Scent wine to feel its lip the soberer;
Behold soft byssus, ivory, and plumes
To say, “They’re fair, but I will none of them,”
And flout Enticement in the very face.

The day Mr. Gascoigne rode up to Offendene’s overgrown iron gates, he carried what he believed was the kindest possible news for his niece: Mrs. Mompert had fixed the following Tuesday as the date for Gwendolen to travel to Wanchester and take up her new post as governess to her three unruly, wilful daughters. He said nothing of the stray bit of local gossip he’d overheard, that Mr. Grandcourt had returned to his Diplow estate—he knew no more than Gwendolen herself that the wealthy, mysterious bachelor had trailed her all the way to Leubronn on the Continent, and he saw no mercy in stirring up the ghost of the glittering, once-within-reach prospect of marrying him now that their family fortunes had crashed so thoroughly. Privately the rector regretted his niece’s inexplicable rejection of Grandcourt, but he excused her: the man himself had acted so strangely, vanishing mid-courtship just when he was on the cusp of making his intentions perfectly clear, that no one could blame her for taking offence. His only duty now, he was certain, was to urge Gwendolen to face the hard new path laid out before her, for there was no sign any miracle would come to save her from it.

“You will find a new richness in varied experience, my dear,” he told her gently, “and I have no doubt you will grow into a far more capable, admirable woman for the part you are being called to play now.”

“I can’t pretend I will enjoy it,” Gwendolen snapped, sharp with a rare petulance she had never directed at her uncle before. “But I know full well I have no choice but to bear it.” She remembered, not long ago, when he had urged her to look forward to that very different, very glamorous prospect that had been Grandcourt’s courtship, and the bitter contrast stung.

“Your good sense will guide you to behave as you ought,” Mr. Gascoigne said, a little more sternly. “I am sure Mrs. Mompert will take a great liking to you. You will know exactly how to comport yourself around a woman who is, in every sense, your superior. This hardship has fallen on you young, which makes it lighter in many ways, and there is profit in all chastisement if we only bend our minds to accept it.”

That was the very last thing Gwendolen could bring herself to do. The moment her uncle left, the slow, hot tears she had refused to shed through weeks of worsening misfortune began to roll down her cheeks as she sat alone in the empty drawing room. Her heart railed against the idea that her suffering was easier just because she was young. When was she supposed to have any joy at all, if not in the bright bloom of her youth? She no longer harbored the old childish fantasies that she could pluck every prize of life without ever tasting a bitter core, but that new disenchantment—with the world, and even with herself, now that she knew she was not nearly extraordinary enough to command fate to bow to her will—only made her lonelier. Ahead of her stretched a barren, endless stretch of dull duty, and she had no strength to set foot on the path. She was in that first, fiery young rebellion against the absence of joy, that blinding, furious grief of disappointment that hits you the very first morning you wake up and realize life will not hand you the happy ending you always assumed you were owed. Who among us, softened by years of accumulated hardship, does not forget how sharp that sting was, when you first reeled at the unfairness that it was you, and not some stranger, who had been dealt the blow? Even those who later went on to brave pain in silence for the people they loved, to build goodness out of their own wounds, started there—raging at the tiny, burning wasp sting of a denied wish, as if the whole universe had conspired to hurt them alone.

That was exactly Gwendolen’s state now. Who cared if hundreds of other girls had fallen from the same height of comfort and privilege to become governesses? All that mattered to her was that it was *her* fate. It was the weight of Klesmer’s brutal truth that she was not talented enough to outrun circumstance, the dread of Mrs. Mompert’s constant exacting eye, the drudgery of forcing cheer for three sullen Miss Momperts day in and day out, the small humiliation of having to swallow every part of her proud, sparkling self to please strangers. To be dethroned from a crown you never asked to wear is nothing compared to falling from the illusion that you are special, that you can do no wrong, that all eyes will always turn to admire you. There she was, the spoiled child with the lovely full lips, the bright bold eyes, the tall, regal bearing that had once turned every head at every party, and all her old magic was gone.

She got up from the low ottoman where she had been sitting motionless, pacing the drawing room, one elbow propped in her palm, her cheek resting heavy on her hand, another slow tear dripping down. “Ever since I was little I knew mamma was never happy,” she thought, “and now I’ll be even unhappier than she ever was.” She pictured herself fading away, her youth slipping by, no longer caring what she did from one day to the next—but the vision quickly filled with the face of her mother. “Poor mamma. This will be worse for her, too. At least I’ll be able to send her a little money. That’s the only thing that matters now.” For the first time, she imagined her mother growing old, her hair turning white, while she too faded, no longer young, no longer bright, the two of them holding onto each other through all their loss, and she broke into soft, heaving sobs, not from anger, but from a tender, aching misery.

She looked up and saw her mother walk through the door, her eyes so heavy with tears she barely registered the movement. She fumbled for her handkerchief, pressing it hard to her eyes, but before she could dry her face she felt her mother’s arms wrap around her, and she sobbed all the harder, no longer able to hide her pain. Mrs. Davilow was pale and trembling, clutching a letter in her hand that had already set her heart racing, and she held her daughter tight until Gwendolen’s sobs softened.

“It’s nothing, mamma,” Gwendolen said, breathing deep as she pulled back, dabbing at her wet cheeks. “It’s all over now.”

But she saw the letter in her mother’s hand, and a sharp, bitter jab of fear hit her. “What is that? More bad news?”

“I don’t know what you’ll think of it, my dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, holding the note out carefully. “You’ll never guess who it’s from.”

“Please don’t make me guess anything,” Gwendolen snapped, as if someone was pressing on an old bruise.

“It’s addressed to you. It came from Diplow.”

Gwendolen recognized Grandcourt’s faint, slanted handwriting instantly, and her face flushed bright red—but by the time she finished reading the short, formal note, all the color had drained away, even her lips pale. She held it out to her mother, her hands shaking a little:
*Mr. Grandcourt presents his compliments to Miss Harleth, and begs to know whether he may be permitted to call at Offendene to-morrow after two and to see her alone. Mr. Grandcourt has just returned from Leubronn, where he had hoped to find Miss Harleth.*

Mrs. Davilow read it, then looked at her daughter, bewildered. Gwendolen let the note slip from her fingers to the floor, turning away.

“You have to answer it, darling,” Mrs. Davilow said softly. “The messenger is waiting downstairs.”

Gwendolen sank onto the settee, clasping her hands tight in her lap, staring straight ahead at nothing. A few minutes before, she had been trapped on an inescapable path of dull, hateful drudgery, no choice at all—and now, suddenly, a choice had fallen right into her lap. She felt a spark of triumph, hot and bright: even at her lowest, stripped of all her fortune, the man everyone in the county envied was still chasing her, had followed her all the way to Leubronn to find her. She was not invisible, after all. But the triumph curdled into terror fast. All the fragments of that old memory rushed back at her: the pretty dark-haired woman, the little boy clinging to her skirts, the unspoken promise she had made to herself that she would never marry Grandcourt, the bitter disillusionment that had settled over her after that awful confrontation. What did she want, now? Nothing had changed. But a new, quiet thought curled in the back of her mind: I wish I never got this letter. She would give anything to not have to make this choice.

It felt like an eternity to Mrs. Davilow before she spoke again, gentle as ever. “You need to write back, dear. Or would you like me to draft a reply for you, and you can tell me what to say?”

“No, mamma,” Gwendolen said, taking a long, steadying breath. “Just lay out the pen and paper for me.”

That bought her time. She could turn him away, shut the door, go back to the fate she had already accepted, no risk, no mess. But the young, wild, restless part of her that had always craved excitement, craved to feel powerful again, was thrumming under her skin. The day ahead no longer stretched before her empty and dead.

“There’s no reason to rush, you know,” Gwendolen said, a little sharp, as her mother laid the writing supplies on the table in front of her. “Servants are paid to wait. There’s no need for me to dash off a note instantly.”

“Of course not, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, picking up a bit of needlework from the side table. “He can wait another quarter hour, no trouble at all.”

The quiet, gentle acceptance of it made Gwendolen *anxious* to hurry, to avoid sitting and picking apart every last pro and con of this choice until she drove herself mad. She stood up, smoothing back the dark curls that had fallen loose around her face. “I don’t mean he has to wait long enough for you to finish that horrible needlework,” she said, walking over to the writing desk and sinking into the chair. Her mind was racing, half of her thinking of how quickly she could send the note off, half of her wondering what harm there could be in letting him come. He wasn’t forcing her to do anything. He had chased her all the way to Leubronn, he was clearly going to propose—why shouldn’t she let him show up, just so she could reject him, and prove to herself she still had that power?

“Mr. Grandcourt only just got back from Leubronn,” Mrs. Davilow said softly. “I wonder if he’s heard what happened to our fortune.”

“A man with his money wouldn’t care about something like that,” Gwendolen said, a little scornful.

“Most men would,” Mrs. Davilow persisted. “They wouldn’t want to marry a girl whose family is nearly beggared, you know. Imagine if he found us living crammed in that tiny cottage at Sawyer’s Lane. Most men hate the thought of being bored or burdened by their wife’s relations. If he *does* know, and he’s still writing to you? That’s very strong proof of how much he cares for you.”

The words hit Gwendolen like a jolt. A whole new world of possibilities opened up before her: Grandcourt was rich, he could make sure her mother never had to want for anything ever again, never had to scrape by on charity, never had to live in a tiny cramped cottage. The thought made her fingers move fast, before she could stop and think any more, and she scribbled the note in a hurry:

“Ring the bell, please mamma,” she said, leaning back in her chair, breathless, after she sealed it. She had barely even thought about what she was agreeing to. It was only after the footman had taken the letter away that she stretched, pressing a hand to her forehead, a low half-moan that was half relief, half tension, escaping her.

“What did you write, Gwen?” Mrs. Davilow asked, softly.

“I wrote that I would be at home tomorrow,” Gwendolen said, lifting her chin, proud. “But don’t go getting any ideas that something is going to happen, mamma. Just because he’s coming to call doesn’t mean I’m going to say yes.”

“I’m not expecting anything, dear. I only want you to follow your own heart. You never did tell me exactly how you felt about him.”

“What’s the point of talking about it?” Gwendolen said, stung. “If I had something nice to tell you, you’d be the first to hear it.”

“But Mr. Grandcourt will take that note as an acceptance of his suit, won’t he?” Mrs. Davilow said. “His note says plain as day he’s coming to propose.”

“Good. Then I get to enjoy the pleasure of turning him down.” Gwendolen stood up, shoving the pen and paper away from her, the air in the drawing room suddenly feeling thick and stifling. “Put down that awful needlework, will you? Let’s walk in the avenue. I can’t breathe in here.”
