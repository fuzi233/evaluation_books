One could almost envy Daniel Deronda as he prepared new garments for Mordecai, delighting in the vision of how the simple elegance of gray flannel and a robe resembling a monk’s humble frock would frame the man’s solemn features. Much of his satisfaction came from imagining Mirah’s joy—seeing her brother through her eyes, ensuring that nothing marred the purity of her happiness. Yet even as he arranged each detail, doubt crept in. Was he underestimating her? Would she not, like him, find a deeper beauty in the marks of hardship, the very traces of a life endured?

But there were the Meyricks to consider—their goodwill toward this brother so foreign to their world. Deronda caught himself smoothing away every sign of poverty, as if to soften the edges of their potential discomfort. He knew too well how unseeing eyes might recoil from what they did not understand.

That evening, by the warmth of Mrs. Meyrick’s hearth, the news was shared. Hans, lounging with his usual irreverence, reacted with a groan.

"Confound it!" he exclaimed, as if the revelation were a personal affront.

Mab scolded him, Kate sighed, and Amy rolled her eyes. Only Mrs. Meyrick remained steadfast, her voice gentle but firm as she recounted Deronda’s admiration for Mordecai, his belief that Mirah would understand her brother perfectly.

Hans, restless and scowling, paced the room. "Deronda’s obsession with these Jews is absurd," he muttered. "Next thing, Mirah will forget she’s an artist—bury herself in prayers and prophecies. Everything ruined."

The girls protested, but Hans was already seizing his hat, his farewells brusque. The door slammed behind him, leaving silence in his wake.

——

The next morning, Deronda sent word—let Mrs. Meyrick be the one to tell Mirah. He could not bring himself to stand before her again as the bearer of grand tidings, the orchestrator of her joy. Better to let the moment unfold naturally, without the weight of his presence.

When the time came, Mirah descended the stairs in the same worn cloak and hat she had worn when Deronda first found her.

"You wish to go like this?" Mrs. Meyrick asked softly.

"My brother is poor," Mirah replied. "I want to look as much like him as I can."

Deronda said nothing, but his heart stung with sudden shame at his own careful arrangements.

Then—the meeting.

Mordecai rose as they entered, his gaze fixed on the door. Mirah stopped just inside, trembling. For a long moment, neither moved. It was not just the sight of one another that held them, but the echo of a shared past, a love preserved across years of separation.

"Ezra," Mirah whispered, her voice exactly as it had been when she spoke of their mother’s call.

Mordecai stepped forward, hands resting on her shoulders. "That was our mother’s voice," he murmured. "Do you remember?"

"Yes. And how you answered her—*Mother!*—and I knew you loved her."

Then she was in his arms, her hat tumbling away as she clung to him, her kisses as free and tender as a child’s. Mordecai touched her curls, his voice thick with emotion. "Ah, the dear head, the dear head..."

"You are ill," she said softly, studying his face.

"Yes, little one. I will not be long with you in this world."

"But we have now," she said fiercely. "I will tell you everything. You will teach me—to be as our mother wished. And I will work, and we shall be together always."

Only then did she remember the others, turning with a grace that seemed almost unconscious, one hand still on Mordecai’s arm. She reached for Mrs. Meyrick’s hand, pressing it into her brother’s. "This lady," she said, "took me in when I was lost. She has been my mother when I had none."

Mordecai bowed his head. "The Eternal has been kind."

Deronda, sensing the sacredness of the moment, gently guided Mrs. Meyrick away. Some reunions were not meant for witnesses.
