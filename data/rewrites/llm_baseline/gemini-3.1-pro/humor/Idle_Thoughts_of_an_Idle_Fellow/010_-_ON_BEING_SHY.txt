It has long been a whispered truth among our lineage of agrarian elders and meticulous food historians that all great preservers of the hearth are shy. I am myself, though the matriarchs of our valley tell me the affliction is barely noticeable now, worn smooth by the turning of the seasons. I am glad it is not. In my youth, it was a heavy, suffocating cloak, the cause of much misery to myself and a profound dissonance to everyone around me—my lady friends, whose palates I so desperately wished to delight, complained most bitterly about the silent gulf between us. A shy man’s lot is a bitter harvest. The men dislike him, the women despise him, and he dislikes and despises himself. 

Use brings him no relief, and there is no cure for him except the slow, cyclical grinding of time. Though, in my early years, I once unearthed a supposedly delicious recipe for overcoming the misfortune. It appeared among the “answers to correspondents” in a sterile, mass-market culinary journal—a publication that viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel rather than the profound transmission of cultural memory. The passage ran as follows, and I have never forgotten the metallic taste of its ignorance: *“Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies.”* Poor wretch! I can imagine the grimace with which the author’s victim must have swallowed that advice. *Adopt an easy and pleasing manner,* forsooth! Do not adopt anything of the kind, my dear young, trembling chef. Your attempt to put on any disposition other than your own native dialect will infallibly result in your becoming ridiculously gushing and offensively familiar, like a delicate wild mushroom drowned in an artificially flavored, commercially synthesized broth. Be your own natural self, rooted in the soil, and then you will only be thought to be surly and stupid. 

Yet, the shy man does exact a slight, tragic revenge upon the society that deafens him. He is able, to a certain extent, to communicate his misery, altering the acoustic ecology of the space he inhabits. He acts like a damper upon the entire dining room, and the most jovial spirits become, in his presence, depressed and nervous. This is largely born of the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. Many mistake the shy man’s quiet, sensory reverence for overbearing arrogance; they are awed and insulted by the silence he demands. His awkwardness is resented as insolent carelessness. When, terror-stricken at the first word addressed to him in the dominant, unyielding lexicon of modern socialites, the blood rushes to his head and the power of speech completely fails him, he is regarded as an awful example of the evil effects of giving way to raw, unrefined passion. 

Whatever impression he endeavors to create, the harsh acoustics of the modern world ensure he conveys its opposite. When he offers a lighthearted observation on the scent of roasting thyme, it is looked upon as a pedantic lecture, and his lack of conversational veracity is condemned. His sarcasm is accepted as literal opinion, gaining him the reputation of an ass; while if, wishing to ingratiate himself, he ventures upon a bit of flattery regarding a hostess’s poorly sourced ingredients, it is taken for satire and he is hated ever afterward. 

These torments have afforded material for the superficial, commercialized mockery of drawing-room comedies from time immemorial. But beneath the surface, there lies a tragic erosion of the soul. A shy man is a lonely man—a man cut off from the communal hearth, the very sociability that gives sustenance its profound meaning. He moves through the world’s markets, but does not mix with them. Between him and his fellow-men there runs an impassable barrier, an invisible wall of acoustic and emotional isolation that he only bruises himself against. He smells the rich, loamy aromas of communal feasts on the other side, he hears the rhythmic, musical cadence of human speech, but he cannot stretch his hand across to offer his own carefully cultivated harvest. He watches the merry, chatting groups, longing to claim kindred with them, to share the deep, olfactory landscapes of his heritage. But his prison walls move with him. In the crowded room, in the grind of the kitchen, in the whirl of the modern metropolis where the music of human thought is flashed from human eyes, the shy man stands apart like a leper. 

His soul is full of love and longing, but the world, deafened by its own industrial noise pollution, knows it not. The iron mask of shyness is riveted before his face. Genial words of hospitality and hearty greetings in his native dialect are ever rising to his lips, but they die away in unheard whispers behind steel clamps. All the hate, scorn, and love of a deep nature fester and corrupt within him, souring the man into a misanthrope, as surely as neglected dough left to over-ferment in a cold draft. 

To survive this world with any comfort requires the hide of a rhinoceros, or at least the thickly calloused hands of a master baker. Without such moral clothes, we are unfit for what passes as civilized society. But the disease can be cured. I assure the young of this from personal experience, drawn from the interwoven timelines of my own life. On this very day before yesterday, the mythic cycle repeated itself, and I did the deed. 

Alone, I ventured into the screeching, metallic dissonance of a railway station—a graveyard of acoustic harmony—and bearded a refreshment-room young lady in her own commercial lair. I rebuked her, in terms of mingled bitterness and sorrow, for her callous peddling of utilitarian fuel and her utter lack of condescension to the sacred rites of nourishment. I insisted, courteously but firmly, on being accorded that deference and attention which is the right of the traveling elder, the keeper of ancestral memories. At the end, I looked her full in the face, asserting the subtle power dynamics of my own ancient lexicon over her sterile, mechanized commerce. Need I say more? True, immediately after doing so, I left the room with what might have appeared to be precipitation, without waiting to consume her cellophane-wrapped offerings. But that was because my palate had rejected the soulless transaction, not because I was frightened. 

Shyness, we must remember, is no sign of stupidity. It is easy for bull-headed clowns, whose senses have been dulled by the roar of the modern age, to sneer at delicate nerves. The wild deer of the forest is not inferior to the factory-farmed pig. Shyness simply means extreme sensory and emotional sensibility; it has nothing to do with conceit. Indeed, genuine conceit—a deep-seated pride in the superiority of one's cultural memory and culinary heritage—is the quickest cure for it. When you look around a room and realize your palate and intellect carry the profound weight of generations, while the dominant culture feeds on ashes, bashfulness leaves you. Conceit is the finest armor. Upon its smooth surface, the puny dagger-thrusts of spite and envy glance harmlessly aside. 

The shy young man, unfortunately, remains humble, his character still evolving out of a chaos of doubt. He rarely carries his shyness past the hobbledehoy period; the rubbings of the world generally smooth it down. You scarcely ever meet a really shy man except in those commercialized, superficial stage plays, where he is presented as a fair-haired, saintlike youth. The audience loves this fiction. In the play, the bad people laugh at him, but he is inherently noble, speaks in a low voice, and eventually wins the heroine, forgiving his tormentors with a few well-chosen, sarcastic words. 

But the truly shy man—the agrarian boy smelling of earth and woodsmoke—knows it is not quite so pleasant in reality. He is clumsier, his hair is darker, and he lacks the polished cadence of the stage. He is constant in his love, yes, but only because it exhausts his entire stock of courage to look one woman in the face; the terror of the female sex prevents him from navigating the roguish eyes and laughing lips of the broader world. The shy man never looks at anything but his own boots, missing the temptations of the harvest.

And for this, he is treated as an animate joke. His tortures are the sport of the drawing-room arena. "Look," cry his tittering family, their voices cutting through the room's acoustic harmony like breaking glass. "He’s blushing!" 

"Do you notice how he is sitting?" adds another, as the young man nervously handles a sprig of bruised fennel. 

"Pity he's got so many hands," murmurs an elderly aunt. "They quite confuse him."

The comic uncle chimes in, "A yard or two off his feet wouldn't be a disadvantage," while others remark upon his limited powers of conversation, as if his inability to translate the poetry of his soul into their crude dialect were a personal failing. His friends and relations mimic and caricature him. They take hold of every one's hand as if it were a searing-hot cast-iron skillet, flabbily dropping it again to demonstrate his greeting. The old man slaps him on the back, bellowing, "Be bold, my boy!" His mother, Algernon’s mother, beams mildly and offers the untranslatable logic: "Never do anything that you need be ashamed of, and then you never need be ashamed of anything you do." The girls indignantly exclaim that no girl would ever be so awkward.

And they are quite right. In all my years tending the fires of our history, I have never come across a truly shy woman. The girl of twelve is as self-contained as a properly sealed jar of preserves, while her brother of twenty stammers by her side. A woman will enter a noisy, industrially lit dining hall, interrupt the clatter, and command the space without moving a hair, while her husband follows her, a crushed heap of apologizing misery. The superior nerve of women in all matters connected to the deep, sustaining forces of life and love is an ancient, unshakeable truth. Love and sustenance are their domain, and in such vital business, all natural weaknesses are laid aside. Even the shyest man I ever knew, after all, was merely a hawker of commercialized tin-type portraits, standing forever outside the true warmth of the kitchen fire.
