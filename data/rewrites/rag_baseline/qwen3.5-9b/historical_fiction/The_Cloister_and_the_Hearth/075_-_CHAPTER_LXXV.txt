Clement exhaled, a long, weary sound that seemed to carry the weight of every unspoken warning history had ever issued. A flicker of doubt crossed his mind—a rare intrusion for a man of such rigid conviction—regarding the wisdom of engaging with a spirit so violently passionate. Yet, even in his youth, he had absorbed the profound, silent lessons of the cloister. He knew the ancient rule: when faced with the turbulent eddies of human difficulty, one must cease all action and speech, allowing the waters to settle until clarity emerges. Thus, he chose silence, offering only a prayer for divine illumination. His sole physical act was a gentle withdrawal of his foot, a retreat meant to signal safety.

But the penitent woman reacted with a ferocity that defied reason. She flung her arms around his ankle, her body convulsing in a desperate embrace, and erupted in a cry of raw anguish, weeping openly over the limb she sought to hold. In that moment, the crushing agony of shame mingled with her contrition, flushing her pale skin with a vivid crimson that climbed high to her throat. She lay trembling at his feet, a living testament to her brokenness.

"My daughter," Clement spoke softly, his voice steady amidst her storm. "Take courage. Cease tormenting yourself over this Gerard, for he is nothing. As for me, I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven has dispatched to you today solely to comfort you and aid you in saving your soul. Since you have named me your confessor, I now claim your obedience."

"Oh, yes," she sobbed, her voice thick with emotion. "Leave this pilgrimage immediately and return to Rome. Penitence abroad holds little value. There, amidst the fires of our daily lives, lie the specific temptations we must conquer or perish; we must not flee to hide among strangers, masking our faces, when the true battle lies with those closest to us. It is easy to wash the feet of masked travelers, but hard to remain meek and charitable with the people who surround us."

"I swear, I will never, never touch her again."

"Nay," Clement corrected gently. "I speak not merely of servants, but of dependents, kin, and friends. This is your penance: at the last hour of the night and the first after Matins, recall your sin and God's boundless goodness; cultivate humility and gentleness toward the flaws of those near you. The world courts the rich, but you must seek the poor—not the beggar, for they are often dishonest or falsely destitute. Rather, find those who blush to ask for your help yet desperately need it. To give to them is to lend directly to Heaven. And regarding your future..."

"Me? I will never marry."

"You shall marry within the year," Clement declared firmly. "I entreat and command you to wed a man who fears God. For you are but clay; matched poorly, you will become nothing. But united with a worthy husband, by God's grace, you may live as a pious princess and die a saint."

"You."

He then urged her to rise and begin the good work he had assigned. She rose to her knees, removed her veil, and cast an eloquent, searching gaze upon him before lowering her eyes in meek submission. "I will obey you as I would an angel," she whispered. "How happy I am, yet how unhappy; for oh, my heart tells me I shall never look upon you again. I cannot leave until I have dried your feet."

"It is unnecessary," Clement replied. "I have excused you from this bootless penance."

"'Tis no penance to me," she insisted, her voice trembling. "Ah! If you will not let me dry your poor feet, then you do not forgive me."

"So be it," Clement said, resigning himself to her persistence. *Levius quid foemina,* he thought internally—the lighter burden women bear. Yet, these fragile creatures, who naturally gravitate toward the small just as celestial bodies turn to the massive, possess moments of startling angelic intelligence. Once the princess had finished drying the friar's feet, she gazed at him with tears shimmering in her beautiful eyes, murmuring with a singular tenderness, "May I have masses said for her soul?" she added timidly.

A faint blush colored the monk's cheek, and moisture gathered in his cold blue eyes. It was a reaction born of surprise, coming from a woman he had just rated so lowly. "It is a gracious thought," he admitted. "Do as thou wilt; often such acts return to the doer like blessed dew. Remember, I am your confessor, not hers; it is your soul I must strive to save, or woe betide my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good and evil angels battling for your soul this very day, right now. Fight on your own side. Dost thou remember everything I commanded?"

"Remember!" the princess affirmed. "Sweet saint, every syllable of yours is engraved in my heart."

"But one final word," Clement continued. "Pray much to Christ, and little to his saints."

"I will."

"And that is the finest counsel I can offer you. With that, we part. You go to the place that suits you best—your father's house—and I return to my holy mother's work."

"Adieu," the princess faltered.

"Adieu, thou whom I have loved too well, hated too ill, known and revered too late; forgiving angel, adieu—for ever."

The monk caught the words, though they broke forth only as a sigh. "For ever?" he cried aloud, his voice suddenly ardent. "Christians live 'forever,' and love 'forever,' but they never part 'forever.' They separate, as the earth and sun do, only to meet more brilliantly in a little while. You and I part here for this earthly life. But what is our life? Merely one line in the grand story of the Church, whose children we both are; one handful in the sands of time, one drop in the ocean of eternity. Adieu—for the brief moment called 'a life!' We part in trouble, yet we shall meet in peace; we part as creatures of clay, yet we shall meet as immortal spirits; we part in a world of sin and sorrow, yet we shall meet where all is purity and divine love, where no ill passions exist, but only Christ and His saints arrayed in white. There, in the turning of an hourglass, in the breaking of a bubble, in the passing of a cloud, she, thou, and I shall meet again; and sit at the feet of angels, archangels, apostles, and saints, beaming with joy unspeakable in the light of God's shadow upon His throne, FOREVER—AND EVER—AND EVER."

Thus they parted. The monk stood erect, his eyes turned heavenward, glowing with the sacred fire of zeal; the princess withdrew slowly, turning repeatedly to cast lingering glances of awe and tender regret upon that inspired figure. She departed home subdued and purified. Clement, in due course, arrived in Basel and took up his duties, teaching in the university and preaching throughout the town and its environs. He led a life that could be summarized in two words: deep study and mortification. By Basel, he had advanced significantly in holy zeal and knowledge. The brethren of his order began to perceive in him a true descendant of the saints and martyrs.
