Long ago, before the world had quite lost its appetite for the absurd, old Deleglise presided over Sunday suppers that became legendary. Dressed from head to toe in spotless linen, he would cook in his cavernous kitchen, his flushed, earnest face darting about a gleaming stove. Behind him, guests waited in hushed reverence around a massive oak table glittering with cut glass and silver, where Madeline, his ancient white-capped Bonne, fluttered her deft hands. These gatherings drew artists, musicians, actors, and writers—the "drolls" and scholars whose names, then and now, echo through the English-speaking world. Their epigrams may seem less brilliant today, their jokes less uproarious, simply because fashion in humor shifts as rapidly as the seasons. But for those of us who sat there, listening to whispers turn to roars whenever the old master turned radiant with culinary triumph, those moments were magic.

I, however, am not concerned with second-hand records of those charmed circles. My concern is with a quieter, domestic theater: the afternoon "At Homes" hosted by Dan and myself on the second floor of a Georgian house in Queen Square. Here, the stakes were lower, but the comedy was sharper. To ensure success on these important occasions, Dan and I instituted a system of mutual sabotage: I was to critique his cooking, and he, my housework.

"It needs more soda!" I would declare, sampling a cake with the solemnity of a judge.
"You silly Juggins! It *is* a soda cake!"
"I know that. It isn't meant to *taste* like soda. It needs flavor! This is nothing but solidified Seidlitz powder. You can't feed people powdered medicine for tea!"
Dan would fume, his chest heaving, yet I remained immovable. Eventually, the disaster would be discarded, and we would attempt again. Dan was obstinate about his cookery; he would never admit failure, devouring his most ghastly creations later with pretended enjoyment, claiming a dry sponge cake tasted like dust yet insisting it required a teaspoon for the center. Only afterwards, in a strange silence, would he drain a wine-glass of brandy.

Then it was Dan's turn.
"Call these knives clean?" he would demand, pointing an accusing finger.
"Yes, I do."
Dan would drag his finger across a blade, producing a streak of *chiaroscuro*.
"Not if you go fingering them! Why don't you leave them alone and focus?"
"You've just wiped them!"
"Well, there's no 'knife-powder.'"
"Yes, there is."
"Besides, over-cleaning ruins the edge. We need them sharp to cut your lemon buns."
"Over-cleaning! You don't take any pride in this place!"
"Good Lord! Don't I work from morning to night?"
"You lazy young devil!"
"Makes one lazy, your cooking. How can a man work when he suffers from indigestion all day?"

Eventually, Dan would be satisfied, and the board would be scrubbed. Once the maid and cook vanished, leaving two carefully dressed gentlemen to receive their guests, the real performance began. Our audience was a motley crew drawn from Dan's journalistic acquaintances and my theatrical friends. Occasionally, the formidable Mrs. Peedles would arrive, breathless on our landing.

Left to myself, I might have deemed them unsuitable company, but Dan, once introduced, overrode my objections with a flourish.
"My dear Lord Chamberlain," Dan would announce, "an ounce of originality is worth a ton of convention! Little tin ladies and gentlemen, all made to pattern! Your friends would be an acquisition to any society."
"But are they quite good form?" I would hint, terrified.
"We'll forget that Mrs. Peedles runs a lodging-house in Blackfriars," Dan countered cheerfully. "We will speak of her as 'that dear, quaint old creature, Lady P.' A title that suits her costume perfectly! My dear Paul, your society novelist would make a fortune out of her. The editors of gossip columns will pay me a shilling a line for tales featuring Earl Blank and the Baroness de Dash. Jarman—yes, Jarman shall be the son of a South American millionaire. Vulgar? Nonsense! You mean *raconteur*. Minikin—he looks more like forty than twenty—shall be an eminent scientist. His glass eye will be the result of a chemical experiment, a touch of distinction. We'll make him buy a yard of red ribbon and wear it across his shirt-front, addressing him as 'Herr Professor.' It explains his grammar and accent. They shall be our lions."

And so, to my terror, Dan persisted. Jarman entered the spirit of the joke with gusto. The more he swaggered, the more he boasted, the greater his success. While I watched my actress friends lure him into corners with languishing eyes, trying to trap him with their tricks, my conscience pricked. Yet, Jarman proved to be a gentleman of uncommon sense. He enjoyed the game but ultimately married the eldest daughter of a small blacking factory—a plain, pleasant girl who eventually brought the factory into his possession. Now, stout and rubicund, he appears to me as a man who has finally attained his ideals.

Minikin required more explanation. Guests possessing scientific smatterings had to be gently guided away from discussing shop. Those with unexpected knowledge of German were politely reminded that the *Herr Professor* was there solely to learn English and had taken a vow against his native tongue. His rudeness, however, was a constant challenge. Informing a lady he "didn't like them silly," or telling a friend he bought his ribbon for fourpence, forced us to concoct elaborate backstories: a love disappointment, a soured genius. Altogether, Minikin took a great deal of explaining.

Mrs. Peedles, meanwhile, was universally declared to be the widow of some obscure City knight. They mocked her behind her back but treated her with effusive politeness to her face. "My dear Lady Peedles" became a common refrain in our rooms. At the theatre, she became a figure of legend. My own social standing improved markedly due to her evident favor for me; it went abroad that I was her presumptive heir. I was courted as a gentleman of expectations.

Then there was the fishy-eyed young man, a regular guest whom Dan won over by never laughing at him, despite his obvious desire to be laughed at.
"I like talking to you," the young man confided one afternoon. "You don't go into fits of laughter when I remark that it has been a fine day. Most people do. Of course, on the stage I don't mind... I get my living by being a funny little devil. There's a photograph of me hanging in the lobby. I saw a workman stop and look at it the other day... He burst into a roar. 'Little--! He makes me laugh to look at him!' ... Well, that's all right; I want the man in the gallery to think me funny, but it annoys me when people laugh at me off the stage. I want my dinner. It makes me very angry sometimes."
Dan sympathized deeply. "The world never grasps the fact that man is a collection, not a single exhibit."

The young man played a principal role in the opera and undoubtedly drew the houses, yet he was unhappy. "I am not a comic actor, really," he explained. "I could play Romeo... But of course, it's no good to me with this face of mine."
Dan, ever the philosopher, countered, "Are you not sinning your mercies? Look at young Kelver here. At school, his trouble was that he could give us a good time and make us laugh, which nobody else could do. His ambition was to kick a ball as well as anyone. He could tell a good story now if he would only write what the Almighty intended him to write, instead of gloomy rigmaroles about suffering princesses."
The little man sighed. "The poorest tragedian that ever lived never wished himself the best of low comedians. The court fool had an excellent salary... But not a wooden-headed man-at-arms but looked down upon him. Every gallery boy who pays a shilling to laugh at me regards himself as my intellectual superior."
"There I cannot follow you," Dan replied. "I can understand the artist who would rather be the man of action... though personally my sympathies are precisely the other way—with Wolfe who thought it a more glorious work, the writing of a great poem, than the burning of so many cities."
"Do you really care so little how the wooden-headed man-at-arms regards you?"
"Yes, it does," retorted Goggles. "Because we happen to agree with them."
"If I could earn five pounds a week as juvenile lead, I would never play a comic part again."
"There I cannot follow you," returned Dan. "We all serve the community. Some feed it, some clothe it. The churchman and policeman look after its morals.
