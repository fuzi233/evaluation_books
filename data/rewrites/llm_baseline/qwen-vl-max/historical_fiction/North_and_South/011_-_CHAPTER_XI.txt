“There’s iron, they say, in all our blood,
And a grain or two perhaps is good;
But his, he makes me harshly feel,
Has got a little too much of steel.”

“Margaret!” Mr. Hale called as he returned from showing their guest downstairs. “I couldn’t help watching your face with some concern when Mr. Thornton confessed to having once been a shop-boy. I knew the story already from Mr. Bell, so I was prepared for it—but I half expected you to rise and leave the room.”

“Oh, papa! You don’t mean to say you thought me so shallow?” Margaret replied, her voice warm with surprise. “Honestly, I liked that part of his story more than anything else he said. Everything else repelled me—so cold, so hard. But when he spoke of himself, he did so simply, without pretence, without the kind of showiness that often masks vulgarity in people of trade. And the way he spoke of his mother—so tender, so respectful—that made me feel less inclined to walk out than when he was talking about Milton as if no other place existed, or calmly condemning others for wastefulness while never considering it his duty to lift them up, to give them the same guidance his own mother gave him. No—he telling us he’d once worked in a shop was, in fact, the most honest and human thing he said.”

Her mother looked at her sharply. “I’m astonished, Margaret,” she said. “You who always criticized people for being ‘shoppy’ at Helstone! I don’t think, Mr. Hale, that you were quite right in introducing such a man into our home without warning us of his past. I confess I was afraid to show how deeply some of his words disturbed me. When he mentioned his father dying in ‘miserable circumstances’—well, it might have been in the workhouse.”

“It may well have been worse,” her husband replied quietly. “I learned much of what came before from Mr. Bell before we arrived here. Since he’s shared part of it, I’ll tell you the rest. His father gambled recklessly, failed, and then took his own life—unable to bear the shame. His former friends vanished when the truth came out: debts owed through reckless speculation, money borrowed from others to recoup losses. No one stepped forward to help the widow and her children. There was another child, a girl, too young to earn. Yet no hand reached out—not immediately. Mrs. Thornton, I suspect, isn’t the sort to wait for kindness that comes late. So they left Milton. I knew he had gone into a shop, and that his earnings, along with a small portion of property secured for his mother, kept them alive for years. Mr. Bell said they lived on water-porridge for years—how they managed, he didn’t know. But long after creditors had given up hope of recovering old Mr. Thornton’s debts—perhaps they never truly hoped after the suicide—this young man returned to Milton. He quietly visited each creditor, paid the first instalment, and continued until every debt was settled. No fanfare, no gathering of men—just silence and diligence. One creditor, an old, gruff man, even took him in as a partner, which helped greatly.”

“That is remarkable,” Margaret murmured. “It’s a pity such a nature should be warped by his position as a manufacturer in Milton.”

“How warped?” asked her father.

“Oh, papa, by measuring everything by wealth. When he talked of machinery, he saw only tools to expand trade and increase profit. The poor around him—he sees them not as suffering, but as flawed, lacking self-control, outside his sympathy because they lack the iron will he believes gives him success.”

“Not flawed,” corrected her father. “He said improvident and self-indulgent.”

Margaret was collecting her mother’s sewing things, preparing for bed. As she turned to go, she hesitated. She wanted to speak—to acknowledge something she believed would please her father—but doing so fully meant admitting a discomfort. Still, she spoke. “Papa, I do think Mr. Thornton is a remarkable man. But personally—I don’t like him at all.”

“And I do!” laughed her father. “Personally, as you say. I don’t make him a hero. But goodnight, child. Your mother looks worn tonight, Margaret.”

She had noticed her mother’s fatigue for days. Her father’s words sent her upstairs with a quiet dread settling in her chest. Life in Milton was so different from Helstone—no longer open air and green fields, but smog-filled streets and constant domestic strain. The very air felt heavy, lifeless. The burdens on the women in the household were new, sordid, relentless. It was no wonder her mother seemed weakened.

There were other signs too. Secret meetings between Mrs. Hale and Dixon, during which the maid would emerge tearful and agitated. Once, Margaret slipped into her mother’s room soon after Dixon left and found her on her knees—praying, she could hear, for strength and patience to endure great pain. A prayer for endurance. Not for healing.

Margaret longed to restore the closeness they once shared—the bond broken by her years with Aunt Shaw. She tried gentle touches, soft words, but though her mother responded warmly, there was a hidden sorrow, a guarded secret. And Margaret suspected it concerned her health.

That night, she lay awake, planning ways to ease the toll Milton was taking. A servant to assist Dixon permanently—she would search for one herself, even if it meant visiting every registry office, speaking to anyone, however unlikely. For several days, this became her mission.

One afternoon, she met Bessy Higgins on the street and stopped.

“Well, Bessy, how are you? Better now the wind has shifted?”

“Better and not better,” Bessy replied, her voice low. “I’m better for not coughing myself apart at night. But I’m tired—so weary of Milton. Longing to escape to the land of Beulah. And when I think I’m farther off than ever, my heart sinks—and I’m worse.”

Margaret walked beside her, silent for a moment. Then softly: “Bessy… do you wish to die?”

She shrank from the thought herself—life was precious, especially to one young and strong. But Bessy remained still for a time, then whispered: “If you’d lived as I have, grown tired as I am, thought sometimes, *maybe this goes on fifty, sixty years*—and seen each year spin round, mocking you with its endless hours—oh, wench, you’d be glad enough when the doctor said he feared you’d never see another winter.”

“Why, Bessy, what kind of life have you led?”

“Nought worse than many others’, I reckon. Only I’ve fought against it. Others just accepted.”

“But what was it? I’m new here—I don’t understand the ways of Milton as you do.”

“If you’d come to our house when you promised, I could’ve told you. But father says you’re just like the rest—out of sight, out of mind.”

“I don’t know who the rest are,” Margaret said gently. “I’ve been busy. And I must admit—I forgot my promise.”

“You offered it. We asked for nothing.”

“I did forget—for a time. But I’ll remember again. May I come with you now?”

Bessy studied her face—searching for sincerity. The sharpness in her eyes softened into longing. “I’ve none to care for me. If you care—you may come.”

They walked in silence. At the entrance to a grimy courtyard, Bessy said, “Don’t be startled if Father’s home. He speaks rough at first. But he liked you—he thought a lot of your coming. And because he liked you, he was put out.”

“I won’t be daunted,” Margaret said.

But Nicholas was not there. Instead, a younger, taller girl stood over the wash-tub, banging furniture, making noise. Margaret winced—sympathizing with Bessy, who sank into a chair, exhausted.

She asked the sister for water. While the girl rushed off—knocking over fire-irons, tripping over chairs—Margaret unfastened Bessy’s bonnet strings, easing her labored breathing.

“Do you think a life like this is worth living?” Bessy gasped.

Margaret said nothing. She held the cup to her lips.

Bessy drank deeply, then fell back, eyes closed. Margaret heard her murmur: “They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more. Neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat.”

Margaret leaned down. “Don’t be impatient, Bessy. Whatever your life has been—remember who gave it to you. Who shaped it.”

A voice behind her—Nicholas had entered unseen. “Now, I’ll not have my wench preached to. She’s bad enough with her dreams and visions—cities with golden gates and jewels. But if it amuses her, let it be. But I won’t have more nonsense poured into her.”

“But surely,” Margaret turned, “you believe God gave her life, and determined its course?”

“I believe what I see,” Nicholas said bluntly. “No more. That’s my faith. I don’t believe all I hear—no, not by a long stretch. I did hear a young woman offer to visit us. My daughter thought a lot of it—flushed up many times when she heard a stranger’s step. But now she’s come—and she’s welcome, so long as she keeps from preaching about things she knows nothing of.”

Bessy had watched Margaret closely. Now she sat up slightly, laying a trembling hand on her arm. “Don’t be angry with him. Many think like him—many here. If you could hear them talk, you wouldn’t be shocked. He’s a good man—my father—but oh!” she cried, sinking back, “what he says sometimes makes me long to die more than ever. I want to know so much—and yet I’m tossed about with wonder.”

“Poor wench—poor old wench,” Nicholas muttered. “I hate to vex thee. But a man must speak truth. When I see the world going wrong—busy with things it doesn’t understand, leaving undone what lies right before it—I say: stop this talk about religion. Work on what you see. That’s my creed. Simple. Close at hand. Easy to follow.”

But Bessy clung harder to Margaret. “Don’t judge him—don’t think ill of him. He’s a good man. I sometimes think I’ll be moped with sorrow in the City of God if he’s not there.”

Color flared in her cheeks, fire in her eyes. “But you will be there, father! You shall! Oh, my heart!”

She pressed a hand to her chest and went pale as death.

Margaret cradled her, laid her head upon her bosom, brushed hair from her temples, bathed them with water. Nicholas understood her gestures instantly—love taught him quick recognition. Even the younger sister moved slowly, gently, at Margaret’s quiet “hush.”

The spasm passed. Bessy stirred. “I’ll go to bed—it’s best. But,” she grasped Margaret’s gown, “you’ll come again—won’t you? Just say it!”

“I will come tomorrow,” Margaret said.

Bessy leaned back into her father’s arms as he lifted her toward the stairs. But as Margaret rose to leave, Nicholas struggled to speak. “I could wish there were a God—if only to ask Him to bless thee.”

She walked home, heavy-hearted, late for tea. At Helstone, such lateness would have drawn a scolding. But now, even these small things seemed to matter less. She almost missed the familiar complaints.

“Have you found a servant, dear?” her mother asked.

“No, mamma. Anne Buckley wouldn’t do.”

“Suppose I try,” said Mr. Hale. “Everyone else has had a turn at this difficulty. Let me try. I may be the Cinderella who fits the slipper.”

Margaret could barely smile—her thoughts still haunted by the Higginses.

“What would you do, papa? How would you begin?”

“I’d ask a good housekeeper to recommend someone known to her or her staff.”

“Very good. But we must first catch our house-mother.”

“You’ve caught her. Or rather, she’s walking into the trap. You’ll catch her tomorrow—if you’re clever.”

“What do you mean, Mr. Hale?” his wife asked, intrigued.

“My paragon pupil—Margaret calls him that—has told me his mother intends to call on us tomorrow.”

“Mrs. Thornton!” exclaimed Mrs. Hale.

“The mother he spoke of?” asked Margaret.

“Mrs. Thornton—the only mother he has, I believe,” Mr. Hale said quietly.

“I’d like to meet her,” said Mrs. Hale. “She must be an extraordinary woman.”

“Perhaps she has a relation who might suit us,” Margaret added. “She seemed so careful, so economical. Anyone from her family might be ideal.”

“My dear,” Mr. Hale said, alarmed. “Please don’t go down that path. I suspect Mrs. Thornton is as proud and aloof in her way as our little Margaret is in hers. She likely ignores entirely the poverty and hardship her son speaks of. I’m certain she wouldn’t want strangers to know about it.”

“Take note,” Margaret said, “that’s not my kind of pride, if I have any at all—which I deny, though you’re always accusing me of it.”

“I don’t know for sure,” her father replied, “but from little hints he’s dropped, I suspect it’s hers.”

They cared too little to ask how he spoke of her. Margaret only wondered if she must stay in to receive the visitor—knowing it would delay her visit to Bessy until late. And then she remembered: her mother must not be left alone to entertain such a distinguished guest.
