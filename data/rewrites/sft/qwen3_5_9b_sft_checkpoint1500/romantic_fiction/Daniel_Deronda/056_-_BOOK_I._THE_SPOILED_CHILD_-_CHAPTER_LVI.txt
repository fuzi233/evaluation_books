Deronda did not undress that night. Gwendolen, after insisting on seeing him again before consenting to be stripped, had fallen into a profound, heavy silence. She had whispered to him with a repressed, burning eagerness, asking only that he promise to return when she summoned him in the morning. Yet a shadow lingered within him, a foreboding born of the fragility of her state. The possibility that a sudden fever might seize her, or that the lingering shock of the catastrophe could manifest in wild, broken words, weighed heavily on his mind. He instructed her attendant to call him instantly at any sign of alarm, making it clear to everyone that he was in constant communication with her friends in England and felt a binding duty to guard her safety. This role fell easily to him, for he was well known to Grandcourt's sole remaining servant, the old valet who had accompanied the late voyage.

When exhaustion from the wildly varied emotions of the day finally claimed Deronda, he slept, only to be torn from it by a tangled web of nightmares spun from yesterday's events. These visions woke him with an image drawn by his pressing anxiety. But morning had come, and there had been no summons—an augury that cheered him as he dressed, leading him to reflect that it was perhaps too early to inquire further. Later, he learned that she had endured a wakeful night but showed no violent signs of agitation, eventually succumbing to slumber. He marveled at the sheer force dwelling in this creature, so acutely alive to dread; he carried an irresistible impression that even under the effects of a severe physical shock, she was mastering herself with a determination of absolute concealment. As for his own sensibilities, he believed they had been dulled by the raw intensity of meeting his mother. To him, his current cares for Gwendolen seemed merely the fulfillment of claims, his more passionate sympathy lying in abeyance. Having lived so keenly in an experience quite apart from Gwendolen's lot, his present attentions felt like a revisiting of familiar scenes, lacking yet the complete revival of inner response.

In the meantime, Deronda set about obtaining a formal, legally recognized statement from the fishermen who had rescued Gwendolen. Few details emerged. The yacht in which Grandcourt had gone out was found drifting with its sail loose and was towed back. The fishermen surmised that Grandcourt had been knocked overboard by the flapping sail while tacking, noting that he likely could not swim. However, though they were nearby, their attention had first been arrested by a cry resembling that of a man in distress. While they hastened with their oars, they heard a shriek from the lady and saw her plunge into the water.

Upon returning to the hotel, Deronda was informed that Gwendolen had risen and desired to see him. He was shown into a room darkened by blinds and heavy curtains. Seated therein, she wore a white shawl wrapped around her, looking toward the open door like one waiting uneasily. Her long hair was gathered up and coiled carefully, and through it all, the blue stars in her ears retained their place. As she started impulsively to her full height, sheathed in white shawl, her face and neck not less pallid—save for a purple line beneath her eyes, her lips slightly apart with the peculiar expression of one accused and helpless—she appeared like the unhappy ghost of Gwendolen Harleth whom Deronda had last seen turning from her losses at the gaming table with firm lips and proud self-possession. The sight pierced him with pity, and the effects of all their past relations began to revive within him.

"I beseech you to rest—not to stand," said Deronda as he approached her. She obeyed, falling back into her chair.

"Will you sit down near me?" she asked. "I want to speak very low."

She sat in a large armchair, and he drew a small one near her side. The action touched her peculiarly. Turning her pale face fully upon his, which hovered very close, she spoke in the lowest audible tone, "You know I am a guilty woman?"

Deronda himself turned paler as he replied, "I know nothing." He dared not say more.

"He is dead." She uttered these words with the same undertoned decision.

"Yes," said Deronda, in a mournful suspense that made him reluctant to speak.

"His face will not be seen above the water again," Gwendolen said, her tone not louder but of suppressed, frantic eagerness, holding both hands clenched.

"No."

"Not by any one else—only by me. A dead face... I shall never get away from it."

With an inward voice of desperate self-repression, she spoke these final words, gazing away from Deronda toward something distant on the floor. She was seeing the whole event—her own acts included—through an exaggerating medium of excitement and horror. Was she in a state of delirium, entering a realm of concealment and necessity for self-repression? Such thoughts flashed through Deronda as a sort of hope. But imagine the conflict of feeling that kept him silent. She was bent on confession, and he dreaded hearing it. Against his better will, he shrank from the task laid upon him; he wished, and yet rebuked the wish as cowardly, that she could bury her secrets in her own bosom. He was not a priest. He dreaded the weight of this woman's soul flung upon his own with imploring dependence.

Yet she spoke again, hurriedly, looking at him.

"You will not say that I ought to tell the world? You will not say that I ought to be disgraced? I could not do it. I could not bear it. I cannot have my mother know. Not if I were dead. I could not have her know. I must tell you; but you will not say that any one else should know."

"I can say nothing in my ignorance," said Deronda, mournfully, "except that I desire to help you."

"I told you from the beginning—as soon as I could—that I was afraid of myself." There was a piteous pleading in the low murmur to which Deronda turned his ear only. Her face afflicted him too much. "I felt a hatred in me that was always working like an evil spirit—contriving things. Everything I could do to free myself came into my mind; and it got worse—all things got worse. That is why I asked you to come to me in town. I thought then I would tell you the worst about myself. I tried. But I could not tell everything. And *he* came in."

She paused, while a shudder passed through her, before continuing.

"I will tell you everything now. Do you think a woman who cried, and prayed, and struggled to be saved from herself, could be a murderess?"

"Great God!" said Deronda, in a deep, shaken voice. "Don't torture me needlessly. You have not murdered him. You threw yourself into the water with the impulse to save him. Tell me the rest afterward. This death was an accident that you could not have hindered."

"Don't be impatient with me." The tremor, the childlike beseeching in these words compelled Deronda to turn his head and look at her face. The poor quivering lips continued. "You said—you used to say—you felt more for those who had done something wicked and were miserable; you said they might get better—they might be scourged into something better. If you had not spoken in that way, everything would have been worse. I *did* remember all you said to me. It came to me always. It came to me at the very last—that was the reason why I—But now, if you cannot bear with me when I tell you everything—if you turn away from me and forsake me, what shall I do? Am I worse than I was when you found me and wanted to make me better? All the wrong I have done was in me then—and more—and more—if you had not come and been patient with me. And now—will you forsake me?"

Her hands, which had been so tightly clenched moments before, were now helplessly relaxed and trembling on the arm of her chair. Her quivering lips remained parted as she ceased speaking. Deronda could not answer; he was obliged to look away. He took one of her hands and clasped it as if they were going to walk together like two children; it was the only way in which he could answer, "I will not forsake you." And all the while he felt as if he were putting his name to a blank paper which might be filled up terribly. Their attitude, his averred face with its expression of a suffering which he was solemnly resolved to undergo, might have told half the truth of the situation to a beholder who had suddenly entered.

That grasp was an entirely new experience to Gwendolen: she had never before received from any man a sign of tenderness which her own being had needed, and she interpreted its powerful effect upon her into a promise of inexhaustible patience and constancy. The stream of renewed strength made it possible for her to proceed as she had begun—with that fitful, wandering confession where the sameness of experience seemed to nullify the sense of time or order in events.
