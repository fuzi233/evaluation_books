Tito’s carefully laid plans had unraveled not through any flaw in his intellect, but by the capricious hand of chance—those small, unforeseen turns of fate that no strategy can contain. It was rare for him to walk with Romola in the evenings, yet tonight, when her presence could not have been more ill-timed, he found himself beside her. Life, he reflected, was a game so intricate that even the most calculated moves could be undone by the lightest breath of wind, like thistle-down carried on a sudden gust.

He did not grieve over Spini’s failed scheme; what troubled him was the delicate balance he now faced. How could he warn Savonarola without arousing suspicion? And how could he inform Spini without revealing his own shift in allegiance? If he rushed to the monastery before the monks retired, it would appear as though he had betrayed Spini’s plot at the very moment he delivered the forged letters—a connection too obvious to ignore. Yet if he delayed until morning, he risked losing the chance to alert Spini that Savonarola had changed his mind. The Compagnacci, enraged by their disappointment, might turn against him. Still, Tito chose the delay, trusting that he could calm Spini with the explanation that the Frate had simply grown cautious.

But the decision left him restless. He had no desire to see Romola again and spent the night wandering the city streets, avoiding home. Meanwhile, Romola remained awake, dressed in her night clothes, listening to the rain grow heavier. She welcomed the storm—the thunderous sky seemed to silence human schemes, forcing men to stay within. But her mind was far from still. Doubt gnawed at her: Was Tito lying to her? What hidden purpose might he harbor? Every person who trusted him was at risk. She tried to convince herself otherwise, but the truth was unbearable.

And then came the deeper question: Was she being selfish, driven by pride, in refusing to expose her husband? If he were guilty, should she not stand beside him in prison? That she could accept. But was it not her duty, as a wife, to prevent harm before it was done? Could she allow him to commit acts that would make him a criminal, when she might stop him? Prayer felt impossible—her thoughts were too fierce, too active, for quiet submission. The tension grew, feeding her imagination with visions of ways Tito might have evaded her warnings.

At last, the rain eased, the clouds broke, and dawn spilled across the city in clear, cold light. The clarity only deepened her unease. She wrapped her mantle tightly and ran to the loggia, scanning the landscape as if some sign might emerge from the hills or rooftops. But all she saw were buildings—no clue, no certainty. Perhaps she could go to her godfather, urge him to act without revealing everything, to block Savonarola’s passage through the gates. But time was slipping away. She realized, with fresh anguish, that she had learned nothing concrete from Tito. It was past seven. There was no choice: she must go to San Marco.

She descended the stairs swiftly, ignoring her sick patients, and stepped into the street. She moved quickly along the Via de’ Bardi toward the Ponte Vecchio, choosing the heart of Florence—the shortest route, and one where she might still meet Tito. Perhaps he would confirm the Frate’s safety, ending her torment.

At the Palazzo Vecchio, she searched the colonnaded courtyard, then swept her gaze across the Piazza. But there was no sign of him—not the figure once cherished in her youth, now etched into her soul with pain. She pressed on to the Piazza del Duomo, where the morning bustle had already begun. Worshippers climbed the marble steps, merchants carried goods, and voices rose in conversation. Then, among the crowd, she saw him.

Tito stood outside Nello’s shop, leaning casually against the doorpost, hat in hand, hair freshly combed, talking with ease. His relaxed demeanor struck her like a blow. This man, who had stirred such dread in her, now appeared untroubled, almost carefree. A wave of fury surged through her. She recognized Cronaca and two others from San Marco nearby. In that instant, an idea flashed: *I will force him to speak before them.*

She approached silently, her step light but swift. As she neared, Cronaca turned and said, “Here comes Madonna Romola.”

A jolt passed through Tito’s body. He met her eyes—hollowed by sleepless watching, yet blazing with something fiercer than fear.

“Is the Frate gone beyond the gates?” she asked, voice low but sharp.

“No,” he replied, struggling to keep his composure. He felt utterly powerless before her.

“And you are certain he is not going?” she pressed.

“I am certain he is not going.”

“That is enough,” she said, turning abruptly and ascending the steps into the Duomo.

Tito watched her retreat, a feeling rising in him colder than hatred—something closer to revulsion.

In the square, others stood near: true followers of Savonarola, yes—but also Ser Ceccone, the notary, a secret agent of the Medici, like Tito himself. Ser Ceccone was no man of grace. Unlearned, unattractive, unkind, he lacked charm even in betrayal. And so, he held no affection for Tito Melema.
