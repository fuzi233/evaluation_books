Besides Madame Beck’s mother, there was yet another power with a voice sharp enough to cut through any covenant M. Paul and I might fancy we had made. We lived beneath a vigilance that never slept. Rome—silent as incense, patient as stone—watched her son through the mystic lattice where I had once knelt, and whither he drew near month by month: the sliding panel of the confessional.

Yes, he had long been my friend; yes, he had shown, more than once, a certain partiality in feeling. Yet I had hungered to hear him say it plainly, warmly, with that earnestness which makes a word into a vow. I had liked his modest hesitations, his tender deference, his trust that longed to rest and seemed grateful when it was taught how. He had called me “sister.” It was well. He might call me what he pleased, so long as he confided in me. I would be his sister on one condition only—that he should not, one day, invite me to fill that relation towards some future wife of his; and as he stood tacitly vowed to celibacy, the snare appeared unlikely.

Most of the succeeding night I went over our evening’s interview as one might keep returning to a remembered flavour—first sweet, then strange, then troubling, yet impossible to set aside. I watched for morning; I listened for the bell; I rose, dressed, and found prayers and breakfast intolerably slow. All the hours loitered until the lesson of literature, and my wish was simple: to understand what this new, named alliance meant; to see how brotherly he would be when we met again; to test what sisterhood lay in me; to discover whether I could summon a sister’s courage, and he a brother’s frankness.

He came; and life, made perversely, will not match expectation.

All that day he never accosted me. His lesson was given more quietly than usual, more mildly, and with a gravity that chilled. He was fatherly to his pupils; he was not brotherly to me. I expected, before he left the classe, a smile—at least a word. There was neither. For my portion fell one nod—hurried, shy.

“This distance,” I argued, “is accidental; involuntary; patience, and it will pass.” It did not pass. It continued for days; it increased. I smothered surprise and swallowed whatever else began to rise.

Well might I have asked, when he offered fraternity, “Dare I rely on you?” Well might he, knowing himself, have withheld a pledge. True, he had bade me make my own experiments—to tease and try him. Vain injunction! Privilege nominal and unattainable. Some women could have used it. Nothing in my instincts placed me amongst that brave band. Left alone, I was passive; repulsed, I withdrew; forgotten—my lips could not shape a reminder, nor my eyes dart one. Somewhere there had been an error in my reckoning; time must disclose it.

The customary evening came when he was to give me a lesson. One evening in seven he had long bestowed on me—generously, steadily—devoting it to the week’s review and the week to come. On such occasions my schoolroom was anywhere: wherever the pupils and teachers were, or close to them; often in the large second division, where, when the day-pupils were gone and the boarders sat in a knot near the surveillante’s estrade, one could choose a quiet nook and pretend the world had narrowed into paper and ink.

At the proper hour I gathered my books and papers, pen and ink, and went to the large division.

In classe there was no one, and it lay in cool, deep shadow; but through the open double doors the carré was visible—full of pupils and full of light. The westering sun blushed over hall and figures; its ruddy warmth fused wall-colours and the variegated dresses into a single glow, like fruit steeping in syrup. The girls were seated, working or studying; in the midst stood M. Emanuel, speaking good-humouredly to a teacher. His dark paletôt and jetty hair caught crimson reflections; his Spanish face, when he turned it, answered the sun’s kiss with an animated smile. I took my place at a desk and set my hands in order upon my things as if order could steady a heart.

Near the windows the orange-trees and other plants—bright with bloom—had basked all day and now asked water. He had a taste for gardening; he liked to tend and foster living green things. I had long thought that the slow labour among shrubs—with spade or watering-pot—quieted his nerves, as kneading quiets a cook’s impatience: the same hands that could be quick with chalk and rod could also be exact and gentle with root and leaf. Now he looked to the orange-trees, the geraniums, the gorgeous cactuses, and revived them with the refreshment their drought required. His lips at the same time held his precious cigar—the first necessity and prime luxury of his life; its blue wreaths curled among the blossoms in the warm evening light, spoiling, for me, the sweetness with a sharp, foreign bitterness.

He spoke no more to pupils or mistresses, but gave endearing words to a small, spanieless thing—if one may coin a word—which nominally belonged to the house, but in truth owned him for master. She was delicate, silky, loving, lovable, trotting at his side, looking up into his face with an attachment that needed no language. Whenever he dropped his bonnet-grec or handkerchief—sometimes in play—she crouched beside it like a tiny lion guarding a flag.

There were many plants; and as the amateur gardener fetched water from the well with his own active hands, his work spun on. The great school-clock ticked. Another hour struck. The carré and its youthful circle lost the illusion of sunset. Day drooped. My lesson, I perceived, must to-night be short. But the orange-trees, cacti, camellias—all were served now. Was it my turn?

Alas! in the garden there were more favourites: rose-bushes, choice flowers. Little Sylvie’s glad bark and whine followed the receding paletôt down the alleys. I put up some of my books; I should not want them all. I sat and waited, feeling twilight creep like cold water along the floor of my patience.

Sylvie emerged again, frisking—heralding the returning paletôt. The watering-pot was set by the well; its duty done. How glad I was! Monsieur washed his hands in a little stone bowl. There was scarcely time now for any lesson; soon the prayer-bell must ring. Still—we should meet; he would speak; I might read in his eyes the riddle of that shyness which had turned into distance.

His ablutions done, he stood, slowly re-arranging his cuffs, looking at the horn of a young moon set pale in an opal sky, glimmering faint on Jean Baptiste’s oriel. Sylvie watched his contemplative stillness and could not bear it; she whined, jumped, tried to break the hush. He looked down.

“Petite exigeante,” said he; “you must not be forgotten one moment, it seems.”

He lifted her, sauntered across the court within a yard of the windows near which I sat—sauntered lingeringly, fondling the little creature in his bosom, calling her tender names in a tender voice. On the front-door steps he turned; again he looked at the moon, at the grey cathedral, over spires and roofs fading into a blue sea of night-mist. He tasted the sweet breath of dusk and noted the folded bloom of the garden. Then, suddenly, he looked round; a keen beam from his eye rased the white façade of the classes, swept the long line of windows. I think he bowed; if he did, I had no time to return it. In a moment he was gone; the moonlit threshold lay pale and shadowless before the closed door.

I gathered up the unused heap from my desk and carried it back to its place in the third classe. The prayer-bell rang; I obeyed.

The morrow would not restore him to the Rue Fossette: it was his college day. I taught; I endured the intermediate hours; I saw evening coming and braced myself for its heavy ennui. Whether it was worse to sit with my co-inmates or alone, I had not weighed; I took, by nature, the latter. If comfort existed, no heart or head in this house could yield it; only under the lid of my desk might it lodge—nestling between leaves, clinging to a pencil-point, the nib of a pen, or tinting the black pool in the ink-glass. With a heavy heart I opened the desk; with a weary hand I turned its contents.

One by one, well-accustomed books—volumes sewn in familiar covers—came out and went back. They had no charm; they could not comfort. Then: something new—a pamphlet in lilac. I had not seen it before; and I had arranged my desk this very day, this very afternoon. It must have been slipped in within the last hour, while we were at dinner.

I opened it. What was it? What did it mean to say to me?

It was neither tale nor poem, neither essay nor history. It did not sing, relate, or discuss. It preached—and persuaded.

Small as it was, it had its own spell: its voice—honeyed, unctuous—bound attention at once. It preached Romanism; it persuaded to conversion. Here were no thunders, no blasting breath of displeasure. The Protestant was to turn Papist, not so much from dread of the heretic’s hell, as from longing for the comfort, indulgence, tenderness Holy Church offered. She threaten? She coerce? Oh dear no; she only wished to guide and win.

It was milk for babes; the mild effluence of a mother’s love towards her youngest—addressed to those whose head is reached through the heart. It appealed not to intellect; it sought to win the affectionate through their affections, the sympathising through their sympathies. St. Vincent de Paul gathering orphans about him could not have spoken more sweetly.

One inducement to apostasy was held out with particular care: the Catholic who had lost dear friends could enjoy the solace of praying them out of purgatory. The writer did not touch the firmer peace of those whose belief dispenses with purgatory altogether; but I thought of it, and, on the whole, preferred that doctrine as the most consolatory. The little book amused rather than displeased me. It was canting, sentimental, shallow; yet something in its soft, coaxing art cheered my gloom and made me smile—as if I watched the gambols of an unlicked wolf-cub muffled in fleece and practising the bleat of a lamb. It reminded me of certain Methodist tracts read in childhood—flavoured with the same seasoning of excitation to fanaticism. He that wrote it was perhaps no bad man; and though trained cunning—system’s cloven hoof—showed itself perpetually, I paused before accusing him of insincerity. His judgment, however, wanted surgical props: it was rickety.

I smiled over this dose of maternal tenderness coming from the ruddy old lady of the Seven Hills; smiled, too, at my own disinclination—almost disability—to meet these melting favours. I glanced at the title-page and read: “Père Silas.” A fly-leaf bore, in small but clear pencil characters well known to me: “From P. C. D. E. to L—y.” When I saw that, I laughed—but not in my former spirit. I was revived.

A mortal bewilderment cleared from head and sight. The Sphinx-riddle was answered. The conjunction of those two names—Père Silas and Paul Emanuel—gave the key. The penitent had been with his director; permitted to withhold nothing; suffered to keep no corner of his heart sacred to God and himself. Our late interview had been drawn from him; he had avowed the covenant of fraternity, spoken of his adopted sister. How could such a covenant be sanctioned? Fraternal communion with a heretic! I seemed to hear Père Silas annulling the pact, warning of perils, entreating, enjoining reserve—nay, commanding, by the authority of his office and by the memory of all M. Emanuel held most dear, the enforcement of that new system whose frost had pierced to the marrow of my bones.

These are not pleasant conjectures. Yet, by comparison, they were welcome: the vision of a ghostly troubler hovering in the background was as nothing beside the fear of spontaneous change arising in M. Paul himself.

At this distance I cannot be sure how much these conjectures were self-suggested, and how far they were confirmed from another quarter. Help was not wanting.

This evening brought no bright sunset. West and east were one cloud; no summer night-mist, blue yet rose-tinged, softened the distance. A clammy fog from the marshes crept grey round Villette. Rain had drizzled all afternoon and still fell—fast, quiet, persistent—ticking on leaves and window-frames with a sound that made the house feel hollow. It was no weather for rambling under dripping trees; so I started to hear Sylvie’s sudden bark in the garden—her bark of welcome. Surely she was not accompanied; yet that glad, quick bark was never given save in homage to one presence.

Through the glass door and arching berceau I commanded the deep vista of the allée défendue. Thither rushed Sylvie, glistening through gloom like a white guelder-rose. She ran to and fro, whining, springing, harassing little birds among the bushes. I watched five minutes; no fulfilment followed. I returned to my books; her bark ceased.

Again I looked up. She stood not many yards distant, wagging her feathery tail as fast as muscle could work, watching intently the operations of a spade plied fast by an indefatigable hand. There was M. Emanuel, bent over the soil, digging in wet mould among rain-laden shrubs, working hard—as if his day’s pittance were still to earn by literal sweat.

In that sign I read a ruffled mood. He would dig thus in frozen snow on the coldest winter day when urged inwardly by painful emotion—nervous excitation or sad self-reproach. He would dig by the hour, brow knit, teeth set, never lifting his head, never opening his lips.

Sylvie watched until tired. Then, scampering devious—snuffing and sniffing everywhere—she discovered me in classe. Instantly she flew barking at the panes, urging me forth to share either her pleasure or her master’s toil; she had seen me walk that alley with M. Paul and doubtless judged it my duty to join him now, wet as it was.

She made such a bustle that at last he looked up and, of course, perceived why and at whom she barked. He whistled to call her off; she barked louder—bent on having the glass door opened. Tired, I suppose, of her importunity, he threw down his spade, approached, and pushed the door ajar.

Sylvie burst in, sprang to my lap, and with paws at my neck—and little nose and tongue somewhat too busy about my face, mouth, and eyes—flourished her tail over the desk and scattered books and papers far and wide. The small animal’s warm breath smelled of wet garden earth and the faint sour of rain; her coat held the damp as herbs hold dew.

M. Emanuel advanced to still the clamour and repair the wreck. He gathered up the books; he captured Sylvie and stowed her away under his paletôt, where she nestled quiet as a mouse, her head just peeping forth. She was tiny and had the prettiest innocent face, silkiest long ears, and the finest dark eyes. I never saw her without thinking of Paulina de Bassompierre; forgive the association—it would occur.

He petted and patted her. The endearments were natural; she invited affection by beauty and vivid life.

While caressing the little dog, his eye roved over the papers just replaced. It settled on the religious tract. His lips moved; he half checked an impulse to speak—as if a promise of silence tied him and his nature strained against it. If he had vowed never to address me more, his better self broke the vow; with a second effort he spoke.

“You have not yet read the brochure, I presume? It is not sufficiently inviting?”

I replied that I had read it.

He waited, as if wishing me to offer an opinion unasked. Unasked, however, I was in no mood to make concessions or advances. If any were demanded, let the docile pupil of Père Silas make them—not I. His eye rested on me gently; in the blue ray there was mildness, solicitude, a shade of pathos; meanings composite and contrasted—reproach melting into remorse. He seemed, for an instant, to hunger for some emotion in me. I could not show it. Another minute, and I might have betrayed confusion; so I bethought myself of quill-pens and drew some from my desk, beginning soberly to mend them.

I knew this would turn his mood. He never liked to see me mend pens. My knife was always dull; my hand unskilful; I hacked and chipped. On this occasion I cut my finger—half on purpose. I wanted to restore him to his natural state, to set him at ease, to hear him chide.

“Maladroit!” he cried at last. “She will make mincemeat of her hands.”

He put Sylvie down, making her lie quiet beside his bonnet-grec; then, taking my pens and penknife, he began to slice, nib, and point with the accuracy and quickness of a machine. The sound of the blade on quill—small, crisp, orderly—was like the calming crackle of a clean fire.

“Did you like the little book?” he asked.

Suppressing a yawn, I said I hardly knew.

“Had it moved you?”

“I think it made me a little sleepy.”

After a pause—impatient, incredulous—he broke out that it was of no use taking that tone with him. Bad as I was—and he would be sorry to name all my faults at a breath—God and nature had given me “trop de sensibilité et de sympathie” not to be profoundly affected by an appeal so touching.

“Indeed!” I answered, rousing myself. “I was not affected at all—not a whit.”

And in proof I drew from my pocket a perfectly dry handkerchief, still clean and folded.

Then came a string of strictures, more piquant than polite. I listened with zest. After two days of unnatural silence, it was better than music to hear M. Paul haranguing again in his old fashion. While he spoke, I solaced myself—and Sylvie—with the contents of a bonbonnière which his gifts kept supplied with chocolate comfits. The sweet cracked under the tooth; the cocoa rose warm and bitter after the sugar; it grounded me in my body when my mind would have flown into worry. It pleased him to see even such a trifle from his hand duly appreciated. He looked at me and the spaniel as we shared the spoil; then he put up his penknife. Touching my hand with the bundle of newly cut quills, he said:

“Dites donc, petite sœur—speak frankly—what have you thought of me during the last two days?”

I took no notice. The purport made my eyes fill. I bent over Sylvie, caressing her with an attention too steady to be casual. He leaned over the desk, bending towards me.

“I called myself your brother,” he said. “I hardly know what I am—brother—friend—I cannot tell. I know I think of you—I feel I wish you well—but I must check myself; you are to be feared. My best friends point out danger, and whisper caution.”

“You do right to listen to your friends. By all means be cautious.”

“It is your religion—your strange, self-reliant, invulnerable creed—whose influence seems to clothe you in, I know not what, unblessed panoply. You are good—Père Silas calls you good, and loves you—but your terrible, proud, earnest Protestantism—there is the danger. It expresses itself by your eye at times; and again it gives you certain tones and certain gestures that make my flesh creep. You are not demonstrative, and yet just now—when you handled that tract—my God! I thought Lucifer smiled.”

“Certainly I don’t respect that tract—what then?”

“Not respect that tract? But it is the pure essence of faith, love, charity! I thought it would touch you: in its gentleness, I trusted it could not fail. I laid it in your desk with a prayer. I must indeed be a sinner: Heaven will not hear the petitions that come warmest from my heart. You scorn my little offering. Oh, cela me fait mal!”

“Monsieur, I don’t scorn it—at least, not as your gift. Monsieur, sit down; listen to me. I am not a heathen. I am not hard-hearted. I am not unchristian. I am not dangerous, as they tell you. I would not trouble your faith. You believe in God and Christ and the Bible, and so do I.”

“But do you believe in the Bible? Do you receive Revelation? What limits are there to the wild, careless daring of your country and sect? Père Silas dropped dark hints.”

By dint of persuasion I made him half-define these hints; they amounted to crafty slanders—little poisoned needles, slipped into language as one might slip a bitter herb into a dish meant to please. That night M. Paul and I talked seriously and closely. He pleaded; he argued. I could not argue—a fortunate incapacity; it needed but a triumphant, logical opposition to effect what his director wished. But I could talk in my own way—the way he was used to—whose meanderings he could follow, whose pauses he could fill, whose strange stammerings were strange to him no longer. At ease with him, I defended my creed as I could; I lulled, in some degree, his prejudices.

He was not satisfied when he went away; hardly was he appeased. Yet he was made thoroughly to feel that Protestants were not necessarily the irreverent pagans his director had insinuated; he was brought to comprehend something of their manner of honouring the Light, the Life, the Word; and he was enabled, partly, to perceive that while their veneration for things venerable was not like that cultivated in his Church, it had its own—perhaps deeper—power, its own more solemn awe.
