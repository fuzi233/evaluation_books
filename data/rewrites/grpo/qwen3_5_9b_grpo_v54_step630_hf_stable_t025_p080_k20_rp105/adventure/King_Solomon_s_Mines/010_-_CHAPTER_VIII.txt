The afternoon sun beat down upon the magnificent roadway as we pressed steadily north-westward. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, their own followers marching a hundred paces ahead, a disciplined phalanx of silence.

"Infadoos," I asked eventually, breaking the rhythmic tramp of our boots, "who made this road?"

"It was made, my lord, in ancient times," the guide replied, his voice carrying the weight of generations. "None know how or when, not even the wise woman Gagool, who has lived for countless cycles. We are too young to remember its creation. No mortal hand can fashion such a path today, yet the King forbids a single blade of grass to touch its surface."

"And the writings on the walls of the caves we have traversed?" I continued, gesturing to the Egyptian-like carvings that lined our passage. "Whose handiwork are they?"

"The hands that forged the road also penned the wonders," Infadoos said. "We know not the author."

"When did the Kukuana people first arrive in this land?"

"The race descended like the breath of a storm ten thousand moons ago," he answered, pointing toward the distant north. "They came from the great lands beyond. They could not travel further due to the high mountains that ring the territory, as the old voices of our fathers tell us, confirmed by Gagool herself, the smeller-out of witches. The land was fertile, so they settled, grew strong, and multiplied until our numbers are like the sea-sand. When King Twala calls his regiments, their plumes cover the plain until the eye cannot distinguish one warrior from another."

"If the land is walled in by mountains, who are your regiments fighting?"

"Nay, my lord. The country opens to the north. Warriors sweep down upon us in clouds from unknown lands, and we slay them. It is the third part of a man's life spent in war since that first invasion. Thousands died, but we destroyed those who came to devour us. Since then, there has been peace."

"Your warriors must grow weary of resting on their spears, Infadoos."

"There was one war, just after we repelled the invaders," he admitted. "But it was a civil war; dog ate dog."

"How was that?"

"Our father, Kafa, died when we came to manhood. My brother Imotu was made king. He reigned briefly and had a son by his favorite wife. When the babe was three years old, a famine struck the land during a great war, leaving no man to sow or reap. The people murmured, starving like lions seeking prey. Then Gagool, the terrible woman who does not die, proclaimed to the crowd, 'The king Imotu is no king.'

"At that moment, Imotu was sick, lying in his kraal unable to move. Gagool led Twala, my half-brother, whom she had hidden among the caves since birth, before the people. Stripping the *moocha* from his loins, she revealed the mark of the sacred snake coiled around his middle—the sign of the eldest son—and cried, 'Behold your king whom I have saved!'

"The people, mad with hunger and bereft of reason, shouted, 'The king! The king!' I knew it was false; Imotu was the elder twin and the lawful ruler. Just as the tumult peaked, Imotu, though gravely ill, crawled from his hut, holding his wife's hand, followed by his young son, Ignosi—the Lightning.

"'What is this noise?' he asked. 'Why cry ye The king! The king!'

"Then Twala, his twin brother born of the same womb and hour, ran to him, seized him by the hair, and stabbed him through the heart with his knife. The people, fickle and eager to worship the rising sun, clapped their hands and cried, 'Twala is king! Now we know that Twala is king!'"

"And what became of Imotu's wife and her son, Ignosi? Did Twala kill them too?"

"Nay, my lord. When she saw her lord fall, the queen seized the child and fled. Two days later, starving and rejected by all men who now hated the unfortunate, she sought shelter. At nightfall, a little girl brought her corn, and she blessed the child before leading her boy toward the mountains before sunrise. There she must have perished; none have seen her or Ignosi since."

"Then if that child Ignosi had lived, he would be the true king?"

"That is so, my lord. The sacred snake marks him. If he lives, he is king; but alas, he is long dead."

Infadoos pointed to a vast collection of huts surrounded by a fence and encircled by a great ditch on the plain below. "That is the kraal where the wife of Imotu was last seen with the child Ignosi. It is there we shall sleep tonight, if, indeed, my lords sleep at all upon this earth."

"When we are among the Kukuanas, my good friend Infadoos, we do as the Kukuanas do," I declared majestically. I turned to address Good, who tramped sullenly behind, vainly trying to stop his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. To my surprise, I bumped into Umbopa, walking immediately behind me. His expression was peculiar, suggesting a man struggling to recall something long forgotten.

We pressed on toward the undulating plain. The mountains we had crossed now loomed high above us, and Sheba's Breasts were veiled in diaphanous wreaths of mist. The country grew increasingly lovely; the vegetation was luxuriant yet not tropical, the sun bright and warm, and a gracious breeze swept along the odorous slopes. This new land was little less than an earthly paradise, surpassing even the Transvaal in beauty, natural wealth, and climate.

As we started, Infadoos dispatched a runner to warn the people of the kraal, which fell under his military command, of our arrival. The man departed at extraordinary speed, a feat Infadoos noted was common among his people.

The result of this warning became apparent within two miles. Companies of men issued from the gates, marching toward us. Sir Henry laid his hand on my arm, remarking that the reception looked warm. Something in his tone caught Infadoos' attention.

"Let not my lords be afraid," he said hastily. "In my breast dwells no guile. This regiment is under my command and comes out by my orders to greet you."

I nodded easily, though unease lingered in my mind.

Half a mile from the gates, on a rising stretch of ground, the companies formed. It was a splendid sight: each company, about three hundred strong, charged swiftly up the rise with flashing spears and waving plumes to take their appointed places. By the time we reached the slope, twelve such companies—three thousand six hundred men in total—had passed out and arrayed themselves along the road.

We approached the first company in astonishment. They were the most magnificent warriors I had ever seen: men of mature age, mostly veterans of forty, none under six feet tall, many standing six feet three or four. They wore heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers on their heads, similar to those of our guides. Around their waists and beneath their right knees were bound circlets of white ox tails. In their left hands, they carried round shields about twenty inches across, constructed of thin iron plates stretched with milk-white ox-hide.

Their weapons were simple but deadly: a short, heavy two-edged spear with a wooden shaft, the blade six inches wide at its widest point. Not for throwing, but for close-quarters combat like the Zulu *bangwan*, the wound inflicted was terrible. Additionally, each man carried three large, heavy knives, or *tollas*, weighing about two pounds each. One was fixed in the ox-tail girdle, the other two attached to the back of the shield. The Kukuanas could cast these with great accuracy to fifty yards, hurling a volley as they charged to close quarters.

Each company stood still as bronze statues until we were opposite. At a signal from the commanding officer, distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak, every spear was raised, and from three hundred throats sprang forth the royal salute of "Koom." Once we passed, the company formed behind us, following toward the kraal until the entire regiment of the "Greys"—named for their white shields—the crack corps of the Kukuana people, marched in our rear with a tread that shook the ground.

Branching off from Solomon's Great Road, we reached the wide fosse surrounding the kraal, a mile in circumference, fenced with a strong palisade of tree trunks. At the gateway, a primitive drawbridge was lowered to admit us. The kraal was excellently laid out. A wide pathway ran through the center, intersected at right angles by others, cutting the huts into square blocks, each quartering a company. The huts were dome-shaped, built of wattle and beautifully thatched with grass, but unlike Zulu huts, they featured doorways wide enough for men to walk through. They were larger, surrounded by verandas about six feet wide, paved with powdered lime trodden hard.

Along the central pathway, hundreds of women stood ranged, drawn by curiosity to view us. For a native race, they were exceedingly handsome: tall, graceful, with wonderfully fine figures. Their hair was short and curly rather than woolly, their features often aquiline, and their lips not unpleasantly thick. What struck us most was their quiet, dignified air. They were as well-bred as the *habituées* of a fashionable drawing-room, differing sharply from Zulu women or the Masai beyond Zanzibar. Their curiosity brought them out, yet they allowed no rude expressions of astonishment or savage criticism to escape their lips as we trudged wearily before them. Even when old Infadoos surreptitiously pointed out the crowning wonder of Poor Good's "beautiful white legs," the women merely fixed their dark eyes upon this snowy loveliness. It was quite enough for Good, who was modest by nature.

Reaching the center of the kraal, Infadoos halted at the door of a large hut surrounded by a circle of smaller ones.

"Enter, Sons of the Stars," he said in a magniloquent voice. "Deign to rest awhile in our humble habitations. A little food shall be brought to you, so that ye may have no need to draw your belts tight from hunger; some honey, some milk, an ox or two, and a few sheep; not much, my lords, but still a little food."

"It is good," I said. "Infadoos, we are weary with travelling through realms of air; now let us rest."

We entered the hut, finding it amply prepared for our comfort. Couches of tanned skins awaited us, and water was placed for washing.

Soon, shouting erupted outside. Stepping to the door, we saw a line of damsels bearing milk, roasted mealies, and honey in a pot. Behind them, youths drove a fat young ox. We received the gifts, and one of the young men dexterously cut the ox's throat. In ten minutes, it was dead, skinned, and jointed. The best meat was cut for us, while the rest, in the name of our party, I presented to the warriors around us, who took it and distributed the "white lords' gift."

Umbopa began to boil our portion in a large earthenware pot over an outdoor fire, assisted by an extremely prepossessing young woman. When the meal was nearly ready, we sent a message to Infadoos and Scragga, the king's son, inviting them to join us.

They arrived shortly, sitting on small stools, for the Kukuanas generally do not squat like the Zulus. They helped us dine. The old gentleman was affable and polite, but I noted that the young one regarded us with doubt. Like the rest, he had been overawed by our white appearance and perceived magic; yet, discovering that we ate, drank, and slept like ordinary mortals, his awe was wearing off, replaced by a sullen suspicion that made me feel uncomfortable.

During the meal, Sir Henry suggested we inquire if our hosts knew anything of his brother's fate. However, I thought it wiser to remain silent; explaining a relative lost from "the Stars" was difficult.

After supper, we produced our pipes. The act filled Infadoos and Scragga with astonishment; the Kukuanas were unacquainted with the divine delights of tobacco smoke. Though the herb was grown extensively among them, they used it only as snuff, failing to identify it in its new form.

I asked Infadoos when we would depart. Delighted to learn that preparations were made for us to leave the following morning, I learned that messengers had already been sent to inform King Twala of our coming.

Twala was at his principal place, known as Loo, preparing for the great annual feast scheduled for the first week of June. At this gathering, all regiments, except certain garrison detachments, would parade before the king, followed by the great annual witch-hunt.

We were to start at dawn. Infadoos, who would accompany us, expected we would reach Loo on the night of the second day, barring accidents or swollen rivers.

With this information, our visitors bade us good-night. Having arranged to watch turn and turn about, three of us flung ourselves down to sleep the sweet sleep of the weary, while the fourth sat up on the lookout for possible treachery.
