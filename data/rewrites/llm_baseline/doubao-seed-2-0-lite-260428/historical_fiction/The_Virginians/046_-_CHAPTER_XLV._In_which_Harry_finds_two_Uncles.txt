Long years moving through the world have put a vast parade of characters before my eyes, but I confess I have met scarcely one of that stock figure so common to plays and novels: the unalloyed religious hypocrite, who preaches piety from dawn till dusk and does not believe a single word of his own sermons—the infidel in broad brim and sable gown, exhorting, condemning, and blessing his neighbors without any real faith in heaven or fear of hell. Look at the procession trundling to Sunday evening services: the hobnail boots clumping down the lane, the rustling maidservants in new ribbons trailed by grinning young apprentices, the neat little lines of schoolboys, the trim young maidens and staid matrons clutching glistening prayer-books, all stepping to the chime of the chapel bell (even as the nearby Ebenezer congregation, a sea of great bonnets, pattens, and propped-up umbrellas, gathers under flaring gas-lamps). How many of those assembled are hypocrites, do you suppose? The maid is very likely thinking of her sweetheart; the grocer turns over in his head how to secure a much-needed shipment of sugar and whether the County Bank will accept his next note; the head schoolboy crams Latin verses he has not yet learned for Monday's exercise; the psalm clerk whose voice drones through the service is fretting over his daughter's secret trouble, scarcely aware of the words he recites; the very moment the parson bows his face to his prayer cushion, he may well be thinking of a bill that falls due on Monday. None of these people are heavenly-minded: they are of the world, worldly, their feet still planted firmly on its soil, but they are not hypocrites, not by half. Most people stow their religion away in a handy mental lock-up, a sort of valuable tonic they bring out only when life's ills strike. And everyone is eager to administer that tonic to their neighbor, same as the old 18th century country ladies, every one of whom kept a still-room and medicine chest stocked with pills, powders, and cordials to dose every villager round about.

My Lady Warrington, for her part, took full charge of both the consciences and the digestions of her husband's tenants and entire household, and tended to the spiritual and physical health of the servants' hall as a sacred personal duty. Heaven alone knows if her prescriptions, spiritual or medicinal, were always sound—but she dispensed both with unshakable faith in her own absolute authority, and all her household swallowed them down obediently. She was fully convinced she was one of the most virtuous, self-denying, wise, and learned women alive, and dinned that opinion so incessantly into the ears of everyone around her that a great many of them soon came to believe it too.

At Sir Miles Warrington's dinner table, the sideboard groaned under so much glittering silver plate, and so many liveried footmen circulated through the room, that it took a sharp observer to notice the ale poured from the massive silver tankards was thin, and the grandest silver serving dish on the table held nothing but a single plain joint of mutton. When Sir Miles toasted the King, smacking his lips over his glass, he eyed the wine and his assembled guests as if he were drinking ambrosia itself. He leaned over to ask Harry if Virginia ever produced port half so fine, and declared this vintage was nothing next to the cellars waiting for him in Norfolk. He praised the wine so lavishly that Harry almost convinced himself it was delicious, squinting into his own glass to try and spot all the wonderful qualities his uncle swore were hidden in the ruby liquid.

Just as so many well-to-do families of the era did, the Warringtons had their two celebrated paragons of young womanhood. The two grown daughters, Lady Warrington informed Harry in all confidence, were the finest creatures alive: the elder, Flora, was the realm's most renowned beauty, already betrothed to the kind, estimable Tom Claypool; the younger, Dora, was a genius and an angel, forever determined to refine Harry's wits and uplift his morals. She played the harpsichord for him for hours (though Harry privately thought her performance rather out of tune for a heavenly creature), and was never short of advice, instruction, and conversation—far too much of it, Harry thought, who would far rather have passed his time with his quiet little young cousin, who reminded him of Fanny Mountain back on the Virginia plantation. That small girl, though, was always sent off to the nursery as soon as the dinner ended. Flora drifted off to her own pursuits, Lady Warrington left to visit her poor tenants or scribble her lengthy, voluminous religious correspondence, and Sir Miles dozed off in his armchair, leaving Dora to keep their young cousin company. Harry found the calm, steady rhythm of the Warrington household a pleasant refuge from the raucous gambling halls and dissipated society he had been mixing with in London, and he could not have asked for a warmer welcome: the doors were thrown open to him at every hour of the day, and if Dora was not at home to greet him, Flora always was. Within days of his first visit, he and his little young cousin Miles had arranged to ride a galloping match in the park, and Harry, generous to a fault with everyone around him, was already planning to buy the boy a new horse far finer than the scraggy little beast he was currently riding—when disaster struck, and every last one of Harry's "coaches and horses" came to a sudden, crashing halt.

Though Sir Miles had once imagined Virginia was a tiny island somewhere far across the sea, the Warrington ladies were far better schooled in geography, and endlessly eager to hear every detail of Harry's home and native estate. Harry, for his part, was more than happy to talk. He described the sprawling bounds of his land, the long rivers that edged his property, the rich crops his fields bore. He had dabbled in surveying with a friend back in his boyhood, and he drew them a full map of his home county, dotting it here and there with fine, bustling towns that in truth were little more than rough log huts—though for the honor of his homeland, he saw no harm in making them look as handsome as possible. He marked the Potomac, the James River, the wharves where his mother's tobacco ships loaded their cargo to sail out to the sea. In truth, his whole estate was as large as an entire English county. He did not brag overmuch about his holdings, but to see the handsome young man in his velvet suit and silver lace, bent over his map, pointing out wooded hills and broad forests, anyone would have thought he was a traveling prince describing the vast dominions of his mother the queen. At moments, he almost fancied himself one. He owned miles of land for every single acre the average English gentleman could call his own.

Not just Dora hung on his every word—even the beautiful Flora bowed her fair head and listened with full attention. After all, what was young Tom Claypool, heir to a paltry five thousand Norfolk acres, loud and clumsy in his great mud boots, next to this charming American prince who spoke so easily of lords and macaronis, of March and Chesterfield, Selwyn and Bolingbroke? Even Dora, for all her supposed angelic temper, grew sharp and jealous, teasing Flora for shamelessly flirting with their handsome cousin. Tom Claypool in his gaudy red waistcoat would sit dumb and stammering before Harry in his fine ruffles and lace, spinning stories of London's highest society. Lady Warrington grew fonder of Harry by the day, treating him as her very own son. She fretted endlessly over the spiritual welfare of the native tribes and the enslaved people on his Virginia lands, desperate to help the sainted Madam Esmond (as she insisted Harry's mother must be) with her missionary work. Her pious butler and housekeeper were utterly charmed by Harry's servant Gumbo, gushing over his sweet singing and devout demeanor. "Ah Harry, Harry, you wild, dear boy!" Lady Warrington would exclaim, lifting her eyes to the ceiling in her usual pious pose. "Why did you not come to us sooner, and waste all those years running around with spendthrifts and vain, wicked company? But it is not too late! We must reclaim you, dear Harry! Mustn't we, Sir Miles? Mustn't we Dora? Mustn't we, Flora?" All three of the women nodded, eyes turned heavenward, all determined to rescue their dear prodigal—each competing to be the one to win him over first. Dora watched Flora jealously every time Harry spoke, and when the girls were out of the room, Lady Warrington spoke to him more tenderly and seriously still, promising to be a second mother to him in the absence of his own admirable parent. She pressed a new hymn-book into his hands, and kissed him gently on the forehead, every action motivated, she was certain, by nothing but pure, selfless love, tenderness, and religious devotion for her dear, wayward, amiable nephew.

While all these sentimentalities were unfolding within the Warrington walls, Harry's affairs out of doors had reached the direst of straits. The fortune that had favored him for so many weeks suddenly abandoned him entirely, and just a handful of days was all it took to kick down his entire heap of winnings. We would never dare insinuate that his own kinsman Lord Castlewood acted dishonorably over the green baize table, or stripped Harry of his fortune across a couple of private afternoon gaming sessions, out of respect for his lordship's good name—but there was no denying that every last legitimate penny Harry had owned was gone. Lord Castlewood would have gambled with him for his entire future inheritance too, if Harry had not finally thrown up his hands, too beaten to play another hand. Remembering that he still held one small remaining sum, set aside expressly for the poor clergyman Sampson, Harry risked that at the tables too. That last scrap of capital vanished along with all the rest, and Fortune fluttered off into the storm, leaving the luckless young adventurer almost stripped bare on the shore.

When a man is young, hearty, and generous as Harry was, the loss of money does not weigh on him overmuch. He would sell his horses and his carriage, scale back his entire household and expenses. If he needed quick cash, was not his wealthy Aunt Bernstein his willing banker? Or his kinsman Lord Castlewood who had won so heavily from him? Or his kind Uncle and Aunt Warrington, who talked endlessly of virtue and benevolence and swore they loved him as a son? He could call on any of them, whenever he pleased. For the moment, though, Sampson's tale of his landlord's desperate poverty weighed on his mind, and to raise a quick sum to help the clergyman, he gathered up all his trinkets and carried them to a pawnbroker's shop in St. Martin's Lane. That pawnbroker was a relative and business partner of the very Mr. Sparks of Tavistock Street from whom Harry had bought the diamond-handled enamelled repeater he had intended to give as a gift to his friends back at Oakhurst. When Mr. Sparks dropped by his partner's shop a short time after Harry had pawned his goods, he spotted the repeater at once, and burst into a stream of angry, unkind remarks about Harry that I will not repeat here, to avoid being accused of speaking far too plainly.

A man who counts a pawnbroker among his friends is sure to count a bailiff or two among his acquaintances, and those bailiffs' officers serve the law with perfect impartiality: they will lay hands as readily on the shoulder of the fiercest army captain with his epaulets glinting as on the finest dressed macaroni in all of London. The very same men who had arrested Lady Maria at Tunbridge Wells were soon set on Harry. They easily wheedled the information out of garrulous Gumbo, who was off courting the maid from the Lambert household next door, that his master was staying regularly at Sir Miles Warrington's house in Hill Street. The two bailiffs' men settled down to wait outside the door, while Harry sat inside blissfully enjoying the warm, loving family gathering around his aunt's dinner table. That evening had never been more pleasant: Sir Miles was more jovial than ever, Lady Warrington more gentle, gracious, and affectionate, Flora looked unusually lovely, and Dora was far more amiable than Harry had ever seen her. At parting, my lady pressed both his hands, and called down a shower of blessings on his head from the ceiling above them. "Hang it, nephew!" Sir Miles boomed in his heartiest tone. "When I was your age I would have stolen a kiss from two such pretty girls as Do and Flo, my own flesh and blood too! Don't look so bashful, oddsfish! It's the boy blushing, not the girls!" "Papa!" the two virgins cried out in unison. "Sir Miles!" his august wife chided him at the very same instant. But before anyone could protest further, the harmless little osculatory transaction was complete, and blushing Harry had pressed a chaste kiss to the damask cheek of Flora, then to that of Dora.

As Harry followed his uncle down the stairs, Lady Warrington turned to her daughters, staring fixedly up at the ceiling cornice as usual. "What precious qualities your dear cousin has!" she said. "What shrewdness mingled with his simple sweetness, what a fine, genteel manner—though of course I set very little store by mere worldly elegance. What a dreadful pity that such a fine soul should ever be lost! We must rescue him, my loves. We must draw him away from those wicked companions and those horrible Castlewoods—not that I would ever speak ill of my neighbors, of course. But I shall hope, I shall pray, that he may be pulled free from his evil courses!" She glowered at the cornice with such determined ferocity that the girls, their own eyes fixed wistfully on the door behind which their interesting young cousin had just vanished, did not dare speak.

His uncle walked Harry all the way to the street door, calling "God bless you, my boy!" with the utmost affection, squeezing Harry's hand and repeating his valuable benediction one more time at the threshold. The moment the door clicked shut behind Harry, and the light from the hall within had fully illuminated his face and figure, the two men who had been loitering on the opposite side of the street stepped forward rapidly. One pulled a scrap of legal paper from his pocket, clapped a hand firmly on Harry's shoulder, and declared him his prisoner. A hackney coach was waiting by the curb, and before he could even cry out in surprise, poor Harry was being carted off to Chancery Lane, to spend the night locked up in a squalid debtor's spunging-house on Cursitor Street. Only think of it: the son of Madam Esmond, the Virginian prince, his shoulder prodded by a ragged bailiff's follower, locked in a London jail. I do not envy our young prodigal his rest on that dismal night. My beloved young friends, let us not waste pity on him, now that he is down. Imagine the sharp stings of remorse keeping him awake on his dingy, scratchy pillow, the raucous jollifications of other hardened inmates ringing in his ears from the room next door where they sit carousing, the hot rage, shame, and utter discomfiture churning in his gut. After all, you good, honest young gentlemen have never indulged in extravagance or folly, or had to wake to pay the bitter reckoning of your own remorse, have you?
