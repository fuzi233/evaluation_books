It seems the entire human race is hopelessly vain, and women, in particular, seem to have taken it as their life's mission. Men are hardly behind them, and children? Children are the undisputed champions of the vanity game. Right now, one of these little tyrants is hammering on my legs, demanding my verdict on her latest footwear. To be honest, I see little merit in them; they lack symmetry, curve, and dignity, possessing a lumpy, indefinable quality that suggests they might be on the wrong feet entirely. But honesty is not what she desires. She wants adoration. So, I pour out a torrent of praise so excessive it feels like a crime, just to keep this self-important cherub satisfied. I once tried the "conscientious friend" approach, hoping to offer some gentle guidance on her behavior. She asked, "Wot oo tink of me? Oo peased wi' me?" Seizing the moment, I replied, "No, I am not pleased with you." I reminded her of her morning's exploits: waking the house at five, knocking over a water jug at seven, attempting to bathe the cat at eight, and sitting on her father's hat at nine-thirty-five.

Did she take this to heart? Did she resolve to become a better person? Not a bit of it. She immediately erupted into a howl of abuse, declaring me a "bad unkie" and threatening to tell her mother. Since that fiasco, I have learned my lesson. When asked for my opinion, I now reserve my true thoughts for my diary and offer boundless, ridiculous admiration for everything she does, regardless of its merit. She approves, nods sagely, and trots off to broadcast my praise to the household, using it as currency to negotiate for extra biscuits. There she goes now, staring rapturously at her toes, murmuring "pittie"—a towering monument of two-foot-ten inches of pure conceit.

They are all alike. I recall sitting in a London garden when a shrill voice from a top-floor window cried out to a neighbor, "Gamma, me dood boy... me dot on Bob's knickiebockies." Even animals are guilty. I once saw a Newfoundland dog in Regent's Circus gazing into a shop window mirror with a smug satisfaction that would make a vestry meeting jealous. At a farmhouse during a festival, they placed a flower garland on a cow's head. That beast strutted around all day like a schoolgirl in a new dress; when they removed the wreath, she became sulky and refused to be milked until it was replaced.

Cats are nearly as vain as humans. I have known one walk out of a room simply because a visitor made a slight remark about felines, yet purr for an hour over a neat compliment. Dogs, conversely, are "Hail, fellow, well met" to everyone. You can slap a dog's head and roll him over, and he'll lie there gaping, utterly unbothered. Try that with a cat, and she will never speak to you again. To win a cat's affection, you must start with "Poor pussy," add a tone of feigned sympathy, and wait for her to rub her nose against you. Only then can you venture to tickle her head, at which point she will likely scratch your legs, and suddenly, friendship is achieved.

The poem says, "I love little pussy... And if I don't tease her she'll do me no harm." The last two lines reveal the cat's true philosophy: goodness is defined solely by stroking, patting, and feeding. This narrow view of virtue is shared by everyone. A good man is simply one who is good to *us*; a bad man is one who doesn't do what we want. Deep down, we all believe the universe was created as an appendage to ourselves. We are the center of our own cosmos. You were born to read my books and pay for them; I was born to write them for you. The stars exist merely to make the night sky interesting for our stargazing, and the moon is a convenient backdrop for our romantic flirts.

We are all like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, convinced the sun rises specifically to hear us crow. Vanity is the engine of the world. I doubt a single man has ever existed without it; a man devoid of vanity would be a fascinating but utterly unlovable specimen, a hero to be admired from a distance but never embraced as a brother. We connect not through our noble virtues, which often isolate us, but through our follies. In our vanity, we find common ground. From the Indian hunter proud of his scalps to the European general swollen with medals; from the Chinese gleeful at his pigtail to the "professional beauty" enduring torture to shrink her waist; from the ragged girl strutting with a tattered parasol to the princess with a four-yard train; from the country lad winning laughs with cheap jokes to the statesman savoring polite applause; from the African trader exchanging ivory for glass beads to the Christian maiden selling herself for a title—all march and fight beneath the banner of vanity.

Yes, vanity is the motive power of humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning. To succeed, you must flatter everyone, high and low, rich and poor. Praise a man's virtues and another's vices; compliment everyone on everything, especially what they lack. Admire the handsome for their looks, the foolish for their wit, and the boorish for their breeding. Your discernment will be hailed as genius.

Everyone can be swayed by flattery. The "belted earl"—whatever that strange creature may be—is easily won over, just as is the duchess, the poet, or the plowboy. Though butter sinks better into wheaten bread than oaten cakes, making the poet easier to please than the farmer. Love itself is fueled by flattery. As a witty Frenchman once noted (though I can't recall his name, a tragedy for a writer), filling someone with love for themselves causes that love to overflow into yours. Tell a girl she is more angelic than an angel, more graceful than a goddess, more fairy-like than Titania, and more beautiful than Venus. Her trusting heart will believe every word.

These dear little souls claim to hate flattery, yet when you tell them, without exaggeration, that they are the most beautiful, good, and divine creature on earth, they will smile approvingly and lean against your shoulder, calling you a "dear good fellow." Imagine, however, a man attempting love based on strict truth! Gazing into a mistress's eyes and whispering that she is merely "not bad-looking," holding up her hand to note its "light drab color shot with red," and telling her her nose is "rather pretty for a turned-up one." What a hopeless case he would be compared to the man who calls her face a "fresh blush rose" and her eyes "evening stars."

Flattery comes in many forms. Some like it thick and heavy, applied with a trowel. Others require delicate suggestion. Many prefer it wrapped in an insult—"Oh, you are a perfect fool, you'd give your last sixpence to a beggar." Still others only accept it through a third party, where Friend B tells Person A that C thinks A is splendid, begging B not to mention it directly. And those stubborn John Bulls who claim to "hate flattery"? Simply flatter them on their lack of vanity, and they are yours to command.

Ultimately, vanity is as much a virtue as a vice. While copybooks condemn it, it is a passion capable of driving us to greatness. Ambition is merely ennobled vanity. We seek fame and praise, leading us to write great books, paint masterpieces, and toil in laboratories. We strive for wealth not just for comfort, but to have bigger houses, finer horses, and more servants, to dress our families in absurdly expensive clothes, and to host lavish dinners we never eat. In doing so, we aid the world's progress, spreading commerce and civilization.

Do not despise vanity. Honor it. It is the highest form of self-respect. It is not limited to the fashionable; the peacock and the eagle are vain, as are snobs and heroes. Come, my young brothers, let us be vain together. Let us be vain of brave hearts and working hands, of truth and purity. Let us be too vain to stoop to meanness, too vain for petty envy, too vain to speak unkindly. Let us pride ourselves on being upright gentlemen in a world of knaves. Let us be vain of our high thoughts and noble deeds.
