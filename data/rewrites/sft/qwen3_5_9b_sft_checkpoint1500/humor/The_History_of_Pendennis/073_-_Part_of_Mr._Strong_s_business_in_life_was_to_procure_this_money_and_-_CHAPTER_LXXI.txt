The dinner had barely been cleared when Arthur Pendennis stumbled back into the room, his face a mask of ghastly pallor. Lady Rockminster, whose patience was as thin as her temper, immediately launched into a tirade about his tardiness. But Laura, watching her cousin, saw the terror in his eyes and cut her aunt short with a gasp of tender alarm. "Arthur!" she cried, "What has happened? Are you ill?"

To drown out the questions, Arthur downed a massive bumper of sherry, his hands trembling. "I have heard news so extraordinary I must tell you later," he stammered, casting a nervous glance at the servants. "Please, stop kicking the floor under the table," Lady Rockminster chided, pointing out that Arthur had trampled on her dog, Fido, and knocked over his saucer, whereas Mr. Warrington managed to keep his boots perfectly silent. "And you," she added, addressing the group, "you've been tramping and beating the carpet like madmen."

By dessert, the atmosphere was heavy with unspoken dread. "This dinner has been positively stupid," Lady Rockminster declared, tossing her head with theatrical indignance. "I suspect something grave has occurred, and you wish to speak to Laura. I shall retire to take a nap; I doubt whether I shall drink any tea. Good night, Mr. Warrington. Do come again, but let us hope there is no business to discuss." With a flourish of dignity, the old lady departed, followed by George and the others. As Warrington rose to bid Laura farewell, Arthur stopped him. "Pray, stay, George," he urged, his voice thick with agitation. "You must hear this news and offer your counsel. I am lost without it."

"It concerns Blanche, Arthur," Laura whispered, her heart hammering against her ribs, her cheeks flushing a color she had never known before.

"Yes," Pen confirmed, leaning forward, "and it is the strangest story imaginable. When I left you to visit my uncle's lodgings, I found his ancient servant, Morgan, standing guard at the door. He informed me that he and his master had parted ways that very morning; my uncle had fled to this hotel. Morgan insisted I enter, claiming he possessed information of vital importance. It turns out the scoundrel has hoarded a fortune while serving my uncle and is now a millionaire. He revealed a terrible secret: Blanche's father is not dead. The marriage between Clavering and the Begum was void."

"And therefore Blanche is her grandfather's heir?" Warrington reasoned.

"Perhaps," Pen said grimly, "but consider the father! Amory is an escaped convict. Clavering knows it; my uncle knows it. They used this fact to blackmail Clavering into yielding his parliamentary seat to me."

"But Blanche doesn't know," Laura interjected softly. "Nor does Lady Clavering?"

"No," Pen replied, his voice dropping. "Blanche believes her father drowned in New South Wales, a convict, not a captain as she was led to believe. Her grandmother tells me they were miserable together and that Amory was a bad man. Lady Clavering wept when she told me this, saying it was hard for a woman to admit being glad her husband was dead, and that twice in her life she had chosen terribly. Now, what is to be done? If Amory returns, Morgan will use the secret to extort money from us all. The villain assumed I knew, threatening me with an annuity and demanding I buy a seat in Parliament through Clavering. Was my uncle mad to conspire in this treachery? To treat my mother's son like a pawn?"

"I cannot imagine it, dear Arthur," Laura said, seizing his hand and pressing a kiss to it. "Her father's shame is not Blanche's fault. If you were married, would you desert her because she is innocent? You are pledged to her. If she suffers, surely you would console her? Mother would have done the same." She wrapped her arms around him, burying her face in his chest.

"Our mother is an angel to God," Pen sobbed, his composure cracking. "And you are the dearest, the kindest woman. Teach me my duty. Pray for me that I may fulfill it with a pure heart. God bless you, my sister!"

"Amen," groaned Warrington, his head bowed in his hands. "She speaks truly. That girl cannot do wrong." Indeed, she looked like an angel, radiant and kind. After a moment, she placed her small hand on the table. "And now," she said, looking at the men, "we shall have some tea."

Before this mundane resolution could settle, a servant announced that Major Pendennis had returned to the hotel and awaited his nephew. Laura exchanged a worried glance with Pen. *Behave,* her eyes seemed to say, *hold fast to the right.* She excused herself, retreating to her chambers, leaving the two gentlemen alone. Warrington, usually indifferent to tea, regretted the impending beverage deeply. *Why couldn't old Pendennis have arrived an hour later?* he mused. *An hour sooner or later matters little. The hour has struck. The inevitable moment to say farewell has come.*

As Arthur walked through the hotel corridors, his anger swelled. He was furious that the old man was using him as a puppet, compromising his honor. Yet, when he met the Major, the old gentleman's hand was cold and shaking. He coughed miserably by the fire, complaining of Morgan's drunken insolence and ingratitude, cursing the impudent scoundrel who had stolen ten thousand pounds and deserved hanging. The Major spoke of his failing health, of packing his library, and of a Bible belonging to Arthur's poor mother that he wished the younger man to keep. His age and kindness slowly disarmed Arthur's rage, filling him with guilt.

Arthur prepared to deliver the blow. "I visited your lodgings at Bury Street, Uncle," he began slowly. "I spoke with Morgan."

"The cat is out of the bag," the Major muttered, his face flushing. "A devilishly awkward affair, eh?"

"He told me a story that shocked me to the core," Arthur said. "Concerning Miss Amory's father, Lady Clavering's first husband, and his true identity."

"Hem," the Major rubbed his nose nervously. "I've suspected this circumstance for some time."

"I wish I had known sooner, or not at all," Arthur said gloomily. "There is no reason for the women to know, and I shall never tell them. Morgan intends to trade on this secret and has offered me ransom. I find myself engaged to a convict's daughter."

"That is exactly why I withheld it from you, my dear boy," the Major said, his tone shifting to a desperate cheerfulness. "Miss Amory is not a convict's daughter. She is the legitimate daughter of Lady Clavering, worth fifty or sixty thousand pounds. Her father-in-law, a reputable Baronet, approves of the match and yields his seat to her husband. What could be simpler?"

"Is it true, sir?" Arthur asked, his voice hollow.

"Begad, yes! Of course it's true. Amory is dead. The first sign of life he shows, he dies. We have him at a deadlock, like the fellow in the play 'The Critic.' A monstrous witty man, Sheridan, and so was his son. By Gad, when I was at the Cape..."

Arthur interrupted him sharply. "If you had told me this sooner, you would have spared us both immense pain. I am bound by an engagement I cannot honorably uphold."

"No, begad, we've fixed you!" the Major exclaimed, oblivious. "A man fixed to a seat in Parliament and a pretty girl with an income is fixed to no bad thing."

"Great Heavens, sir!" Arthur cried, his eyes wide with horror. "Are you blind? Can't you see?"

"See what, young gentleman?"

"See that I would rather join my father-in-law at the hulks than accept a parliamentary seat bought by Clavering's silence! See that I would rather take the spoons off the table than marry a felon's daughter for money! You have condemned me to poverty and shame, cursed my career when it might have flourished! We have played a guilty game, Uncle, and been overreached. In marrying for money and a seat, I degraded myself and prostituted my honor."

"What in Heaven's name do you mean?" shrieked the old man.

"I mean there is a limit to baseness I cannot cross!" Arthur shouted. "I have felt for months that my conduct was wicked and sordid. I am rightly punished by the loss of both the fortune and the seat."

"How do you mean you lose them?" the Major screamed. "Who the devil will take your fortune or seat away? Clavering shall give you every shilling of eighty thousand pounds!"

"I will keep my promise to Miss Amory, sir."

"And her parents shall keep theirs to you."

"Not so, please God," Arthur answered firmly. "I have sinned, but Heaven help me, I will sin no more. I will release Clavering from the bargain made without my knowledge. I will take no money from Blanche beyond her original settlement, and I will strive to make her happy. You have brought this misery upon me, sir, but you knew no better. I forgive you."
