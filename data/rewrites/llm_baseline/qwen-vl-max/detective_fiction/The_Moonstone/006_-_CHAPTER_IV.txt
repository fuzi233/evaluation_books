I regret to keep you waiting, but I must tell this story as it happened—step by step, and without skipping over what might seem unimportant. A quiet old man sitting in a sunlit yard may not hold your attention long, but patience now will bring us closer to the truth. Mr. Franklin Blake is expected later today, and until he arrives, I must continue.

Just after my daughter Penelope left me, I was startled by the clatter of dishes from the servants’ hall—a sure sign dinner was ready. As I usually eat in my own room, I had no part in their meal, except wishing them all good appetites before settling back into my chair. I was about to stretch my legs when Nancy, the kitchen-maid, burst out of the house. She nearly collided with me, and I noticed at once that her face was sulky—a look I never allow to pass unchallenged.

“What’s wrong?” I asked, stepping aside. “Why are you turning your back on dinner?”

She tried to brush past without answering. That was enough for me. I rose and took her gently by the ear—a habit I’ve kept since my youth, not out of cruelty, but as a way of showing approval. She’s a sturdy, pleasant girl, and she always responds better to a little firmness than to silence.

“Now then,” I said, “what’s troubling you?”

“Rosanna’s late again,” she muttered. “I’m supposed to fetch her in. All the work falls on me. Let me be, Mr. Betteredge!”

Rosanna Spearman was our second housemaid—new to the household, though not new to sorrow. I felt a certain pity for her, and seeing how irritated Nancy was, I decided to go myself. There was nothing pressing to do, and I thought Rosanna would listen more readily to me than to any other servant.

“Where is she?” I asked.

“At the sands,” Nancy snapped. “She had another fainting spell this morning and begged to go out for fresh air. I’ve no patience with her!”

“Go back to your dinner,” I said. “I’ll go see her.”

Nancy smiled—she always does when she’s pleased—and I gave her a friendly pat under the chin. It’s not improper; it’s just something I’ve done for years.

I took my walking stick and headed toward the beach. But first, let me pause. You must understand the place—the sands—and the woman who haunted them. This isn’t merely a digression; it’s essential to what follows. The Diamond, you see, touches both deeply.

Rosanna had come to us only four months earlier. My lady had visited a reformatory in London, one meant to help women who’d been imprisoned regain their footing in life. The matron introduced her to a young woman named Rosanna Spearman, whose story was so painful I won’t repeat it here. Suffice it to say: she had been a thief, caught in a world where such crimes lead directly to prison. But the matron believed—deeply—that Rosanna was different. Not because she hadn’t sinned, but because she carried herself with dignity, even in disgrace. And so my lady offered her a chance.

Rosanna entered our service quietly, unknown to all but Miss Rachel and myself. I advised my lady on most matters, and when she asked my opinion of Rosanna, I agreed wholeheartedly. No girl could have had a fairer start. She earned her wages like the others, received no special treatment, yet was given kindness and encouragement in private. In return, she worked diligently, humbly, and with care—though she was often weak and prone to sudden fainting spells.

Yet she remained apart. Not hostile, but distant. She didn’t join the other maids in gossip or laughter. When she went out, it was usually alone. She read or sewed while others chattered. Her plain appearance—her shoulders uneven, her face unremarkable—wasn’t cause for envy. But her quietness, her reserve, stirred unease. They accused her of being proud—of “giving herself airs”—though there was no real reason for it. Still, they resented her. And there was something else: a grace in her voice, a stillness in her eyes, that made them feel she belonged somewhere else.

And so she found solace not among people, but in solitude—at the sands.

Our house stands high on the Yorkshire coast, overlooking the sea. We have fine walks in every direction—except one. That one leads through a gloomy fir plantation to a narrow, desolate bay. Between low cliffs, the sandhills slope down to the water, ending in two rocky spits—North and South. Between them lies the Shivering Sand, the worst quicksand along the entire shore. At tide change, the deeps beneath stir, making the surface tremble, ripple, and shiver—as if hundreds of unseen hands were clawing upward from below. The sea rolls in silently, broken by a reef half a mile out, and covers the sand without a sound. No boats come near. No children play there. Even birds avoid it.

It is a lonely, dreadful place. And yet—this was Rosanna’s favorite walk.

So it was that I now walked there to fetch her to dinner.

I saw no sign of her in the firs. But when I emerged onto the beach, there she was—alone, in her straw bonnet and grey cloak, staring out at the quivering sand. She jumped when I approached, turned away.

Not looking me in the eye—another thing I cannot permit—so I turned her gently toward me. And then I saw: she was crying.

My handkerchief—given to me by my lady among six matching ones—was in my pocket. I pulled it out.

“Come sit beside me,” I said. “Let me dry your tears. Then I’ll ask what troubles you.”

At my age, sitting on a sloping beach is no small feat. By the time I was settled, she had already wiped her eyes with her own cheap cambric handkerchief. She sat quietly beside me, wretched but composed.

When comforting a woman, the shortest path is to take her on your knee. I remembered that rule—but Rosanna wasn’t Nancy. She wouldn’t have accepted it.

“Now,” I said, “tell me what has you weeping?”

“About the years gone by,” she said softly. “My past still comes back.”

“Your past is behind you,” I insisted. “Why can’t you forget it?”

She reached up and touched the lapel of my coat. The day before, she had removed a stain with a new cleaning paste. The grease was gone, but a dull patch remained.

“The stain is gone,” she whispered, “but the mark remains. The place shows.”

A remark like that—coming from a servant, pointing to a flaw in my own clothing—stung. Yet something in her eyes—those gentle brown ones—made me feel sorry for her. She looked at me with a kind of reverence, as if I lived a life she could never know: peaceful, honest, untouched by shame.

I couldn’t comfort her. So I did the next best thing.

“Help me up,” I said. “You’re late for dinner. I came to bring you in.”

“You?” she murmured.

“They told Nancy to fetch you,” I said. “But I thought you might prefer a scolding from me.”

Instead of helping me rise, she slipped her hand into mine and squeezed gently. She fought back tears, and succeeded—earning my respect.

“You’re very kind,” she said. “But I don’t want dinner. Let me stay a little longer.”

“What draws you here?” I asked. “Why do you come to this terrible place?”

“Something pulls me,” she said, drawing patterns in the sand. “I try to resist. But I can’t. Sometimes… sometimes I think my grave waits for me here.”

“There’s roast mutton and suet pudding waiting,” I replied sharply. “This is what happens when you dwell on emptiness!”

She didn’t hear. She placed her hand on my shoulder and held me fast.

“I think the place has cast a spell on me,” she whispered. “I dream of it every night. I think of it when I stitch. I know I am grateful—I try to deserve your kindness, and my lady’s trust. But sometimes I wonder… is this life too quiet, too good, for someone like me? After everything I’ve been through…”

She broke off, snatched her hand away, and suddenly pointed at the quicksand.

“Look!” she said. “Isn’t it wonderful? Isn’t it terrible? I’ve seen it dozens of times, and it’s always new.”

I looked. The tide was shifting. The sand began to shiver—slowly at first, then trembling all over, like a living thing struggling beneath.

“What does it look like to *you*?” she asked, gripping my shoulder again.

“It looks,” she breathed, “as if hundreds of people are drowning beneath it—struggling to rise, sinking deeper… Throw a stone in, Mr. Betteredge! See how it swallows it!”

Unsettling talk. An empty stomach feeding on a troubled mind. I opened my mouth to reply—when suddenly a voice rang out from the sandhills.

“Betteredge! Where are you?”

“Here!” I shouted back, unaware of who called.

Rosanna leapt to her feet, staring toward the sound. I was about to stand when I saw a dramatic change in her face. Her cheeks flushed crimson—deeper than I’d ever seen. Her breath caught. She seemed transfixed.

“Who is it?” I asked.

She echoed me: “Oh—*who is it?*”—softly, almost to herself.

I turned. Coming from the hills was a young man—bright-eyed, dressed in a fine fawn-colored suit, gloves and hat to match, a rose in his buttonhole, and a smile that lit the gloom around him.

Before I could rise, he dropped beside me, put an arm around my neck, and hugged me tightly—foreign fashion—until I gasped.

“Dear old Betteredge!” he exclaimed. “I owe you seven-and-sixpence. Now—do you know who I am?”

Lord bless us—here was Mr. Franklin Blake, arriving four hours early!

Before I could speak, he glanced up from me to Rosanna. I followed his gaze.

She was blushing fiercely—now a deeper red—and then, without a word, without a curtsey, she turned and fled, vanishing among the sandhills.

Very unlike her. A more courteous, respectful servant you’d not find.

“That’s an odd girl,” Mr. Franklin said, watching her go. “What does she see in me to surprise her so?”

“I suppose,” I replied, teasing him lightly, “it’s the varnish from foreign parts.”

I record this exchange—not for humor, but as proof that even the sharpest minds can miss what’s plain. Neither Mr. Franklin, with all his travels, nor I, with my years, understood what Rosanna’s reaction truly meant.

She was gone before we saw the last flutter of her grey cloak.

And now you may ask: What of it?

Wait. Read on. For soon you will understand—perhaps as clearly as I did—why Rosanna Spearman’s silence spoke louder than any words.
