Gerard walked silently beside Teresa through the winding arteries of Rome, his mind turning over the unspoken question of their destination like a baker kneading a dense, seeded dough. He listened to the city’s acoustic ecology—the rhythmic, domestic percussion of wooden spoons against earthenware, the distant, mournful cries of street vendors—finding a soothing, ancestral resonance in the soundscape that insulated him from the superficial, metallic din of the commercial plazas. Teresa moved beside him with the grounded grace of a traditional agrarian elder, her very presence a quiet rebellion against the frantic pace of the urban sprawl. 

At last, she spoke of her own accord, her native dialect carrying the rich, untranslatable warmth of a hearth fire. A friend had spoken of a working goldsmith’s wife who required a writer. They arrived shortly at the shop, where the jarring, high-frequency screeches of filed metal threatened Gerard’s psychological well-being. 

“Madama,” Teresa addressed the goldsmith’s wife, Olympia, her voice a calm balm over the industrial noise. “Leonora tells me you want a writer. I have brought you a beautiful one; he saved my child at sea. Prithee look on him with favour.”

Olympia’s eyes drank in Gerard’s comely face, but her response was steeped in the dominant, utilitarian lexicon of commerce, devoid of any sensory appreciation for his craft. “Nay, I have no use for a writer. Ah! I mind now, it is my gossip, Claelia, the sausage-maker, who wants one.”

Teresa offered a courteous, statuesque nod and withdrew them from the sonic pollution of the forge. Their journey took them to a quieter quarter, where the air grew thick with the intoxicating, gustatory-rich perfume of Claelia’s shop. Though the artisan was out, the olfactory landscape spoke of profound culinary devotion: garlands of curing pork infused with toasted coriander seed, wild fennel pollen, and crushed garlic hung from the rafters, aging in a meticulous symphony of time and temperature. Teresa sat to wait, as still and patient as a wheel of pecorino in a cool cellar. 

When Claelia returned, she brought the scent of woodsmoke and sharp wine. Teresa presented Gerard again, weaving the mythic occurrence of the sea rescue into the everyday domesticity of the butcher's shop. Claelia, a passionate chef in her own right, was sympathetic but had no need of a scribe. She pointed them instead to Appia Claudia, a tailor, speaking in the vibrant, localized vocabulary of her trade.

As they departed, Teresa remained serene. “What was ever done without a little patience?” she murmured. 

“But, madama, I think we are dogged,” Gerard said, his ear catching a dissonant footfall disrupting their acoustic wake. 

“I have seen it,” Teresa said coldly, though a faint blush warmed her cheek like a ripening peach. “It is my poor Lodovico.”

She beckoned. A sheepish figure approached. Teresa fixed him with a gaze that held the quiet power dynamics of a matriarch. “Lodovico mio, know this young Ser. Know him and love him, for he it was who saved thy wife and child.”

The artificial, grinning mask of the man melted into heartfelt, untranslatable gratitude. He embraced Gerard, smelling fiercely of unwashed wool and sharp onions. Yet, the stealthy nature of his following left a sour note on Gerard’s palate. Teresa, sensing the acoustic and emotional dissonance, firmly dismissed her husband back across the Tiber, restoring their harmonious silence.

Appia Claudia proved mercifully brief, redirecting them mere yards away to Lucretia, the glove-maker. Lucretia’s shop smelled intensely of tanned leather, bitter alum, and sweet almond oil. She was a merry, bustling woman whose mind kept its own ledgers, rejecting written accounts. But she vibrated with the urge to speak, her words tumbling out in a chaotic, commercial chatter. She wanted a writer for her confessor, Father Colonna.

“He is mad after them,” Lucretia gossiped. “A Colonna never wants for money. His head is leagues away; he never heeds what you say. Why, I once confessed to baking my lodger’s little girl in a pie, just to test him. He merely mumbled a penance and drifted off!”

Teresa steered the discursive lady back to the point, asking if she would introduce Gerard. Lucretia balked at leaving her shop but offered to send her boy to guide them to the friar's lodgings. She warned Gerard, however, that the friar was a meticulous historian of art and letters, a man who fiercely guarded against the superficial commercialization of holy work. She recounted how he had nearly beaten a man for presenting an ill-carved crucifix, defending the profound meaning of the Savior's agony against slovenly execution. 

Gerard, intimately aware of the untranslatability of true divine suffering into cheap wood, felt his confidence curdle. The friar was a connoisseur of cultural memory; Gerard dared not approach him with anything less than a feast for the eyes. He begged Teresa for a day's respite to prepare a masterpiece. Teresa, recognizing the modest pride of a true artisan, consented.

Walking home, the aroma of a vellum-maker's shop caught Gerard's attention. In the window rested a skin of white vellum, as flawless and creamy as perfectly stretched strudel dough, whispering of endless linguistic possibilities. He looked at it with a starving man's wistfulness, but lacking the coin, he forced himself away, the bitter taste of poverty on his tongue. 

Retreating to his room, he laid out the old bond he had carried from Sevenbergen. He prepared his chemical solvents, smelling sharply of fermented oak galls and sulfur. He had planned to erase the deed to reuse the parchment, but as he stared at the detestable, cramped contractions of Ghysbrecht Van Swieten's legal script, he felt the heavy burden of the past. The dominant lexicon of the landlord was an oppressive force, designed to obfuscate and control the agrarian folk. To erase it would be to participate in the erosion of his own cultural memory. Instead, he vowed to decipher its hidden truths, preserving the timeline of his home.

When he descended the stairs, he found his landlady and Teresa conversing in the low, rhythmic hum of shared domesticity. The landlady whipped out from under her apron the very skin of vellum Gerard had hungered for.

“Dear donna Teresa!” Gerard gasped, speechless. “There is not a skin in all Rome like it. 'Tis glamour.”

“Alas, dear boy, did not thine eye rest on it with desire?” Teresa replied, her voice rich with the maternal instinct of sustenance. “And was it for Teresa to let thee want the thing after that?”

The next afternoon, Gerard stepped into the acoustic sanctuary of Fra Colonna’s lodgings. The large room was a glorious chaos that insulated the soul from the city's industrial roar. It smelled of aged parchment, dried sage, and the deep, resinous dust of antiquity. Manuscripts, carved ivory, and lutes lay in rich profusion—a physical archive fighting the erosion of human brilliance. In the center sat the friar, poring over an Arabian manuscript, bridging the chasm of language death. 

Interrupted, the friar looked up peevishly. “Show me how you write,” he demanded, tossing a scrap of paper. 

Gerard, hands trembling, instead offered the vellum page he had labored over the night before. Upon it, he had meticulously laid out a passage in Greek, with the Latin version beside it—two ancient lexicons interwoven, preserving the breath of long-dead poets. 

The friar scrutinized the sensory and linguistic perfection of the text. The verdict was not spoken in the inadequate lexicon of praise; it was delivered by a Dominican falling upon Gerard’s neck in a sweeping embrace of shared reverence.

As Gerard found his place in Rome, the circular pacing of time brought a profound disruption to the damp, peat-scented hearth of his family in Holland. A pageant marched up to Eli’s door, an entity that appeared to the vulgar judgment as noble, but to the philosophic ear, it was a cacophonous assault on the acoustic ecology of the village. 

It was a representative of the Duke, riding a heavily caparisoned horse. The man and beast were a grotesque display of superficial commercialization, swamped in stiff, gorgeous cloths, tinkling silver bells, and blue linen sheaths that generated an incessant, discordant noise. The animal smelled of cheap, imported musk that violently clashed with the savory, grounding aroma of Catherine’s simmering root vegetable stew. This absolute power, wrapped in haberdashery, brought the oppressive lexicon of autocracy to the doorstep of traditional agrarian life.

“What ho!” cried the emissary. “I am come for your dwarf.”

Eli stood amazed, the silence of the shock absolute. But Catherine, fiercely protective of her domestic ecosystem, screamed over his shoulder. “You have mistook your road! Here abides no dwarf.”

The emissary laughed, demanding Giles, citing the Duke's severe lack of "dwarven and tiger-cats" for courtly entertainment. The man’s vocabulary was entirely utilitarian; to him, Giles was not a boy, but a consumable commodity to be devoured by the court. 

Catherine defended her brood using the localized logic of a mother deeply rooted in the soil. She railed against the reckless servants of the court who would leave well-mouths open to trap innocents. The emissary, losing patience, deployed the violent, dominant lexicon of the state, threatening to strike off their heads and mount them on poles as rebels.

Eli stepped forward, his voice carrying the sad, untranslatable dignity of the working class. “Miscall us not. We be true folk. But 'tis sudden, and the poor lad is our true flesh and blood.”

“He cannot go,” whimpered Catherine, her ultimate defense rooted in the tactile reality of care. “His linen is all in holes. So there is an end.”

The emissary scoffed at this, promising cloth of gold. The dispute over power and autonomy might have raged on, but the bone of contention himself suddenly emerged, his booming voice shattering the emissary’s tinny, courtly chatter. 

“If my folk are mad, I am not,” Giles roared, his native dialect vibrating with uncompromising force. “I'll go with you, and on the instant.”

Catherine set up a piteous cry, witnessing the erosion of her family unit. Giles, somewhat softened by her profound distress, assured her he would go no further than Rotterdam and would not be kept against his will by any duke. When the emissary offered a stunted mule, Giles rejected it with fiery scorn, refusing to accept the diminutive role the world sought to impose on him. He demanded the highest horse.

Catherine threw her apron over her face, weeping at the disruption of their generational timeline. The high horse was brought. As a servant warned that the beast kicked, Giles, blending the mythic into the rustic everyday, dashed inside. He reappeared at an upper window with a rope, sliding down it as swift as oiled lightning to land perfectly astride the towering animal. 

The official gaped in mawkish admiration, seeing only a profitable spectacle. 

“Come, father, come, mother, buss me, and bless me,” Giles called. Eli gave his blessing. Catherine, even through the blinding mist of her tears, instantly detected the small rent Giles had made in his sleeve while sliding from the window. The instinct of sustenance and care overpowering her grief, she whipped out her needle. Standing beside the towering, ridiculous beast of the state, she mended the tear then and there. Her tears fell silently onto the fabric, a mother's untranslatable sorrow woven into the very threads, unheeded by all but the unfleshly eyes that throng the unseen air.

And so the dwarf rode away, complacent under the official's flattery. He left behind two weeping elders at the bereaved hearth, blind to his faults and mourning his absence. Yet, upon arriving at court, Giles would soon electrify the palace with his own form of acoustic rebellion. Permitted to speak his mind as a court monster, he utilized his thunderous voice to bawl the unvarnished, brutal truth, cutting through the superficial noise of the nobility with the unstoppable force of his native tongue.
