Pen fretted upon the Tunbridge platform as if the very rails had been laid to torment him. The place had its own little orchestra: the gulls quarrelling over crusts, the porters’ boots keeping time on the boards, the iron-stiff cough of a luggage-truck, and—over all—those modern whistles that split a man’s thought in two like a cleaver through gristle. Half an hour of that was six hours to a heart that had been simmering all day.

At last the evening train came up snorting and panting like a great black kitchen-stove that had eaten too much coal. Pen flung himself into it, and away they went, with the steady clack-clack of wheels that can sound like comfort to the hopeful and like a mortuary bell to the miserable. London received him with its usual stew: damp stone, horse-sweat, fried fish, stale porter, and that thin metallic tang which seems distilled from perpetual motion. Some gentleman forgot his carpet-bag, rushed at a cab, and cried, “Drive as hard as you can go to Jermyn Street.” The cabman—though a hansom-man, and therefore by nature suspicious of promises—said “Thank you” for the coin put in his hand; and Pen, who could have out-run the horse had despair been a spur, bounded up the hotel stairs to Lady Rockminster’s rooms.

Laura was alone in the drawing-room, pale by the lamplight, a book open before her which she was not reading so much as staring through—like a cook who has set a sauce to thicken and, hearing a footstep, forgets whether she has salted it. She looked up. The great moments of life, which poets paint with thunder, come as quietly as the click of a latch; your doom is spoken in two hurried sentences, and the world, with all its plates and puddings and ledgers, goes on as before.

Pen did not wait for greetings. He crossed the room, seized the hand she had half risen to give him—warm, trembling, honest—and dropped to his knees as if the floor had drawn him down.

“I have seen her,” he said quickly. “She has engaged herself to Harry Foker—and—and now, Laura?”

Her fingers answered first; the pressure was a whole vocabulary, a native dialect of the heart which no fashionable language can translate. Her eyes shone; her lips quivered with words that could not get themselves made. Pen’s head sank into her lap, and he sobbed out like a boy who has been starved and suddenly set before bread, “Come and bless us, dear mother.”

And indeed, in that instant, it was as though Helen’s presence—soft as the smell of clean linen warmed by the fire—folded him. Laura’s arms went round him, tender as any mother’s, and the room, a moment before so cold with suspense, seemed filled with a domestic sunshine that did not come from the lamp.

Of course, just then, in came Lady Rockminster, who had been taking her after-dinner nap—like any sensible person who knows the value of resting between courses. She entered her sitting-room and, seeing the tableau, delivered herself of her first remark, which was as much a knock on the head as a remark can be.

“Upon my word, young people!” she cried; and her attendant, Beck, made great wondering eyes over her shoulder, as if she had discovered a new recipe for scandal.

“Beck! leave the room,” Lady Rockminster snapped. “What do you want poking your nose in here?”

Pen sprang up, triumphant, still holding Laura’s hand as if it were the title-deed of his future.

“She is consoling me for my misfortune, ma’am,” he said.

“What do you mean by kissing her hand? I don’t know what you will be next doing.”

Pen, to show that his audacities were evenly distributed, kissed her Ladyship’s hand too. “I have been to Tunbridge,” he said, “and seen Miss Amory; and find on my arrival that—that a villain has transplanted me in her affections.”

“Is that all? Is that what you were whimpering on your knees about?” the old lady cried, her indignation rising like milk in a neglected saucepan. “You might have kept the news till to-morrow.”

“Yes—another has superseded me,” Pen went on, with the air of a tragedian who has been hired for the part of Hamlet and finds himself asked to play a footman. “But why call him villain? He is brave, he is constant, he is young, he is wealthy, he is beautiful.”

“What stuff are you talking, sir? What has happened?”

“Miss Amory has jilted me, and accepted Henry Foker, Esq. I found her warbling ditties to him as he lay at her feet; presents had been accepted, vows exchanged, these ten days. Harry was old Mrs. Planter’s rheumatism, which kept dearest Laura out of the house. He is the most constant and generous of men. He has promised the living of Logwood to Lady Ann’s husband, and given her a splendid present on her marriage; and he rushed to fling himself at Blanche’s feet the instant he found he was free.”

“And so, as you can’t get Blanche, you put up with Laura; is that it, sir?” asked Lady Rockminster, who could cut you a slice of truth thinner than any cook can shave truffle.

“He acted nobly,” Laura said, lifting her head with that gentle firmness which is more terrible than a scold.

“I acted as she bade me,” said Pen.

“Never mind how, Lady Rockminster,” he continued, warming now, “but to the best of my knowledge and power. And if you mean that I am not worthy of Laura, I know it, and pray Heaven to better me; and if the love and company of the best and purest creature in the world can do so, at least I shall have these to help me.”

“Hmm,” said the old lady, looking at them with an air that had softened a shade—as hard butter yields if you only keep it long enough near the fire. “It is all very well; but I should have preferred Bluebeard.”

Pen, to turn the knife from a place where it might cut too deep, bethought him suddenly of his morning’s meeting with Huxter and of Fanny Bolton’s whirlwind of a destiny—matters he had let slip under the immediate boil and bubble of his own emotions. So he told his tale, and told it with that particular relish a man has when he is both happy and anxious to prove himself innocent. He described how Huxter had elevated Fanny to the rank of wife, and how he shook at the thought of his father’s arrival; and Pen, who could season a story as neatly as any sauce, dwelt especially on Fanny’s coquetry, her irrepressible appetite for admiration, her way of tossing her little nets over the whole male species—meaning, without saying: You see, Laura, it wasn’t I that went fishing; I was the fish that escaped.

Laura understood, as women do, the meaning behind the words and the pleading behind the humour.

“If you did any wrong, you repented, dear Pen,” she said; and then, with eyes and blushes that confessed as much as any speech, she added, “and you know that I have no right to reproach you.”

“Hm!” grumbled Lady Rockminster. “I should have preferred Bluebeard.”

“The past is broken away. The morrow is before us,” Pen said. “I will do my best to make your morrow happy, dear Laura.”

He spoke bravely; but his heart was humbled by the nearness of his happiness, awestricken as a poor man is when first he sits down in a great house and is afraid of spoiling the napery. And she—she was shining with that strange, quiet radiance which comes when a long hunger ends, and the soul at last is fed. It seemed to her almost as if Helen stood there indeed, unseen but real: as one smells, in a new kitchen, the faint ghost of the herbs that have been chopped there for years. Laura moved with a different step; she bloomed with a beauty that was not paint nor fashion but something deeper, like bread risen properly—by patience and warmth.

Arthur saw the change. Lady Rockminster saw it too, because old women, if they have any honesty in them, are better historians of young hearts than young hearts are of themselves. Leaning near, she whispered to Laura while Pen was laughing over Huxter’s panic—

“What a sly demure little wretch you have been! And how you have kept your secret!”

“How are we to help the young couple?” Laura asked aloud then; for generous lovers are like generous cooks—they can’t be content with their own full table, but must be planning dinners for other people.

“We must go and see them,” said Pen.

“Of course we must go and see them,” said Laura. “I intend to be very fond of Fanny. Let us go this instant. Lady Rockminster, may I have the carriage?”

“Go now!” cried the old lady. “Why, you stupid creature, it is eleven o’clock at night. Mr. and Mrs. Huxter have got their nightcaps on, I dare say. And it is time for you to go now. Good night, Mr. Pendennis.”

They begged for ten minutes more, as if time could be begged like sugar.

“We will go to-morrow morning, then,” Pen said. “I will come and fetch you with Martha.”

“An earl’s coronet,” Pen added, pleased as a boy with a new toy, “will have a great effect in Lamb Court and Smithfield. Stay—Lady Rockminster, will you join us in a little conspiracy?”

“How do you mean conspiracy, young man?”

“Will you please to be a little ill to-morrow; and when old Mr. Huxter arrives, will you let me call him in? If he is put into a good humour at the notion of attending a baronet in the country, what influence won’t a countess have on him? When he is softened—when he is quite ripe, we will break the secret upon him; bring in the young people, extort the paternal benediction, and finish the comedy.”

“A parcel of stuff,” said Lady Rockminster. “Take your hat, sir. Come away, miss. There—my head is turned another way. Good night, young people.”

And who knows but, nodding and humming to herself as she went away on Laura’s arm, she tasted—like some old country dish remembered for a moment—the flavour of her own earlier days?

With the early morning came Laura and Martha according to appointment; and the desired sensation, let us hope, was produced in Lamb Court—where sensation is not usually on the bill. From thence the three proceeded to Charterhouse Lane, to wait upon Mr. and Mrs. Samuel Huxter.

There was a small domestic hush in Fanny’s lodging—a neatness with pretensions, like a tart set out with more pride than fruit. The sounds were intimate: a kettle’s faint complaint in the grate; a cat padding as silently as a thief; the distant grind of carts in the lane like the sea heard through walls. Laura and Fanny looked at each other with great interest, and with no little emotion on Fanny’s part. She had not seen her “guardian”—as she liked to call Pen in consequence of his bequest—since the event which had united her to Mr. Huxter.

“Samuel told me how kind you had been,” she said. “You were always very kind, Mr. Pendennis. And—and I hope your friend is better, who was took ill in Shepherd’s Inn, ma’am.”

“My name is Laura,” Laura said, blushing. “I am—that is, I was—that is, I am Arthur’s sister; and we shall always love you for being so good to him when he was ill. And when we live in the country, I hope we shall see each other. And I shall be always happy to hear of your happiness, Fanny.”

“We are going to do what you and Huxter have done, Fanny,” Pen cried, unable to keep his joy from skipping. “Where is Huxter? What nice, snug lodgings you’ve got! What a pretty cat!”

While Fanny fluttered answers—half curtsies, half confessions—Laura, in her own mind, was doing what women do best: tasting, judging, revising.

“Well, now really! is this the creature about whom we were all so frightened? What could he see in her? She’s a homely little thing, but such manners! Well, she was very kind to him,—bless her for that.”

Mr. Samuel had gone out to meet his Pa. Mrs. Huxter said the old gentleman was to arrive that day at the Somerset Coffee-house in the Strand; and Fanny confessed she was in a sad tremor about the meeting.

“If his parent casts him off, what are we to do?” she cried. “I shall never pardon myself for bringing ruing on my ’usband’s ’ead. You must intercede for us, Mr. Arthur. If mortal man can, you can bend and influence Mr. Huxter senior.”

Fanny still regarded Pen as a superior being; and Pen, seeing her little tragedy-airs and trepidations, her vanity kneaded with genuine terror, could not help remembering the past—how easily a life may be altered by a few wrong ingredients and a too-hasty heat.

As soon as the interview was over, in came Messrs. Linton and Blades, of course to visit Huxter, bringing with them a fine fragrance of tobacco, like two walking chimneys. They had watched the carriage at the baker’s door and remarked the coronet with awe; and they asked Fanny who that uncommonly heavy swell was who had just driven off. They pronounced the countess “of the right sort.” Then, hearing it was Mr. Pendennis and his sister, they remarked—because envy always keeps its own little dictionary—that Pen’s father was only a sawbones, and that he gave himself confounded airs; they had been in Huxter’s company on the night of his little altercation with Pen in the Back Kitchen, and never forgot a grudge any more than they forgot their pipes.

Returning home through Fleet Street, and as Laura—very prettily and conscientiously—was explaining to Pen’s infinite amusement that Fanny was very well, but that really there was no beauty in her (there might be, but she could not see it), they were delayed near Temple Bar by the usual roar and jostle: wheel against wheel, voices hawking, bells, oaths, the eternal iron clatter of London chewing its own cud.

There they saw young Huxter hurrying back to his bride.

“The governor has arrived,” he panted. “He’s at the Somerset Coffee-house. He’s in tolerable good-humour—something about the railway. But I’ve been afraid to speak about—about that business. Would Mr. Pendennis try it on?”

Pen said he would go at that moment and call upon Mr. Huxter, and see what might be done. Huxter junior would lurk outside while that awful interview took place. The coronet on the carriage inspired his soul also with wonder.

And old Mr. Huxter himself beheld that coronet with delight from the coffee-house window, as he looked down the Strand—always a treat to him, like a grand market-street to a man from a village, full of sights and sounds to carry home and recount.

“And I can afford to give myself a lark, sir,” said Mr. Huxter, shaking Pen’s hand with cordiality. “Of course you know the news? We have got our bill, sir. We shall have our branch line—our shares are up, sir—and we buy your three fields along the Brawl, and put a pretty penny into your pocket, Mr. Pendennis.”

“Indeed!—that is good news,” Pen said; remembering suddenly a letter from Mr. Tatham which had been waiting at Chambers these three days—unopened, because a man in love can let money lie as carelessly as a cook lets the roast drip when the kitchen is on fire.

“I hope you don’t intend to grow rich, and give up practice,” Pen added. “We can’t lose you at Clavering, Mr. Huxter; though I hear very good accounts of your son. My friend, Dr. Goodenough, speaks most highly of his talents. It is hard that a man of your eminence should be kept in a country town.”

“The metropolis would have been my sphere of action, sir,” Mr. Huxter said, surveying the Strand as if the very paving-stones were patients waiting for him. “But a man takes his business where he finds it; and I succeeded to that of my father.”

“It was my father’s, too,” said Pen. “I sometimes wish I had followed it.”

“You, sir, have taken a more lofty career,” said the old gentleman. “You aspire to the senate, and to literary honours. You wield the poet’s pen, sir, and move in the circles of fashion. We keep an eye upon you at Clavering. We read your name in the lists of the select parties of the nobility. Why, it was only the other day that my wife was remarking how odd it was that at a party at the Earl of Kidderminster’s your name was not mentioned. To what member of the aristocracy may I ask does that equipage belong from which I saw you descend? The Countess Dowager of Rockminster? How is her Ladyship?”

“Her Ladyship is not very well,” Pen said; “and when I heard that you were coming to town, I strongly urged her to see you, Mr. Huxter.”

Old Huxter felt that if he had a hundred votes for Clavering he would give them all to Pen on the spot. The machinery of flattery is simple and very old: you oil a man’s vanity, and he runs without squeaking.

“There is an old friend of yours in the carriage—a Clavering lady too—will you come out and speak to her?” Pen asked.

The old surgeon was delighted, and ran out bowing and smiling to the coroneted carriage in the midst of the full Strand, where the sound of fashion is as loud as the sound of wheels. Huxter junior, dodging about like a guilty mouse, saw his father meet Laura; saw her put out her hand; and presently, after a little colloquy with Pen, saw his father actually jump into the carriage and drive away with Miss Bell.

There was no room for Arthur. He came back laughing to the young surgeon and told him where his parent was bound.

During the whole journey Laura coaxed and wheedled and cajoled the old gentleman with an art so domestic and so perfect that one might have called it cookery: she seasoned praise just so, she let the conversation simmer where his pride might soften, she never once let it boil over into offence. And Lady Rockminster, who affected to despise conspiracies and yet could conduct one as if she had invented the trade, achieved the final victory by complimenting him on his skill and professing anxiety to consult him.

What were her Ladyship’s symptoms? Should he meet her Ladyship’s usual medical attendant? Mr. Jones was called out of town? He should be delighted—delighted—to devote his very best energies and experience to her Ladyship’s service. He was so charmed with his noble patient that he wrote home about her to his wife and family, and talked of nothing but Lady Rockminster to Samuel when that youth came in the evening to partake of beefsteak and oyster-sauce and accompany his parent to the play.

There was, Mr. Huxter declared, a simple grandeur, a polite urbanity, a high-bred grace about her Ladyship which he had never witnessed in any woman. Her symptoms did not seem alarming; he had prescribed Spir: Ammon: Aromat: with a little Spir: Menth: Pip: and orange-flower—like a cook brightening a heavy dish with mint and blossom, and feeling proud of the touch.

“Miss Bell,” he said, “seems to be on the most confidential and affectionate footing with her Ladyship. She is about to form a matrimonial connexion. All young people ought to marry. Such were her Ladyship’s words. And the Countess condescended to ask respecting my own family; and I mentioned you by name to her Ladyship, Sam, my boy. I shall look in to-morrow; and if the remedies I have prescribed have the effect I anticipate, I shall probably follow them up by a little Spir: Lavend: Comp:—and so set my noble patient up. What is the theatre most frequented by the—by the higher classes in town, hey, Sam? and to what amusement will you take an old country doctor to-night, hey, sir?”

On the next day, when Mr. Huxter called in Jermyn Street at twelve o’clock, Lady Rockminster had not yet left her room; but Miss Bell and Mr. Pendennis were in waiting to receive him. Her Ladyship, they said, had had a most comfortable night and was getting on as well as possible.

How had Mr. Huxter amused himself? At the theatre? With his son? What a capital piece it was, and how charmingly Mrs. O’Leary looked and sang it! and what a good fellow young Huxter was—liked by everybody, an honour to his profession.

“He has not his father’s manners, I grant you,” Pen said, “or that old-world tone which is passing away from us; but a more excellent, sterling fellow never lived.”

“He ought to practise in the country whatever you do, sir,” Arthur added—“he ought to marry—other people are going to do so—and settle.”

“The very words that her Ladyship used yesterday, Mr. Pendennis,” cried the old gentleman, delighted to find his own new creed echoed. “He ought to marry. Sam should marry, sir.”

“The town is full of temptations, sir,” Pen continued.

The old gentleman’s mind, treacherous as a tongue in a sweet-shop, made an involuntary excursion to that houri, Mrs. O’Leary.

“There is no better safeguard for a young man than an early marriage with an honest affectionate creature.”

“No better, sir, no better.”

“And love is better than money, isn’t it?” Pen asked, with a glance toward Laura that made the question sound like a hymn.

“Indeed it is,” said Miss Bell.

“I agree with so fair an authority,” said the old gentleman, bowing.

“And—suppose, sir,” Pen said, “that I had a piece of news to communicate to you.”

“God bless my soul, Mr. Pendennis! what do you mean?” asked Mr. Huxter, alarmed and pleased at once, like a man who smells something burning and hopes it is not his own dinner.

“Suppose I had to tell you that a young man, carried away by an irresistible passion for an admirable and most virtuous young creature—whom everybody falls in love with—had consulted the dictates of reason and his heart, and had married. Suppose I were to tell you that that man is my friend; that our excellent, our truly noble friend the Countess Dowager of Rockminster is truly interested about him (and you may fancy what a young man can do in life when that family is interested for him); suppose I were to tell you that you know him—that he is here—that he is—”

“Sam married! God bless my soul, sir, you don’t mean that!”

“And to such a nice creature, dear Mr. Huxter,” Laura said softly—another pinch of salt in the right place.

“Her Ladyship is charmed with her,” Pen said—telling, perhaps, almost the first fib of his life in this history; but then it was a fib told as a cook tells you the pie is “light”—for a purpose of peace.

“Married! the rascal, is he?” the old gentleman thought, with that peculiar paternal outrage which is half anger and half pride that the boy has dared to be a man.

“They will do it, sir,” said Pen; and he went and opened the door.

Mr. and Mrs. Samuel Huxter issued thence; and both came and knelt down before the old gentleman.

The kneeling little Fanny—crying and smiling at once, like rain in sunshine—found favour in his sight. There must have been something attractive about her, in spite of Laura’s earlier verdict: some warm domestic quality that cannot be measured by beauty, as the best broth cannot be judged by its colour alone.

“Will never do so any more, sir,” said Sam, in a voice meant to be penitent and sounding chiefly terrified.

“Get up, sir,” said Mr. Huxter.

They got up; and Fanny crept a little nearer, and a little nearer still, and looked so pretty and so pitiful, that somehow the old man found himself kissing the little crying-laughing thing, and feeling—against his own resolutions, like a man caught stealing a biscuit—that he liked it.

“What’s your name, my dear?” he asked, after a minute of this sport.

“Fanny, papa,” said Mrs. Samuel.
