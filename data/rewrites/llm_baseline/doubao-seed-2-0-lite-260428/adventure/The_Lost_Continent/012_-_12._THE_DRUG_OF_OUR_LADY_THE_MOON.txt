The Sun God of Atlantis hung low, his golden rays gilding the roiling black smoke that belched from the burning volcanic mountain far inland. Phorenice twisted the seawater out of her sodden tunic, her short red hair plastered to her sharp, delighted face, and shivered in the lagoon wind.
“Put your back into those paddles, Deucalion,” she ordered, grinning, “and get us through the water-gate before I catch my death. That fat merchant we stole these clothes from would weep to see his fine silks ruined, and I weep a little too—what woman does not grieve to spoil good finery, when there are no other pretty garments to steal nearby?” She glanced at me sidelong, flicking wet hair out of her eyes. “What do you make of my cleverness, to ride out to that fishing village with no guard but you?”
“The Empress can do no wrong,” I quoted the old royal formula with a dry smile.
“And I proved I can swing a blade, too,” she laughed. “I caught you watching, you know, approving how I carved through those rebel fishermen. You were such a stiff, hard man to thaw, Deucalion, but you’re warming fast the more time you spend near me. If this keeps up, we’ll be a pair of simpering rustic sweethearts yet. I’m glad I thought of the trick of luring us out to fight those smelly coastal oafs.”
So that was her whole plan: the unsupervised litter ride, the surprise scrap, all to show off her fighting skill, and test how I carried myself under pressure too. If we were to rule Atlantis side by side as husband and wife, it was only sensible we each knew the full measure of the other’s strength. As a veteran of a hundred battlefields who lived for the clean, brutal joy of a fair fight, I couldn’t even find it in me to be angry. But unbidden, my mind turned to Nais: given the same chance, Nais would have spared those squalid rebel fishermen out of simple pity, while Phorenice had carved through forty of her own hardiest subjects just to satisfy a playful whim.
I pushed the disloyal comparison away, clinging to the vows I had sworn to the High Council on the Sacred Mountain. As I pulled at the oars, driving our small fishing skiff toward the harbour forts, I prayed to the High Gods to grant me the smooth, honeyed speech Phorenice adored, for the good of all Atlantis. The Gods answered. By the time we neared the reef barrier, Phorenice’s eyes were glowing on me, bright as embers.
That stretch of fishing beach was cut off from the open sea by a jagged wall of submerged rock, a maze of reefs only local fishermen could navigate. I had no knowledge of the hidden passes, no skill to leap overboard and heft the skiff over the shallow stones, and I waited for the omen: would the Gods let this impious, brilliant Empress reign, and let me stand at her side as her consort?
The answer came without ambiguity. An earthquake tremor rippled through the lagoon, and a great curling swell rose under our hull, hurling us forward clean over the entire reef barrier. We never scraped a single stone, not even brushing the weed-slick rock edges. I bowed my head over the oars, rowing hard for the harbour. It was clear the High Gods would not take their vengeance on Phorenice, not yet.
The fort sentries were half-asleep in the growing dusk, their drums thudding through the routine watch, ready to let our anonymous fishing craft drift unchallenged. But two senior officers were taking the evening air on a stone parapet turret, recognized our shouts, and before long torches were lowered to confirm our faces, the massive harbour chain was splashed into the water, and a royal galley pulled alongside to tow us in.
Dry, warm clothes were brought for us to change into in the galley’s cabin, to ward off the fevers that fester in wet fabric after sunset. I knew Phorenice well enough now to see she had no divine delusions when it came to mortal sickness, shivering even as she pulled on the soft fresh linen.
The galley glided across the calm inner harbour to tie up at the golden-runged royal quay. As we waited for our litters, I spotted Phorenice’s captured mammoth half-submerged in the harbour mud, its red bulk glinting in the guard boat’s lantern light, trunk tilted up as it snuffled nervously. I made no move to beg for its safety. That ride on its swaying back had left me humiliated and helpless, a sensation I loathed more than any battle wound. If the beast sank deeper into the mire that night, I would have borne the loss with perfect equanimity.
Phorenice did not waste time asking after Nais, either. When the port captain bowed before us, she demanded immediately to know where her rival was held, and learned the girl was locked in the deepest dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, the guard captain having sworn his life for her safe keeping.
“Let us hope he knows his work,” Phorenice purred. “That pretty Nais knows every secret tunnel in that pyramid better than most of the royal masons. I’d hate to have him sent to the tormentors for sticking her in a cell with a hidden exit. You’d be pleased if she escaped, wouldn’t you, Deucalion?”
“I would,” I said frankly, making no secret of it. “I bear no Nais ill will.”
“But I bear her every ill will,” she snapped viciously. “And I still mean to lock your loyalty to me for good with that wedding gift we spoke of.”
“All for the Empress of Atlantis,” I said, bowing.
“Pah, Deucalion, you are far too stiff,” she laughed, tugging my hand to help her climb into the litter. “You should be flattered I am jealous enough to fight over you. Climb in beside me, keep me warm against the night wind. Guards, move back against the walls! That cheap incense you’re burning chokes me.”
That night the royal banqueting hall held another feast, and I sat at Phorenice’s side on the raised dais beneath the carved symbols of the sacred serpent and the outstretched hand of royal power. This time our coming marriage was not a private rumor, but proclaimed openly to the court: Phorenice herself named me her future husband, and every jewel-draped lord and lady of the realm hailed me as their superior. The courtiers had already scented out my preference for simple, old-fashioned custom. No one drank loud, toasting speeches over my health; instead, every guest stepped forward in turn to spill a goblet of wine at my feet. When I invited any of them to take a bite from my platter, they ate it simply and gratefully, no absurd bowing or fawning gestures.
The flaring torches of burning natural gas showed their dress had grown plainer too, their manner far more sober than the usual decadent court frivolity. Phorenice leaned against me, her red curls brushing my cheek, and teased me in a whisper that I had already stolen all her royal authority before we were even wed.
“Sir,” she mocked, “you will make the realm far too ascetic to be fun. I gave no orders for this. There are no perfumes in the hall tonight, the food is plain enough to make me burn the cook half way through, and look at their clothes! They look like they stepped straight out of my dead father’s boring old reign, before I introduced bright dyes and pretty jewels to the court. Even their beards! Half the men have trimmed off those ridiculous fancy curls they spent three hours twisting into shape yesterday. By my crown, I swear they’d shave their faces smooth to match yours tomorrow if I didn’t put a stop to it.”
“Why stop them, if they prefer it?”
“Because there is only one man in all of Atlantis who gets to be clean-shaven,” she said firmly, tapping my jaw with her finger. “And that man is my husband. Rulers must keep small, exclusive marks of their rank, Deucalion. If we let everyone copy our every hairstyle and robe fold, they will stop admiring us and start resenting our arrogance. But if we reserve a few small, pretty privileges only for ourselves, the people will admire them, and envy gently, and never turn restive.”
“Ruling is far more clever than I ever guessed,” I said.
“And yet everyone tells me you ruled your own northern lands very well,” she laughed, smacking my arm playfully. “You are turning into as fine a courtier as any of them, you know. No fan-girl here to cool my blushing cheeks tonight, either. I need to pick a new one—but it won’t be Ylga. Ylga looks at you far too fondly for my taste. If she’s smart, she’ll go live in her estate on the far side of the city, busy herself with her embroidery and her slaves. I won’t be cruel to her, but no other woman in this kingdom will treat you with undue favour.”
“Then how am I to object,” I teased, “when every man in Atlantis stares at you as if they are hopelessly in love?”
“You cannot ask me to do the impossible,” she said. “I rule their bodies, but not their silly hearts. Though if you like, you can make proclamation that I am yours, body and soul, and any man who dares to look at me too long gets handed over to the tormentors. It would be a very pretty show of jealousy, Deucalion. I hate that I am the only one jealous here.”
“A man who has spent a lifetime far from soft feelings cannot learn all the little tricks of love in a single day,” I told her. “I think I am doing very well, considering.”
“Then you are easily satisfied,” she pouted, but her eyes were soft.
The feast did not drag on late. Phorenice had fought, ridden, and schemed from dawn to dusk, and for all her claims to be a living goddess, her body was still mortal. Dark rings of weariness pooled under her bright eyes, and she leaned back against the cushions, limp and exhausted. When the dancers filed in to perform for us, she tossed them a jewel and waved them away halfway through their routine. The poet who arrived to sing of that day’s battles got no hearing at all.
“Come back tomorrow,” she told him, voice thick with fatigue. “I will pay you well if your poem is short. But if you fill it with useless long-winded verses, I will pay you with a whipping, not gold.”
She rose to her feet, the courtiers bowed their farewells, and I led her down the maze of secret, stone-door lined hallways that led to her private chambers. She leaned heavy on my arm, and when we paused to let a massive sliding stone block grind slowly open, she rested her head on my shoulder. Her warm breath fanned my cheek, her red lips were inches from mine, and she waited, clearly expecting me to kiss her. But the part I had to play weighed heavy on my chest, and I could not bring myself to cross that line. When the stone slid fully open, she drew back with a soft, disappointed sigh, and we walked the rest of the way in silence.
“May the High Gods guard you well,” I said, when we reached the door to her bedchamber.
“I am my own goddess,” she snapped, “except for this one thing. You are the most tardy wooer in all Atlantis, Deucalion. Where do you think you are going?”
“To my own chambers, to sleep.”
“Go then, go!” she huffed. “Nothing you could do would suit your stiff, old-fashioned honour, anyway. You are a terrible wooer. Don’t you know that women value things all the more when they are taken by a little rough force?”
“I admit I know very little of women,” I said.
“You have never spoken a truer word,” she said, laughing bitterly. “And your coldness only makes everyone respect you more, doesn’t it? Go on then, run off. Let the High Gods keep you, if you truly believe they can. Good night.”
I had copied the full map of the royal pyramid’s secret tunnels out of the archives before the feast, and I knew every twist by heart, threading my way through the maze without pause. I was bone-weary, but I could not waste a minute on sleep. Nais was marked for death, I knew—but a plan had come to me that might let me save her life, if I moved fast.
I rounded a dark corner and nearly ran into Ylga, who stepped out of the shadows to block my path, sullen and angry.
“I know I’ve lost my place as your favourite,” she said. “Phorenice never intended to let me near you, not from the start. I was a fool to ever think otherwise. You probably think I’m a silly, meddling old woman.”
“I never forget a kindness,” I said.
“Oh, you will learn all the court tricks soon enough,” she snapped. “Everyone in the city is talking about you marrying her for the throne. I thought you were different. I thought you were the kind of man who didn’t chase pretty red hair and power just because it was handed to you.”
“Ylga,” I said, “do not speak ill of the Empress. But I need your help. Go wait for me in my private chambers. I will come to you shortly.”
Her eyes widened. “At this hour? It is not proper for a maiden to come to a man’s rooms alone.”
“I am Deucalion,” I said. “If you are afraid, do not come.”
She snorted. “Everyone knows you are as cold as a northern mountain. I was a fool to doubt that. Fine. I will come.”
I waited till she was out of sight, then turned toward the hidden tunnel that linked the royal pyramid straight to the Temple of Our Lady the Moon. The passage was known only to the seven highest priest initiates, and almost no one had trod it for generations. I fumbled my way through the dark, recalling every twist and turn from my initiation lessons, till I reached the secret spy hatch that opened onto the main temple. The great hall was empty, dust lay thick on the altars, no worshippers had come to honour the Moon Goddess in years.
I spoke the required initiation prayer, pulled the hidden metal stair from its nook in the wall, and climbed it up to rest against the giant stone face of the Moon Goddess. My heart hammered in my chest. I was a veteran of a thousand battles, but tampering with the ancient sacred drug was a risk no man had dared take in centuries. I reached up, pressed the jewel set in the Goddess’s forehead, then pushed at the corner of her stone eyebrow. With a soft creak, the massive stone eyeball swung inward, revealing the hollow chamber hidden inside her skull.
I had only ever heard of the drugs in lessons, never seen them before. Three small, rough, nut-sized balls of grass-green, beeswax-soft substance lay in the hidden nook. No one had ever told me what they were made of, only that their power could put a human being into a death-like sleep that would last for years, unaging, unhurt, till they woke again. I took one, tucked it in my pouch, left the other two exactly as I found them, and climbed back out, sealing the eyeball hatch shut behind me. I spared one last look at the vast, calm stone statue of the Moon Goddess, glowing in the eternal gas torchlight, then slipped back through the secret tunnel to the pyramid.
When I walked into my private chambers, Ylga was hiding behind the curtain, peering out to make sure I was alone. She stepped out, still sullen, still ready to snap at me for being a fraud who chased Phorenice’s crown.
“Enough of your complaints,” I said, cutting her off. “You are not here to judge me. The choice is yours now. Tomorrow, Nais will either die by the Empress’s order—or I can give her this sacred drug, and she will fall asleep, and wake on a day far happier than this one. All Atlantis will think she is dead. No one will harm her, no one will torment her. And it is you who will help me pull this off.”
