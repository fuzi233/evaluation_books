Outside the town, the road was thick with fresh tracks—paw prints of wolves that had passed in great numbers. The snow, once pristine, now bore witness to their restless passage.

“We did well to heed the old man’s warning, Denys,” Gerard said quietly.

“Ay, we did,” Denys replied. “Now I recall—I heard them last night, skittering beneath our window, howling like starving beasts in the market square. But no fat burgher tossed them a scrap from his plate. Poor vagabonds.”

Gerard smiled, but it was a distant smile, as if his thoughts were far away. They walked on in silence, each lost in their own world.

“What are you thinking so deeply about?” Denys asked at last.

“Your goodness,” Gerard answered.

Denys frowned. He could endure much nonsense, especially when it came wrapped in good humor; but sometimes—without reason—he would flare up, as if insulted by mere words.

“A civil question deserves a civil answer,” he said dryly.

“I meant no other,” Gerard insisted.

“Then why pretend you were pondering my goodness, when you know I have none beneath my skin?”

“If another had said that, I’d have told him, ‘You lie.’ But to you, I say: You see only women’s qualities, not men’s. And again, I defy your unjust anger. I *was* thinking of your goodness—last night, when you offered to marry me off to the *Tête d’Or*, or rather to the *tête de veau dorée*, and leave yourself alone.”

“Oh, are you there, lad?” Denys laughed, regaining his usual warmth. “Well, let me be honest—I didn’t mean it for goodness, but for friendship, true fellowship. No more. And truly, my young master, my conscience pricks me even now for letting you turn your back on fortune and peace. A truer friend than I might have taken your leg and crippled you—then you’d have lain healing at the *Tête d’Or* for a month, nursed by that flighty lass, and all would have been well. I had the blade ready, but remembering how you hate pain—even a scratch—my coward heart failed me at the last moment.”

He looked down, ashamed, as though confessing a failure of courage.

Gerard raised his eyebrows in surprise—this was monstrous, yet entirely characteristic of Denys. Yet the matter was never spoken of again: whether one should, out of love, cripple a friend to save him from harm. For an event intervened.

“Here comes someone behind us—riding a neighbor’s mule!” shouted Denys.

Gerard turned.

“And how do you know it’s not his own?” he asked.

“Oh, blind! Because he rides it like a madman.”

Indeed, the man came galloping wildly, eyes wide with alarm. When he reached them, he reined in so sharply the mule nearly fell, sliding between them like a four-legged table on wheels.

“You’re from the *Tête d’Or*, aren’t you?” he gasped.

They nodded.

“Which of you is the younger?”

“The one born later,” Denys replied, winking at Gerard.

“Gramercy for the news!”

“Come, divine then!”

“And shall. Your beard is full-grown, his is still green. He’s the younger. Here, youngster.” He handed Gerard a folded packet. “You left this at the *Tête d’Or*. Our mistress sends it.”

“No,” Gerard said, searching his pouch. “I don’t remember leaving anything.”

“Would you make our burgess a liar?” the man reproached. “And shall I get no *pourboire*? I came *ventre à terre*!”

“Well, you shall have your tip,” Gerard said, handing over a small coin.

“A la bonne heure!” the man cheered, beaming with joy. “The Virgin go with you! Come up, Jenny!” And he rode off, “stomach to earth,” as they say in the country.

Gerard opened the packet. It was six inches square, and inside lay another, and within that another, and so on. At the fourth layer, he threw the whole thing into the snow.

Denys pulled it out. “Now what’s this? Don’t throw things about like that.”

“I hate affectation,” Gerard muttered.

“But look—no affectation here. Only woman’s wit. Clearly, she wrapped it carefully because she wouldn’t want her servant to see what she gave a young man—perhaps a garter.”

“I wear no garters.”

“Then hers. Or a lock of hair. What’s this? A piece of raw silk, fresh from the worm. Well, of all love tokens!”

“Who ever thought she’d send me such a thing? I saw no harm in her—except her hands.”

“Stay, something hard is hidden in this soft nest. Come forth, little bird!” Denys exclaimed. “Saints and pikestaves! Look!”

It was a gold ring, set with a large amethyst—deep, glowing, pure as crystal.

“How lovely,” Gerard whispered.

“And here’s a note. Read it. I’m not so quick at reading when I don’t know what’s coming.”

Gerard took the paper. “It’s a posy—well written.”

He read aloud, blushing like a girl:

> Youth, with thee my heart is fled,
> Come back to the 'golden Hedde!'
> Wilt not? yet this token keep
> Of hir who doeth thy going weep.
> Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,
> Come back to 'the Hedde of gold.'

“The little dove,” murmured Denys.

“The great owl!” he added, shaking his head. “To risk her name like this. Thank Heaven she chose an honest lad—one who’ll never expose her folly. But oh, the absurdity! Why couldn’t she give it to *me*?”

Denys sighed and shrugged.

“To you, who are just as ripe for foolishness as she?”

Denys admitted, “That’s exactly what I was thinking.”

It seemed strange to him that a woman with eyes could pass over a man and fall for a boy. Still, he acknowledged nature’s bounty: boys were human beings too. Without such caprice, they’d be forgotten—blighted, never growing into men. Only beauty could shape a man from such raw material.

Gerard broke in, offering the ring to Denys.

He refused firmly. “No. You must return to the *Tête d’Or* right now—with the ring, and everything. Like a man. Don’t let a poor girl reach out to you in vain.”

“Her hands, you mean.”

“Her hand—with the *Tête d’Or* in it.”

When that failed, Denys tried to put the ring on Gerard’s finger.

Gerard declined. “I already wear one.”

“That sorry trinket? That’s pewter or tin. This is virgin gold—and the jewel!”

“Yes, but Margaret gave me this. I value it above rubies. I’ll never part with it—or give it a rival.” He kissed the plain metal. “Let it fear nothing.”

“I see the owl has sent her ring to a goose,” Denys said sadly.

But he finally persuaded Gerard to tuck the ring inside his bonnet.

The boy agreed readily. Certain jewels were believed to hold sovereign powers—the amethyst among the most potent talismans.

Once settled, Gerard begged Denys to drop the subject. Talking of women made him ache for Margaret, and every step farther from her felt like a wound.

“I’m not a general lover, Denys. There’s room in my heart for one sweetheart, and one friend. I’m far from my dear Margaret. And soon—just a few leagues more—I’ll lose you too. Let me drink your friendship pure while I may. Don’t dilute it with any of these silly females.”

“And you shall, honey-pot, and you shall,” Denys said kindly. “But as for me leaving you at Remiremont—don’t count on it! If I do—(three oaths) I’ll go with you to Rome. Why not? His Holiness the Pope always needs soldiers. A Burgundian is welcome in his ranks.”

At this, Gerard opened his heart.

“Denys, before I met you, I often stopped on the road—too weak to go on. My heart pulled me back. Then I’d pray to the saints, rise, and drag myself forward. But since I’ve walked with you, my courage has grown. I’ve seen the truth of the ancients: better a bright companion on the weary path than a horse-litter. Oh, brother—when I think of all we’ve done, all we’ve suffered together! You saved my life from the bear, from worse thieves. Even when I was poor, I managed to pull you from the Rhine—and from that hour, I loved you doubly. How many bonds between us! If I had my way, I’d never, never, never part with you—this side of death. Alas, God’s will be done.”

“No,” Denys roared. “My will shall be done this time. Le bon Dieu has bigger fish to fry than us. I’ll go with you to Rome. There’s my hand on it.”

“You say that? It’s impossible. Too selfish of me.”

“I tell you—it’s settled. Nothing can change me. At Remiremont, I borrow ten pieces from my uncle. We go. It’s fixed.”

They shook hands.

Then Gerard said nothing. His heart was too full. Instead, he ran twice around Denys, danced backward before him, and finally took his hand. So they walked—hand in hand, like lovers—until a company of fifty mounted soldiers rose into view on a hilltop.

“Look—the banner of Burgundy!” Denys said joyfully. “I’ll find a comrade among them.”

“How glorious it shines in the sun!” Gerard exclaimed. “And the leaders—velvet, feathers, steel breastplates like glass mirrors!”

As they drew near, Denys gasped. “Why, it’s the Bastard of Burgundy himself! Then there’s fighting afoot. A bold leader—values his life no higher than a private’s, and a soldier’s no higher than a sparrow’s. That’s the captain for me.”

“And see,” Gerard said, “even the mules—brass frontlets, fine trappings—seem proud to carry them. No wonder men long to be soldiers.”

In the midst of this admiration, the troop reached them.

“Halt!” a deep voice commanded.

They halted.

The Bastard of Burgundy glared at Denys. “How now, archer? Why face south when every loyal hand and heart hurries north?”

Denys bowed. “I’m returning home after years of service—to see my family at Remiremont.”

“Good. But not now. The duchy is troubled. Bring the dead soldier’s mule forward. Mount and ride with us to Flanders.”

“So please your highness,” Denys said firmly, “that cannot be. My home is close. I haven’t seen it in three years. Above all, I have this youth under my care. I cannot, must not leave him until I see him safely shipped for Rome.”

“Do you defy me?” the commander thundered, turning wrathful. “Are you tired of life? Let go the boy’s hand and mount—no more idle talk.”

Denys said nothing. But he held Gerard’s hand tighter, and stared back with defiance.

The Bastard roared, “Jarnac! Dismount six archers—shoot this white-livered cur dead where he stands. For an example.”

Young Count de Jarnac gave the order. Six men dismounted.

“Strip him naked,” the Bastard ordered coldly. “Put his arms on the spare mule. We may find a clown worthy to wear them.”

Denys groaned. “Am I to be shamed *and* killed?”

“Oh, no! No! No!” cried Gerard, snapping from his shock. “He shall go with you—immediately. I’d rather lose him forever than see a hair of his head harmed. Oh, sir, my lord—give a poor boy just a minute to bid his only friend farewell! He’ll go. I swear he will.”

The leader nodded, cold and scornful. “Jarnac, stay with them. Bring him on—alive or dead. Forward!”

And he led his men away, leaving only Jarnac and six archers, one holding the spare mule.

Denys and Gerard stared at each other—haggard, silent.

What a look!

Then, in frantic haste, they spoke.

“You’re going to Holland. You know where she lives. Tell her everything. She’ll be kind—for my sake.”

“Oh, what a tale I’ll bring her! For God’s sake, go back to the *Tête d’Or*. I’m mad!”

“Hush! Let me think—have I nothing to say? My head—my head!”

“Ah! I have it. Go to Strasbourg, Gerard. The Rhine. Downstream to Rotterdam. Margaret is there. I’ll go there. I’ll tell her you’re coming. We’ll all be together.”

“My lads, hurry,” Jarnac said firmly, but gently now. “We’re wasting time.”

“Oh, sir—just one moment! One little moment!” Gerard gasped.

“Cursed be the land I was born in! Cursed be the race of man! And he who made them so!” Denys screamed.

“Hush, Denys, hush! Blaspheme not! God forgive him—he knows not what he says. Be patient. Though we meet no more on earth, let us meet in a better world—where no blasphemer may enter. To my heart, lost friend. Words are gone now.”

He opened his arms. They embraced—tight, unyielding. They kissed again and again, speechless, tears streaming down both faces.

Count Jarnac watched, amazed. But the rougher soldiers—those who knew what comrade meant—looked on with quiet pity.

Then, at Jarnac’s signal, they lifted Denys onto the mule, half-carrying him, and rode after their leader.

Gerard ran wildly after them—though the lane curved. The last he saw: Denys rocking on the mule, tearing his hair.

At that sight, something rose in Gerard’s throat—so high, so sharp—he could run no more. He gasped, leaned against a snow-covered hedge, clutching it, choking.

Thorns pierced his hand.

After a bitter struggle, he breathed again.

Now he began to feel his loss—slowly, painfully.

He staggered forward, not knowing where he went. Every few steps, he stopped. His arms dropped. His head sank. He stood still.

Then he whispered: “Can this be real? This must be a dream. Five minutes ago, we were happy—walking hand in hand, going to Rome together. We admired their banners, their helmets—oh hearts of hell!”

All nature seemed as lonely as he.

No living thing in sight.

Only white.

He, a ghost of his former self, wandered among ghostly trees, fields, hedges.

Desolate. Desolate. Desolate.

He knelt, gathered snow. “No—I do not dream. This is snow. Cold as the world’s heart. And bloody—what does that mean? Fool! It’s from my hand. I don’t remember the wound. Ah—thorns. Welcome, kind foes. I felt you not. You did not pierce my heart. You are not cruel like men.”

He rose, dragging his leaden limbs.

Then—hoofbeats. Gay voices.

He turned, hoping—perhaps the soldiers had relented, brought Denys back.

But no. A gay cavalcade approached: a nobleman in velvet and furs, his favorites, and armed retainers in buff jerkins.

They swept past.

Gerard never looked at them again. Just shadows—gay, fleeting.

Then suddenly—a harsh cry: “Stand and deliver!”

Three servants blocked his path. They had ridden back to rob him.

“How, ye false knaves,” Gerard said calmly, “would you shame your noble master? He’ll hang you from the nearest tree.”

With that, he drew his sword, backed against the hedge.

One leveled a petronel.

Another stepped forward. “Don’t be hasty! And don’t be mad! Look yonder!”

Gerard looked. A hundred yards away, the nobleman and his friends had halted—watching, too proud to dirty their hands, but eager to reap the reward.

The softer servant said, “Resistance is useless. Common thieves kill as well as steal. And it costs a mint to be a gentleman. Your master lost money last night. He’s going to visit his mistress. So he takes what he finds. Now, good youth—consider: we don’t rob for ourselves. Hand over that purse without fuss. Or we slit your throat and take it anyway.”

“This man is right,” Gerard said quietly, aloud—but to himself. “I ought not to throw away my life. Margaret would grieve. Take the poor man’s purse to the rich man’s pouch. And with it—this: tell him, I pray the Holy Trinity, each coin may burn his hand, freeze his heart, and blast his soul forever. Begone. Leave me to my sorrow.”

He flung the purse.

They rode off, muttering. His words stung them—just a little.

He staggered on, penniless now, friendless.

Until he reached the edge of a wood.

His heart could barely feel the blow. But his mind did.

What was the use?

He sat on the hard road, clawing his hair, trying to think.

Rome was impossible without money.

Denys had said: “Go to Strasbourg. Down the Rhine.”

He would obey.

But how?

Then—suddenly—the lines rang in his ears:

> Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,
> Come back to the hedde of gold.

“If I do,” he whispered, “I must go as her servant. I who am Margaret’s. I am weary. I will sleep. Dream all is as it was. Ah me—how happy we were an hour ago. We knew not how happy. There is a house—its owner well-to-do. What if I tell him my story, beg help to retrieve my purse, and reach the Rhine?”

Fool! Is he not a man—like the rest? He’d scorn me. Trample me lower.

Denys cursed the race of men.

I will not curse them. But oh—I begin to loathe them.

No. I’ll lie here till sunset. Then, in darkness, creep into his barn. Steal milk or grain—just enough to live. God, who saw the rich rob me, may forgive me.

They say it’s ill sleeping on snow. Death creeps with muffled feet and honey breath.

But what can I do? I am weary. So weary.

Is this the wood where the wolves dwell—the old man warned of?

I must trust them. They are not men.

And I am so weary.

He crawled to the roadside, stretched out on the snow, sighing deeply.

“Ah, tear not thine hair so! It breaks my heart to see thee.”

“Margaret. Never see me more. Poor Margaret.”

And the tender heart stilled.

The constant lover, the true friend of ancient mold, lay silent on the snow—threatened by cold, by beasts, by hunger, friendless, penniless, in a strange land, not halfway to Rome.
