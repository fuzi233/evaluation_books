Something Wanting.

"Experience, like a pale musician, holds / A dulcimer of patience in his hand;"
So ran the verse that lingered in Margaret's mind, framing the quiet rhythm of her days since returning to Thornton House. The air within the manor still hummed with the echoes of the summer wedding, a vibrant, chaotic symphony now played down to a gentle, domestic cadence. Dixon, fresh from Milton, became the vessel for these stories, carrying them in with the dust of the road and the chatter of the market. She spoke of Martha's departure, the opulent trappings of the ceremony, and the whispered judgments of the town regarding Mr. Thornton's financial state—a man who had lost heavily on the strike yet celebrated with a flourish that seemed to mock his precarious balance.

Dixon recounted the auction with a relish that bordered on the gossipy, detailing how Mrs. Thornton had secured bargains before Mr. Thornton arrived, only to bid against his own wife in a theatrical display of rivalry that amused the bystanders. "If Mrs. Thornton pays too little," Dixon noted, "Mr. Thornton pays too much," a balancing act that somehow satisfied Dixon's need for order. Yet, beneath the surface of these tales lay a deeper, more troubling silence. Dixon spoke little of the Higginses, her aristocratic biases blurring the details of their lives, though she mentioned Nicholas's persistent inquiries for Miss Hale. It was a peculiar rumor, however, that caught Dixon's imagination—that Mary Higgins had taken employment at Mr. Thornton's mill, compelled by her father to learn the trade of cooking. Margaret found the tale incoherent, a dream-like absurdity, yet it offered a fleeting connection to the world outside her window.

More than the gossip of neighbors, however, Margaret harbored a strange, undefined longing for Mr. Bell. Since their conversation at Helstone, it had been tacitly agreed that any necessary explanation must wait for a personal audience with Mr. Thornton, delivered by word of mouth. Letters held no weight in this matter, yet Mr. Bell continued to write, if not with the warmth of before, then with a brittle, complaining edge. His words carried a bitterness unusual for him, suggesting a weariness with the present and a regret for the past. Margaret, reading between the lines, feared for his health, though his brief replies dismissed ailments as merely the "spleen," inviting her indulgence in his grumblings without demanding medical updates.

The stagnation of Mr. Bell's movements at Oxford began to weigh on her spirit. Then came a fragment of truth dropped by Edith, revealing a conversation Mr. Bell had held in London. He had half-conceived the notion of traveling to Cadiz in the autumn, to witness the aftermath of the mutiny and, perhaps, to introduce Margaret to her new sister-in-law. To Edith, this plan sounded like a mere fantasy, a "Château en Espagne," yet to Margaret, it ignited a spark of desperate hope. It promised an escape from the monotonous circle of her existence, a chance to breathe air different from the stale atmosphere of Thornton House.

Yet, Edith's reaction was one of wounded affection. She cried, convinced that any pleasure Margaret might find away from home was a tacit affront, a proof of indifference. "Do you not care?" Edith sobbed, her jealousy a mirror reflecting Margaret's own isolation. Margaret, trying to soothe her friend, found herself confessing to Dixon, while dressing for dinner, a longing she could not suppress. Would Dixon not wish to accompany her to see Master Frederick and his wife?

"She's a Papist, Miss, isn't she?" Dixon asked, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper.
"I believe—oh, yes, certainly!" Margaret replied, a flicker of apprehension dimming her spirits.
"And they live in a Popish country?"
"Yes."
Dixon's face fell. "Then I'm afraid I must say that my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick, his own dear self. I should be in a perpetual terror, Miss, lest I should be converted."

"Oh," Margaret said gently, "I do not know that I am going. And if I go, I am not such a fine lady as to be unable to travel without you. You shall have a long holiday, if we go."
"But I'm afraid it is a long 'if,'" Dixon murmured, her eyes betraying a hidden curiosity about Spain and its mysteries.
Margaret, sensing Dixon's reluctance, pushed the thought aside. The Spanish venture remained a phantom, a bright outlet that slipped through her fingers, just as the reality of explaining her feelings to Mr. Thornton remained elusive. Mr. Bell seemed stationary, bound by some unseen restraint, and the mist of misunderstanding hung heavier than ever.

Amidst these uncertainties, a small joy anchored Margaret's daily life: Edith's son. He was a creature of strong will, prone to stormy passions that left Edith exhausted and sighing for help. Yet, Margaret found a profound sweetness in battling him out in private rooms, where she wielded a firm, gentle power to subdue his tantrums. As he rubbed his tear-streaked face against hers, seeking solace, those moments tasted of a future she felt denied to her forever. In his trust, she found a glimpse of the unconditional bond she craved.

Another figure had entered the household with a cold brilliance: Mr. Henry Lennox. He spoke with a sharp intellect that flavored the otherwise insipid conversations of the house. Margaret detected a subtle contempt in his gaze for his brother's frivolous lifestyle, a disdain that manifested in sharp questions about whether Captain Lennox truly intended to abandon his profession. When his brother replied that he had enough to live upon, Mr. Lennox curled his lip, dismissing the material security as a hollow foundation.

Though the brothers were tightly bound by a dynamic of leadership and obedience, Mr. Lennox stood somewhat apart from the rest of the family. He was calculating, far-seeing, and proud, cultivating connections with profound intent. Their interactions, though frequent, carried a distance born of divergent paths. They had drifted apart from their former alignment in opinion and taste, separated by the chasm of Frederick's misfortunes. Yet, when Mr. Lennox spoke with epigrammatic force, his eyes would seek Margaret's countenance, listening with a deference that was both reluctant and deeply concealing. In her, he found an intellectual resonance that transcended the superficialities of their shared domestic sphere, a silent acknowledgment of a kindred spirit drifting in separate currents.
