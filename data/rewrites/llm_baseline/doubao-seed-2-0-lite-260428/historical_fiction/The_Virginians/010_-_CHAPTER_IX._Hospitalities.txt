His Excellency the Commander-in-Chief set out to pay his formal call on Madam Esmond in a state and splendour befitting the highest ranking authority across all His Majesty’s North American colonies, plantations, and dominions. Ahead of him out of Williamsburg rode his guard of dragoons, greeted by a roar of cheers from a loyal crowd made up overwhelmingly of the town’s enslaved Black population. The General himself travelled in his own heavy, emblazoned coach, Captain Talmadge, his Master of the Horse, at the carriage door before mounting to ride at its flank for the whole journey to Castlewood. Seated up front with Major Danvers, his aide-de-camp, was the little Philadelphia postmaster Mr. Franklin: once a printer’s boy, he was widely acknowledged by the General and his staff as a man of uncommon shrewdness, brimming with curious, precise knowledge of the colonies, and of England too, where he had travelled more than once.
“‘Tis quite extraordinary how a man of such humble origins could have amassed such breadth of learning, and such natural politeness besides, Mr. Franklin!” his Excellency remarked, tipping his hat graciously to the postmaster.
Franklin bowed low, his wide buckled wig shifting to send a small puff of powder into the air. He replied that he had been fortunate enough to fall into the company of gentlemen of the General’s calibre from time to time, and had done his best to study their manners and adapt his own conduct to match theirs as far as he was able. Of formal education, he could not boast much: his father had been a man of straitened means, and schooling opportunities in his native New England were thin on the ground. He had read and observed everything he could, but insisted he still knew nothing compared to the learned men of England.
Mr. Braddock burst out laughing. “As for education, there are officers in the army, by George, who couldn’t tell you if ‘bull’ was spelled with two b’s or one. I’ve heard the Duke of Marlborough himself was no great hand with a pen. I never had the honour to serve under him, his Grace was a generation before my time, but he thrashed the French soundly all the same, and no one ever called him a poor commander for being no scholar.”
Mr. Franklin said he was well aware of that fact.
“Nor is my own Duke a man of books,” Braddock went on, grinning. “Aha, I see that little wink in your eye, Postmaster, you’ve heard that story too!”
Franklin instantly wiped the satirical spark from his eye, turning a face as guileless as an infant’s up to the General’s ruddy, jolly features. “He is no scholar, true, but he is more than a match for any French general who ever made a joke about serving up Englishmen as frog fricassee. He saved the crown for our most gracious sovereign, King George, his royal father.”
Off went Franklin’s hat in a deep bow, sending a whole cloud of white powder billowing from his wig.
“He is the soldiers’ truest friend, and the unflinching enemy of all those beggarly red-shanked Scottish rebels and scheming Roman Jesuits who would rob us of our liberty and our Protestant faith, by George. His Royal Highness, my gracious master, is no scholar either, but he is one of the finest gentlemen who ever walked the earth.”
“I saw His Royal Highness on horseback once, at a Guards review in Hyde Park,” Franklin said, smiling. “He cuts a very fine figure in the saddle, no denying.”
“You shall drink his health to-day, Postmaster. He is the best of masters, the best of friends, the best of sons to his old royal father; the finest gentleman ever to wear an epaulet.”
“Epaulets are quite out of my line, sir,” Franklin laughed. “You know I live in the Quaker City.”
“Of course they are, my good man. Every man to his own work. You and your sort pore over your books, and you’re welcome to it. We don’t forbid it, we encourage it. Our work is to fight the enemy and govern the country. Hey gentlemen? Lord, but these roads in the colony are something dreadful! This cursed coach is lurching like a ship in a gale. Who have we yonder, riding that fine gelding with two liveried negro boys behind him?”
“That is Mr. Washington,” the aide-de-camp answered.
“I’d take him for a corporal in the Horse Grenadiers any day,” the General pronounced. “He cuts a perfect figure on a horse. And he knows this country inside out, don’t he, Mr. Franklin?”
“Indeed he does.”
“And monstrous genteel, for a provincial. I’d have sworn he had the polish of a man who’d made the grand tour of Europe, by George I would.”
Franklin nodded, his face innocent, as he glanced at the stout chief lurching side to side across the carriage seat, his face as scarlet as his uniform coat, who swore every other breath, knew nothing outside parade ground rules save for the merits of a fine bottle and a pretty woman, was absurdly proud of his own no-distinguished ancestry, brave as a bulldog, savage one minute and generous the next, easy to laugh with, dull of wit, entirely unread, and utterly convinced England was the greatest nation on earth and he himself the equal of any gentleman alive. “Yes, he is mighty well for a provincial, to be sure. He was beaten last year at that Fort What’s-it-called, down by the Thingamy River. What was the name of the place, Talmadge?”
“Lord knows, sir,” said Talmadge. “And I’ll wager the Postmaster knows too, and is laughing at us both for forgetting it.”
“Oh Captain, you do me wrong!”
“He was caught in a perfect trap, that’s what. He only had militia and raw Indian levies with him. Fine day, Mr. Washington. A very pretty nag you’re riding. That was your first action, last year?”
“At Fort Necessity, sir, yes,” the gentleman replied, gravely saluting as he rode up, two sharp, well-dressed negro grooms at his heels in smart livery coats and velvet hunting caps. “I began it very badly, sir. I had never been in action before that unlucky day.”
“You were all raw levies, my good fellow. You should have seen our own militia run from the Highland Scots, and cursed be to them for it. You ought to have had regular troops with you.”
“Your Excellency knows it is my dearest wish to serve alongside them,” Mr. Washington said.
“By George, we shall see that you get that chance, sir,” the General swore with one of his characteristic hearty oaths. The heavy coach rumbled onwards towards Castlewood, while Mr. Washington asked permission to gallop ahead to warn Madam Esmond of their imminent arrival.
The Commander-in-Chief’s progress was so slow that a number of humbler local gentry invited to the gathering soon overtook his carriage. Unwilling to pass the great man on the road, they fell in behind his chariot wheels, forming a long, comical procession through the dust. First came Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor of the province, attended by his own negro servants, riding alongside Parson Broadbent, the jolly Williamsburg chaplain. Presently they were joined by little Mr. Dempster, the boys’ schoolmaster, wearing his grand Ramillies wig reserved only for state occasions. Soon after that, Judge Laws and his wife came into view, Madam Laws riding pillion behind him, their negro servant trailing behind on a mule, carrying a locked box that held her ladyship’s best cap. The whole rear guard looked so ludicrous that Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin spied them and laughed outright, quiet enough not to wake the General, who was fast asleep by then. They called out to the party of local gentry to ride ahead, there was more than enough room at Castlewood for everyone when the Commander-in-Chief and his dragoon escort followed at their leisure. There would be meat, drink, and the finest tobacco for His Majesty’s soldiers; laughter and plenty of cheer for the enslaved servants; and a warm, overflowing welcome for all their masters.
The honest General made sure to sample nearly every dish on the table, and reach for second helpings of most, holding out his glass for refills constantly. He declared Nathan’s spiced sangaree to be perfection, and had drunk a large draught of it even before dinner was served. There was cider, ale, brandy, and plenty of good Bordeaux wine, some of it brought back to the colony by Madam Esmond’s late husband Colonel Esmond, fit for pontificum coenis—“a feast fit for priests,” little Mr. Dempster whispered with a wink to Parson Broadbent, the clergyman from the adjoining parish. Broadbent winked back and nodded, drinking the wine without a care for the Latin line, which was no surprise: he had never troubled himself much with dead languages. He was a gambling, guzzling, cock-fighting divine who had spent a good portion of his life in the Fleet Prison, at Newmarket racecourse, and at the rough sporting grounds of Hockley-in-the-Hole. He had run all manner of errands for his old patron Lord Cingbars, son of Lord Ringwood (it was widely rumoured that Broadbent’s mother had been Lady Cingbars’ waiting woman, and modern readers need not pry too closely into the details of his paternity), before he was posted out to a rural Virginia living. He and young George Esmond had fought many a cock fight together, hunted roe side by side, hauled in countless shad and salmon, shot wild geese, swans, pigeons, and plover, and bagged myriads of canvas-backed ducks. The envious whispered that Broadbent had once been caught poaching on Mr. Washington’s land, and that Washington had set his dogs on him and thrashed him soundly on the riverbank at Mount Vernon. The parson had broken free of Washington’s grip and scrambled to his boat in the dark, but he was laid up for two full Sundays afterwards, and when he reappeared in public he still bore the fading mark of a black eye, and wore a brand new coat collar. George Esmond and Parson Broadbent had played every game of cards under the sun together, besides hunting every bird in the air, every beast in the forest, and every fish in the rivers. It was widely suspected that while the twins rode off to their lessons with Mr. Dempster, Harry would often slip away and take lessons from Broadbent in the European arts of hunting, drinking, and sport, while George went his own way reading his books, and of course never told tales on his younger brother.
Every game bird of the Virginia woods, every fish in the season’s catch, was laid out on Madam Esmond’s board to feed the General and the rest of the English and colonial gentlemen. The gumbo was pronounced absolute perfection (young Mr. George’s black servant was even named after the dish: the late Colonel had caught him hiding behind a door with his face buried in a bowl of the rich stew, and named him Gumbo on the spot). The shad were rich and fresh, the stewed terrapins were declared worthy of a London alderman’s table—by George, the General said, he would be proud to serve it up to the Duke himself. The enslaved women of Castlewood’s kitchens had a genius for cookery, trained from childhood under the sharp, exacting eye of both the late Colonel and Madam Esmond herself. Many of the sweets and flans Madam Esmond made with her own neat, dexterous hands, following the old hospitable custom of the time, rolling back the laced lappets of her sleeves to show off her pretty round forearms and small, delicate wrists as she carved the principal roasts and fowls at the head of the table. The unwritten old rule of the Virginia table was that the mistress must press her guests to eat with gracious eagerness, watching to encourage those who might be shy to take more, knowing every culinary secret and every trick of carving fowls, fish, game, and joints, cheering her guests to new helpings, and whispering to the General, “I saved the salmon’s jowl just for your Excellency—there’s no taking no for an answer! Mr. Franklin, you drink only water, sir? But our cellar has wholesome wine that never gives a man a headache—won’t you try just a sip? Mr. Justice, you are fond of woodcock pie, I know.”
“Because I know who makes the pastry,” Judge Laws said, bowing low to her. “I wish my own wife could make a pie crust half as light as yours, madam. I tell her all the time, my dear, I wish you had Madam Esmond’s hands.”
“A very pretty hand it is too, I’m sure more than one gentleman would not mind having it,” the Boston postmaster remarked, and Mr. Esmond looked at him half displeased at the familiar line.
“Such a hand for a light, flaky crust,” the Judge continued, “my very best respects to you, madam.” He thought the widow would surely be flattered by the compliment. She replied simply that she had learned all her cookery and carving skills when she was sent to England for her education as a girl, that her own mother had taught her the family recipes, and her father and two sons had always loved those dishes. She was only too glad if they pleased her guests.
More dishes came out, more compliments, more toasts: ten times as much meat as was strictly needed for the whole company. Mr. Washington did not join much in the light banter, but sat deep in conversation with Talmadge, Danvers, and Mr. Franklin, tracing out lines across the table with bits of bread to mark roads, rivers, supply routes, pack horse stops, and positions for the artillery train. He rested his finger on little bread crumb markers for the forts and camps along the Potomac and Ohio, until a passing negro servant clearing the dishes brushed the Potomac right off the table with his napkin, and swept the marked-up Ohio into a spoon when he cleared away the crumbs.
At the end of the dinner Parson Broadbent got up and walked behind the Lieutenant-Governor’s chair to say a short grace over the meal, before returning to his seat to pick up his knife and fork again. The puddings and sweets came next: a dozen different varieties, all made to Madam Esmond’s exact family recipes, though few young ladies of the present day would care to hear a long list of puddings eaten a hundred years ago. When the table was cleared, Nathan, the chief butler, set a glass down before every guest, then filled Madam Esmond’s. Bowing to the whole company, she said she would only propose one toast, and knew every gentleman there would join her with all their hearts. “His Majesty,” she said, bowing to Mr. Braddock, who, with his aides and all the colonial gentlemen, repeated the name of their beloved sovereign loyally. She drank her glass of wine, curtsied to the whole company, and retired from the dining room between two lines of liveried negro servants, giving one of her finest, most graceful curtsies at the parlour door.
The mistress of Castlewood had presided over the whole entertainment with admirable spirit, looking so gay and handsome, speaking with such cheer to every guest, that the few other ladies at the table had crowded around her to congratulate her on the elegance of the feast, and her poise hosting it. But no sooner had they stepped into her drawing room than the brave mask of composure fell, and she burst into tears on the sofa beside Mrs. Laws, mid-compliment from that lady. “Ah, madam!” she said. “It may be a great honour, as you say, to host the King’s representative under my roof, and the Esmond family has hosted far greater personages than Mr. Braddock in our time. But he has come to take one of my sons away from me. Who knows if my boy will ever come home, or how? I dreamed of him last night, wounded, his face as white as a sheet, blood streaming from a gash in his side. I would never be so ill-mannered as to let my grief show before the gentlemen, but you, Mrs. Justice, who have parted with your own children and carry a mother’s heart, would not think the less of me if mine was heavy this evening.”
The ladies offered her all the gentle comfort they could, and Madam Esmond forced herself to pull back from her melancholy, remembering she still had duties to perform before she could give way to her private grief. “There will be time enough to be sad once they are all gone,” she told Mrs. Laws, her good neighbour. “My boy must not see me watching him leave with a wistful face, and make our parting all the more painful with my weakness. It is right that young men of his rank should step forward to answer their country’s call. That has always been the way of the Esmonds, and the same Providence that watched over my dear father through twenty great battles under Queen Anne, I trust and pray, will keep my son safe now that his turn has come to do his duty.” She did not speak of her sorrow again, but sat down with the other ladies to play cards and sip coffee, while the gentlemen remained in the adjoining parlour, still drinking their wine and calling out toast after toast. When one lady remarked that the gentlemen were lingering over their drinks rather too long, Madam Esmond only smiled and said, “It will do my boys good to spend time in the company of these English gentlemen. Such society is very rarely found in our remote province, and though their conversation may sometimes be a little free, I am sure men of their rank and breeding will respect the youth of my sons, and say nothing in front of them that young people ought not to hear.”
It was very clear that the English gentlemen were making the most of Madam Esmond’s generous hospitality. While the ladies were still at their cards, Nathan slipped in to whisper to Mrs. Mountain, the housekeeper, who at first cried out in protest: “No! I will not bring out any more of that special claret! They are welcome to all the ordinary Bordeaux they could want, but I will not touch the Colonel’s private stock.” It turned out that the entire dozen bottles of the prized late Colonel’s Bordeaux had already been finished by the gentlemen, “not to mention all the ale, cider, Burgundy, Lisbon wine, and Madeira,” Nathan added, ticking them off on his fingers.
