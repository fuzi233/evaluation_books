On the eighth day from that night which had broken and remade her, Romola stood where the land ended and the Mediterranean began—just above what was then the small fishing village of Viareggio. The sea had the slow, summer breathing of a creature asleep: a hush that rose and fell, and in its hush the shore answered with a soft, repeated sound, as if the pebbles were being taught one syllable and could never learn another. After Florence, that unceasing clatter of wheels and voices and iron in the blood, the plain music of water was like a hand laid on a fevered brow.

Again she had fled. Again the grey religious dress was on her—rough cloth that rasped the wrists when she moved, a smell of stale wool warmed by her body. This time she wore it with an indifference that was more desolate than any disguise. What did a badge matter, or a name? She could not find, beneath the name she had been given, any single duty that held fast and did not crumble the moment she touched it.

Duty—men spoke it as if it were a loaf that could be cut and shared, as if its substance were the same for every mouth. But what force made duty holy, what kneaded it and baked it into something one could live on, if not love believing in some good beyond the self? The bonds of affection that had once drawn her toward endurance seemed snapped, and their broken ends only whipped her when she tried to move. Even in marriage—the bond she had been taught to imagine mystic, indissoluble by its own nature—she no longer saw a sacred union, no longer even the plain obligation of a chosen pledge. Had she not proved that the thing pledged to was impossible? Freedom pressed on her with an overmastering impulse, yet it offered only the exchange of one calamity for another. There is no compensation for the woman who feels the chief relation of her life has been only a mistake: she has lost her crown. Some deepest secret of blessedness has brushed her ear, half-whispered, and then withdrawn for ever, leaving not wisdom but a hungry ache.

What had supported her through that supreme sorrow had slipped away too. The sense of a great end—some purpose that could lift the commonest act, as bread is lifted by heat into something airy and fit to sustain—was eclipsed now. She saw instead a confusion of human things, as if all effort were only fingers tugging at a net of tangled threads; all fellowship, whether of resistance or advocacy, only unfairness and exclusiveness in a different dress.

And what, then, was the man she had reverenced as the very type of heroism—not the heroism of hard endurance, but of willing self-offering love? What was the cause he struggled for? Romola had lost her trust in Savonarola. That fervour of admiration which had once made her overlook his aberrations and see only the grand sweep of his course was gone. Now that her tenderness for her godfather had set her in antagonism to the Frate, she saw, with a painful, magnifying lucidity, every harsh inconsistency in his teaching. In the bitterness of disappointment she told herself that his striving for the renovation of Church and world was a striving after a name—no more nourishing than the title of a book. A name which, in practice, came to mean measures to strengthen his own place in Florence; even questionable words and deeds, undertaken to keep his influence from suffering through his own errors.

That political reform which had once given her a new appetite for life seemed to dwindle, in this new light, into narrow devices for Florence’s safety—contemptibly at odds with alternating professions of blind trust in Divine care. It was inevitable that she should judge him unfairly where an individual’s suffering was concerned: she looked with the eyes of personal tenderness, and he with the eyes of theoretic conviction. When he declared that his party’s cause was the cause of God’s kingdom, she heard only the ring of egoism. Perhaps such words are rarely spoken without that meaner ring; yet they are the very formula of all energetic belief. And if energetic belief, pursuing a grand and distant end, is always in danger of turning into demon-worship—letting son and daughter pass through fire with a readiness that scarcely looks like sacrifice—so too the nearest fellow-feeling has its peril. It can grow timid and sceptical toward the larger aims without which life cannot rise into religion.

So Romola was being blinded by her tears. Whoever has known what it is to lose faith in a fellow-man whom one has profoundly loved and revered will not lightly suppose the shock leaves faith in Invisible Goodness untouched. When high human trust sinks, the dignity of life sinks too; we cease to believe in our own better self, since that self is part of a common nature now degraded in our thought. The finer impulses are dulled, as delicate herbs lose their scent if bruised too long under rough hands.

She felt even the springs of her once active pity drying, leaving only a barren, egoistic complaining. Had not she suffered too? Few had cared for her, while she had cared for many. She had done enough; she had strained toward the impossible, and was weary of this stifling, crowded life. She longed for repose in mere sensation—the old girlish dream from sultry afternoons, when she had fancied herself floating, naïad-like, in waters that asked nothing and judged nothing.

The clear waves seemed to invite her, cool as a well in summer. She wished she could lie down to sleep on them and pass from sleep into death. But she could not directly seek death: the fulness of young life in her forbade the clear, deliberate act. She could only wish that death would come to her, as storms come.

Where she had paused, the shore curved inward into a deep bend. A small boat with a sail was moored there, rocking with a patience that seemed almost human. She stood looking at it, and—like a remembered taste that turns suddenly into craving—her mind slipped back to a story she had once loved in Boccaccio. In those days, when her father fell asleep and she slid from her stool to sit on the floor with the forbidden sweetness of the Decamerone, she had read of fair Gostanza, who, lovelorn and despairing, desired to live no longer, yet had not the courage to strike at her young life. She had put herself into a boat, pushed off to sea, wrapped her mantle over her head, hoping to be wrecked—hoping fear would be made helpless, unable to flee from death.

It had been only a thought then, a story held at arm’s length like a tale of salt and wind. But now, as Romola paced and paused again, she saw another boat gliding black against the red-gold water—one man in it, rowing toward the bend where the smaller boat waited. When she walked on a little and watched, she saw him land, pull his boat ashore, and begin to unload his catch and gear.

Perhaps he owned the small boat too. He would be gone soon; the chance would be gone with him—the chance, at least, of possessing that frail means of drifting away. She had not yet admitted to herself that she meant to use it, but the eagerness that rose in her disclosed how a half-unconscious thought had ripened into desire.

“Is that little boat yours also?” she said.

The fisherman looked up, startled by the tall grey figure that had come upon him in the evening solitude. He made a reverence, taking her for a holy Sister wandering in some mysterious errand. His hands smelled of seaweed and fish; his clothes held the day’s brine. Yes, it was his boat: an old one, hardly seaworthy, yet worth mending to any man who would buy it. By the blessing of San Antonio—whose chapel stood in the village yonder—his fishing had prospered; he had now a better boat, which had once been Gianni’s, before Gianni died. But he had not sold the old one.

Romola asked the price. While he was still busy with his unloading—nets folded, damp ropes coiled like sleeping snakes—she thrust the money into a little satchel on the ground, where the remnant of his dinner lay: bread with a hard crust softened at the broken edge by oil, perhaps a pinch of salt that clung to everything in that place as if the air itself were seasoned.

Then she watched him furl the sail, and asked, with a steadiness that made her seem merely curious, how he would set it if he wanted to go out to sea. The fisherman answered, pointing and demonstrating with the practical exactness of a man who knows wind the way a baker knows heat. She listened, storing the small instructions as if they were nothing more than household knowledge—how to fasten, how to loosen, how to let the cloth take the breeze without tearing itself to pieces.

After that she paced up and down and waited, counting not minutes but changes in light and sound. Her imagination of herself gliding away in that boat on darkening water grew more urgent, as the thought of a cool brook in sultriness becomes thirst that hurts. To be freed from choice when every motive was bruised; to commit herself, sleeping, to destiny which would either bring death, or else bring her to new necessities that might rouse a new life—this beckoned her the more because the soft evening air made her long to rest in still solitude instead of returning to the village’s heat and noise.

At last the slow fisherman gathered up his movables and walked away, his footsteps a brief rasp on the sand. Soon the gold shrank and darkened in sea and sky. No living thing was in sight; no sound but the lulling monotony of waves that kept time without music. There was no tide in this sea to bear her outward if she waited for an ebb; but Romola felt the breeze from the land rising a little, and it seemed like a hand pushing.

She got into the boat. The wood was worn smooth where hands had gripped it; it smelled of tar and old wet, of long days when hunger and hope had been measured by nets. She unfurled the sail, fastened it as she had been shown in that first brief lesson. The cloth caught the light breeze—she saw it fill, saw the boat answer—and that was all she cared for.

Then she loosed the boat from its moorings and urged it with an oar, pushing until the shore was no longer near enough to accuse her with its nearness. Farther out: until the sea was dark even toward the west, and the stars disclosed themselves like a palpitating life over the wide heavens.

At last she rested. She threw back her cowl, and took off the kerchief beneath it that confined her hair. She doubled cowl and kerchief under her head for a pillow against one of the boat’s ribs. The fair head was still very young and could bear a hard pillow.

So she lay, the soft night air breathing on her while she glided on water that felt, by its very sound, like a vast cradle. She was alone now. She had freed herself from all claims; she had freed herself even from that burden of choice which presses heavier when claims have loosened their guiding hold.

Had she found the dream of her girlhood—repose in mere sensation? No. Memories clung to her like broken wings, heavy, useless, impossible to lift. Memories of human sympathy—sympathy that, even when it wounds, leaves a thirst that the Great Mother has no milk to still. In the wide spaces of sea and sky, Romola felt orphaned. The far-off writing of the heavens sent her no message of love. The stars were bright, indifferent, and in their clean light she felt herself looked at without being seen.

A great sob rose in her, salt as the air, and she wished that she might be gliding into death.

She drew the cowl over her head again, choosing darkness rather than that starlight which seemed to her like the hard light of eyes. Presently she felt as if she were in the grave, and yet not resting there: as if her hands were touching the hands of beloved dead beside her, and she was trying to wake them.
