Lieutenant Sutch was the first to arrive at Broad Place, General Feversham’s ancestral home nestled on the southern slopes of the Surrey hills. He reached the old red-brick mansion just before five o’clock on a bright June afternoon, when sunlight gilded the pines and turned the house into a glowing jewel. The air hummed with warmth and stillness.

Limping slightly—his leg a relic of a scaling-ladder collapse in the Crimea—he crossed the hall, where generations of Fevershams watched from their portraits, lined one above the other like silent sentinels. He stepped out onto the stone terrace at the back, where General Feversham sat upright as if carved from granite, staring southward toward the Sussex Downs.

“How’s the leg?” the general asked, rising sharply from his chair.

He was small and wiry, his white hair framing a sharp, bony face. His eyes were steel-blue and unreadable, betraying no emotion, no depth—only a kind of mechanical alertness. It was the body that moved; the mind seemed frozen.

“It gave me trouble through the winter,” Sutch replied. “But that was expected.”

Feversham nodded, then fell quiet. From the terrace, the land dropped steeply into a wide plain—brown earth, emerald fields, dark groves. Voices rose faintly from below, clear and crisp in the golden hush. Far off, near Horsham, a train’s smoke curled through the trees like a ribbon. The Downs loomed on the horizon, streaked with chalk.

“I thought you’d be here,” Sutch said.

“Your wife’s favorite spot,” Feversham replied flatly. “She’d sit here for hours. She liked empty spaces—wide, open ones.”

“Yes,” Sutch said softly. “She had imagination. Her thoughts could fill them.”

Feversham glanced at him, as though the idea was alien. But he didn’t ask. He never did. What he couldn’t grasp, he simply ignored.

“The table will be short tonight,” he said instead.

“Collins, Barberton, Vaughan—all gone this winter,” Sutch said. “We’re all on half-pay now. The obituary column is just the final notice—our official farewell to service.” He stretched his leg, the same leg crushed fourteen years earlier. “I’m glad you came first,” Feversham added. “I wanted your opinion. This day means more to me than the anniversary of the Redan assault. At the exact moment we stood under arms in the dark—”

“To the west of the quarries,” Sutch interrupted, breathing deeply. “How could one forget?”

“At that very hour,” Feversham continued, “Harry was born in this house. So I thought—if you don’t mind—he might join us tonight. He’s home. He’ll enter the service. And perhaps he might learn something… useful. One never knows.”

“By all means,” Sutch agreed readily. For though he visited only once a year, on this night, he had never seen Harry Feversham. And he had long wondered what had drawn Muriel Graham—so refined, so intelligent, so beautiful—to a man so rigid, so unlike her in every way. Courage? Yes. Self-assurance? Absolutely. But nothing else seemed to shine through the cold surface of Feversham’s character.

Sutch let his thoughts drift back twenty years, to a time before the Redan, before the Naval Brigade, before the war. He remembered London, fresh from China, eager and untested. Now, he was curious to see the son of such a union.

That evening, Harry took his place at the dinner table. Around him, the older men recounted tales of the Crimean winter—of death, of narrow escapes, of hunger and snow. They spoke in clipped tones, as if recalling distant dreams. Their voices were calm, almost detached. A story would begin before the last one ended. There were no dramatic pauses, no deep sighs—just a quiet, relentless flow of memory.

But Harry listened differently.

His dark eyes—Muriel’s eyes—shifted from speaker to speaker, wide and fixed. Each tale became real to him. The bullet whistles, the charge of cavalry, the roar of cannons—they played across his face like scenes unfolding in front of him. His features twitched, tensed, flinched. Once, when an artillery major described the agonizing wait before battle, Harry’s shoulders trembled. Then, without warning, he glanced behind him—a single, fleeting look—quick and uncertain.

Sutch froze.

He knew that look. He’d seen it on recruits, on men facing their first battle. That moment of hesitation—the split second when fear takes hold. He remembered Inkermann: a tall soldier charging forward, then stopping abruptly, turning back—not in defiance, but in dread. The man had smiled, sickly, before being pierced by a Cossack’s lance.

And now—here—Harry had flashed that same glance.

Sutch looked around the table. No one noticed. The others were too busy telling stories, waiting for their turn.

He exhaled slowly and turned to Harry.

“You bring those days back,” Sutch said gently. “Even with this heat, I feel the chill of the Crimea.”

Harry blinked, then whispered, “The stories do.”

“No,” Sutch corrected. “It’s you listening.”

Before Harry could reply, General Feversham snapped from the head of the table:

“Harry, look at the clock!”

All eyes turned. The hands formed a sharp angle—almost midnight. Harry had been sitting there since eight, not a word spoken, not a movement made.

“Must I go, father?” he asked quietly.

The guests protested in unison. “Let him stay!” said the artillery major. “It’s his birthday. And he’s clearly engaged. A boy of fourteen wouldn’t sit through this unless he was fascinated.”

For once, Feversham softened. “Very well. One hour’s furlough. No harm done.”

Harry looked up—just for a second—at his father. His gaze lingered, steady, searching. Sutch felt a pang. In that look, he saw a question: *Are you blind?*

But Feversham was already speaking again. Harry lowered his head, propped his chin on his hands, and returned to the spell of the stories.

He wasn’t entertained. He was trapped.

His face grew pale. His eyes widened. The candlelight flickered through the haze of tobacco smoke. The wine decanters emptied. The silence between words thickened.

Then Feversham spoke again—abruptly, as if struck by memory.

“Lord Wilmington. One of England’s finest names. Ever seen his house in Warwickshire? Every inch seems to whisper: ‘Be a man.’ But the truth… it began as rumor. Whispered at Alma. Spoken at Inkermann. Shouted at Balaclava. By Sebastopol, it was proven.

Wilmington was galloper to his general. I believe the general chose him—so he could redeem himself. Three hundred yards of open ground. A message to deliver. If he fell, the whispers died. If he made it, he’d earn honor.

But he refused. Sat shaking on his horse. You should have seen the general. His face turned the color of that Burgundy. ‘No doubt you have a previous engagement,’ he said—calmly, politely. A previous engagement on the battlefield!

I nearly laughed. But it broke Wilmington. He fled to London. Doors closed. Women spat. He vanished. And one night, in a back room off Haymarket, he blew his brains out.

Curious, isn’t it? He couldn’t face bullets when his name was at stake—but he could kill himself afterward.”

Sutch glanced at the clock. Quarter to one. Harry had fifteen minutes left.

Across the table, a retired surgeon-general with a great, wagging beard leaned forward.

“I can tell you something even stranger,” he said. “A man in my profession—never seen combat. But life and death were his daily work. Not in danger. Just… startled.

We were camped in a valley in India. Pathans sniped from the hills. A bullet tore through the hospital tent—nothing more. The surgeon crept to his quarters. Half an hour later, his orderly found him—stone-dead, lying in his own blood.”

“Hit?” the major asked.

“Not a mark,” the surgeon said. “He opened his instrument case in the dark. Took out a lancet. Severed his femoral artery. Pure panic. At the sound of a bullet.”

A silence fell. Even hardened men stirred uneasily. Some swallowed hard. Others shifted in their seats as if rejecting the horror.

Only one remained still.

Harry.

He sat forward, hands clenched on his knees, eyes blazing. His face was white. His expression—ferocious. Like an animal cornered. Sutch feared he might leap across the table, strike out in rage.

He reached out—then stopped.

Feversham’s voice cut through the tension.

“Queer things happen. Two of them. You can only say they’re true—and pray you forget them. But you can’t explain. Because you can’t understand.”

Sutch hesitated, then laid a hand on Harry’s shoulder.

“Can you?” he asked.

He regretted it instantly.

Harry turned—slowly—looked into Sutch’s eyes. Not with guilt. With stillness. With depth.

No answer.

But Feversham snapped, “Understand? How should he? He’s a Feversham.”

Sutch’s gaze locked with the general’s. And in that instant, he knew—*the lie*. The father was blind. The son bore none of his traits.

Harry had Muriel’s eyes—dark, haunted. Her forehead, her profile, her imagination. He was not like any Feversham before him.

“Look at the clock, Harry,” Feversham said.

The hour was up.

Harry rose, drew breath. “Good night, sir,” he said, and walked to the door.

The servants were asleep. The hall was black, gaping like a mouth.

For a moment, he hesitated—half-stepping back as if afraid of the dark.

Then he stepped through.

Behind him, the decanter passed. Bottles popped. Conversation resumed.

Harry was forgotten—except by Sutch.

He sat, disturbed. Then, driven by impulse, he slipped out noiselessly, closing the door behind him.

In the hall, Harry stood, candle held high. The flame danced, illuminating the line of Feversham portraits—soldiers from every age, in every uniform: lace collars, bucket boots, steel breastplates, shakos, swallowtails. All men of iron—lean, stern, with narrow brows and cold blue eyes. Men of courage, yes. But no subtlety. No imagination. Men who fought, not thought.

To Harry, they were judges.

He stood before them—like a criminal awaiting sentence. He read condemnation in their unblinking stares.

Sutch understood why the flame flickered. No wind stirred. But the boy’s hand shook.

Finally, as if hearing the verdict, Harry bowed—slowly, deeply—to the wall.

As he raised his head, he saw Sutch in the doorway.

No start. No word. Just stillness. Waiting.

Sutch felt the awkwardness. He spoke carefully—equal to a comrade.

“Harry,” he said, “we meet tonight for the first time. But I knew your mother long ago. I’d call her… friend. Is there anything you want to tell me?”

“Nothing,” Harry said.

“Sometimes,” Sutch said, “just saying it helps.”

“Thank you,” Harry replied. “You’re kind.”

Sutch took out his card. “Here’s my address. Perhaps one day you’ll come hunting with me. A few days. My invitation stands.”

For a second, pain crossed Harry’s face—fleeting, buried.

“Thank you, sir,” he repeated. “You’re very kind.”

“And if ever you need someone to talk to—someone older—I’m here.”

He spoke formally, deliberately, so Harry wouldn’t think him mocking.

Harry took the card. Said his thanks again.

Then he went upstairs.

Sutch waited in the hall until the candlelight vanished.

Something was wrong. He knew it. He should have said more. But he hadn’t known how.

He returned to the dining room, filled his glass, and called for silence.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “today is June 15th.”

Applause. Rapping on tables.

“It’s the anniversary of our attack on the Redan. And it’s Harry Feversham’s birthday. Our work is done. Let us drink to the future. To one of the young men who are replacing us. May Harry carry on the Feversham tradition. May he add glory to a noble name!”

The room erupted.

“Harry Feversham! Harry Feversham!”

They shouted it again and again. The glasses rang. Feversham sat flushed with pride.

High in the house, Harry lay awake.

He heard the muffled chorus—*“For he’s a jolly good fellow…”*—and believed they were drinking his father’s health.

He turned over, shivering.

In his mind: a broken officer sneaking through London streets at night. A tent flap lifted. A man dead in his blood, a lancet clutched in his hand.

And the faces—both faces—were the same.

The face of the coward. The face of the suicide.

And both were his.

The face of Harry Feversham.

Thirteen years had passed, and in the same month of June, Harry Feversham’s health was toasted once more—though this time in quieter surroundings and among fewer guests. The gathering took place in a high-rise flat atop a grim, fortress-like building that loomed over Westminster like a silent sentinel. To a stranger crossing St. James’s Park at night, the tiered glow of lit windows might have seemed less like a city and more like a mountain range pierced by firelight, where unseen workers toiled deep within.

On the tenth floor of this structure, Harry had settled for his annual leave from his regiment in India. It was here, in the warm, dimly furnished dining room of his flat, that the evening’s small celebration unfolded. The air was thick with smoke and quiet conversation, the chill of early summer making the fire crackle warmly in the hearth. A bay window, its blinds drawn back, framed a sweeping view of London below—its streets aglow, its silence broken only by distant hums and echoes.

Four men sat around the table. Harry Feversham had changed little since his youth, save for a neatly trimmed fair moustache that contrasted sharply with his dark hair. He stood now as a man of average height, lean and strong, built like an athlete, yet his face remained unchanged—the same features that had once been studied so closely by Lieutenant Sutch. Two of his companions were fellow officers on leave: Captain Trench, a compact man with a receding hairline and sharp, intelligent eyes that darted quickly from one thought to another; and Lieutenant Willoughby, whose appearance suggested dullness—a round forehead, a broad nose, and eyes that seemed lost in distant thoughts. He spoke rarely, and when he did, it was often about something long forgotten, twisting his upward-curled moustache as if wrestling with invisible ideas. He wasn’t clever, but stubborn beyond reason—so stubborn that even his own mistakes could not be acknowledged. One might dismiss him at first glance, but second thoughts would reveal a mind that clung fiercely to its few convictions.

The fourth man was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham’s closest friend. He had come in response to a telegram—arriving just in time for dinner.

It was June 1882, and across England, civilians fretted over events unfolding in Egypt. Soldiers, meanwhile, felt a stirring excitement. Arabi Pasha, defying warnings, had fortified Alexandria. And farther south, along the Nile, a new storm gathered. A year earlier, a young Dongolawi named Mohammed Ahmed had begun preaching through the villages, declaring himself a prophet—the coming savior of the people. His message caught fire among those oppressed under Turkish rule. By 1882, he had proclaimed himself the Mahdi and won his first victories against Ottoman forces.

“There will be trouble,” said Trench, and the words became the theme of the evening’s talk.

But Harry Feversham broke the rhythm with a different announcement. “I’m glad you all could join me tonight,” he began, addressing Durrance. “I sent a wire to Castleton too—he’s one of ours—but he’s dining with someone important in the War Office and then heading to Scotland. Couldn’t make it. But I’ve got news.”

The others leaned forward, still preoccupied with war.

“I only arrived in London this morning from Dublin,” Harry continued, a faint blush coloring his cheeks. “Been there for several weeks.”

Durrance looked up slowly. “Yes?” he asked, steady-eyed.

“I’ve come back engaged to be married.”

Durrance raised his glass. “Well, here’s luck to you, Harry,” he said simply. That was all. No flourish, no sentiment—yet Feversham heard everything. Their friendship was unspoken, profound. It didn’t need words. It was a bond forged in shared duty and mutual respect, solid as steel, never worn down by time or circumstance. Neither man ever spoke of it directly, nor would they ever mention the sacrifices such loyalty demanded. They understood: if needed, each would give without hesitation—and never ask for thanks.

“Thank you, Jack,” Feversham said. “I’m grateful. You introduced me to Ethne—I’ll never forget that.”

Durrance set his glass down gently. Silence followed. He stared at the tablecloth, hands resting lightly on the edge. Then, quietly: “Yes. I did you a good turn.”

He hesitated, as if about to say more, but Captain Trench cut in with his brisk, practical tone.

“Will this change anything?” he asked.

Feversham paused, drawing his cigar back into his mouth. “You mean—will I leave the service?”

“I don’t know,” Feversham replied slowly. And Durrance rose abruptly, moving to the window, his back turned.

Feversham took it as a rebuke and addressed the other man’s back. “I don’t know,” he repeated. “It needs thinking. There’s my father, my career… and then there’s her father—Dermod Eustace.”

“He wants you to quit your commission?” Willoughby asked.

“Probably,” Trench said with a smirk. “Irishmen tend to distrust authority. But do you have to agree with him, Feversham?”

“It’s not just that.” Feversham kept his gaze fixed on Durrance’s silhouette. “Dermod’s old. His lands are failing. There are other things. You know, Jack?”

Durrance answered without turning. “Yes, I know.” Then, almost automatically: “If you want whiskey, rap twice on the floor. The servants understand.”

“Precisely,” Feversham said.

He went on, choosing his words carefully, still watching his friend through the others’ shoulders. “And then there’s Ethne herself. Dermod gave her that name well—she *is* of her land. She belongs to Donegal—the smell of peat, the rivers, the hills. I don’t think she’d ever be happy far from it. Not in India. Not anywhere beyond reach of home. That has to count.”

He waited. No reply came.

Durrance wasn’t reproaching him. He was simply staring out at the city—Pall Mall’s glare climbing into the sky, the chain of lights rising northward, the low rumble of traffic like a distant thunder. Below, St. James’s Park lay dark and still, a black pool amid the glitter. Durrance longed to step outside, to vanish into that quiet space. But he couldn’t. So he stayed, leaning his forehead against the glass, hoping Feversham would keep talking.

Because Durrance knew what was coming—the kind of sacrifice that must never be spoken aloud.

Feversham did speak again. And though Durrance didn’t listen, Captain Trench listened closely. What Harry said was not evasion—it was careful reflection. In the end, Trench nodded.

“Well, I drink to you, Feversham,” he said, raising his glass. “With all due respect.”

“Me too, old man,” Willoughby added, following suit.

They drank. As their empty glasses clinked together, a knock sounded at the door.

The three guests glanced up. Durrance turned from the window. Feversham said, “Come in.”

His servant entered, handing him a telegram.

Feversham tore open the envelope casually, read the message, and froze. His face grew solemn. He sat motionless, eyes fixed on the pink slip of paper. Time stretched. The room fell utterly silent.

The others avoided his gaze. Durrance returned to the window. Willoughby twisted his moustache, staring at the ceiling. Trench turned his chair toward the fire, studying the flames.

It was as if misfortune had followed joy to the doorstep.

“There is no answer,” Harry said, and lapsed into silence.

Once, he lifted his head and looked at Trench, as if to speak—but thought better of it. He returned to the telegram, lost in thought.

Then, from outside, came the sudden sound of drums and fifes—sharp, clear, echoing through the open window. The tattoo from Wellington Barracks rang out, cutting through the hush. It swelled, then faded as the band marched away, growing soft and distant.

Feversham didn’t move. But his expression shifted—now he was listening, deeply, as if the music carried meaning.

Years later, each of the three men would recall that moment—the firelit room, the open window framing London’s lights, Harry seated with the telegram before him, the music fading into silence. The image would remain vivid, though its meaning remained hidden.

Then, just as the tattoo ended, Feversham rose suddenly. He crumpled the telegram, tossed it into the fire, and leaned back against the chimney piece.

“I don’t know,” he said again—quietly, firmly—as if dismissing the message entirely, returning to the weight of his earlier decision.

The silence broke.

The fire seized the paper. It writhed, unrolled briefly—revealing a glimpse of words in the flame—then curled and blackened into ash.

Captain Trench watched it burn.

“You’re going back to Dublin?” Durrance asked, stepping back into the room.

“To Dublin? No. I’m going to Donegal in three weeks. There’s a dance. You should come.”

“I’m not sure I can,” Durrance replied. “There’s a chance—if things go south in the East—I might be called to the staff.”

The conversation drifted back to war and peace, lingering until the Westminster clock struck eleven.

Trench stood. Willoughby and Durrance followed.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” Durrance said.

“As usual,” Harry replied.

The three left, walking across the park. At Pall Mall, they parted. Durrance headed up St. James’s Street. Trench and Willoughby crossed into St. James’s Square.

There, Trench slipped his arm through Willoughby’s—an unusual gesture for such a reserved man.

“You know Castleton’s address?” he asked.

“Albemarle Street,” Willoughby said. “Number 47.”

“He leaves Euston at twelve. It’s ten past eleven now. Are you curious, Willoughby? I confess—I am. I’m methodical, and when a man gets a telegram telling him to tell Trench something—and tells him nothing—I become a philosopher of curiosity. Castleton is the only other officer of our regiment in London. Likely, the telegram came from him. And he was dining with someone important in the War Office. If we take a cab to Albemarle Street, we might catch him just as he steps out.”

Willoughby, though baffled, agreed heartily. “I think it would be prudent,” he said, flagging down a passing hansom.

A moment later, the two men were riding toward Albemarle Street—driven by the quiet urgency of unanswered questions.

Durrance walked alone to his lodgings, the memory of a day two years past rising in his mind. It was then, by a strange whim of old Dermod Eustace, that he had been summoned against his will to the house beside the Lennon River in Donegal. There, unexpectedly, he met Dermod’s daughter, Ethne. She surprised everyone who first spoke with her father—though not for reasons one might expect. Durrance stayed just one night, but that evening remained etched in his memory. Seated with her back to the room as she played, Ethne drew melodies from her violin that seemed to speak not only of music, but of life itself. The strings responded to the touch of her bow like thoughts given voice.

One piece in particular—the *Melusine* overture—carried the mournful sigh of ocean waves. As he listened then, Durrance felt the instrument tell stories far beyond what its player could know: tales of long journeys across uncharted seas, of distant lands under moonlit skies, of voices calling from the edges of deserts. The music deepened into something more profound—of joys too vast to grasp, and sorrows so great they carried a kind of dignity. It whispered of longing without bitterness, of dreams untouched by words. Yet never once did it sound like complaint.

That night, while Ethne’s face remained turned away, Durrance sensed a truth in the music: true art does not lament. And now, knowing he would never again see her face turned toward him, that truth echoed in his heart.

The next morning, he rode into the Row, his blue eyes bright beneath a bronzed complexion, no less friendly than ever. He waited at the familiar clump of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the sand—but Harry Feversham did not appear. Not that morning, nor for the following three weeks. Since their days at Oxford, it had been their custom to meet here when both were in town. Now, Durrance felt a quiet unease. Had he lost his friend?

Meanwhile, news of war grew certain. When Feversham finally arrived, Durrance had news of his own.

“I told you luck might favor me,” he said, his voice tinged with excitement. “Well, it has. I’m going to Egypt on General Graham’s staff. We might even push down the Red Sea to Suakin afterward.”

His enthusiasm sparked a flicker of envy in Feversham’s eyes—something Durrance found strange, even at that moment of triumph. Yet it brought a quiet pleasure, as though the world still held meaning through friendship.

“It’s rough on you,” Durrance said gently, “that your regiment stays behind.”

Feversham rode beside him in silence. Then, as they reached the shaded chairs beneath the trees, he spoke.

“That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers.”

“The night?” Durrance asked, turning sharply in his saddle.

“After we’d gone,” Feversham corrected.

Durrance said nothing. But he wondered if the timing had been intentional. Still, he let it pass.

Then, suddenly, he laughed.

“I remember—you gave us your reasons that night. But honestly, I can’t help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?”

“To-night.”

“So soon?”

They turned their horses and rode westward along the tree-lined path. The morning air was crisp and fresh. The lime and chestnut trees still wore their early green, and because May had come late that year, delicate white blossoms clung to the branches like snow, glowing red against dark rhododendrons. Sunlight shimmered over the park, and the distant roar of the city sounded like rushing water.

“It’s been ages since we swam in Sandford Lasher,” Durrance remarked.

“Or froze in the Easter holidays in the big snow-gully on Great End,” Feversham replied.

Both men felt it—the end of an era. This ride marked the closing of a chapter in their lives, one filled with shared laughter, adventures, and camaraderie. They didn’t know what lay ahead, and so they looked back, savoring the memories before putting them away.

“You must stay with us when you return, Jack,” Feversham said.

Durrance kept his expression steady, though his hand tightened slightly on the reins—a detail unseen by his friend.

“If I come back,” he said quietly. “You know my creed. I could never pity a man who died on active service. I’d rather die that way myself.”

It was a simple belief, rooted in the honesty of the man who spoke it. To him, dying with honor was worth more than many years of life lived safely. He said it without sorrow, without fear. Yet he feared his friend might misread the words.

He glanced at Feversham—and saw again that same puzzling look of envy.

“There are worse things,” Durrance continued, “like being disabled. Clever men might manage. But what would I do if I had to sit in a chair all my life? Just thinking about it makes me shiver.” He shook his broad shoulders as if to shake off the thought.

“Well,” he said, “this is our last ride. Let’s gallop.”

And so they raced down the sand, side by side, until they reached the bottom of the Row. There, they stopped, shook hands, exchanged a brief nod, and parted. Feversham rode out of the park. Durrance turned back, walking his horse slowly toward the shaded seats.

Even as a boy in Southpool, Devonshire—on the wooded banks of the Salcombe estuary—he had always felt a restless pull toward the sea. A dream of foreign lands, strange peoples, and endless horizons stirred within him. That restlessness had only grown stronger. Though he had inherited Guessens, with its farms and land, it had never truly felt like home—only a place to return to, not a life to live.

Now, he deliberately amplified that restlessness, contrasting it with words Feversham had spoken—words Durrance knew to be true. Ethne Eustace would likely never thrive outside her beloved Donegal. So perhaps, even if fate had been different, there would have been conflict. Maybe it was better this way—Harry Feversham marrying Ethne, and not someone else.

As he rode, Durrance argued with himself, until the riders vanished from sight and the ladies in colorful dresses faded beneath the trees. The trees themselves thinned into ragged mimosa, the sand beneath his feet spread wide and golden like honey. Black stones began to pile up like coal, glinting in the sun like mirrors.

He was deep in visions of the Sudan when a soft voice called his name.

He looked up and found himself near the railings.

“How do you do, Mrs. Adair?” he said, stopping his horse.

She offered her hand across the rails. She was his neighbor from Southpool, a few years older—tall, with thick brown hair in many shades, and a pale, almost translucent complexion. But now, her face had warmed; a faint blush touched her cheeks.

“I have news for you,” she said. “Two special items. First—Harry Feversham is to be married.”

“To whom?” she asked eagerly.

“You should know. It was in your house in Hill Street that he first met her. I introduced them. He’s been cultivating the acquaintance in Dublin.”

Mrs. Adair already understood—and clearly, the news pleased her.

“Ethne Eustace!” she exclaimed. “When will they marry?”

“There’s nothing to stop it.”

“I’m glad,” she murmured, as though relieved. “What’s your second item?”

“As good as the first. I’m joining General Graham’s staff.”

Mrs. Adair fell silent. Anxiety crept into her eyes, and the color drained from her face.

“You’re very glad, I suppose?” she asked slowly.

Durrance’s tone left no doubt.

“I should think so. I’m leaving soon—sooner the better. I’ll come dine with you one night before I go.”

“My husband will be pleased to see you,” she said, a little coldly.

But Durrance didn’t notice. He was too caught up in his own excitement, pouring out his feelings not so much for her benefit, but to affirm them to himself. He barely knew her—just that she was strikingly beautiful in a foreign way common along the Devon and Cornish coasts, with fine hair and always well-dressed. She was kind, and she had befriended Ethne Eustace—that was enough for him.

But he was about to learn more of Mrs. Adair.

He rode out of the park, carrying the old ache of parting—his fortune and his friend’s now irrevocably split. In truth, that very morning, he had unknowingly begun weaving a new thread that would bind them again—in ways neither could imagine.

Mrs. Adair followed him out of the park, walking home in quiet thought.

Durrance had just one week to prepare—packing his gear, settling affairs at Guessens. The days passed in a blur of haste, and each evening, his newspaper lay unfolded in his rooms, unread.

General Graham was traveling overland to Brindisi. On a stormy July night, Durrance stepped from the Dover pier onto the mailboat bound for Calais.

Despite the rain and the gloom, a small crowd had gathered to see the general off. As the ropes were cast loose, a weak cheer rose—then died quickly.

Durrance leaned on the bulwarks, staring into the darkness. Was this his last view of England? He wished someone had come to say goodbye. And then, suddenly—it seemed his wish was granted.

Under a gaslamp on the quay, he glimpsed a figure—tall, unmistakable in build and bearing. It was Harry Feversham.

He rubbed his eyes and looked again. But the wind flickered the flame, the rain blurred the scene. All he could be sure of was a man standing there, a pale face dimly visible beneath the light.

An illusion, he told himself. Feversham was probably listening to Ethne play under a clear sky in Donegal.

Yet as he turned away, the wind stilled. The lights burned bright and steady. And the face leaped from the shadows—clear, distinct, full of pain.

Durrance leaned forward.

“Harry!” he shouted, voice cracking with surprise.

But the figure did not move.

The wind blew again, the paddles churned the water, and the boat pulled away from the pier.

It was an illusion, he repeated. A coincidence. A stranger who looked like Feversham.

But the face he had seen—briefly, clearly—was not the face of his friend. It was haggard, hollow-eyed, worn with a terrible sorrow. The face of a man cast aside, broken by some unseen burden.

Durrance had been too busy to remember the telegram. His newspaper lay open in his room. But now, as the boat sailed into the night, he realized with a start: Harry Feversham had come to see him off.

Feversham had traveled to Dublin by the night mail after his ride with Durrance along the Row. The next morning, he crossed Lough Swilly aboard a small cargo steamer that made the weekly journey up the Lennon River as far as Ramelton. On the quay, Ethne waited for him in her dog-cart, offering her hand and a smile that carried the warmth of familiarity.

“You’re surprised to see me,” she said, catching the flicker of astonishment on his face.

“I always am,” he replied. “Because you always exceed what I imagine you to be.” Her smile deepened—no longer just friendly, but something richer, more intimate.

“I’ll drive slowly,” she said once his belongings were secured in the cart. “I didn’t bring a groom on purpose. There’ll be guests arriving tomorrow. We have only today.”

They moved along the wide causeway beside the river, then turned into the narrow, steep street of Ramelton. Feversham sat quietly at her side. It was his first visit to the town, and he watched it all with quiet wonder—the dark forest rising on the opposite bank, the ancient grey bridge, the water singing over shallow stones, and the sleepy stillness of the place. He felt a strange sense of belonging, as though this quiet corner of Ireland belonged to Ethne, and thus, in some way, to him too.

She was twenty-one, tall and strong, with shoulders broad and balanced for her height. She lacked the exaggerated sway favored by earlier generations, yet moved with a grace so natural it seemed effortless—light-footed as a deer. Her hair was dark brown, coiled neatly at the nape of her neck. A healthy flush warmed her cheeks, and her eyes, a clear, steady grey, met others’ gazes with openness and honesty. Her character mirrored her appearance: honest, direct, grounded in strength but softened by gentleness. She had no cruelty in her nature, though she had courage that spoke louder than words.

There was a story still told in Ramelton about her bravery—one Feversham never heard without a shiver. Once, descending the steep hill toward the river, her horse startled at the clatter of a pail and bolted. The reins slipped from her hands before she could grasp them. Trapped in the cart, she faced the danger not with panic, but calm resolve. As the vehicle lurched down the slope, she climbed over the front, balancing along the shafts, reached the horse’s neck, and brought it to a stop just ten yards before the curve of the bridge. But like all strengths, hers had its shadows—even if Feversham did not yet know them.

For much of the drive, Ethne was nearly as silent as he. When she did speak, it was with a distant air, as though her thoughts were elsewhere. Not until they left the town and reached the open road toward Letterkenny did she turn to him suddenly and say:

“This morning I saw your regiment had been ordered from India to Egypt. You could have gone with it—if I hadn’t stood in your way. There would have been chances for honor. I’ve stopped you, and I’m truly sorry. Of course, you couldn’t have known, but I understand how hard it must be to stay behind. I blame myself.”

Feversham stared ahead, then spoke in a voice thick with emotion.

“You needn’t.”

“But how can I help it?” she pressed. “I blame myself even more because I don’t see things like other women do. Suppose you’d gone to Egypt—and the worst happened? I’d have felt lonely, yes, for all my days. But I’d have known, beyond doubt, that when those days ended, we’d meet again.”

She spoke plainly, without drama, as if stating an unshakable truth. Feversham caught his breath as if struck. Yet her eyes held his, unwavering. He remained still, unable to answer. Then she continued:

“You see, I bear absence better than most. I don’t feel I’ve lost anyone I care about. People come into our lives—sometimes in a dull way, sometimes suddenly. One face rises from a crowd, and you know instantly—it’s a friend. Or rather, you recognize it, even if you’ve never seen it before. Like finding someone long sought, now found. Such friends are rare—but they’re the ones that matter. And we don’t lose them, whether they’re away… or gone.”

“Unless,” Feversham said slowly, “you’ve made a mistake. What if that face in the crowd is a mask? Then you might be wrong.”

Ethne shook her head firmly.

“Not like that. You may think you’ve made a mistake, and perhaps for years. But in time, you’ll know you haven:’t.”

Her certainty gripped him, twisted him. He could no longer remain silent.

“Ethne,” he began, “you don’t know—”

But at that moment, she reined in the horse, laughed, and pointed with her whip.

They had reached the crest of a hill, two miles from Ramelton. To the left, stone walls bordered open fields; to the right, a grove of oaks and beeches. A bright red letter box was set into the wall—and that was where she pointed.

“I wanted to show you this,” she interrupted. “This is where I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times.”

And so, Feversham let the words die on his lips.

“The house is behind the trees to the right,” she added.

“The letter-box is very convenient,” he said.

“Yes,” she replied, driving on until she stopped where the park wall had collapsed. “That’s where I used to climb over to send the letters. There’s a tree on the other side—just as handy as the box. I’d run down the half-mile avenue at night.”

“There might have been thieves,” he said sharply.

“There were thorns,” she answered, turning through the gates and heading toward the long, irregular grey house. “Well, we’ve still a day before the dance.”

“I suppose the whole countryside is coming,” he remarked.

“It daren’t do anything else,” she laughed. “My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away—just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance here. By the way, Mr. Durrance sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin.”

The door opened, and an old man stepped out—thin, lank, with a fierce, bird-like face. His expression softened when he saw Feversham, and a faint smile played across his lips. A stranger might have thought he winked—but his left eyelid drooped perpetually over the eye.

“How do you do?” he said. “Glad to see you. Make yourself at home. If you want whiskey, stamp twice on the floor. The servants know,” and with that, he retreated back inside.

* * * * *

The biographer of Dermod Eustace would need caution. Though less than twenty years have passed since his death, he has already become legend. Tales cling to him like barnacles, and any storyteller in the region can invent a tale and attach it to Dermod, knowing it will be believed. Still, certain truths endure. He upheld an old-fashioned, tyrannical hospitality—keeping his house open to all travelers, even forcing strangers to dine and sleep under his roof, as Durrance had discovered. He was a man of another age, glaring at a world he despised, reconciling himself only after generous drink. He was like an intoxicated Coriolanus—believing people should be ruled with a rod, yet always respectful, especially toward women. The townsfolk of Ramelton took pride in saying that, even at his worst—galloping down the cobbled streets on his seventeen-hand white mare, accompanied by his collie dog, swaying drunk but somehow staying in the saddle—he had never knocked down a woman. Two facts stand out: he feared his daughter, who kept him perpetually guessing whether she was angry or not, and he had grown fond of Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of Dermod that day. The old man retired to his office, while Feversham and Ethne spent the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon River. It was a peaceful afternoon, hushed as a Sunday. From the house, lawns sloped steeply down to a valley where the river flowed swiftly, dark and shaded by overhanging branches. There was a waterfall where the water slid smoothly over rocks, appearing solid except at one point where a rocky spur caused it to break back in a golden wave, glowing in the sunlight.

They sat opposite the spur for hours, talking softly, mostly listening to the rush of water and watching its endless motion. At last, sunset came, casting long shadows. They rose, exchanged smiles, and walked slowly back to the house. It was an afternoon Feversham would remember forever—for the next night was the ball.

As the band struck up the fourth waltz, Ethne left her place at the drawing-room door, took Feversham’s arm, and led him into the hall.

The hall was empty. The front door stood open, letting in the cool summer night. Music drifted from the ballroom, mingling with the laughter of dancers. Ethne breathed in relief at being freed from her duties. Then, releasing his arm, she crossed to a side table.

“The post has arrived,” she said. “Letters—three for you—and a small box.”

She handed him a tiny white jeweler’s box, sealed and tied. He frowned.

“It must be empty,” she said, noting its lightness.

Yet it was carefully closed. Feversham broke the seal and untied the string. He read the address—it had been forwarded from his lodgings. The handwriting was unfamiliar.

“There’s been a mistake,” he said, opening the lid. Then he froze.

Three white feathers fluttered out, floating gently in the air before settling like snowflakes on the polished floor. They lay pale and still. But Feversham’s face had turned ashen.

He stood staring, transfixed, until he felt a light touch on his arm. He looked down and saw Ethne’s gloved hand resting on his sleeve.

“What does it mean?” she asked. Her voice held confusion, but no fear. Her smile lingered, her eyes full of trust. She believed his next word would explain everything.

“That there are things which cannot be hidden,” he said.

For a moment, Ethne said nothing. The music floated into the hall, and leaves rustled outside. Then she touched his arm gently, laughed softly, and spoke as if pleading with a child.

“I don’t think you understand, Harry. Three white feathers. Sent in jest? Of course, in jest. But what a cruel joke—”

“They were sent in deadly earnest.”

He looked straight into her eyes. Ethne dropped her hand.

“Who sent them?” she asked.

Feversham hadn’t considered that. The message was everything. The men who sent it meant nothing. But Ethne took the box from him. Inside, three visiting cards lay at the bottom. She read them aloud.

“Captain Trench, Mr. Castleton, Mr. Willoughby. Do you know them?”

“All three were officers in my old regiment.”

She looked stunned. Kneeling, she gathered the feathers into her palm, as if touching them might bring understanding. They rested on her glove. She blew gently, and they rose, danced in the air, then drifted down. She caught them again, slowly forming her next question.

“Were they justly sent?” she asked.

“Yes,” said Harry Feversham.

He offered no denial. He simply accepted the horror he had long dreaded—the truth now written in public. He was known as a coward. The word that had burned in his mind now stood exposed. He stood before her, mute, condemned. But Ethne denied it—still kneeling.

“I don’t believe it,” she said. “You couldn’t look me in the eye like this if it were true. Your eyes would look down, not at mine.”

“Yet it is.”

“Three little white feathers,” she whispered. Then, with a sob in her throat, “This afternoon, we were under the elms by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry? Just you and I. And now come three little white feathers, and the world ends.”

“Oh, don’t!” he pleaded, his voice breaking. Until then, he had spoken steadily, matching his gaze. But her words—her memory, her sorrow—struck him like a blow. Yet Ethne seemed not to hear. She listened to the ballroom. Voices grew louder. She realized the music had stopped. Rising quickly, she clenched the feathers in her hand and opened a door—into her sitting room.

“Come,” she said.

He followed. She shut the door, blocking out the noise.

“Now,” she said, “will you tell me—why were the feathers sent?”

She stood calmly before him, pale but composed. Her expression gave no sign of anger, only a fierce desire for truth. She spoke quietly. He answered directly, without evasion.

“A telegram came. Castleton sent it. It reached me while Captain Trench and Mr. Willoughby were dining with me. It said my regiment would be ordered to Egypt. Castleton was dining with someone likely to know—I trusted the message. He told me to tell Trench. I didn’t. I thought it over. Castleton was leaving for Scotland that night—he’d go straight to rejoin the regiment. He wouldn’t see Trench for weeks. By then, the telegram might be forgotten. So I didn’t tell Trench. I burned the telegram and submitted my resignation that night. But Trench found out somehow. Durrance was at dinner too—God, Durrance!” he burst out. “He probably knows, like everyone else.”

It struck him anew—his friend Durrance, who once looked up to him, now likely saw him as a disgrace. But Ethne was speaking.

“And is this all?” she asked.

“Surely it is enough,” he said.

“I think not,” she replied, lowering her voice. “We agreed—we’d never let misunderstandings come between us. We promised frankness. So be frank with me. Please. I have a right to know. I ask this once in my life.”

There was a reason—something he remembered—but it seemed trivial against the weight of the truth. Ethne had released her grip. The feathers lay on the table. They couldn’t be explained away. He wore the label of cowardice. And he could never make her understand. But she deserved the explanation. She had asked with a generosity rare in women.

So Feversham gathered his thoughts.

“All my life, I’ve feared I’d act like a coward. I knew I was meant for the army. I kept it to myself. No one to tell. My mother was dead. My father—” He paused, breathing deeply. He saw his father now—alone, seated on the terrace, gazing over moonlit fields toward the Sussex Downs, dreaming of honors his son might win in Egypt. That stern heart would break. The full horror of what he’d done, the pain he’d caused, flooded over him. He dropped his head into his hands and groaned.

“My father,” he continued, “would never have understood. He faced danger without hesitation—but he didn’t foresee. That was my problem. I foresaw. Every risk, every peril—I saw it coming. I saw the possibility of cowardice. Then, one evening, during a Crimean night gathering, two stories were told—of an officer and a surgeon who both fled. I was confronted with the reality. I took those stories to bed. They never left me. I saw myself acting like them—failing in battle, bringing ruin to my country, dishonoring my father and all the ancestors whose portraits lined the hall. I tried to overcome my fears. I hunted—but with a map in my mind. I foresaw every hedge, every ditch, every treacherous bank.”

“Yet you rode straight,” Ethne interrupted. “Durrance told me so.”

“Did I?” Feversham mused. “Perhaps I did—once the hounds were off. But Durrance never knew what those waiting moments before the hunt meant to me. So when the telegram came, I took the chance it offered. I resigned.”

He had spoken cautiously—something still hidden. But Ethne sensed it.

“Were you afraid of disgracing me?” she asked. “Was I the reason you resigned?”

Feversham looked into her eyes and lied.

“No.”

“If you hadn’t been engaged to me, would you still have resigned?”

“Yes.”

Ethne slowly removed her glove. Feversham turned away.

“I think I’m like your father,” she said. “I don’t understand.” In the silence, he heard a soft rattle on the table. He looked—and saw her engagement ring lying there, the stones glinting.

“And all this—all you’ve told me,” she said suddenly, her face stern, “you would have hidden from me? You would have married me and kept it secret, had these feathers not come?”

The words had been ready from the start. She had withheld them, hoping he might offer a miracle of explanation. But now she struck, exposing the deepest betrayal. Feversham flinched. He said nothing—letting silence be his answer.

Yet Ethne was fair. She needed to know the full truth before she buried it.

“But yesterday,” she said, “you were going to tell me something. I stopped you to show you the letter-box.” She laughed, strangely hollow. “Was it about the feathers?”

“Yes,” Feversham answered, weary. What did it matter now? The feathers had come. Her ring lay there, blinking in the dim light.

“Yes,” he said. “You forced me to speak.”

“I remember,” she said, cutting in quickly. “About seeing each other again—afterward. We won’t speak of such things again.” Feversham swayed, as if about to fall.

“I remember,” she continued. “You said one could make mistakes. You were right. I was wrong. One can do more than seem to make them. Will you, please, take back your ring?”

Feversham picked it up, holding it in his palm. He stood still. Never had he loved her more, never seen her worth more—than in this moment of loss. She shone in the quiet room, radiant from the flowers in her hair to the white slipper on her foot. It was impossible to believe he had ever won her. And yet he had—only to lose her through disloyalty.

Then her voice broke in.

“These, too, are yours. Will you take them?”

She pointed with her fan to the feathers on the table. Feversham reached out—then pulled back in surprise.

“There are four,” he said.

Ethne said nothing. Looking at her fan, Feversham understood. It was ivory and white feathers. She had broken one off—added it herself—to the three.

The ballroom hummed with life, laughter, and the easy rhythm of conversation. Habit carried them through—each movement, each smile, each word spoken seemed to flow naturally, as though nothing had changed between them. At Lennon House that night, no one suspected the quiet fracture that had split their world in two. Harry Feversham watched Ethne move through the crowd, laughing, chatting, her face alight with joy. He found himself continually surprised by her apparent ease, never realizing that he, too, was wearing a mask—his own facade of cheerfulness carefully constructed to hide the storm within.

When she danced past him, her steps light and graceful, for a moment he almost believed her heart was truly in it. She seemed capable of summoning color to her cheeks at will, of controlling every expression, every breath. They even danced together, side by side, yet beneath the surface, both were weighed down by invisible burdens. Ethne felt the crushing weight of pain and shame pressing upon her, a burden she held up with trembling hands, knowing it could collapse at any moment. And Feversham? He felt the four feathers burning against his chest like live coals, hidden beneath his coat but scorching his soul.

It amazed him that no one else saw what he carried. Every time he approached a partner, he braced himself for the worst—for her to turn on him with the word that had become his name in her eyes: *coward*. Yet he did not fear it. In truth, he would have welcomed it. The truth had already been spoken in silence. He had lost Ethne. And though he looked around the room, searching among the guests for someone who might compare to her, he knew there was no one. Not one woman there—not pretty, not graceful, not beautiful—could hold a candle to her.

Her beauty was not merely physical. It was in the sweep of her forehead, the perfect arch of her brows, the steady gray of her eyes, the fullness of her lips that could soften into tenderness or harden with resolve. Her bearing was regal, her presence commanding. To Feversham, she stood apart—unmatched, unreplaceable. And now, watching her from afar, he was struck with a terrible, helpless wonder: how had he ever thought he could possess her?

Only once did her strength falter—and only for a heartbeat. They were dancing together when she glanced toward the windows. Beyond the blinds, the first pale streaks of dawn crept across the sky. “Look!” she whispered.

Feversham felt her body tense, then sag against him. Her face drained of color, turning ashen and weary. Her eyes closed tightly, then snapped open again. For a moment, he feared she would faint.

“The morning at last!” she breathed, her voice thin and tired. Then, softer still: “I wonder… whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain.”

“Hush,” he murmured, holding her steady. “Courage. Just a few minutes more—only a few!”

He stopped dancing, standing before her until her breath steadied, her color returned. When she finally spoke, it was with quiet gratitude. “Thank you.” And then, like a wheel caught in motion, they stepped back into the dance.

Strange, that he should be urging her to endure, and she thanking him for support. But neither noticed the irony—the fleeting reversal of roles. Ethne was too spent by the strain of the night, too close to breaking. And Feversham? He had seen the depth of her suffering in that single moment—the lines etched into her face, the anguish in her eyes. That look shattered whatever illusion he had left. He no longer said, *I have lost her.* He didn’t think of loss at all. Instead, he heard her words echoing in his mind: *I wonder whether it is right…* Those words would follow him forever, ringing in his ears, spoken in the tone of her voice, haunting him until the end. For it was not right. It was not just. And he knew it.

The ball ended soon after. The last carriage rolled away, and those staying at the house drifted to the smoking room or climbed the stairs to bed. But Feversham lingered in the hall, waiting. Ethne understood why.

“There’s no need,” she said, lighting a candle with her back turned to him. “I’ve told my father. Everything.”

He bowed his head, accepting her words without protest. “Still,” he said quietly, “I must wait and see him.”

She hesitated, then turned sharply, her brow furrowed in confusion. Waiting for her father under such circumstances—after what had happened—was an act of courage. And she, too, felt a tremor of dread as she heard the study door creak open and footsteps approach.

Dermod came forward, older than ever, his face lined with sorrow and bewilderment. He stood before Feversham, staring at him as if trying to understand a mystery beyond words. Twice he opened his mouth, but no sound came out. Finally, he turned to the table, lit his own candle, then Feversham’s. Then he turned back, stared again—longer this time—and Ethne took a step forward, ready to intervene. But Dermod said nothing. He simply picked up his candle.

“Well…” he began, and paused. He snipped the wick with scissors. “Well…” Again, he stopped.

He stared into the flame, silent, searching for something—anything—to say. But nothing came. He could not speak. Could not condemn. Could not forgive. So instead, he offered a strange, practical instruction.

“If you want any whiskey,” he said slowly, “stamp twice on the floor. The servants understand.”

Then he turned and walked heavily up the stairs.

His silence was worse than anger. His restraint—his refusal to confront, to curse, to break—was perhaps the cruelest punishment of all.

By the time Ethne was alone in her room, the sun had risen fully. She drew up the blinds, flung open the windows, and let the cool morning air rush in—like spring water washing over her skin. Outside, the world was still dim, untouched by color. The trees stood black against the sky, the paths white and ghostly, the lawns dull and gray beneath a veil of dew. Yet it was alive—noisy with birdsong, vibrant with the murmur of the Lennon River winding through the trees.

She stepped back from the window. There was much to do before sleep. With quiet determination, she resolved to sever every tie to Harry Feversham. She wanted no reminder—no object, no memory—that could bring him back into her thoughts. She began collecting his gifts: the books, the scarf, the trinkets he had given her over the years. Each item brought a memory—a shared laugh, a quiet evening, a moment of closeness now shattered.

But as she gathered them, her resolve weakened. The penknife he had lent her, forgotten and returned later—she reached for it, then dropped it quickly. Superstition, she thought, though she didn’t believe in it. Better safe than sorry.

Instead, she chose his photograph. Small, unassuming, yet precious. She locked it away in a drawer, then packed the rest of the gifts into a box, sealed it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall for the servants to send off.

Back in her room, she took the letters he had written—pages filled with love, hope, promises. She placed them on the hearth, one by one, forming a small pile. Then she lit a match.

The flames rose slowly, consuming the paper, blackening the ink like spilled ink, curling the edges until all that remained were ashes—fine, white flakes, drifting like feathers.

She sat upright in her chair, watching them burn, until the last spark died.

Then, from outside, came a cautious step on the gravel.

It was broad daylight, but her candle still burned. Instinctively, she reached out and extinguished it.

She sat still, listening. The blackbirds called from the trees. The river sang its endless song. Then—footsteps again, retreating.

And suddenly, despite everything she had done, despite the letters burned, the gifts sent away—she was overcome. Not by pain. Not by shame. But by loneliness.

She felt as though she sat atop a ruined world, vast and empty. Alone.

She rose quickly, her eyes falling on a violin case resting in the corner. With a sigh, she opened it.

A little while later, some of the guests stirred awake in their rooms. Through the corridors, soft music floated—melancholy, tender, played with deep feeling. A violin, low and loving, weaving a quiet lament.

Ethne did not know it was Durrance’s Guarnerius. She only knew she was no longer alone.

It was the night of August 30. A month had passed since the ball at Lennon House, and still the quiet countryside of Donegal buzzed with talk of Harry Feversham’s sudden vanishing. The townsfolk in their climbing street, the gentry at their dinner tables—everyone had a theory. Some swore they’d seen him that very morning after the dance, just before six o’clock—though Mrs. Brien O’Brien insisted it was ten minutes past. He was said to have been in full dress, pale as death, hurrying along the causeway by the Lennon Bridge. One man even suggested dragging the entire county with nets to find him. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, who lived near Rathmullen, refused salmon on principle, declaring he wasn’t a cannibal—a jest that quickly spread.

Yet none of their guesses came close to the truth. Only two people knew what had happened—and they carried on as if nothing had changed. Perhaps Ethne was a little quieter, Dermod a touch sharper in temper. But otherwise, life went on. Gossip ruled the day.

Meanwhile, Harry Feversham was in London. Lieutenant Sutch discovered this on the evening of the 30th. All day, the city had trembled under news of a great battle fought at Kassassin, east of Ismailia. Messengers raced through the streets, shouting victory and defeat in equal measure. There had been a moonlit charge by General Drury-Lowe’s Cavalry Brigade, which had crushed Arabi’s left flank and seized his guns. Rumors flew: an English general dead, the York and Lancaster Regiment broken. London was tense. By eleven o’clock, a silent crowd gathered beneath the gaslights of Pall Mall, staring up at the War Office windows. Every flicker behind the blinds sent a ripple through the crowd—hope, fear, breath held.

Sutch stood on the edge, leaning against the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club, guarding his wounded leg. Then a hand touched his arm. He turned—and there was Harry Feversham.

His face was drawn, fever-bright, his eyes burning. For a moment, Sutch couldn’t place him. “I might have been out there tonight,” Harry whispered, voice raw. “In Egypt. Sitting by a campfire, talking over the battle with Jack Durrance—or dead. What would it matter? I could have been there.”

The sight of him, the way he spoke—it told Sutch something terrible had gone wrong. But he asked no questions. Instead, he took Harry’s arm and led him away from the crowd.

“I saw you,” Harry said. “I wanted to speak to you. Do you remember—long ago—you gave me your card? I’ve kept it. I always feared I’d need it. You said—if one is in trouble, speaking might help.”

Sutch stopped. “Come with me. We’ll go inside. There’s a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room.” They were standing by the steps of the Army and Navy Club.

“Good God, not there!” Harry hissed, pulling back into the shadows. “Not tonight. It’s late. Tomorrow—after dark. Somewhere quiet. I don’t go out in daylight.”

Sutch didn’t argue. “I know a restaurant,” he said. “We can dine there at nine. No one we know will be there. Meet me at Swallow Street corner just before nine.”

They met again the next evening, seated in a far corner of the Criterion grill-room. Harry scanned the room nervously. “I come here often when I’m in town,” Sutch said, smiling faintly. “Listen—the hum of the electric lights… it sounds like a ship’s engines. Makes me feel like I’m back in the gun-room.”

He ordered dinner. “Then you’ll tell me everything.”

“You’ve heard nothing?” Harry asked, uneasy.

“Not a word.” And Harry exhaled—relief washing over him. He’d thought everyone knew. He’d imagined judgment in every passing face.

But now, under the dim light, Sutch saw him clearly. Harry was thin, hollow-eyed, restless. His hands shook slightly. His nerves were frayed. Between courses, he tried to begin—but Sutch waited until the cloth was cleared.

“Now,” he said, offering his cigar case. “Take your time, Harry.”

And so, without flinching or exaggerating, Harry told the whole story—from the Crimean night at Broad Place to the ball at Lennon House. He spoke slowly, deliberately, as if recounting someone else’s tale. Yet Sutch felt every word.

When Harry reached the part about hiding the telegram, Sutch raised his hand to his forehead and stayed there—his face hidden. He did not want Harry to see the scorn he might have felt. But Harry didn’t stop. He told it all, straight through.

Afterward, Sutch still kept his hand raised. He didn’t speak for a long while.

When he finally did, his voice cracked—not with anger, but with sorrow.

“I am much to blame,” he said. “I should have spoken that night at Broad Place. I held my tongue. I won’t forgive myself.”

He thought of Muriel Graham’s son—this young man who had destroyed himself out of pride, shame, and silence. He felt the weight of a duty unmet, though it had never been spoken aloud.

“You see,” Sutch continued, “I understood. Your father… I’m afraid he never would.”

“He never will,” Harry said.

“No,” Sutch agreed. “Your mother—had she lived—would have known. But few women do. Brute courage! Women make a god of it. That girl—Ethne—”

Harry interrupted. “Don’t blame her. I was deceiving her into marriage.”

Sutch lowered his hand. “Suppose you’d never met her. Would you still have resigned?”

“I think not,” Harry said slowly. “I wanted to be fair. I could risk disgracing my name—my father’s memory—even those men in the hall. But I couldn’t risk disgracing her.”

Sutch slammed his fist on the table. “If only I had spoken that night! Harry, why didn’t you let me? I might have saved you years of torment. Can you imagine—growing up with that fear alone? It makes me shiver. I might even have saved you from this.”

Sutch saw deeper into Harry’s mind than Harry saw himself. He understood the loneliness, the silence, the constant dread—how it had shaped him, how it had driven him to this point.

“Did you ever read *Hamlet*?” he asked.

“Of course.”

“But did you really consider it?” Sutch pressed. “The same flaw—foreseeing danger, imagining failure, punishing oneself—yet when the moment comes, action follows. Hamlet doesn’t fail. He excels. Because he thinks. Just as you did. ‘Am I a coward?’—do you remember? ‘Who calls me villain? Breaks my pate across? Plucks off my beard, and blows it in my face?’ That’s exactly it. If only I had spoken that night!”

A few patrons left the room. Sutch glanced around. Nearly empty. He checked his watch—eleven o’clock. Something must be decided tonight. Hearing the story wasn’t enough. What now? How would Harry rebuild his life? How could he hide this disgrace?

“You can’t stay in London,” Sutch said, shivering. “Hiding by day, sneaking by night—that’s too like…”

“Wilmington,” Harry finished quietly. “But Wilmington’s end won’t be mine. I won’t stay.”

He spoke with resolve. He already had a plan.

Sutch leaned forward. “Who knows about the feathers? Who else?”

“Trench, Castleton, Willoughby,” Harry began. “All three are in Egypt. They’ll likely keep quiet—for the honor of their regiment. Anyone else?”

“Dermod Eustace and Ethne.”

“They won’t speak.”

“And you… Durrance perhaps… and my father.”

Sutch stared. “You told your father?”

“No. I went to Surrey. I told him in person.”

Again, Sutch felt the sting of regret. “Why didn’t I speak that night?” he muttered. “You—going down to Surrey, facing your father, telling him that? Not writing—standing there, face to face? Harry, I call myself brave, but I couldn’t have done that.”

“It wasn’t pleasant,” Harry said simply. That was all he offered.

He then described the outcome: his father still sent his allowance—though he wouldn’t return home. “I didn’t mean to go back anyway.”

From his pocketbook, Harry pulled out four white feathers. He laid them on the table.

“You’ve kept them?” Sutch asked.

“I treasure them,” Harry replied. “To you, they’re symbols of disgrace. To me—they’re my chance to redeem it.”

He looked around, separated three feathers, pushed them forward, then leaned toward Sutch.

“What if I could make Trench, Castleton, and Willoughby take back their feathers—one by one? I don’t say it’s likely. Or even possible. But there’s a chance. And I’ll wait for it.”

He paused. “All three are in Egypt. I leave tomorrow.”

Sutch’s face lit with unexpected joy. This—he hadn’t seen coming. And yet, once he did, it was the only path. He ignored caution, logic, doubt. In his mind, the feathers were already returned.

“Still—there are difficulties,” Sutch said. “You’re a civilian. They’re soldiers. Surrounded by soldiers. Harder for you to approach.”

“But they don’t need to be in danger themselves,” Harry countered. “Just convinced the fault is repaired.”

“Oh, yes,” Sutch agreed. “There may be other ways.”

The idea had come to Harry suddenly—when Ethne handed him the fourth feather. He’d been ready to tear them apart. Then, in that moment, the plan formed. He’d thought it over these past weeks, listening to bugles from the barracks below his window.

“There’s no other way,” he said. “But it’s worth trying. Because—if they take back their feathers…” He took a deep breath, voice low, eyes fixed on the table. “…then maybe she might take hers back too.”

“Will she wait?” Sutch asked.

“No,” Harry said sharply. “I never thought of that. She doesn’t even suspect what I’m doing. I don’t want her to—until it’s done. My reason is different.” He hesitated, then spoke slowly. “Ethne said something to me the day before the feathers came—something sacred. I’ll tell you, because it’s what drives me. Without it, I might never have thought of this. It gives me hope. It may seem strange to you, Mr. Sutch—but believe me, it’s real. She said—when she knew only that my regiment was going to Egypt—she blamed herself for my resignation. She said—‘Had you fallen, although I would have felt lonely all my life, I would still have known that we would see each other—afterwards.’”

Harry looked down, voice barely above a whisper. “Do you understand? If this fault can be repaired—if I can reclaim those feathers—then perhaps… we might still see each other—afterwards.”

It was a strange thing to say over a stained tablecloth in a public restaurant. But neither man found it odd. They were speaking of life, death, honor—of things greater than setting or circumstance.

Sutch didn’t speak for a long time. When he finally did, Harry looked up—expecting mockery. But instead, he saw Sutch’s hand reaching out.

“When I come back,” Harry said, rising. He gathered the feathers and slipped them into his pocketbook. “I’ve told you everything. I wait for opportunity. The three may never come. Or only after many years. So I wanted just one person to know the truth—just in case I don’t return. If you hear I won’t come back… please tell my father.”

“Of course,” Sutch said. “But not everything. Not what I just said—about Ethne, about my reason. I don’t think he’d understand. Otherwise—no word. Promise.”

Sutch promised—once, then again, without hesitation. It was natural Harry should insist. A disclosure might sound like madness. And Sutch saw no reason to refuse.

So he pledged silence. And in that pledge, he bound himself. He realized—though Harry had died with his mission undone, Sutch would hide from the father what was best in the son. And that, he knew, was right. Because the father could not understand.

Sutch sat, lost in thought—back to the roots of it all: the mother’s early death, the father’s blindness, his own silence at Broad Place. “If only I had spoken,” he murmured. He dropped his cigar into his coffee cup, rose, and reached for his hat.

“Many things are irrevocable, Harry,” he said. “But we never know until we try. It’s always worth finding out.”

The next evening, Harry crossed to Calais. The night was wild—as fierce as the night Durrance had sailed. And like Durrance, Harry had a friend to see him off. As the packet boat swung away from the pier, the last thing Harry saw was Sutch’s face beneath a gaslamp.

The lieutenant stayed, watching until the throb of the paddles faded into silence. Then he limped through the rain to his hotel, aware—and deeply regretful—that he was growing old. It had been years since he’d felt such regret. Since Crimea, he’d lived on half-pay, waiting for age to arrive as a familiar guest. But tonight, he prayed only one thing: that he might live long enough to welcome back Muriel Graham’s son—with honor restored, and his great fault atoned.

“No one,” said Durrance, slipping his field-glasses into their leather case at his side.
“No one, sir,” Captain Mather echoed. “We’ll move forward.”

The scouts advanced ahead, the troops reformed into line, and the two seven-pounder mountain guns closed up behind. Durrance’s detachment of the Camel Corps descended from the shadowed ridge of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—into the open plateau of Sinkat. This was the final reconnaissance in strength before the evacuation of the eastern Sudan.

All morning, the camels had trudged slowly through a narrow ravine of shale, flanked by steep red cliffs. As the rocks receded, they gave way to scattered heaps of broken stone, a desolate landscape where not even a blade of grass or a tattered mimosa branch broke the barren monotony. Yet when they reached the valley below, the green shrubs of Sinkat offered a sudden relief—a verdant park amid the waste. The troopers sat up straighter in their saddles, alert now, as they crossed the plateau diagonally toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet. A silent column moved across a land that seemed to hold its breath.

It was eleven o’clock. The sun climbed high in a pale, cloudless sky, casting sharp, shrinking shadows on the sand. The ground shimmered white, like a beach in the Scilly Isles. There was no wind—no whisper through the foliage, no rustle in the leaves. Shadows lay motionless, so crisp and still they might have been left behind by some ancient storm. Only the clank of weapons, the soft thud of camel hooves, and the occasional flutter of pigeons disturbed the quiet.

But life stirred here—quietly, unseen. As the riders followed the smooth curves of sand between the bushes, like carriage paths through a garden, they would sometimes glimpse a herd of gazelle springing up from the earth, racing silently toward the hills—a flash of dappled brown and white vanishing into the distance.

“It’s strange,” Durrance murmured. “This is the very route the caravans once took southward to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka. The Suakis built summer houses here.”
“And there,” Mather added, pointing ahead, “Tewfik fought—and died with four hundred men.”

For three hours they rode across the plateau. It was May, and the sun beat down with unbearable intensity. Alertness faded. Men rocked drowsily in their saddles, longing for evening and the cool glow of stars. The camels moved with their peculiar, smirking gait, heads bobbing rhythmically—until suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance saw a ruined wall pierced by window openings framing the sky.

“The fort,” he said.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna captured and razed it. But in that time, nature had reclaimed the ruin. Trees had grown thick around it—on the rear, right, and left—so that it appeared only when you were upon it, much like Childe Roland discovering the Dark Tower. In front, the sand stretched clear to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees stood like sentinels, their dark branches spreading wide.

In the shade near the fort’s right front, the soldiers dismounted and prepared their meal. Durrance and Mather walked around the structure. At the southern corner, Durrance stopped.

“Hallo!” he said.

“Some Arab has camped here,” Mather observed, halting beside him.

A small pile of grey ashes rested on a blackened stone.
“And lately,” Durrance said.

Mather stepped into the crumbling archway and wandered through the roofless corridors. Durrance kicked the ashes with his boot. A charred twig glowed faintly red. He pressed down on it—tiny smoke curled upward.

“Very lately,” he muttered, then followed Mather inside.

Young saplings sprouted from every crack in the mud walls, from fissures in the floor, from the corners. Behind the fort rose a steep glacis and a deep ditch. Durrance sat on the parapet above the glacis, watching pigeons wheel overhead. He thought of Tewfik Bey, who must have stood here each day, scanning the pass from Suakin, hoping—like another general far to the south—for the gleam of sunlight on weapons that never came.

Mather sat beside him, lost in different thoughts.
“The Guards are already steaming out past the coral reefs toward Suez. In a week, it’ll be our turn,” he said. “What a cursed place this is.”

“I’ll come back,” Durrance replied simply.

“Why?” Mather asked.

“I like it. I like the people.”

Mather found the sentiment odd—but he knew it explained Durrance’s rise. Sympathy had given him patience, understanding. While others rushed ahead, Durrance had listened, learned, and earned the trust of the tribes. He understood their hatred of Turkish rule, their fervor under Osman Digna’s tyranny—real or feigned. That empathy had carried him farther than talent alone.

“I’ll return,” he said, “in three months. Because we all know—every Englishman in Egypt knows—this isn’t the end. I want to be here when the work begins again. I can’t stand unfinished things.”

The sun blazed. Soldiers lay in the shade, sleeping. Afternoon was as still as morning. For a long while, neither man spoke—drawn into silence by the surrounding hush.

Then Durrance’s gaze shifted from the hills. His eyes sharpened, fixed on the thickets beyond the glacis. He wasn’t thinking of Tewfik anymore. Without turning, he saw Mather looking in the same direction.

“What are you thinking about?” Durrance asked.

Mather laughed softly. “I was planning my first dinner back in London. Alone. At Epitaux. Watermelon to start. And you?”

“I was wondering why the pigeons keep circling one particular tree—beyond the ditch, to the right of two small bushes. Don’t point to it. Just watch.”

Around them, pigeons perched quietly, purple dots among the leaves. But over that one tree, they wheeled nervously, calling in alarm.

“We’ll draw that covert,” Durrance said. “Take a dozen men, surround it quietly.”

He stayed on the glacis, watching. Six soldiers crept in from the left, six from the right. Before they could close the ring, the branches shook violently—and an Arab burst from the undergrowth. Wearing a yellowish dammar roll around his waist, armed with a flat spear and a hide shield, he dashed out between the soldiers and sprinted a few yards across the plain.

Then Mather barked a command. The Arab stopped instantly—as if he’d heard it. No rifle was raised. He turned and walked calmly back to Mather.

They brought him to the glacis. He stood before Durrance with no arrogance, no fear.

“I am Abou Fatma, of the Kabbabish tribe,” he said in Arabic. “I am friendly to the English. I’m going to Suakin.”

“Why did you hide?” Durrance asked.

“It was safer. I knew you were my friends. But… did you know I was yours?”

Durrance paused. Then, swiftly: “You speak English.”

“Yes,” the Arab replied without hesitation. “I know a few words.”

“Where did you learn them?”

“In Khartum.”

After that, Durrance left the others and talked with Abou Fatma alone on the glacis. They spoke for nearly an hour. When it ended, the Arab descended the glacis, crossed the trench, and headed toward the hills.

Durrance ordered the march to resume. Water tanks were filled. Men refilled their zamshyehs—the thirst of afternoon was the worst of all. Camels groaned and snarled as they mounted. The detachment turned northwest, angling sharply from its morning path. It skirted the hills opposite the pass it had descended earlier.

The bushes thinned. The land grew black with stones, dotted sparsely with yellow-tasseled mimosas.

Durrance called Mather to his side.
“That Arab had a strange tale. He was Gordon’s servant in Khartum. In early 1884—eighteen months ago—Gordon gave him a letter to take to Berber. From there, it was to be telegraphed to Cairo. But Berber had just fallen when he arrived. He was arrested the next day. But during that single free day, he hid the letter in a house wall. As far as he knows, it’s still there.”

“He’d have been questioned if it had been found,” Mather said.

“Exactly. And he wasn’t. He escaped three weeks ago. Curious, isn’t it?”

“And the letter remains buried?”

“Possibly. But he had the chain marks on his ankles.”

The cavalcade turned left, climbing into the northern hills, over shale again.

“A letter from Gordon,” Durrance mused. “Maybe scribbled on the rooftop of his palace, beside his telescope—written in haste, his eye already back to the lens, scanning the palm trees for the smoke of steamers. And it travels down the Nile to be hidden in a mud wall in Berber. Yes—it’s curious.”

He turned his face westward, watching the sun dip behind the hills. The sky darkened rapidly to violet; in the west, fire and gold exploded into a blaze of color. Then the fury softened, blending into a delicate rose. That too faded, leaving a sky of pure emerald green, lit from beneath the world’s rim.

“If only they had let us go west last year—to the Nile,” Durrance said, almost pleading. “Before Khartum fell. Before Berber surrendered. But they wouldn’t.”

The sunset’s beauty meant nothing to him now. The letter’s story had struck deep. He thought of Gordon—the honest, great, impractical soldier—despised by officials, undermined by intrigue, a man of few ties, yet relentless in his duty, knowing full well that every step forward was undone the moment he turned away.

Darkness settled. The camels quickened their pace. Cicadas shrieked from every tuft of grass. The detachment moved down toward the well of Disibil.

That night, Durrance lay awake on his camp bed beneath the stars. He forgot the letter. Southward, the Southern Cross hung slanted in the sky. Above, the Great Bear curved like a silver arc. In a week, he would sail for England. He counted the years since the packet had left Dover Pier—and found the tally satisfying.

Kassassin. Tel-el-Kebir. The rush down the Red Sea. Tokar. Tamai. Tamanieb. The moments crowded in—vivid, real. He felt again the thrill of the Hadendowas charging through McNeil’s zareba six miles from Suakin. He remembered the Berkshires holding firm, the Marines steady, the broken troops rallying.

Years of war—years of purpose—had brought him to brevet lieutenant-colonel.

“A week more—only a week,” Mather murmured, drifting off.

“I’ll come back,” Durrance said, laughing softly.

“You have no friends?”

There was a pause.

“Yes,” he said. “I have friends. Three months to see them.”

He hadn’t written to Harry Feversham in years. Writing letters was not his nature. Now, he imagined surprising his friends in Donegal—or finding them in London. He might ride once more in the Row. But in the end, he would return. His friend was married—Ethne Eustace. And Durrance’s own life’s work lay in the Sudan.

He would come back.

And so, turning on his side, he slept dreamlessly beneath the stars, while the heavens rolled above him.

* * * * *

At that very moment, Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe slept beneath a boulder on Khor Gwob. He rose early and continued across the broad plains to the white city of Suakin.

There, he told his story to Captain Willoughby, acting deputy-governor. Later, at the Palace, he repeated it—then again in the native bazaar, speaking in Arabic. A Greek seated outside a nearby café overheard part of it.

The Greek approached Abou Fatma, offered him merissa—enough to intoxicate—and persuaded him to recount the tale a fourth time, slowly and clearly.

“Could you find the house again?” the Greek asked.

Abou Fatma had no doubt. He drew diagrams in the dust—unaware that Berber had been systematically destroyed and rebuilt northward by the Mahdists.

“It will be wise to speak of this to no one but me,” the Greek said, jingling a handful of dollars.

For a long while, the two men talked in secret.

The Greek was Harry Feversham—the very man Durrance planned to visit in Donegal.
Captain Willoughby was Deputy-Governor of Suakin.
And after three years of waiting, one of Harry Feversham’s chances had finally come.

Durrance arrived in London one June morning, and that same afternoon he took his first walk as an exile—into Hyde Park. He sat beneath the trees, watching the grace of English women, their delicate dresses fluttering in the breeze. A solitary figure, sunburnt and marked by the distant world he had left behind, he seemed out of place among the polished city folk. Yet one person noticed him—a woman who smiled as she passed.

She was Mrs. Adair. When she saw him rise from his seat, she stepped beside him, her eyes scanning him quickly, almost shyly. She was comparing the man before her to the image she had carried for three years. Was he changed? Had time worn him down? Her gaze held a quiet apprehension, though Durrance only registered that she wore black.

He asked no question aloud, but she understood.

"My husband died eighteen months ago," she said softly. "He was thrown from his horse during a hunt with the Pytchley. It happened instantly."

"I hadn’t heard," Durrance replied, awkwardly. "I’m sorry."

She sat beside him without speaking. Mrs. Adair was a woman of silence, her pale face calm and composed, revealing nothing of what stirred beneath. Durrance felt uneasy. He remembered Mr. Adair as a kind man, deeply devoted to his wife—but he had never truly considered how she might have seen him. In truth, Mr. Adair had been little more than a shadow in the household, and Durrance found it hard to summon genuine sorrow.

He shifted the subject. "Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?"

Mrs. Adair hesitated. "Not yet," she said, then corrected herself, “I mean—the marriage never took place.”

Durrance, not given to sudden surprise, still paused. “I don’t understand. Why wasn’t it?”

She looked at him sharply, as if measuring his calm tone. “I don’t know why,” she said. “Ethne can keep a secret if she chooses.” Durrance nodded slowly.

“The engagement was broken off on the night of a dance at Lennon House,” she added.

Durrance turned toward her. “Just before I left England three years ago?”

“Yes. Then you knew?”

“No. But now you’ve told me something that happened the very night I left Dover. What has become of Harry?”

Mrs. Adair shrugged. “I don’t know. I’ve met no one who does. I haven’t seen him since that night. He must have left England.”

Durrance pondered this. So the man he’d seen on the pier—pale, haunted, staring into the sea—was Harry Feversham after all. His friend, lost to the world.

“And Miss Eustace?” he asked after a pause, voice oddly hesitant. “Has she married?”

Again, Mrs. Adair delayed. “No.”

“Still at Ramelton?”

She shook her head. “There was a fire at Lennon House a year ago. You ever hear of a constable named Bastable?”

“I did. He introduced me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was traveling from Londonderry to Letterkenny. I received a letter from Mr. Eustace—I didn’t know him—but he’d heard through friends in Letterkenny that I was passing near. He invited me to stay the night. I declined, and the next thing I knew, Bastable arrested me on a magistrate’s warrant as soon as I landed.”

“That’s the man,” Mrs. Adair said. And she told him the story of the fire.

It seemed Bastable’s friendship with Dermod Eustace rested on his ability to brew a certain toddy—perfected only when a single oyster simmered in the saucepan. One June morning at two o’clock, the spirit lamp tipped over. Neither man was sober enough to react. The house burned half down, and the rest was ruined by water from the hoses.

“There were worse consequences,” Mrs. Adair continued. “The fire lit a warning beacon for Dermod’s creditors. Already overwhelmed by debt, he collapsed into ruin in a single day. He was soaked by the hoses and caught a chill that nearly killed him. He’s never recovered. The estates are leased. Ethne now lives with her father in a small mountain village in Donegal.”

She spoke without emotion, her eyes fixed straight ahead, as if reciting facts she could not feel. Durrance watched her, then said quietly: “So she’s lost everything?”

“She still has a home,” Mrs. Adair replied.

“And that means much to her,” Durrance said slowly.

“Yes,” she agreed. “I think you’re right.”

“It means,” she added, “that Ethne, despite all her misfortune, is envied by many women.”

Durrance did not reply directly. He watched the carriages roll past, listened to laughter and chatter, admired the light dresses of the women. His mind worked slowly, forming thoughts too deep for words.

At last, Mrs. Adair turned to him, a flicker of impatience in her voice. “What are you thinking?”

“That women suffer more than men when things go wrong,” he answered. “I don’t know much, only guess. But I believe women absorb their past—make it part of themselves, like a limb. For us, it’s always outside—either a step up or a weight dragging us back. Women look backward; we look forward. So when disaster strikes, it hits them harder. Don’t you agree?”

Mrs. Adair didn’t say yes. But her voice softened, became humble. “The village where Ethne lives is called Glenalla. A path leads up from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton.”

She rose and offered her hand. “Shall I see you?”

“You’re still in Hill Street?” Durrance asked.

“For now,” she said. “I’ll be in London.”

Her eyebrows lifted slightly. She always expected hidden motives. This simple decision—to stay in town—puzzled her. Why not go straight to Donegal, where his thoughts clearly led? She heard of his visits to his club, his endless conversations, and couldn’t understand. Even when he told her he’d visited General Feversham in Surrey, she remained baffled.

That visit had been fruitless. The general talked only of the campaign, refusing to speak of his son. At the mention of Harry’s name, his face hardened like stone. “We’ll talk of something else,” he said. Durrance returned to London empty-handed.

For the rest of June, he sat under the great tree in the inner courtyard of his club, talking to anyone who passed. No friend of Feversham’s escaped his questions. But each answer was the same—a shrug, a muttered “Hanged if I know!” Harry Feversham had vanished completely, even from the gossip of those who once knew him.

Then, near the end of June, an old naval officer limped into the courtyard. He saw Durrance, hesitated, and began to move away.

Durrance stood. “Mr. Sutch,” he said. “You’ve forgotten me?”

“Colonel Durrance,” the lieutenant stammered. “Of course. We met… let me see… where was it? An old man’s memory is like a leaky ship—comes into harbour with its cargo swamped.”

Durrance noticed both the hesitation and the embarrassment. “We met at Broad Place,” he said. “I need news of my friend Feversham. Why was his engagement with Miss Eustace broken off? Where is he now?”

Sutch’s eyes lit with a momentary satisfaction. He had long wondered whether Durrance knew about Harry’s disgrace. Clearly, he didn’t.

“There’s only one person in the world,” Sutch said, “who can answer both your questions.”

Durrance showed no surprise. “Yes. I’ve waited here a month for you.”

Sutch ran his fingers through his beard, stared down at Durrance. “Well—it’s true. I *can* answer. But I won’t.”

“Harry Feversham is my friend.”

“General Feversham is his father—and he knows only half the truth. Miss Eustace was betrothed to him—and she knows nothing. I promised Harry I would keep silent.”

“It’s not curiosity,” Durrance said. “It’s friendship.”

“I’m sure it is,” Sutch replied warmly. “But there’s another side. I won suffice ask you to answer my questions—but I’ll ask you one instead. A harder one for me to ask than for you to answer. Would a friend of Harry Feversham be disloyal—if”—and Durrance flushed beneath his tan—“if he tried his luck with Miss Eustace?”

Sutch froze. “You?” he exclaimed, studying Durrance—his rapid rise, his courage, his presence. He had never imagined this possibility. A fierce loyalty had grown in him, like a mother’s for a favorite son. He had dreamed of Harry returning, restored, like a king reclaiming his throne. Now that dream was shattered.

He saw Durrance—not just a soldier, but a man of honor, strength, and quiet dignity. And he thought of women. “They make gods of brute courage,” he muttered.

“Well?” Durrance asked.

Sutch looked up at the sky, then down at the ground. He remembered Harry’s warning—that this might happen. That Ethne shouldn’t wait. He pictured Harry somewhere under the burning sun, lost and broken—then contrasted it with Durrance, sitting calmly in his club, successful, respected.

He wanted to break his promise. To tell the whole truth. To answer both questions. But then came the demand again—sharp, insistent.

“Well?”

“No,” Sutch said, regretfully. “There would be no disloyalty.”

That evening, Durrance boarded the train for Holyhead.

The farmhouse stood a mile above the village, nestled in a wild stretch of moorland. Heather crept into its garden, and the bridle path ended at its door. On three sides, a ring of hills—ever-changing with the seasons, their colors shifting from day to day like a living tapestry—curved around it like a protective embrace. No trees broke the granite slopes; moss and heather clung to the stone, yet the hills seemed kind, as if they wore their green, purple, and russet hues deliberately to please the girl who lived within.

The house faced a long slope leading down to the inlet of Lough Swilly. From its windows, one could see across scattered thickets, a few tilled fields, whitewashed cottages, and the tall woods along the water’s edge—then catch sight of the bright surface of the lake and the gulls wheeling above it.

Durrance rode up the track one afternoon and recognized the house at once. As he drew near, music drifted from the windows—a violin’s melody, soft and welcoming. His hand paused on the reins. A sudden hope, long nursed in secret, surged within him, stilling his breath. He tied his horse and stepped through the gate.

Outside, the building looked like a rough barrack; inside, it was warm and inviting. The room he was shown into sparkled with brass and polished oak, and the wide view beyond the window matched the brightness of the afternoon. Durrance imagined it on a winter night—blinds drawn, fire glowing, wind howling against the panes—still feeling safe and full of life.

Ethne greeted him without surprise. “I thought you’d come,” she said, a quiet smile lighting her face.

Durrance laughed suddenly as they shook hands. She looked puzzled, then followed his gaze to the violin resting beside her on the table. It was pale in color, with a small patch near the bridge where worm-eaten wood had been replaced.

“It’s yours,” she said. “You were in Egypt. I couldn’t send it back.”

“I’ve hoped lately,” he replied, “that you might keep it anyway.”

“You see I have,” she said, meeting his eyes. “And I accepted it some time ago. There was a time when I needed proof that I had true friends. Something real helped. I was glad to have it.”

Durrance lifted the violin carefully, as though it were sacred. “Have you played it?” he asked. “The Musoline overture, perhaps?”

She smiled. “Yes. But only recently. For a long while, I put it away. It reminded me too much of things I wanted to forget.” Her voice was calm, her eyes steady.

The next day, Durrance brought his luggage from Rathmullen and stayed at the farm for a week. Yet not once did either of them mention Harry Feversham—not until the final hour of his visit. Though they spent much time together, it was as if the name hung unspoken between them.

Dermod Eustace was worse than Mrs. Adair had described. His speech had shrunk to single words; his body had wasted away, his clothes hanging loosely on him. Even his height seemed diminished. The fire in his eyes had dimmed. He rarely left the house, spending most days dozing by the hearth—even in July. His longest walk was to the little grey church on a nearby mound, visible from the windows. That journey drained him. He was no longer the man he once was, but a shadow, broken and nearly unrecognizable. His collie dog seemed to age with him. To watch them side by side was to feel a shared sorrow.

With Dermod confined, Durrance and Ethne found themselves together more often. They walked the hills each day—through rain or sun—while she showed him her land with pride, her cheeks flushed, her eyes sharp with passion. In the evenings, she would take up her violin again, sitting with her face turned aside, letting the music speak of the vastness around them.

Durrance watched her—the sweep of her arm, the focus in her expression—and quietly counted his chances. He hadn’t come to Glenalla expecting to win her. He knew the ghost of Harry Feversham loomed large. And he understood, too, that poverty would weigh less heavily on her than on most women. Though Lennon House was ruined and her lands lost, she remained among her people. They still welcomed her, still saw her as their princess.

Yet she took pleasure in his company. She made him talk about his three years in the East. No detail was too small for her questions. As he spoke, her eyes studied him, her lips held a quiet approval.

Durrance didn’t know what she was testing. Perhaps no one could, unless they knew what had passed between her and Feversham. To her, he was a mystery—a dark lantern. Was there a flame inside, or only emptiness? She was trying to see.

On the last day, they sat together on the hillside, beside a stream that leapt from rock to rock, tumbling through a narrow gorge. The water ran black and swift, swirling over deep pools.

“I once traveled four days through mirages,” he said. “Lagoons, still as mirrors, lined with misty trees. You could almost walk your camel knee-deep before the water vanished and the sand blazed. The heat—oh, you can’t imagine it. Every stone dances like a signal mirror. You can actually see the air shimmer, rising breast-high, faster than this stream. And then the sun sets right before your eyes! Then comes night—silent, endless—cooled by a gentle wind blowing across the horizon. Your bed is laid under a sky full of stars. Oh,” he breathed, “that place grows on you. Like the Southern Cross—four stars that seem dull at first, but after a week, you start looking for them. And when you go north, you miss them.”

He turned toward her. “Do you know—I never feel alone in those empty places. On the contrary, I feel closer to the people I care about.”

Her eyes shone. She smiled softly.

He moved closer, sitting cross-legged, feet tucked beneath him. “I used to imagine you out there,” he said. “You’d have loved it—from dawn to campfire. You’d have felt at home. I used to think that as I lay awake, wondering how my friends fared.”

“And you’re going back?” she asked.

He hesitated. The roar of the stream filled the silence. When he answered, his voice was quiet, without fire. “To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so.”

She knelt beside him. “I’ll miss you.”

She was behind him, just slightly to one side. Another silence fell.

“What are you thinking?” she asked.

“That you don’t need to miss me,” he said. “I could shorten my leave. Or avoid Halfa altogether. I still have half my furlough.”

She didn’t reply. Didn’t move. Durrance felt a chill. He knew that stillness meant pain for her—more than any cry ever could.

He turned to her. Her head was bent, but she raised it. Her lips curved into a smile, but her eyes were troubled.

He was a man like any other. His first thought: *Is there something preventing her?* “There’s your father,” he said.

“Yes,” she whispered. “There’s my father. I couldn’t leave him.”

“Nor should you,” he said quickly. “That’s not what I meant. Honestly, I wasn’t thinking of him.”

“Nor was I,” she said.

He turned away, staring into the pool below. The truth was clear now—the shadow of Feversham stretched between them.

“I know,” he said, “that you wouldn’t be torn in two, like so many. You wouldn’t care lightly, nor forget easily.”

She lowered her voice. “I remember enough to make your words hurt. One day, I may tell you what happened at that ball three years ago. Then you’ll understand why I’m distressed. All I can say now is this: I fear I may have caused another man’s ruin. Not that I was to blame—but if I hadn’t known him, his life might not have ended so abruptly. I’m not sure. But I’m afraid. I asked, and I was told ‘no.’ But I suspect generosity shaped that answer. And the fear remains. I lie awake with it. Then you come—someone I value—and you ask quietly, ‘Will you please ruin my career too?’” She struck her palm sharply against her other hand. “But that I will not do.”

He leaned forward. “There’s no need. Wadi Halfa isn’t the only place a soldier can serve.”

His voice had warmed. He had listened closely. She hadn’t said friendship was all she offered. So he read more into her words. Women, especially good ones, spoke in hints. A man could interpret deeper meaning. This was his chance.

“No doubt,” she said, “a soldier can find work elsewhere. But *this* soldier—can he find anything so fitting? Listen. I was so glad to hear about your travels, your service. I was even gladder because of the joy in your voice. It seemed to me, watching you, that you’d found the one true path—where your life could flow smoothly, freely. So few people do.” She wrung her hands. “And now you spoil it all.”

He turned fully to her. “I am for you, Ethne!” he said, voice rising. “That’s the true path. And I believe you’re for me. I admit—I once thought I’d spend my life in the East. That idea satisfied me. But I trained myself to be content, because I believed you were married.”

She flinched slightly. He realized his voice had been too loud—almost harsh.

“Do I hurt you?” he asked. “I’m sorry. But I must speak plainly. I can’t afford to hide. I want you to know everything. I love you. Yes, I could have said it five years ago. Five years since your father arrested me at the ferry—because I wanted to go straight to Letterkenny, not stop with a stranger. Five years since I first saw you, first heard your violin. I remember how you sat with your back to me. The light caught your hair. I saw your lashes, your cheeks. I remember the movement of your arm…”

“My dear,” he said, “you are for me. I am for you.”

But she pulled back from his hands.

“No,” she said gently, but firmly. “No.”

She saw deeper into his mind than he saw himself. The restlessness of the wanderer, the longing for distant lands, strange skies, and constant motion—these were part of his nature. Passion might blind him for a time, but the call would return. And when it did, it would torment him. Home would become a prison. Conflict would grow within. No happiness could last.

She looked down the hill, across the brown earth. To her right, the woods waved near Ramelton; at her feet, the lake flashed. This was her world. She belonged here—child of the land, sister to its people.

“No,” she repeated, rising.

He stood with her. He wasn’t crushed—only aware of a misstep. He’d framed it wrong. He should never have let her think marriage would interrupt his duty.

“We’ll say good-bye here,” she said, “out in the open. We’ll still be friends, even if three thousand miles separate us.”

They shook hands.

“I’ll be in England again in a year,” he said. “May I come back?”

Her eyes and smile gave consent. “I’d be sorry to lose you entirely. Even if I never see you, I’d know I haven’t lost your friendship. And I’d like to hear news of you—if you have time.”

“May I write?” he asked eagerly.

“Yes,” she said, pausing slightly. “If you think it fair. I mean—it might be better for you to let me go completely from your thoughts.”

Durrance laughed—without bitterness. Soon, Ethne found herself laughing too, though she couldn’t say why.

“Very well,” he said. “I’ll write.”

She added dryly, “But it will be about—other things.”

Again, he read more into her words than she meant. She meant exactly what she said.

She stood where he left her—tall, strong, full of quiet strength—until he disappeared from sight. Then she walked down to the house, went to her room, and took out a violin. But it was the one Durrance had given her. Before she touched the bow, she recognized it. She closed the case abruptly, snapped it shut.

For a moment, she sat still. Then she crossed the room, took her keys, and unlocked a drawer. At the bottom lay a photograph. She stared at it long and wistfully.

Meanwhile, Durrance walked down to the trap waiting at the gate. Dermod Eustace stood in the road, hat in hand.

“I’ll walk a few yards with you, Colonel Durrance,” he said. “I have a word for your ear.”

Durrance adjusted his pace to match the old man’s slow steps. They walked behind the dog-cart in silence.

It wasn’t just personal disappointment that weighed on Durrance. He couldn’t see as Ethne did. As he looked over that quiet corner of Donegal, he felt a deep sadness—for her life, trapped in isolation, her grave one day under the wall of the tiny church, her memory fading in a few scattered cottages, remembered only briefly.

Then Dermod’s hand pressed his elbow. The old man’s faded eyes held a question.

“You have news?” he asked, hesitating. “Of Harry Feversham? I thought I’d ask before you left.”

“None,” Durrance said.

“Sorry,” Dermod murmured, wistfully. “Though I have no reason to be. He struck us a cruel blow, Colonel Durr ness. I’d have nothing but curses for him—my heart and mouth both. A black-throated coward, my reason calls him. And yet… I’d still be glad to know how he fares. You were his friend. But you don’t know?”

It was indeed Feversham he spoke of. Durrance, seeing the old man’s sorrow, felt a pang of guilt—for letting Ethne push his friend from his mind.

Later, in the evenings on his verandah above the Nile at Wadi Halfa, he would sometimes wonder about Feversham, piecing together fragments. “A black-throated coward”—those were Dermod’s words. And Ethne had said enough to show that something far deeper than a quarrel had ended their engagement. But he couldn’t guess the cause. Only anger grew—real, bitter anger—at the man who had once been his friend.

So the winter passed, and summer came to the Sudan. May arrived.

In the month of May, Durrance turned his gaze from Wadi Halfa and began to look longingly toward home. But far to the south—five hundred miles beyond his frontier, across the vast Nubian desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones—events were unfolding that would shape his fate, though he knew nothing of them.

On a desolate stretch of track between Berber and Suakin lay the wells of Obak, buried deep in shifting sands. To the east, a narrow strip of trees marked the edge of the dunes, beyond which rose a barren plain of granite hills. To the west, the desert stretched for fifty-eight waterless miles to Mahobey and Berber on the Nile—a flat, unbroken expanse where even a tuft of grass seemed a distant tree, and a pile of stones loomed like a mountain. In this May, no place could have been more forsaken.

The sun blazed from dawn until dusk with a heat that seared the soul. At night, icy winds swept across the land, sculpting the sand into towering pyramids only to level them again, carving valleys and burying slopes. The landscape changed constantly, as if the earth itself breathed and dreamed. Vultures circled overhead, and sand-grouse skittered through the silence. Scattered among the mounds were bleached camel bones—remnants of caravans long gone—and the crumbling frame of a hut built from bent branches, once home to wandering Arabs who had herded goats and lived off the land.

Now, only the wind and the sky remained. Silence pressed down like a shroud, making the place feel alive with unseen purpose.

Yet one man lingered here, hidden and alone. Each morning at sunrise, he led two swift Bisharin camels from the shelter of the trees to the well. He watered them, then sat quietly by the mouth of the well for three hours, gazing toward the dunes and Berber. Then he returned the camels to their shade, fed them carefully with dhoura laid out on cloth, and vanished for the rest of the day. For five mornings, no one came near him.

But on the sixth, as he was about to return to his hiding place, he saw figures rise suddenly against the horizon—a man and a donkey silhouetted atop a sand crest.

The Arab by the well glanced first at the animal. Its grey coat caught his eye, and he half rose. But then he looked at the rider. Though the man’s jellab covered his face, his bare legs stood out starkly against the sand—black as ebony. A negro.

The Arab sat back, unmoved, waiting. He did not flinch when the man stepped close, boots crunching over the loose grains.

"Salam aleikum," the negro said, placing a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield upon the ground before sitting beside him.

"Aleikum es salam," the Arab replied, offering no more than a nod.

"It is Abou Fatma?" the negro asked.

The Arab nodded.

"Two days ago," the man continued, "a Bisharin named Moussa Fedil stopped me in the market at Berber. Seeing I was hungry, he gave me food. After I ate, he told me to bring this donkey to you at Obak."

Abou Fatma studied the animal casually, as though noticing it for the first time.

"Tayeeb," he said. "The donkey is mine." And he sat still, as if the matter were settled.

But the negro stayed.

"I am to meet Moussa Fedil again in three days’ time, in Berber’s market. Give me a token so he may know I’ve delivered the message and reward me."

Abou Fatma drew his knife from his belt, picked up a stick from the ground, and notched it three times at each end.

"This will be your sign," he said, handing it over.

The negro tied the stick securely into his wrap, removed the water-skin from the donkey, filled it at the well, slung it over his shoulder, and gathered his weapons.

Abou Fatma watched him climb the slope, disappear over the crest, and vanish into the distance.

Then he rose quickly, his movements sharp and urgent.

Six days earlier, Harry Feversham had left Obak, riding this very donkey across fifty-eight miles of barren desert to reach the Nile. Now, Abou Fatma drove the beast down into the grove of trees, tethered it to a sapling, and examined its shoulders.

There, beneath the skin, was a tiny incision—neatly stitched shut with fine thread. Abou Fatma cut the stitches open, parted the wound, and pulled out a small package no bigger than a postage stamp. It was a goat’s bladder, sealed tightly. Inside, wrapped in thin paper, was a note written in Arabic and folded into a tight square.

Abou Fatma read it—not because he was a scholar, but because he had served Gordon, and under him had learned to read.

The letter said:

*“The houses of old Berber are gone. A new town rises with wide streets. There is no longer any mark by which I can find Yusef’s house among the ruins. Yusef himself no longer sells rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week.”*

The writer was Harry Feversham.

Disguised in the patched jubbeh of the Dervishes, his skin stained and weathered, his hair frizzed and matted like seaweed, Feversham wandered through the new town of Berber, searching for a man who might not exist. To the south, separated by a mile of desert, lay the old town—the warren of ruined homes where Abou Fatma had once slept and hidden letters. Walls had been torn down, roofs carried away, leaving only skeletal frames standing in silent rows. The courts were indistinguishable now, all crumbling into ruin. Foxes had already begun to dig burrows beneath the foundations.

Feversham had planned to stay just one night. He meant to slip into Berber after dark, spend the night, and leave before dawn. But now he had to move openly among crowds, pretending to have business, afraid to speak lest his voice betray him. Every passing word, every name shouted in the street, sent a jolt through him. Was there a mention of Yusef? Could he find him?

Despair clung to him like shadow. Fear never left. Yet from these torments, something else grew—madness, yes, but also a fierce, fanatical resolve. He believed now, as the Dervishes believed in their dark faith, that he *must* succeed. That the world could not allow such injustice—to burden the weakest man with the greatest trial, then destroy him for trying.

Three days after leaving Abou Fatma, Feversham stood on a slope, looking toward Berber. Before him stretched the endless sand, rising to meet the dark walls of the town. Beyond, the Nile bank shimmered with palm trees, green and inviting.

Inside those walls, the Dervishes gathered—hundreds of them, fanatics sworn to death and devotion. To enter was madness. To survive, impossible.

Was it right, he wondered, to even try?

He stood there, paralyzed. The mud walls loomed, sinister and watchful. The palms, once a welcome sight, now mocked him—green promises leading only to slaughter. His strength drained away. If he were caught… oh, surely he would be. He imagined the faces around him—wild, furious eyes. Would they mutilate him? Cut off his hands? His tongue?

He shivered, though the sun burned hot. The loneliness of the desert pressed upon him. His knees trembled.

He turned and ran—sprinting blindly across the empty waste, heart pounding, breath ragged. No one chased him. He was alone, utterly alone, fleeing from a fate he could not escape.

But only a few yards into his flight, he stopped.

What was this? Had four years of preparation meant nothing? Had he trained in language, endured hardship, lived in bazaars—all for nothing? Was he still the coward who had sent in his papers?

The quiet confidence he’d shown Lieutenant Sutch at the Criterion grill-room—was that just arrogance? A lie he told himself?

And Ethne… what of her?

He collapsed onto the sand, pulled his coat over his head, and lay still—just another brown patch in the vast emptiness. He closed his eyes and let his mind drift across oceans and continents.

For a moment, he was back in Donegal. Summer night whispered through an open doorway. Music drifted from a nearby room, where people danced. He saw the three feathers falling slowly to the floor. He saw Ethne’s face—pale, troubled.

If he could do this—if he could endure what lay ahead—he might one day see that face free of sorrow.

And then came the words she had spoken: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

As the sun sank low, Feversham rose. He walked steadily toward Berber, passed through the gates, and stepped into the heart of danger.

A month after his return from the Sudan, Durrance arrived in London and found a letter waiting for him at his club. It was from Ethne, brief and quiet, telling him she was staying with Mrs. Adair in Hill Street and would be pleased if he could call. But the paper and envelope were edged in black—a sign of mourning.

The next afternoon, he went to see her. She was alone when he arrived.

"I didn’t write to Wadi Halfa," she said at once, "because I thought you’d already be on your way home before my message could reach you. My father died last month—towards the end of May."

Durrance felt a pang. "I feared that might be the news," he replied. "I’m deeply sorry. You must miss him terribly."

"More than I can say," she answered, her voice low but steady. "He passed away one morning early. I think I should tell you how it happened—if you don’t mind hearing."

She described the slow decline that had led to Dermod’s death—not from any sudden illness, but from a quiet wasting away. Yet there was something remarkable about the final hours. Just before he slipped into silence, he seemed to reclaim a fragment of his old strength.

"We both knew he was dying," Ethne said. "He did too. At seven o’clock that evening—after speaking to me for a little while—he called our dog by name. The dog came instantly to the bed, though his voice was barely a whisper. He crouched close, pushing his muzzle under my father’s hand with a soft whine. Then he told me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. I was to shut the door and not open it again until the dog gave the signal. I obeyed. I waited outside—six long hours—until one o’clock, when a sudden, mournful howl echoed through the house."

She paused. That was the only sign of emotion she showed. After a moment, she continued, her tone calm and clear.

"It was hard to stand there in the corridor as the day faded into night. He wouldn’t allow a lamp in his room. I imagined him lying still in the great four-poster bed, facing the hills, the light fading slowly around him. The room growing dark. The windows growing larger in the dimness. The dog beside him, and no one else—right to the very end. He wanted it that way. But it was cruel—for me."

Durrance said nothing. He offered her what she needed most: the quiet understanding of silence. He could picture her standing there, listening, heart pounding, hands clenched, enduring those endless hours. And he felt a sharp sorrow for Dermod’s stubborn choice—to die alone, even if it meant leaving his daughter to suffer in silence.

It was Ethne who broke the quiet.

"I mentioned that my father spoke to me just before he sent me away. Do you know whom he talked about?"

She looked directly at him, her eyes steady, her voice level. A sudden warmth surged through Durrance—hope, quick and bright—before he realized the danger of it.

"Tell me," he said, leaning forward.

"Mr. Feversham," she said simply.

He recoiled slightly. Not the name he had hoped for. He turned his gaze down to the carpet, so she wouldn’t see the disappointment—or the anger—on his face.

"My father always admired him," she went on gently. "And I wonder… do you know anything about where he is now? Or what he’s doing?"

Durrance did not answer at once. He walked to the window and stood looking out, his back to her. Even so, the bitterness in his voice was unmistakable.

"No," he said, his tone tight. "I’ve never seen him. And I never want to." He took a breath, then spoke more sharply. "I know he was your friend. But I can’t remember that friendship now. All I can think is—if he had been the man we believed him to be, you wouldn’t have had to wait alone in that dark hall for six hours."

He turned abruptly. "You’re going back to Glenalla?"

"Yes."

"You’ll live there alone?"

"Yes."

For a moment, silence filled the room. Then Durrance moved behind her chair.

"You once said you might tell me why your engagement ended."

"But you already know," she said. "What you said earlier—that look—proved it."

"No," he insisted. "Only a few words my father dropped. He asked me about Feversham the last time I saw him. But I don’t know anything certain. Please—tell me."

Ethne shook her head, resting her elbows on her knees. "Not now," she whispered.

Again, silence.

Then Durrance spoke again, his voice suddenly urgent. "I have only one more year in Halfa. It would be wise to leave Egypt then. I don’t expect much will happen in the Sudan soon. I don’t think I’ll stay—no matter what."

Ethne didn’t pretend not to understand. "We’re not children," she said quietly. "You have your whole life ahead. We should be prudent."

"Yes," Durrance replied, his voice rising with frustration. "But the right kind of prudence—the kind that knows it’s worth risking everything for what matters."

She remained still, her back to him, silent and unmoving. At last, she spoke—softly, almost to herself.

"Do you want me so very much?"

Before he could answer, she turned quickly toward him.

"Try not to," she pleaded earnestly. "Just this one year—try not to. You have important things to focus on. Try to forget me completely."

There was a hint of sorrow in her voice—not grief, but regret—for losing a dear friend. It was enough to ease the sting of her words and deepen Durrance’s illusion: that if not for her fear of harming his future, she might have said yes.

Mrs. Adair entered the room just then, cutting off any reply. Durrance left, carrying that false hope with him.

That evening, he dined at his club and sat afterward beneath the great tree where he had spent so many restless nights a year before, searching in vain for news of Harry Feversham. The sky was clear and star-strewn, the air warm and still. Lights flickered from the windows, making the leaves glow like spring green. Carriages rolled past, their rumble like distant waves.

And on this night, another man came into the courtyard—someone who knew Durrance. But unlike others, he did not hesitate. He walked straight up and sat beside him.

Durrance dropped his newspaper and reached out his hand.

"How are you?" he asked.

It was Captain Mather.

"I wondered if I’d find you here," Mather said. "I read the evening paper and thought you might be in town. Have you seen the news?"

"What news?" Durrance asked.

Mather picked up the paper and flipped through it. "Remember our last reconnaissance from Suakin?"

"Of course."

"We stopped at the Sinkat fort at midday. There was an Arab hiding in the trees behind the glacis."

"Yes."

"Have you forgotten what he told us?"

"The story about Gordon’s letters and the wall in Berber? No—I haven’t forgotten."

"Well," Mather said, folding the paper carefully and handing it over, "here’s something that might interest you."

Durrance read the short paragraph between puffs of his cigar. It said only that letters exchanged between the Mahdi and General Gordon—summons to surrender Khartum and invitations to convert, along with copies of Gordon’s curt replies—had been found hidden in a wall in Berber and delivered safely to Captain Willoughby at Suakin.

"The fellow must have gone back to Berber after all," Durrance remarked. "A dangerous thing. Abou Fatma—that was his name."

The article made no mention of Abou Fatma. Only Captain Willoughby was named.

"They weren’t worth risking a life for," Mather said.

"Perhaps not," Durrance replied hesitantly. "But I’m glad they’re recovered. Maybe the copies are in Gordon’s own handwriting. They’re historically significant."

"In a way," Mather agreed. "But they don’t shed any real light on the siege. They won’t change history—or anyone’s life."

"That’s true," Durrance said—and yet, in truth, it wasn’t. For here, in the same place where he had once sought news of Feversham, news had come to him. Only he didn’t know it. He was blind to its meaning.

He set the paper aside and returned to thoughts of Ethne. She had mentioned Feversham—yes—but likely only because her father had spoken of him. Her voice had been steady. She had spoken his name without tremor. Perhaps that meant nothing stirred in her heart anymore.

And then there was her request—that he try to forget her for one year. To Durrance, it seemed almost like a promise. As if she might give him what he longed for—if only he could endure the waiting.

Days passed. Then he returned to Mrs. Adair’s house. But Ethne was gone—back to Donegal.

Mrs. Adair said she had left suddenly, and she didn’t know why.

But Durrance understood. She was following her own advice. If he was to forget her, she would help by disappearing from his sight.

And he was right.

Yet there was one thing Ethne had overlooked. She hadn’t told him to stop writing.

So, in the autumn, the letters began again—from the Sudan.

She welcomed them, yet they troubled her. Though he wrote carefully, avoiding direct feelings, sometimes a phrase would slip through—perhaps repeating a word she had said years ago, long forgotten. And she couldn’t help seeing: he still carried her in his thoughts.

There was also a new brightness in his words—a sense of hope, of color returning to his world. As if life had become musical again.

Ethne had never escaped the guilt that one man’s life had been ruined because of her. She had sworn that no other man would suffer the same fate. But now, reading his letters, she faced a new question: How could she protect him?

Two paths lay before her. Which one would let her keep her vow?

She was no longer certain that his career was everything. The question haunted her. She took the letters with her to the hillside, sat beneath the wind-swept sky, and wrestled with it—without finding an answer.

Even her violin failed to soothe her.

It was a May night, and outside the mess-room at Wadi Halfa, three officers sat on a grassy knoll above the Nile, smoking in silence. The moon hung full and bright, washing the sky with silver light so strong that even the stars seemed to fade. The smaller constellations vanished entirely, and the heavens, stripped of their usual deep blue, shimmered like a pearl-dusted dome. Below them, the Nile flowed sluggishly, its surface still as polished brass, reflecting the moon’s glow. Across the river, the desert stretched endlessly—a vast, white plain dotted with low mounds, where stones glittered like scattered jewels under the pale light. Behind the men, the verandah of the mess-room cast a long, solid shadow across the ground, dark and unbroken.

One of the officers struck a match and lit his cigar. The flame flared briefly, illuminating a face etched with worry.

“I hope nothing has happened to him,” he murmured, tossing the spent match aside. “I wish I could believe it.”

The speaker was Colonel Dawson, middle-aged and in command of a Sudanese battalion. He was answered by Lieutenant Calder, an engineer whose hair had turned grey—though not from age, for youth still lingered in his sharp features. Despite his years, Calder carried himself with the energy of a man in his prime.

“He left Halfa eight weeks ago?” Calder asked, voice low.

“Eight weeks today,” Dawson replied.

It was Major Walters, tall and lean, who broke the mood with a note of quiet optimism.

“It’s too soon to say Durrance is lost,” he said. “You know Durrance. Give him a campfire, a few sheiks, and a good story—and he’ll keep them entertained for a month without blinking. Here, though? There’s only paperwork, offices, and desks—everything he hates. He’ll turn up. Just not in a hurry.”

“He’s three weeks overdue,” Dawson insisted. “And he’s always been punctual. I’m afraid.”

Walters gestured toward the desert beyond the river.

“If he’d gone west, I might agree,” he said. “But he headed east—through the mountains toward Berenice and the Red Sea. The tribes there are peaceful, even during the worst times. Osman Digna never troubled them.”

But Dawson remained unconvinced. He tugged at his moustache.

“He’s three weeks late,” he repeated.

Calder knocked the ash from his pipe and refilled it carefully, pressing the tobacco down with his thumb. Then he leaned forward.

“I wonder,” he said slowly. “It’s possible someone set a trap for Durrance. I didn’t mention it before—because until now I didn’t think it mattered. But now… I’m beginning to doubt. You remember the night before he left?”

“Yes,” Dawson said, shifting closer in his chair.

Calder was the only one in Wadi Halfa who had known Durrance well. Though they were different ranks, they were close in age. When Calder first arrived—fresh from England, eager to learn—Durrance, reserved but kind, had taken him under his wing. So Calder knew things others did not.

“I remember,” Walters said. “He dined at the mess and left early to prepare.”

“He’d already packed,” Calder corrected. “He left early, yes—but not for his quarters. He walked along the riverbank to Tewfikieh.”

Tewfikieh was a small frontier town north of Wadi Halfa, separated by a mile of riverbank. It was a dusty place, home to a few Greek merchants, cheap cafés, and native shops selling tobacco and food. A chaotic mix of languages filled the air—the guttural tones of the Dinka, the clipped speech of the Delta fellahs. Yet despite the noise, the streets were eerily silent beneath it all. People walked barefoot on sand, their steps soundless. At night, when voices rose and cries echoed, there was still a deeper silence underneath—like the breath held by the desert itself.

“That night,” Calder continued, “Durrance went to Tewfikieh around ten. I went to his room at eleven. He hadn’t returned. He was leaving at four in the morning, and I needed to speak with him. So I waited. He came back about fifteen minutes later, saying I should be quick—he was expecting a visitor. He was agitated. Nervous. One moment angry, the next laughing—like he couldn’t help himself. He barely listened to me. He spoke in fragments. I knew something was wrong—deeply wrong. But I couldn’t guess what. He pushed me out, and as I left, I heard him tell his servant to go to bed. ‘I’ll admit the visitor myself,’ he said.”

“And who was this visitor?” Dawson asked.

“I don’t know,” Calder replied. “But here’s what I do know: when the servant woke him at four, Durrance was still sitting on the verandah—waiting. The visitor never came.”

“No message?”

“None. I was awake when he left. I thought he was confused, anxious. I thought he meant to tell me what was going on. But he never did. Even after mounting his camel, he looked down at me once—then changed his mind. He tapped the animal’s flank and rode off, head bowed. I wonder if he walked into a trap. Who was this man he expected? Why come secretly to Wadi Halfa? What business did they have? Something important. Something dangerous. And then—nothing. Like a lure we can’t trace. I’m afraid too, Major.”

A silence followed. Walters broke it, rising from his chair.

“I still don’t think Durrance is lost,” he said.

Dawson stood up.

“I’ll send out a search party tomorrow,” he declared.

At breakfast the next morning, Dawson outlined his plan. Walters, though hopeful, had clearly been thinking. He leaned across the table to Calder.

“Did you ever find out who Durrance talked to in Tewfikieh?” he asked.

“I tried,” Calder said. “But it puzzles me. I couldn’t find anyone else. He stopped at a café where some wandering musicians were playing for lodging. One of them—a Greek—came out and took up a hat. Durrance tossed in a sovereign. They spoke for a while.” He paused, then raised his head sharply. Recognition flashed across his face. His hands gripped the edge of the table. He tensed, ready to rise—but didn’t. Instead, he stared past Walters, eyes wide with sudden confusion. Around the table, no one noticed. Dawson poured cocoa. Walters watched his plate. Calder leaned back, still staring, then continued quietly.

“But I never found he spoke to anyone else. He just walked through the village and back. That’s all.”

“That doesn’t help,” Walters said.

“All I know?” Dawson asked.

“No,” Calder said slowly. “There’s more. For instance—I know the man we’re talking about is standing right in front of me.”

Everyone turned.

“Durrance!” Dawson exclaimed, jumping up.

“When did you get back?” Walters asked.

Durrance stood in the doorway, dust clinging to his clothes, skin burned red as brick. He listened intently to the voices. But Calder, his friend, made no move. He sat still, watching—curious, puzzled. As Durrance stepped down onto the verandah, a look of sudden understanding crossed Calder’s face.

“We expected you three weeks ago,” Dawson said, pulling a chair.

“The delay couldn’t be helped,” Durrance replied, seating himself.

“Does my story explain it?” Calder asked.

“No,” Durrance said, smiling. “I was waiting for the Greek musician. I wanted to know how he ended up playing zither in a café at Tewfikieh. That was all.” He added softly, “That was all.”

Dawson waved him to breakfast.

“You’re forgetting your meal,” he said.

Calder leaned forward slightly, eyes fixed on Durrance. Durrance glanced around, then called the waiter.

“Moussa,” he said, “bring me something cold.”

Calder nodded faintly, as if he understood the cleverness of the request.

“There’s tea, cocoa, coffee,” he said. “Help yourself.”

“Thanks,” Durrance said. “But bring me a brandy-and-soda, please.”

Again, Calder nodded.

Durrance ate and drank, telling stories of his journey—how he’d gone farther east than planned, how the Ababdeh Arabs were calm, loyal neither to Egypt nor Mahommed Achmet. The weather had been good; game plentiful. He seemed content. But Calder watched him closely, disbelieving every word. The others moved on. Calder stayed.

Durrance lingered over his breakfast. When done, he pushed his plate aside and kept talking—asking questions about life in Wadi Halfa, expressing excitement over his return. Finally, he stopped abruptly.

“You’re taking it easy today,” he said, sounding suspicious.

“I’ve got eight weeks’ worth of letters to catch up on,” Calder replied with a laugh.

Durrance’s face tightened. His brow furrowed.

“True,” he said. “I forgot.” He rose, climbed the steps, and entered the mess-room.

Calder sprang up and followed his gaze. Durrance took a key from a nail near the door, walked across the room, and left through the far exit—leaving it open. Calder saw him walk down the path, unlatch the gate, and cross the sand toward his office.

As soon as Durrance disappeared, Calder sat again, elbows on the table, face in his hands. He was troubled. He was Durrance’s closest friend—the only one who truly knew him. He knew there were letters waiting in Durrance’s office—letters from a woman. And now, seeing the changes in Durrance—his aged face, the new lines around his mouth, the unnatural brightness in his eyes—Calder felt a deep unease.

Durrance had grown paler, thinner. His laughter seemed forced. He had sat through breakfast pretending cheerfulness, but Calder saw the strain.

After a long pause, Calder picked up his helmet and walked silently across the sand to the office. He lifted the latch gently, opened the door, and peered inside.

Durrance sat at his desk, head bowed, arms folded. Letters lay untouched beside him.

Calder stepped in and shut the door firmly behind him.

Durrance looked up.

“Well?” he asked.

“I have a paper needing your signature,” Calder said. “The authorization for repairs to C barracks. You remember?”

“Of course,” Durrance said. “I’ll sign it at lunch. Give it to me.”

He reached out. Calder removed his pipe and placed it slowly into Durrance’s hand.

Only when the hot bowl burned his skin did Durrance snatch his arm back. The pipe clattered to the floor.

Neither spoke for a moment. Then Calder put an arm around Durrance’s shoulder and whispered:

“How did it happen?”

Durrance buried his face in his hands. The control he’d maintained shattered. He didn’t speak. Only shook.

“How did it happen?” Calder asked again.

Durrance whispered back:

“How did you find out?”

“You stood in the doorway, listening,” Calder said. “When I looked up and saw you, your eyes were on me—but no recognition. I suspected. When you came down the steps, I was certain. When you wouldn’t reach for food, when you had Moussa hand you the drink—your arm extended, palm out—I knew.”

“I was a fool to try to hide it,” Durrance said. “I knew I couldn’t last long. But those hours—somehow they felt like a victory.”

“How did it happen?”

“There was a high wind,” Durrance said. “It blew my helmet off. It was eight o’clock. I wasn’t planning to move camp. I was outside my tent, shirt-sleeves rolled up. No coat collar to protect my neck. I ran after it—like you’ve seen a hundred times. Each time I bent, it skipped away. Each time I chased it, it stopped. Before I knew it, I’d run a quarter-mile. I was told I collapsed just as I grabbed it. How long ago, I don’t know. I was ill. Then time blurred—no way to count days or nights.”

Durrance was blind.

He explained everything—the journey back to Berber, the order to his men to stay silent. He had tried to conceal it.

Calder listened, then stood.

“There’s a doctor,” he said. “A Syrian. Skilled. I’ll bring him. Maybe it’s temporary.”

The doctor arrived. He examined Durrance, pursed his lips, and shook his head.

“Immediate trip to Cairo,” he advised. “This needs a specialist. I won’t say much—only that there’s always hope.”

“Have you ever had a head injury?” he asked. “Been thrown from a horse? Wounded?”

“No,” Durrance said.

The doctor left without further comment. Both men sat in silence.

Calder didn’t offer comfort. He sensed it would only deepen the pain. Instead, he turned to the pile of letters.

“There are two here,” he said gently. “From a woman. Irish postmark. Would you like to hear them?”

“No!” Durrance snapped, hand flying to Calder’s arm. “Not a chance.”

But Calder didn’t let go.

“I want to know her,” he said quietly. “I’ve heard bits—her name, that she refused you. But I want to know what she’s like.”

Durrance laughed—a harsh, brittle sound.

“You know what I was thinking when I stopped you?” he said. “That I’d read them after you left. It takes time to adjust—when your eyes have served you all your life. You’ll have to help me reply. Read them. Please.”

Calder tore open the envelopes.

The letters were simple—accounts of mountain life in Donegal, written plainly, warmly. But the girl’s spirit shone through. She loved the land, its people, its rhythms. She saw both humor and sorrow in everyday life. Her affection for Durrance wasn’t in grand declarations—it was in the tone, the care, the interest in his adventures. She saw his life as part of hers, even if only as a friend.

When Calder finished, he looked at Durrance—now with relief.

“At least,” Durrance said, “we have something to be thankful for. Suppose I’d been engaged to her? She’d never have let me give it up. What a relief!”

“Relief?” Calder asked.

“You don’t understand. I knew a man—good man, once. A year after he went blind—unrecognizable. He became selfish, demanding, bitter. I don’t blame him. But imagine being married to that. A helpless husband who can’t cross the street without you—then a jealous, questioning beast? God, I’m glad she refused me. Truly grateful.”

“She refused you?” Calder asked, stunned.

“Twice,” Durrance said. “What a relief. You see, Calder, I’ll be worse than that man. I’m not clever. Can’t sit idle. I’ve lived hard, outdoors. That’s all I know. In a year, you’ll barely want to talk to me.”

He laughed again—harsh, weary.

“Oh, stop that,” Calder said. “I’ll read the rest.”

He began, but his thoughts drifted to Ethne Eustace. Was there more than friendship in her words? Girls often refused men for reasons that made no sense. Sometimes they regretted it. Sometimes they meant to accept.

“I must answer the ones from Ireland,” Durrance said when he finished. “The rest can wait.”

Ethne stood at the open window of the drawing room in Hill Street, her gaze fixed on the quiet street below. Mrs. Adair sat across from her, tea untouched, both women waiting—listening—for a sound that would signal his arrival. The afternoon sun bathed London in golden light, June’s warmth filling the city with life. Trees swayed gently, flowers bloomed in windowsills across the way, and carriages rolled smoothly toward Hyde Park. Then came the jingle of bells—a cab approaching.

Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply. "That’s a cab," she said.

"Yes," Ethne replied, leaning forward slightly. But the cab passed by without stopping. The bells faded into silence.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock. "Colonel Durrance is late." She turned to Ethne, sensing something beneath her calm surface. There had been no eagerness in her voice, only tension—almost fear. And when the cab didn’t stop, Ethne seemed to exhale quietly, as though relieved.

"I wonder why you didn’t go to the station?" Mrs. Adair asked slowly.

"He might have thought I came because he needed help," Ethne answered quickly. "I don’t want him to think that. He has his servant."

She looked out again, hesitating, her lips parting once or twice as if to speak, then closing. Finally, she spoke plainly. "You remember the telegram I showed you?"

"The one from Lieutenant Calder? About Colonel Durrance going blind?"

"Yes. I want you to promise never to mention it. Not a word. I don’t want him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair frowned. She had seen the telegram, but not the letter Ethne had sent in reply—her pledge of love, made the moment she read the news. All Ethne had said was, “I am engaged to him.” Mrs. Adair assumed the engagement was long-standing. She never questioned it.

"You’ll promise?" Ethne insisted.

"Certainly, my dear," Mrs. Adair replied, shrugging. "But there must be a reason. Why such secrecy?"

"Two lives must not be ruined because of me."

The words settled between them. Mrs. Adair believed she meant Durrance and Harry Feversham—though she was mistaken. Ethne didn’t correct her. In fact, she was glad for the misunderstanding. It gave her space.

Ethne returned to the window, her mind already mapping the path ahead. Her future was a challenge—one she had chosen, yet feared. She could not afford a single slip, a careless word, an unguarded look. Forgetting him was impossible. So she would hide her feelings. Protect him. Protect herself.

And now, as she waited, fear crept in—not of him, but of failing. Yet the goal was worth every effort: a life lived with purpose, not pity.

Durrance had lost everything that defined him—the adventure, the open sky, the desert nights, the command of men, the joy of travel. Only one thing remained: Ethne. And only so long as she wanted him.

Then, without warning, the cab stopped at the door. The bell rang. Ethne started.

"He’s here!" she whispered.

Durrance entered, not as a man broken, but as one who had faced darkness and walked through it. His face was thinner, his hair greyer, but there was no sign of weakness. He moved with confidence, stepping into the room as if he saw clearly. He reached out once, gently, where a chair should be—just once. Then he smiled.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn’t true, then," she breathed. "You’ve recovered."

He shook his head. "No. I haven’t. But I heard you move at the window. The noise outside grew louder. I knew someone stepped aside. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange was strange—no greeting, no embrace—but it eased the silence. No need for false sympathy. They understood each other too well.

They shook hands. Then Ethne offered tea.

"We must talk," Durrance said seriously.

He chatted lightly about the journey home, even laughed at how helpless he’d felt at first—how he’d tripped over rugs, misjudged distances. Ethne’s anxiety began to fade.

But then he paused. "I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "Do you remember the letter?"

"Quite well," she replied.

"I had a friend hold the paper," he continued, smiling. "Calder—of the Sappers. You don’t know him."

He said the name fast, almost as a test. Ethne froze.

A trap. He was watching her. Waiting for a flinch, a pause, a breath too quick. Did he suspect? Did he know about the telegram? Did he think her letter was written out of mercy?

She studied his face—alert, still, unreadable. Once, she could have read his thoughts in his eyes. Now, she couldn’t see them at all.

"I read your letter easily," she said softly. "It was exactly what you would have written. But I had already written to you. Your news changed nothing. I take back no word."

Durrance leaned forward, hands on his knees. Still, Ethne couldn’t tell if he believed her.

"You know me," he said at last. "You wouldn’t take anything back. But let me speak my side."

He spoke slowly, deliberately, as if rehearsed. "Blindness changes men. It breeds selfishness, unless one fights it constantly. And can one fight it forever? It means isolation. For most men, yes. But for me—it means more. I am a soldier. Not a clever one. No desk work. No writing. No business. Just… nothing. Jealousy. Irritation. A constant need for attention. That’s my danger."

Ethne smiled faintly. "Perhaps we can keep those away."

"Maybe," he admitted. "But they must be considered. I’ve thought about this night after night since I got your letter. I wanted to say yes—so badly. With gratitude. But I couldn’t. Not until I knew—until I was sure."

He paused. "A great trouble clears the mind. At Cairo, I lay awake, sorting things out. The small things fell away. Only truths remained. I had to cling to them."

Ethne nodded. She understood. She had lived through the same kind of clarity. She felt a sudden kinship with him—this man who had stared into emptiness and found meaning.

"Well?" she asked.

"One truth stands out," he said. "Marriage between a man like me—crippled, finished—and a woman like you—full of life, young, strong—would be wrong unless each brings more than friendship. It would be wrong if it meant sacrifice for you."

"It means no sacrifice," she said firmly.

He nodded. It was the tone that mattered—the certainty in her voice. He listened not to the words, but to the conviction behind them.

"Then do you bring more than friendship?" he asked suddenly. "Be honest. Tell me."

Ethne hesitated. She must answer—clearly, honestly. "There is nothing," she said, just as firmly, "nothing in the world I wish for more than marrying you."

It was true. She had no idea of Harry Feversham’s return, of the Gordon letters found in the ruins of Khartoum, of the secret plans unfolding. To her, Harry was gone. Forever.

So her wish was sincere.

But it wasn’t the full truth. And she hoped—prayed—he would listen to the feeling, not the words.

He seemed satisfied. "Thank you, Ethne," he said, taking her hand. "I’m glad."

No more questions. No suspicion. Just peace.

Mrs. Adair entered then, tactful and warm. She greeted Durrance as if nothing had changed—as if he were still whole.

"I suppose Ethne has told you our plan?" she said, accepting her tea.

"No," Ethne said. "What plan?"

"We’ve arranged it," Mrs. Adair said. "You’ll want to go home to Guessens in Devonshire. I live nearby—just two fields away. So Ethne will stay with me until you’re married."

"Very kind," Durrance said. "Because, of course, there will be an interval."

"A short one," Mrs. Adair added.

Durrance nodded. "If there’s any chance I might regain sight, I should act at once. Time matters in these cases."

"Is there a chance?" Ethne asked.

"I’ll see a specialist tomorrow," he said. "And perhaps the oculist in Wiesbaden. But maybe I won’t need to go that far. I expect I can stay at Guessens and come up when necessary. Thank you, Mrs. Adair. It’s a good plan."

He added slowly, "From my point of view, it could not be better."

Later, as he left for his rooms in St. James’s Street, Ethne watched him go. She stood by the window, silent, thoughtful.

The street was now filled with carriages returning from the park. But inside, something had shifted.

She believed Durrance’s suspicions were quieted—for now. He didn’t know about the conversation with Calder. He didn’t know he was being lied to. He didn’t know the hope of recovery was a fiction.

Yet she felt uneasy.

Durrance was different now. Not just sharper in hearing, but in mind. His thoughts were deeper, clearer. He saw more—not with eyes, but with perception.

She realized, with a pang, that she was entering a battle—not of strength, but of wits.

"Two lives shall not be spoilt because of me," she whispered again.

But now it was less a resolution, more a prayer.

For one thing was certain: Durrance saw more clearly than ever—blind as he was.

The summer months of July and August passed with a quiet heaviness for Ethne. Her thoughts, once clear and open as the sea breeze, now grew tangled and restless. One morning, standing at the open window of Mrs. Adair’s house on the banks of the Salcombe estuary, she let her fear slip into words.

“I am afraid,” she said softly, the sunlight catching the gold in her hair as it fell across her shoulders.

Behind her, Mrs. Adair sat quietly, book in hand, a faint smile playing on her lips—though she did not look up. “Afraid of what?” she asked gently.

“Not of Colonel Durrance,” Ethne replied slowly. “He’s coming now—across the lawn.”

Mrs. Adair smiled again, but her eyes remained fixed on the page. “I thought so,” she murmured, barely loud enough to be heard.

Ethne turned from the window, her gaze sweeping the stone terrace and down the shallow steps to the grassy lawn where Durrance walked steadily toward them, his cane tapping lightly against the earth. His movements were sure, even without sight. He seemed to feel the world around him more keenly than ever before.

She stepped forward, hesitating only a moment before descending the steps. The air was warm, still, and filled with the scent of damp earth and saltwater. Beyond the hedge, fields rolled gently into the distance, and beyond that, smoke curled from the chimneys of Durrance’s home.

“You saw your oculist yesterday?” Ethne asked as he approached.

“What did he say?” she pressed.

Durrance shrugged. “Nothing certain. Only time will tell if there’s any hope.”

She leaned forward slightly, studying his face—not to see, but to read between the lines. “But must we wait? Must you and I wait?”

“Surely,” he answered. “It would be wiser—for all of us.”

Then, unexpectedly, he turned the conversation. “It was Mrs. Adair who suggested this plan—me returning to Guessens, you staying here with her?”

Ethne blinked. “Yes. I was overwhelmed when I heard about your accident. I came to London, told Laura, and she proposed it. I welcomed it—wholeheartedly.”

Her voice trembled with sincerity. She needed him to believe her, to confirm her truth.

He smiled. “I know that, Ethne.”

A breath escaped her—relief, fleeting. But then he spoke again, casually, yet with purpose: “I remember Harry Feversham once said something about you—just before I left for Egypt. ‘She is of her country, and more of her county,’ he said. ‘She could never be happy far from Donegal.’ That makes me wonder… is it selfish of me to ask you to stay?”

Ethne shook her head. “I wasn’t thinking of that. I meant—why do *you* insist on waiting? Why won’t you consider marrying me now? Because even if your sight returns—it wouldn’t change anything.”

She paused, letting the weight of her words sink in. “It can make no difference.”

Durrance flinched—as if startled from a dream. “I beg your pardon. I was thinking of Feversham. There’s something I’d like you to tell me. You once said at Glenalla that you might one day reveal something to me. I’d rather know now. You see—he was my friend. And I want to understand what happened that night at Lennon House—what broke off your engagement, sent him away broken and disgraced.”

Ethne was silent. Then, gently: “I’d rather not speak of it. It’s over. I don’t wish to talk about it.”

He didn’t press. “Very well. I won’t ask. If it hurts, I don’t want to cause you pain.”

“It’s not pain,” she insisted. “It’s just… he’s gone. A man I knew well. Now dead.”

As she spoke, she looked up—and saw a small boat grounded near the bank. Its sail was lowered, its mast stuck upright in the grass. A man stood beside it, staring uncertainly toward the house.

“A stranger has come from the creek,” Ethne said. “He seems lost. I’ll go help him.”

She moved quickly, seizing the moment as an escape—a brief respite from the tension that had built between her and Durrance. Like a witness stepping back from the courtroom after a grueling cross-examination, she sought breath, clarity.

The stranger stepped forward. He was average height, with a short nose, thick brown eyes that seemed vacant, and a bristling moustache. He tipped his hat, revealing a balding forehead.

“I sailed from Kingsbridge,” he said. “Never been here before. Is this place called The Pool?”

“Yes,” Ethne replied. “Mrs. Adair lives here. Go up the steps to the terrace.”

“I came to see Miss Eustace,” he added.

Ethne turned sharply. “I am Miss Eustace.”

He studied her. “So I thought.”

He twirled one moustache, then the other. “You’ve forgotten my name, I suppose.”

“I don’t think I’ve ever heard it,” she said calmly.

“Oh, yes—you have. Five years ago. I’m Captain Willoughby.”

Ethne froze. Color drained from her cheeks. Her lips tightened. Her eyes narrowed—cold, hard.

Willoughby didn’t flinch. He spoke slowly, almost as if forgiving a slight. “I understand you may not welcome me. But none of us could have known you’d be present when those three white feathers reached Feversham.”

She cut him off. “How do you know I was there?”

“He told me.”

The words struck her like lightning. “You’ve seen him?” she whispered—no, screamed, though it was only a cry from her heart.

She hadn’t thought of Harry Feversham in years. Hadniced him out of her life. Called him dead. Yet now, hearing that someone had spoken to him—had *seen* him—the lie cracked open.

“You’ve actually seen him?” she repeated, breathless.

“A year ago—at Suakin. Else why would I be here?”

The revelation stunned her. She couldn’t guess the reason—but she feared it.

“Why are you here?” she asked at last.

Willoughby took a letter-case from his breast. With deliberate care, he opened it and drew from one pocket a tiny, soiled white feather. He held it out to her.

“I’ve come to give you this.”

Ethne recoiled. “Why?”

“Three white feathers—three accusations of cowardice—were sent to Feversham by three men. This one was delivered to Ramelton five years ago. I was one of those men. I withdraw my charge. I take back my feather.”

“And you bring it to *me*?”

“He asked me to.”

She took it—slowly, trembling. The feather was light, fragile, yet heavy with meaning. The world began to spin. Trees blurred. The sun dimmed. She felt cold, though the August air was warm.

But then—Captain Willoughby. One of the three she could never forgive. His presence steadied her. She would not show weakness before him. Not now.

“Come,” she said, regaining her composure. “Let me hear your story. I was shocked. Still—I don’t understand.”

She led him to a secluded patch of grass near the creek. Hedges enclosed three sides. Tall elms and poplars rose behind. In front, the water shimmered, breaking into ripples. A gap in the hedge formed a quiet entrance. A garden seat waited in the center.

“Sit,” she said, motioning to the bench. “Take your time. Tell me everything—every word, if you remember. I want to hear what he said.”

She clenched the feather in her palm. Somehow, Harry Feversham had been redeemed. Somehow, she had been wrong. And now, she would learn how.

She did not hurry. No remorse stirred—not yet. Only joy at knowing she had misjudged him. But beneath it, memories surged—repressed long ago: springtime in Dublin, laughter in the gardens, the sudden silence when the feathers arrived.

Willoughby began to speak.

And as he did, the Devonshire landscape faded. The cliffs, the sea, the scent of salt—all vanished. In their place rose the harsh beauty of the East—the desert sands, the jagged black rocks, the endless heat. She saw not England, but the land Durrance had described—the land Harry Feversham had faced.

It was cruel irony: that through the words of one man, she could better understand the courage of another.

“I won’t interrupt you,” Ethne said, as Captain Willoughby settled beside her on the bench beneath the trees. But before he’d spoken more than a few words, she broke her own promise.

“I’m Deputy-Governor of Suakin,” he began. “My superior was on leave in May. You’re lucky not to have seen Suakin then, Miss Eustace—especially in May. No white woman could endure it. The heat is suffocating, thick with salt and sweat, the air so heavy you can barely breathe at night. I remember sitting on the verandah of the palace, up on the first floor, around ten o’clock, looking out over the harbour and the distillation works, wondering if sleep was even worth the effort. That’s when my servant came and said a man—refusing to give his name—was insisting on seeing me.”

Ethne leaned forward. “How did he look?”

Willoughby frowned, scratching his temple. “Honestly? I can’t say. Just like any other man who’s spent a year or two in the Sudan—slightly worn down, maybe a little too thin. Nothing remarkable.”

“Never mind,” she sighed, disappointed. For five years, she had heard nothing of Harry Feversham. She longed for news—not just of his health, but of his voice, his manner, the way he moved. Yet here was Willoughby, dull-eyed and unobservant, unable to convey a single detail that might bring him back to life in her mind. She glanced at him, and knew she’d learn nothing from this man about the man she loved.

“I’m sorry,” she said quietly. “Please go on.”

He nodded. “I asked him what he wanted—and why he hadn’t sent his name. ‘You wouldn’t have seen me if I had,’ he replied. Then he pulled a packet of letters from his pocket.”

Ethne’s breath caught.

Those letters had been written by General Gordon himself in Khartum, carried down the Nile as far as Berber. But the day after they arrived, Berber fell to the Mahdists. The messenger, Abou Fatma, hid them in the wall of an Arab trader named Yusef’s house—the one who sold rock-salt in the market. Abou was arrested, escaped, and made his way to Suakin. The letters stayed hidden until Feversham found them.

“I looked through them,” Willoughby continued, “and saw they were worthless. So I asked him—why would a man who refused to fight with his regiment risk death to recover them?”

On that quiet verandah, with the dark sea stretching ahead and the stars above, Feversham told his story—calmly, without flourish. He spoke of meeting Abou Fatma in Suakin, planning the recovery, traveling together to Obak. When Abou dared not go farther, Feversham rode alone into Berber, driving his grey donkey across the empty desert.

He admitted the moment he first saw the town—its low brown walls, the date palms rising behind it along the Nile—he froze. Fear seized him, turned his limbs to water. He ran, leapt across the sand like a hunted animal, driven by pure terror.

But there was one thing he didn’t tell Willoughby.

He spoke of how he crouched on the hot sand, coat pulled over his head, staring across oceans and continents at a woman’s face—her sorrow a shield against despair. That image kept him alive.

“He went into Berber at sunset,” Willoughby said. That was all.

But it was enough.

Ethne drew a deep breath. A softness came over her face. Her eyes, grey and still, lit with quiet joy. “He went into Berber,” she repeated softly.

“And found the old town destroyed,” Willoughby added. “A new one being built on its southern edge. All the landmarks gone. Roofs torn off, houses gutted, walls collapsed. Narrow alleys of crumbling fives-courts—crisscrossing, open to the sky. Here and there, a broken tower rose like a ghost of wealth. But no trace of Yusef’s hut—no sign of where he once sold salt. The foxes had already claimed the ruins.”

The smile faded from Ethne’s lips—but then she looked down at the feather in her palm. It was stained yellow with desert dust. And she laughed, deeply, contentedly. It was proof—proof that failure had not touched him.

“Go on,” she said.

Willoughby told of sending a negro with the donkey to meet Abou at the Wells of Obak. “Feversham stayed in Berber for two weeks. One week waiting each morning at the well for the man’s return. The next week searching for Yusef—now tending a small dhurra plantation by the river.”

“I doubt you can imagine what those two weeks meant to him,” Willoughby said. “The constant fear. The danger. The certainty that at any moment, a voice might call, a hand fall upon his shoulder—and if it did, death would be the least of it.”

He painted the scene: a town of low houses, wide sandy streets dug into pits for mud, the sun beating down without mercy. No shadows. No cover. Every corner watched. Every man a spy. Feversham couldn’t shelter under a roof at night—he couldn’t trust his tongue. He bought food daily, but avoided conversation. He slept in the ruins of the old city, among the shattered courts. By day, he walked quickly through the streets, pretending urgency, never lingering, never listening—afraid to hear Yusef’s name mentioned, afraid to be drawn into talk.

For fourteen days, he lived like a shadow.

“It was unbearable,” Willoughby said. “And yet—when he told me that night on the balcony, it felt real. Even now, I can almost feel it. The silence. The stars. The Red Sea below. He spoke quietly, deliberately—eyes fixed on mine, as though forcing me to feel what he felt. Even when he lit his cigar, he didn’t look away.”

He paused. “I gave him a chair. A cigar. It was the first time in four years he’d sat with someone equal—someone of his own country, his own rank. He told me so. I wish I could remember everything he said.”

He couldn’t. But he tried.

“At last, the negro found Yusef. Not selling salt anymore, but farming near the river. From him, Feversham learned where the letters were hidden—in the inner wall of a house facing a narrow alley, sixth in the row. The front wall was gone, but the sides and back remained. Three feet from the floor, two feet from the right-hand corner—there, in the mud bricks, the letters lay.”

Feversham dug with his knife. Made a hole. Reached in. His fingers closed around the packet. As he tucked it inside his jibbeh, a lantern light flashed behind him.

Ethne flinched.

She saw it all—the ruined alleys, the broken towers, the silent night, the distant drums and shouts from the new town. Harry Feversham, alone, holding the letters, honor slowly returning—then the sudden glare. The trap closing.

“Well?” she whispered.

He stood with his back to the wall. The light came from the alley behind. He didn’t turn—but from the corner of his eye, he saw a white robe hanging motionless. He secured the package—his hands trembling, but not with panic. With excitement. With clarity. His mind worked swiftly, coolly. He knew exactly what to do.

He turned, drew his knife, and ran.

Two men waited. He charged the one with the lantern. Felt the spear point—ducked, beat it aside with his left arm, lunged forward with his right. The Arab fell. The lantern went out.

Feversham leapt over the body and ran eastward—toward the desert, toward the Wells of Obak.

Not in panic. In control.

The second soldier followed. Feversham had expected it. He needed pursuit to escape. He turned a corner, crouched behind a wall. As the man passed, he sprang—leapt onto his shoulders—and struck again.

Willoughby stopped.

Then he turned to Ethne.

“The strangest part,” he said, “was that Feversham felt no fear. Not once. From the moment the lantern shone on him to the moment he vanished into the desert, he felt nothing but calm.”

It baffled him. This man who had resigned his commission rather than face battle—this man who feared conflict so much—had faced death and felt nothing.

But Ethne understood.

She had known him longer than anyone. She had seen the weight of his childhood, the loss of his mother, the cold distance between him and his father, the lifelong dread of disgrace. She remembered the night he’d told her, in the little room off the hall at Lennon House, how he had grown up haunted by shame.

“Yes,” she said softly. “It was an illusion. I should have known. But I refused to see. When the feathers came, he told me why—simply, with his eyes on mine. When my father heard of them, he stood steady. He faced my father.”

She paused. There was more—more she didn’t know. Harry Feversham had gone to Broad Place in Surrey, confessed to his father with the same courage. But Ethne didn’t need to know it all.

“It wasn’t fear of pain,” she said. “It was fear of cowardice. He shrank from the thought of failing—not from the act itself.”

She looked down. “If only I’d been older. Wiser. Less certain. I might have listened. I might have understood. At least—I wouldn’t have been cruel.”

Her voice trembled. The fourth feather—her own addition—came back to her. The one she’d snapped from her fan and given him, to end it all.

Captain Willoughby frowned. “But these fine distinctions—” he began.

“No,” Ethne interrupted sharply. “They’re not fine. They’re real. And I failed. I failed him. And I failed myself.”

She stared at him. He was stubborn. Proud. Unwilling to admit fault. And yet—she had done the same.

“Who of you three first thought of sending the feathers?” she asked.

“Not me,” he said. “I think it was Trench.”

“Trench,” she murmured, clenching her fist—the one with the feather—into the palm of the other. “I’ll remember that name.”

“But I share the blame,” Willoughby insisted. “I don’t deny it. I regret the pain we caused you. But I don’t believe we were wrong. I take back my feather. I annul my accusation. But it’s your doing.”

“Mine?” she asked.

“A man may live in the Sudan and still understand women,” he said. “You gave the feathers back to Feversham so he could redeem himself. That’s clear.”

Ethne stood up—suddenly, silently—facing him, her back turned, her posture rigid.

Willoughby, unaware, had struck a truth deeper than he ever intended. He’d pointed out a path she might have taken—and hadn’t. And her conscience screamed that she *should* have.

But she didn’t justify herself. She didn’t let him misjudge her.

She realized—she had failed in charity. And yet—her failure had allowed Harry Feversham to rise.

“Repeat what you said,” she whispered. “Slowly.”

“You gave the feathers back into Feversham’s hand—”

“He told you that?” she asked.

“No. I guessed it. Because his disgrace seemed impossible to undo. The chance might never come. It didn’t come for three years—until he waited in the bazaar at Suakin. Only a woman’s faith could believe such a plan. Only a woman’s encouragement could keep him going.”

Ethne smiled—a slow, radiant smile. Her face glowed with pride. Her eyes shone with something beyond joy—something sacred.

“Yes,” she said. “You’re right. I was the woman who planned his redemption.”

She laughed again—happily, freely.

“Did he tell you of a fourth white feather?” she asked.

“No.”

“I’ll tell you the truth,” she said, resuming her seat. “The plan was all his. I didn’t encourage it. Until today, I didn’t even know of it. Since that night in Donegal, I’ve had no word from him. I told him to take the three feathers—because they were his. I wanted him to know I agreed with the accusations they symbolized. That seems cruel? Perhaps. But I did more. I broke off a fourth feather from my fan and gave it to him. I wanted to end everything—our acquaintance, our kindness, our memories. I wanted us both to be strangers—forever.”

She spoke the last words in a whisper.

Willoughby didn’t understand.

Only Sutch and Harry Feversham might have.

“I was heartbroken when I did it,” she continued. “And I’ve been sorry ever since. I’ve never forgotten that feather—or the quiet dignity with which he took it. But today… I am glad.”

Her voice was low, but rich with emotion.

“Oh, very glad. Because this was *his* idea. *His* deed. They are entirely his. I had no part in it. And I am proud of that.”

Yet Willoughby pointed to the feather in her hand.

“He sent this back to you,” he said.

“Yes,” she said. “He knew I would be glad to know.”

And suddenly, she pressed it close to her chest.

She sat like that—still, glowing—for a long while.

Then Willoughby stood.

“By Jove!” he exclaimed. “Jack Durrance!”

Durrance stood in the gap in the hedge—the only entrance to the enclosure.

Ethne had forgotten everything—Colonel Durrance, the world, even time itself. From the moment Captain Willoughby placed that small, stained feather into her hand—a once-white plume now turned yellow with age and journeying—her thoughts had been consumed by one name: Harry Feversham. She had brought Willoughby into the secluded garden enclosure, drawn in by his tale, and since then she had lived only through the echoes of it. As he spoke, she filled in the gaps with memories of Harry’s voice, his eyes, the way he moved—each detail a spark rekindling a flame long buried beneath five years of silence and strength.

She had left behind the sunlit August garden, its vibrant flowers and golden light, and slipped back into those weary years when she had carried herself with quiet dignity, smiling through pain and loss. But now, as she lifted her head and saw Durrance standing at the hedge’s entrance, the past rushed forward like a wave.

“Hush,” she whispered to Willoughby, her face draining of color, her eyes closing tightly as if to block out a sudden wound. Yet there was no time for sorrow. The brief respite was over. She must return to the life she had built—one defined by duty, restraint, and a marriage she could not abandon. If she could not forget Harry Feversham, she would not let anyone know she hadn’t. Durrance must believe her love was pure, unshaken, and whole.

He stood at the gate of the enclosure, then stepped inside. He was too sharp, too quick to read people—especially her. It would be dangerous if he met Willoughby or learned of his visit. Ethne scanned the space around them—no escape. A creek lay ahead; thick hedges rose on three sides. Only one path led in or out, and Durrance blocked it.

“Keep still,” she murmured.

“You know him?” Willoughby asked.

“Of course. We served together at Suakin. I heard he’d gone blind. I’m glad it’s not true.” He glanced at Durrance’s steady walk, his confident stride.

“Speak lower,” Ethne said sharply. “It *is* true. He is blind.”

“One would never guess it,” Willoughby replied. “What can I say to him?”

“Say nothing.”

Durrance remained near the edge of the clearing, facing them. His posture was calm, almost serene. He called out softly, “Ethne.”

The quietness of his voice made it seem as though he could see.

“It’s impossible—he sees us,” Willoughby murmured.

“He sees nothing.”

Again, Durrance called, louder this time—his voice tinged with doubt.

“He’s not sure,” Ethne whispered. “Don’t move. Don’t breathe.”

She gripped Willoughby’s arm, her fingers tight, her body rigid. She stared at Durrance, her face taut with fear, her breath held. Willoughby watched her, struck by her stillness, her intensity. Something in her dread infected him. He began to feel the same invisible danger, though he knew nothing of what she feared.

“He’s coming closer,” he breathed.

“Not a sound. Not a twitch.”

“Why?”

“Because he must not know you’re here.”

Durrance advanced, passing in front of their bench. He stopped, facing them. Willoughby’s heart pounded. Surely he sees? His eyes were fixed. His stance relaxed, as if about to speak. Even Ethne, who knew better, felt a flicker of doubt.

“Ethne!” he called again—softly, as before.

No reply.

He shrugged, turned, and walked toward the creek. His back was to them now. But Ethne, trained by years of watching a man whose face no longer revealed his mind, read the subtle signs—the tilt of his head, the way he swung his stick—everything told her he was listening. Listening intently.

Her grip tightened.

They sat frozen. A minute passed. Then Durrance turned abruptly, stepping quickly toward the bench.

But Ethne was ready. She did not flinch. No rustle. No movement. Her hold on Willoughby kept him still.

“I wonder where she can be,” Durrance muttered aloud. And then he walked away.

Only when he vanished from sight did Ethne release his arm.

“That was close,” Willoughby said, finally allowed to speak.

“What if he’d sat down right on top of us?”

“Then he would have,” Ethne answered calmly. “But he didn’t.”

“You’ve told me everything?”

“So far as I remember.”

“And all this happened last spring?”

“The spring of last year,” Willoughby confirmed.

“I want to ask you something. Why didn’t you bring the feather to me last summer?”

“My leave was short. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin.”

“I see,” Ethne said quietly. “I hope you had good sport.”

“It wasn’t bad.”

Last summer, she had been free. Had Willoughby come then, bearing news of Harry’s redemption, everything might have been different. A silent cry rose in her heart: *Why didn’t you come?* But she gave no sign. Her composure was forged in hardship, and she spoke so lightly that Willoughby believed her curiosity was mere politeness.

“You might have written,” she suggested.

“Feversham didn’t think it urgent. A letter couldn’t explain it properly. He asked me to tell you when I could. I didn’t get the chance until now. Honestly, I thought Feversham might already be back.”

“Oh, no,” Ethne said. “There’s no possibility of that. Two feathers remain unredeemed before he’ll ask me to take back mine.”

Willoughby shook his head. “Feversham can’t persuade Castleton or Trench to retract their accusations the way he did me.”

“Why not?”

“Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai.”

“Killed?” Ethne laughed—a short, sharp sound of satisfaction.

Willoughby stared, stunned.

But her face betrayed no surprise—only relief. Ethne was of Celtic blood, and death, to her, was not the end. She could hate fiercely, and she hated these men deeply. Though she had given Harry the fourth feather to show her agreement, she cared little for that act. She was glad Castleton was gone.

“And Colonel Trench?” she asked.

“No,” Willoughby said. “He was captured during a reconnaissance. He’s a prisoner in Omdurman.”

“Ah.”

“I don’t think you understand what captivity in Omdurman means,” Willoughby said, gravely. “If you did, even if you disliked him, you’d feel pity.”

“Not I,” Ethne replied firmly.

“I’ll tell you what it implies.”

“No,” she said. “I don’t wish to hear about Trench. But you must go. I need to ask you one thing first.” She rose.

“What became of Mr. Feversham after you gave him the feather?”

“I told him he’d done all that could be expected. He accepted my advice. He boarded the first steamer from Suakin bound for Suez and left the Sudan.”

“I must find him,” Ethne said. “He must come back. Did he need money?”

“No. He still drew his allowance. He said he had more than enough.”

“I’m glad,” she said.

She bid Willoughby wait while she checked the garden. The place was empty. Durrance was gone. The house, with its striped sunblinds, seemed asleep. The birds were quiet. Only bees hummed lazily among the blooms, and a boy’s voice drifted across the creek.

She returned.

“You may go now,” she said. “I cannot pretend friendship for you, Captain Willoughby—but thank you for coming. You’re returning to Kingsbridge? I hope so. I do not wish Colonel Durrance to know of your visit—or anything you’ve told me.”

“But Durrance was Feversham’s great friend,” Willoughby objected.

“He doesn’t know about the feathers,” Ethne said. “And I do not wish him to. He does not know why my engagement to Feversham ended. I will not let him learn it. Your story would reveal too much.”

“Why?”

She looked at him, calm and direct. “Because I am engaged to Colonel Durrance.”

It was her deepest fear—that he suspected she did not love him. If he learned Harry had redeemed himself, he would insist she choose between them. And she refused to give up the only thing left to him—her loyalty. She was bound by honor. To betray that now, after Harry’s sacrifice, would be a mockery.

Willoughby whistled softly. “Engaged to Jack Durrance?”

“Then I’ve wasted my time bringing you this feather,” he said, pointing to it in her hand.

She pulled her hand back sharply, as if afraid he might take it.

“I am most grateful,” she said.

“It’s a bit of a muddle, isn’t it?” Willoughby remarked. “Rough on Feversham, perhaps. Rough on Durrance, too—when you think about it.”

Then he looked at her—her careful handling of the feather, the glow in her eyes when he spoke of Harry, the eagerness in her questions.

“I shouldn’t wonder if it’s rough on you, too, Miss Eustace.”

She did not answer.

They walked to the water’s edge. She hurried him down, determined he sail unseen.

But Mrs. Adair had seen everything. From her window, she had watched Ethne meet Durrance, followed by the boat, the man disembarking, the meeting in the trees. She had noted the silence, the hesitation. And when Durrance returned alone, she guessed—*Could it be Harry Feversham?*

She saw Durrance enter the house, asking for Ethne.

“She’s not in the garden,” Mrs. Adair said.

“No,” he replied. “I went in and called. It was empty.”

“Indeed?”

“Are you going?”

“Yes, home.”

“Perhaps you’ll dine with us tonight. Eight o’clock.”

“Thank you. I’ll be pleased.”

He lingered by the window, idly swinging the blind’s tassel.

“I didn’t know until today that you planned for me to return home while Ethne stayed with you until I found out if a cure was possible,” he said. “Very kind of you, Mrs. Adair.”

“It was natural,” she replied.

“And when did you learn of my condition?”

“The day after Calder’s telegram reached her from Wadi Halfa, I suppose.”

Durrance smiled faintly. “Oh, so you knew of that telegram.”

“I thought you didn’t,” Mrs. Adair said. “Ethne asked me not to mention it.”

“Of course I knew,” he said, and walked out.

Mrs. Adair’s thoughts turned to the garden. She had seen Durrance call aloud—and yet someone had been there, hidden, silent. Her suspicion grew.

Then she saw Ethne and Willoughby emerge from the trees. Was it Feversham? She must know.

She ran down to the bank.

As Willoughby pushed off from the shore, he saw her—Mrs. Adair—standing there, eyes wide with disappointment.

She called to Ethne.

“I saw you cross the lawn from my window,” she said.

“Yes?” Ethne replied.

Mrs. Adair did not move.

Ethne sighed. “I was talking to Captain Willoughby. You don’t know him, I think?”

“No,” he said, raising his hat. “But I know Mrs. Adair by name. I know friends of yours—Durrance, for instance—and of course I knew—”

A glance from Ethne cut him short.

He turned to the boat.

“Of course, what?” Mrs. Adair asked, smiling.

“Of course I knew of you, Mrs. Adair.”

She understood—what he had nearly said was not meant to be spoken.

“Captain Willoughby,” she repeated to herself.

“You belong to Colonel Durrance’s regiment?”

“No,” he said. “North Surrey.”

“Ah! Mr. Feversham’s old regiment,” she said pleasantly.

Willoughby had walked straight into her trap.

But Ethne turned to her, calm and clear. “We’ve been talking of Mr. Feversham.”

She held out her hand. “Good-bye, Captain.”

Mrs. Adair interrupted. “Colonel Durrance has gone home, but he dines with us tonight. I came to tell you—and now I’m glad, because I can invite your friend to lunch.”

Willoughby, already half in the boat, stepped back. “That’s very kind—”

“It is,” Ethne said, “but Captain Willoughby’s leave is short. We mustn’t keep him.”

He looked at her, pleading.

She met his gaze—quiet, firm, unyielding.

He bowed, climbed into the boat, and pushed off.

“It’s a little rough on me, too, perhaps, Miss Eustace,” he said.

She laughed—a soft, clear laugh.

And as they walked back to the terrace, she opened her hand and showed Mrs. Adair the small white feather.

She laughed again.

But she said nothing.

It was her secret. And hers alone.

Mrs. Adair made no demand for explanations. Behind her calm, composed face lay a mind sharp and cunning, one that thrived on secrets and preferred to spy through a keyhole rather than walk straight in. To her, knowledge won by cleverness was far more valuable than truth handed over freely. She avoided direct questions as if they were traps, and took pride in coaxing out small revelations from others—trivial bits of news she could have had at breakfast with a simple inquiry. So when Ethne clung to the meaning of a single white feather and reacted so deeply to Captain Willoughby’s news about Harry Feversham, Mrs. Adair said nothing. Instead, she carried on with cheerful conversation during lunch, steering the talk toward light topics. She was good company when she chose to be—and she chose now.

But her efforts were in vain. “I don’t believe you’re hearing a word I’m saying!” she finally exclaimed.

Ethne laughed and admitted it. As soon as the meal ended, she retreated to her room, seeking solitude. Sitting by the window, she replayed in her mind the story Willoughby had told her earlier—the tale of Harry Feversham’s courage, his secret return, his long years of sacrifice. Her heart swelled with emotion, stirred as though by music played from heaven itself. The regret that he hadn’t come home sooner, before she’d become engaged to Durrance, faded into insignificance. What mattered now was the joy that surged within her—a joy so great it eclipsed all sorrow. Her pride, shattered long ago by Harry’s rejection in the hall at Lennon House, had been restored—not by a man who had once wounded her, but by the very man who had done so. And she was profoundly grateful to him for risking everything to reclaim her dignity.

She felt alive again. Youth returned to her in the golden August light. A new warmth hummed through her veins. She unlocked a drawer in her dressing case and pulled out the portrait—the only gift from Harry Feversham she had kept. It was a picture of someone long gone from her life, someone she would never love again. Yet he remained a friend. She looked at it with deep contentment, knowing that Harry had needed no words from her to inspire his courage, no encouragement to keep him true to his purpose. He had acted not for reward, but because it was right.

After replacing the portrait, she slipped the white feather beside it, locked the drawer, and stepped back to the window.

Outside, the breeze danced across the lawn, casting shifting shadows beneath the tall trees. The sunlight softened, turning amber and gold. But Ethne’s thoughts turned northward—to Donegal, to her homeland. It was August, and the first heather would be blooming on the hills. She wished this moment of joy had come there, where the mountains stood guard and the streams sang their ancient songs. There, she believed, the land itself might have shared in her happiness. Great sorrow or great joy always called her home—because endurance was easier, and joy deeper, among familiar places.

Yet she wasn’t entirely alone. Dermod’s old collie dog, now her constant companion, sat quietly by her side. To him she whispered her thoughts, and when her voice trembled with emotion, he listened without judgment. In the dog’s silence, she found comfort.

And in that silence, she pieced together what Willoughby had left unsaid, what Harry himself had never spoken aloud. She imagined those three hidden years in Suakin—how he lived in disguise among crowded streets, watching soldiers march off to battle, returning bloodied and victorious. Each time, he had to slip away, afraid some old friend—Durrance, Willoughby, Trench—might recognize him. She saw the panic in his eyes when he first saw Berber’s dark walls, the desperate night spent in ruined fields, the frantic search for water amid the sand dunes of Obak. These images filled her mind, and each time she thought of them, she asked: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

She sat wrapped in golden light until dusk painted the creek with fading hues. Rooks wheeled overhead, calling out their evening song, reminding her it was time for dinner.

That night, Ethne brought a radiant spirit to the table. Her eyes sparkled like stars, her cheeks glowed with color. Mrs. Adair watched, amazed. She knew something profound had changed. She had never seen Ethne so bright, so full of life. She was certain Captain Willoughby had delivered startling news—but still, she couldn’t guess its nature. Still, she joined the conversation, and the dinner passed with ease, free of tension for the first time since Durrance returned to Guessens.

Even Durrance seemed lighter. His usual stiffness melted. He laughed readily, matched Ethne’s humor, and the weight of his silence lifted. When they rose from the table, he smiled and said, “You’ll play your violin tonight, I think.”

“Yes,” she replied. “With all my heart.”

He laughed and held the door open.

The violin had been locked away for two months. To Durrance, it had become a barometer of Ethne’s soul. When her world was at peace, the instrument spoke; when it was broken, it stayed silent—for fear of reopening wounds too raw for others to see. Ethne knew it well: the violin was an indiscreet confidant. But tonight, it would speak again.

Mrs. Adair waited until Ethne was out of earshot. “You’ve noticed the change in her?” she asked.

“Yes,” Durrance answered. “One has waited for it, hoped for it, despaired of it.”

“Are you glad?”

He raised his head. “Do you wonder? She’s always been kind, generous, selfless. But tonight… there’s something more. Something new.”

A look of pity crossed Mrs. Adair’s face. She turned and left without another word.

Durrance claimed the transformation as his own.

Mrs. Adair drew up the blinds, opened the window, and let the moonlight flood the room. Then, seeing Ethne unlock her violin case, she stepped out onto the terrace. She could not bear to stay inside.

When Durrance entered the drawing room, Ethne was alone. She sat by the window, tightening the strings of her violin. He took a chair behind her, hidden in shadow.

“What shall I play?” she asked.

“The Musoline Overture,” he said. “You played it the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember how you played it—so beautifully. Play it again. I want to compare.”

“I’ve played it since.”

“Never to me.”

They were alone. The windows stood wide open. The night was quiet, silvered by moonlight.

Ethne suddenly crossed to the lamp and extinguished it. She returned to her seat. Durrance remained in darkness, leaning forward, hands resting on his knees, listening—not with casual attention, but with a focus so intense he had not shown all evening. He was applying a final test—one that would determine the course of both their lives. The violin would reveal the truth. Would it speak only of friendship—or something deeper?

She began to play.

As the music rose, Ethne forgot Durrance was even there. The garden breathed softly, warm and sweet. On the creek, moonlight shimmered like liquid silver. Trees stood still, dreaming under the stars. And as the melody floated across the lawn, she imagined it traveling down the creek, past Salcombe Bar, across the sea—until it reached a distant shore, where a man slept beneath southern skies, the desert wind brushing his face.

*If he could only hear,* she thought. *If he could wake and know this music is a message of friendship.*

And so, she played with greater skill than ever before. The violin became a voice—full of empathy, longing, hope. Her fantasy grew: the music was a bridge stretched across the world, and for these few moments, she and Harry Feversham could meet, shake hands, and part again—each continuing their separate paths, yet strengthened by the connection.

The chords rang clear and strong. To Ethne, they proclaimed the pride that had returned to her heart. Her fancy deepened into certainty. It was no longer *if* he heard—but *he must* hear!

And then, a wilder hope flared: *What if he could answer?*

She lingered on the final notes, waiting. When the last sound faded into silence, she sat still, violin cradled in her lap, gazing out into the moonlit garden.

An answer came—but not from the sea or sky. It came from the shadows behind her, spoken in Durrance’s voice.

“Ethne,” he said, “where do you think I heard that overture last played?”

She startled, remembering he was there. “You told me—at Ramelton, when you first came to Lennon House.”

“I’ve heard it since. Not played by you. Not really played at all. Just fragments—a melody hinted at, clumsily plucked on a zither, with many wrong notes—by a Greek in a bare, whitewashed café lit by a single harsh lamp, at Wadi Halfa.”

“This overture?” she whispered. “How strange.”

“Not so strange,” he said. “Because the Greek was Harry Feversham.”

The answer had come. Ethne knew it was real. She sat motionless in the moonlight. If anyone had looked closely, they might have seen her eyelids closed.

Silence stretched between them.

She did not question why Durrance had kept this secret so long. She did not ask what Harry was doing in such a place. But she understood—he had spoken to her as she had spoken to him. The music had built a bridge. And it was not strange that he used Durrance’s voice to cross it.

“When was this?” she asked at last.

“In February of this year. I’ll tell you about it.”

“Yes,” she said softly. “Please, tell me.”

And from the shadows, Durrance began to speak.

Ethne did not turn toward Durrance. She remained motionless, her violin resting on her lap, her gaze fixed across the moonlit garden to the silver band of light that slipped through the trees. Her stillness was deliberate—a shield, a sanctuary. It allowed her to believe that Harry Feversham himself stood before her, speaking in his own voice, even though it was Durrance who spoke. The man beside her faded into silence, irrelevant, almost invisible. She listened with the same intensity as he, yearning for the words to unfold slowly, so the message would stretch out—long enough for her to gather every syllable and hold it close.

"It was the night before I set off eastward into the desert—for the last time," Durrance began, his voice heavy with regret. He lingered on those words, "last time," as if they carried a weight too deep for words. But Ethne felt nothing—no sorrow, no pity. Only the echo of something far more personal.

"Yes," she said softly. "That was in February. The middle of the month, wasn't it? Do you remember the day? I’d like to know exactly."

"The fifteenth," he replied.

She repeated it, letting the date settle in her mind. "I was at Glenalla all February," she murmured. "What was I doing on the fifteenth? It doesn’t matter."

A strange sensation had gripped her earlier that morning, when Willoughby told his story—how she had known nothing, by instinct or intuition, of what had happened. Now, that feeling returned. It was odd, almost cruel, that she should only learn of Harry’s fate now—on this August night, beneath this quiet garden, bathed in moonlight and stillness. A pang of guilt pierced her. *It was my fault,* she thought. *If I had believed in him, I would have known. I am well punished.*

She could not imagine the message bringing anything but good news. It would confirm what she already hoped—the tale would expand, complete, perfecting the moment. That much she knew without doubt.

"Well?" she said. "Go on."

"I'd spent the whole day in my office finishing up work," Durrance continued. "At ten o'clock, I locked the door, relieved to know I wouldn’t open it again for six weeks. Then I walked north along the Nile bank from Wadi Halfa into Tewfikieh. As I stepped into the main street, I saw a crowd gathered outside a café—Arabs, negroes, a few Greeks, Egyptian soldiers—all lit by the glare from within. I heard music: a violin and a zither, both played horribly, jangling out a waltz. I pushed through the crowd and looked inside."

He paused, and Ethne closed her eyes, painting the scene in her mind. She saw Harry bent over his zither, and instantly, the question came: *Why was he there?*

It made sense he wouldn’t return to England. It was certain he wouldn’t come back to her unless she called. And Captain Willoughby had said Harry expected no word from her—he hadn’t left any address. Yet here he was, disguised among beggars and musicians, playing for a meal and a night’s shelter.

"You spoke to him?" she asked abruptly.

"To whom? Oh—Harry?" Durrance replied. "Yes, later, after I realized it was him."

"How did you know?"

"The waltz ended. The old woman collapsed onto a bench. The young man lifted his head from his zither. The old man scraped another chord, and the girl stepped forward to sing. Her voice was fresh, youthful—but utterly lacking in skill. Still, the old man smiled. The mother tapped her foot proudly. The crowd laughed, tossed crude jokes. It was terrible, don’t you think?"

"Yes," Ethne whispered, but her mind was elsewhere. She felt no sympathy for the players—only fascination with Harry’s presence. She sat staring into the moonlight streaming across the floor, as if it were a window to that distant café, glowing under the African sky.

"And then?" she asked.

Durrance went on. "The young man began a solo on the zither. He played wrong notes—no tune at first. The crowd jeered. I turned away, heart sick—until suddenly, a sequence of correct notes caught my ear. I stopped. A melody emerged—thin, weak, almost ghostly—but familiar. I stood there, between hovels and stunted trees, and I was swept back to Ramelton—to a summer night beneath a soft Donegal sky. You were sitting by an open window, just as you are now, playing the Musoline Overture. The same piece you’ve played tonight."

"Was it a melody from that overture?" she breathed.

"Yes. And it was Harry Feversham who played it. I didn’t realize at once. I wasn’t quick then."

"But you are now," she said, smiling faintly. "You’d have known right away."

"True. But then, I was only curious—how had a Greek musician learned such a tune? Still, I decided to reward him. I thought you’d want me to."

"Yes," she whispered.

So when the young man stepped out, hat in hand, passing through the mocking crowd, I threw a sovereign into his hat. He turned—startled. I recognized him. Even with the beard. And before he could stop himself, he gasped, 'Jack!'"

"You couldn’t have been mistaken," Ethne said, breathless. "The man who played the zither was—"

She stopped herself. The name hovered on her lips, but she pulled it back. She remembered how Harry had no ear for music—how he’d never appreciated anything except what she played. "He had no talent," she said, laughing softly. "No taste. You couldn’t invent a discord harsh enough to catch his attention. He couldn’t have remembered a note from the Musoline Overture."

"Yet he did," Durrance insisted. "Somehow, he remembered. I can see it. He had so little to hold onto. That one memory—this melody—was worth everything. He must have practiced it endlessly, humming it, whistling it, making mistakes until finally, one day, it came clear. Then, slowly, laboriously, he shaped it on the zither. Can’t you imagine it? Him alone in the dark, trying again and again, until he could play it—not perfectly, but truly?"

Ethne understood. She had her answer. The overture—her favorite—had carried the message: *I have not forgotten you.* Despite the white feather, despite the shame, Harry still thought of her—with friendship, with care. He hadn’t played that melody in vain.

She was shaken—more deeply than she ever thought possible. She wondered if, as he sat in that bare café, strumming for strangers, he’d dreamed that somehow, across oceans and deserts, the echo of that tune might reach her. She knew now—beyond doubt—that she could never truly forget Harry Feversham. The image of that café, lit against the desert night, would haunt her forever. But she would not let it weaken her resolve. She would continue to pretend to forget—because she would not let Durrance suffer for her past mistakes.

"I told you last year," Durrance said, "at Hill Street, that I never wanted to see Feversham again. I was wrong. The reluctance was all his. Not mine. When he realized he’d called my name, he tried to slip back into the crowd, babbling Greek. But I grabbed his arm. I wouldn’t let go."

"He had done you great wrong," Ethne interrupted sharply. "I know that."

But Durrance shook his head. "Not then. All I remembered was that years ago, Harry Feversham was my friend—my only true friend. We rowed together at Oxford. He was stroke, I was seven. I still see the stripes on his jersey during the final sprint. We swam at Sandford Lasher, ate supper on Kennington Island, skipped lectures, paddled up the Cher to Islip. And here he was—at Wadi Halfa—playing zither for scraps, living like an outcast."

"No," Ethne said firmly. "It wasn’t because he needed money. His father kept sending him allowance. He accepted it."

Durrance blinked. "You’re sure?"

"Quite sure. I heard it today."

She didn’t realize she’d slipped. But Durrance, preoccupied, didn’t notice. Nor did she.

"So you never asked him why he was there?" she pressed. "Why didn’t you?"

Her voice trembled with disappointment. This was the last news—yet incomplete, like a letter torn in half. The truth might never be known.

"I was a fool," Durrance admitted. There was real regret in his voice—equal to hers. "I had him by the arm, and I let him go. I was a fool." He struck his forehead with his fist.

"He tried Arabic," Durrance went on. "Said they were peaceful travelers. Offered to give back the money. But I held him. 'Harry Feversham,' I said, 'that won’t do.' Then he gave in—and whispered in English: 'Let me go, Jack. Let me go.'"

There was a crowd around them. Clearly, Harry had reason to hide. Perhaps shame—shame at his fall.

"I told him to come to my quarters when he could," Durrance said. "Then I let him go."

All night, he waited on the verandah. But Harry never came.

In the morning, he started across the desert. At the very last moment, as his camel rose, he leaned down to speak—then stopped. "I knew nothing about his allowance," he explained. "I thought he’d fallen low. It didn’t seem fair to tell anyone else."

Ethne nodded. She understood. Though her heart ached for the lost words.

"So you never saw him again?"

"I was gone nine weeks. I came back blind," he said simply. The quiet honesty pierced her.

He went straight to Cairo. Calder came with him. He told Calder about seeing Harry at Tewfikieh. He asked Calder to look for him, to help if possible—and to write.

"A week ago," Durrance said, "I received a letter. And I’m troubled—very troubled."

"What did it say?" Ethne asked, leaning forward, her face shadowed. She moved toward him, needing to see his expression—but the darkness hid it. A cold fear crept into her veins.

"The two women and the old Greek," Durrance said, "left on a steamer for Assouan."

"Then Mr. Feversham stayed?" she asked eagerly.

"No," Durrance replied. "Harry Feversham didn’t stay. He slipped past Halfa the day after I left. He went south—in the morning."

"Into the desert?"

"Yes. The enemy’s country. He was seen. There’s no doubt."

Ethne was silent. Then, slowly: "You have the letter?"

"Yes."

"I’d like to read it."

She rose, crossed to him. He handed it over. She took it, walked to the window, where the moonlight was bright. She stood there, one hand pressed to her chest, reading—once, twice.

The letter confirmed it. The café owner said Joseppi—Feversham’s alias—had wandered south, carrying a water-skin and dates. Why? He didn’t know—or wouldn’t say.

Ethne had a question. But she hesitated—too long—before she could speak.

"What will happen to him?" she finally asked.

"At best," Durrance said, "capture. At worst—death. Starvation. Thirst. Or the Dervishes. But there’s a chance—just a hope—he’ll be taken prisoner. He’s white. If caught, they’ll think he’s a spy. They’ll believe he knows our plans. Most likely, they’ll send him to Omdurman. I’ve written to Calder. Spies pass through Wadi Halfa. We hear things from Omdurman. If Feversham is taken there, I’ll find out. But he must have gone mad. That’s the only explanation."

Ethne had another explanation—one she knew to be true. And she spoke it aloud, unguarded.

"Colonel Trench," she said, "is a prisoner at Omdurman."

"Oh," Durrance said, nodding. "Feversham won’t be quite alone. That’s some comfort. Perhaps something can be done. When I hear from Calder, I’ll tell you."

He didn’t understand. He still didn’t know why Harry had gone south. And he never would. For Ethne, even now, refused to break her vow. She stood at the window, clutching the letter, fighting to remain composed. She would not cry. Would not faint. She would speak quietly, calmly—even though she knew Harry had gone south to join Colonel Trench.

But the strength failed her. As Durrance began to speak again, the need to escape overwhelmed her. The garden called—cool, dark, peaceful. She needed to be alone.

"Perhaps you wonder," Durrance said, "why I’ve told you this tonight. I didn’t dare before. I want to explain why."

Ethne didn’t hear him. She didn’t care. The sound of his voice was unbearable. She was near the window. One step—silent, careful—and she was free.

She gathered her dress, stepped over the sill, and vanished into the shadows.

This was the third slip she made that night.

Ethne had meant to slip away unseen. But Mrs. Adair, seated in the shadow of the house on the terrace, watched her from a vantage point near the open drawing-room window. The moonlight spilled across the glass, illuminating the room’s sudden shift from light to darkness as Ethne extinguished the lamp. It was a quiet act—yet it carried weight. Mrs. Adair saw Ethne cross the terrace swiftly, descend the steps, and vanish into the garden. Her movements were hurried, almost frantic. She looked not like one fleeing danger, but like someone escaping herself.

Mrs. Adair sat still, her eyes fixed on the empty chair where Ethne had been moments before. The silence that followed the lamp’s snuffing out was thick with unspoken things. Then came music—soft, tender, full of longing—and after it, hushed conversation, low and intimate, drifting through the night air. It stirred something deep within Mrs. Adair—a memory, a pang, a jealousy that flared like fire in the dark. She had endured weeks of silent torment, watching, listening, knowing she was never truly part of this world Ethne and Durrance shared. Now, this moment—this private exchange—was too much. She did not move. She listened.

And then—Ethne fled.

Mrs. Adair blinked, startled. There was no sign of anger or conflict. Ethne did not storm out; she slipped away like smoke. Yet the man inside remained. Durrance continued speaking, his voice clear now, though his words were lost to her. He spoke with ease, with relief, as if he had finally laid a burden down. And yet—he believed Ethne was still there.

She rose slowly, stepping silently toward the window. She could hear him now: laughing lightly, recounting something that had once troubled him, now resolved.

“I began to see,” he said. “Even on that first afternoon at Hill Street, two months ago, I sensed there was only friendship on your side. My blindness helped me. With your face and eyes in view, I would have believed anything you wanted me to believe. But without those defenses… I began to see. For the first time in my life, I began to see.”

Mrs. Adair stood motionless, breath held. He spoke with confidence, even joy—like a man who had won a battle he didn’t know he was fighting. He confessed his own hesitation, his selfishness, how he had delayed their marriage, lied about hope for recovery, all because he clung to the faint possibility that Ethne’s affection might deepen. He had waited, hoping—praying—for signs: a new warmth in her voice, a lift in her laughter, a depth in her music.

“And tonight,” he said, “I’ve heard them. The softness, the pride—the restraint is gone. When you played, it was as though your heart sang through the notes. I’ve waited so long… Ethne, Ethne!”

At that moment, Ethne was far from the house, in the little enclosed garden by the creek—the place she had led Captain Willoughby earlier that day. Here, beneath the silver moonlight, she was alone. Her collie dog had found her, drawn by her distress. The garden offered sanctuary. She sank onto the bench, trying to breathe in the peace of the night, to find solace in the slow rhythm of nature—the lapping tide, the whisper of leaves, the stars above. But her strength had failed. The events of the day had shattered her composure.

Only that morning, news had come—good news. Harry Feversham was alive. She had thought she had found him again. But now, the truth had struck: he had chosen to go south, to Omdurman, to join Colonel Trench. To redeem himself—not just for her, but for the three men whose honor he had dishonored. And he would pay every last price.

“Surely,” she whispered, “he could have stopped. One redemption—done right—would have been enough.” But no. He had gone, and she could not stop him. And now, she feared, he would not return.

She remembered Captain Willoughby’s warning—how cruel Omdurman could be. She had refused to listen then. Now, she could not help imagining the horrors: the prison, the suffering, the silence. Was he already enduring it? At this very hour? She shut her eyes tight, refusing to let the thought take root.

The tide sighed against the bank. It reminded her of another river—of Donegal, of summer nights spent watching dawn break over hills and streams. That land had always felt sacred to her, untouched, pure. Now, she longed for it more than ever. And with that longing came shame—a fierce, burning shame—for the lies she had told, the masks she wore, the love she kept hidden. She had built walls around herself, pretending to care only for Durrance, when all along, another name lived in her heart.

Yet she would not falter. Two lives—hers and Durrance’s—were at stake. She could not let her feelings destroy them both. Tomorrow, she would gather herself. Until then, she must remain hidden.

Inside the house, Durrance finished his confession.

“So you see,” he said, “I couldn’t speak of Harry Feversham until tonight. I feared hurting you. I feared you still remembered him. But tonight—I could tell you freely. Because I’ve seen the change in you. You’re not the same woman. You’re alive again.”

He waited. Called her name. No answer.

Then he moved. His hands touched the back of the chair. He leaned forward, searching for her presence. “Ethne?” he said again, voice softer now, tinged with doubt.

And then—twang.

A violin string snapped sharply. She had left it behind. His fingers had brushed it.

He froze. A shock passed through him. He stepped back, hand to his forehead, breathing shallow. Then, slowly, he walked to the window.

Mrs. Adair stood beside it, unmoving. The moonlight caught his face. He turned—his blind eyes searching the air, sensing something near.

“Ethne?” he called, tentatively.

“It is not Ethne,” Mrs. Adair said quickly.

Durrance stepped back. “Where has she gone?”

“She ran across the terrace. I saw her. Then I heard you speaking alone. She went into the garden—toward the trees.”

“Can you see her now?”

“No. Only the moonlight.”

He stepped out onto the sill, standing beside her. The silence stretched between them.

He knew now. The flight, the sudden silence, the broken string—it all pointed to one thing. He had spoken of Feversham, of his peril, of his journey into the wilderness. He had assumed she had forgotten. But she hadn’t. She had run because she could not bear to lie anymore.

“She ran as though she was in great trouble,” Mrs. Adair said.

“Did I cause that?”

“Yes.”

She watched his face. He was composed—but not unshaken. She had hurt him. And she took pleasure in it. Not because she loved him, but because she had suffered long enough. The irony of it—him confessing to an empty room, while she stood outside, yearning for what he would never give her—cut deep. So she gave pain in return.

“There’s one thing I don’t understand,” Durrance said. “The change in Ethne tonight. I mistook its cause. But there must have been a reason. Her laughter returned. Her gravity vanished. She became… young again.”

“Exactly,” Mrs. Adair replied. “Just as she was before Mr. Feversham disappeared from Ramelton. You’re quick, Colonel Durrance. Ethne received good news of him this morning.”

He turned sharply toward her. “Are you sure?”

“As sure as you gave her bad news tonight.”

But Durrance didn’t need confirmation. He recalled something else—Ethne mentioning that Feversham still received money from his father. “I heard it only today,” she had said.

Now it made sense. “So she misjudged him five years ago?” he murmured. “Was there really wrongdoing—or only misunderstanding? Has she learned the truth now?”

Mrs. Adair hesitated. Then: “I’ll tell you what I know. It’s not much. But you deserve to know.”

“Wait,” Durrance said sharply. He walked away, leaning on the balustrade, staring into the garden. He didn’t want to hear it. Not yet. He didn’t want to betray Ethne’s secret. But he also knew why she kept it. She didn’t want him to suspect that her heart still belonged to another.

Still, he turned back. “I’m no casuist,” he said. “But here—disloyalty feels like the truest loyalty. Tell me what you know.”

She did.

Captain Willoughby had arrived that morning from Kingsbridge. He had once served in Feversham’s regiment. He brought news—and a token.

“Ethne met him,” Mrs. Adair said. “They walked together into the little garden. I saw them. She led him there without looking back—as though she had forgotten.”

“As though she had forgotten me,” Durrance said quietly.

“No doubt she had.”

They stayed there for a long time. Durrance had left before they emerged. Mrs. Adair had been in the garden, near the water’s edge.

“She introduced him,” she said. “But wouldn’t let him stay. She sent him back immediately.”

“How do you know the news was good?”

“Her manner. Her laugh. She was radiant. And she carried a token—something precious.”

“A token?”

“A white feather,” Mrs. Adair said. “All soiled and speckled with dust. Can you read the riddle of that feather?”

Durrance paused. Then: “Not yet.”

He walked back and forth on the terrace, lost in thought. Finally, he returned to the hall, fetched his cap, and came back.

“It was kind of you to tell me,” he said. “Now—when I was in the room alone, and you came to the window—how much did you hear? What were the first words?”

Mrs. Adair smiled faintly. “You spoke of your blindness. Of your hope being gone. Of using it as an excuse to delay our marriage. She didn’t hear any of it.”

Relief washed over him. “Yes. She escaped the moment she read the letter. She didn’t know I’d given up hope. I’m glad. Very glad.”

He shook her hand. “Goodnight.”

“You see,” he added, almost to himself, “if I hear that Feversham is in Omdurman—something might be done. From Suakin. Or Assouan. Something might be done.”

“Which way did she go?”

“Over to the water.”

“She had her dog with her, I hope.”

“The dog followed her.”

“I’m glad,” Durrance said.

He knew what comfort the dog would bring her. And perhaps, in that moment, he envied it.

Mrs. Adair watched him walk across the garden, steady, purposeful—like a man who sees. There was nothing in his stride to betray the fact that the one thing he had left—his sight, his hope, his belief in the future—had just been taken from him.

And yet, he walked forward.

Durrance found his body-servant waiting for him when he crossed the fields to his home, "Guessens."
"You can turn out the lights and go to bed," he said, stepping through the hall into his study.

The room was more gun-room than study—though it bore that name. It held relics of a life lived boldly, fiercely, in motion. He sat for a time in silence, then rose and began pacing slowly in the dark. The dimness didn’t trouble him; he knew every inch of this place by touch, by memory. Scattered across shelves and tables were trophies: cups, goblets, medals—each one a chapter in a story only he could read.

He reached out, fingers tracing the contours of a thin-stemmed goblet. He remembered the day he won it—a steeplechase at Colchester, rain threatening, the sky gray as ash, the ploughed fields between hedges dull and flat. He ran his hand over the pewter pen-holder on his desk, the one from his college days at Oxford, when he rowed in the freshman fours. A silver hoof, mounted on the mantel, belonged to a horse he’d loved dearly—its name lost now, but not its spirit.

These objects were not mere decorations. They were records. Each one carried a scent, a sound, a heat of memory. And then came the guns—his true companions. He took them down from their racks, one by one. To him, they spoke. Not aloud, but in whispers only he could hear. A Remington rifle brought back a long, sweltering afternoon in the hills west of Berenice, where he had stalked a lion across sun-baked stone and shot it at three hundred yards just before sunset. Another rifle told of his first ibex in the Khor Baraka mountains, of antelope in the highlands north of Suakin. There was a small Greener shotgun—used on winter nights in a boat on the Salcombe estuary. With it, he’d taken his first mallard. He lifted it now, slid his hand along the barrel, felt the stock settle into the hollow of his shoulder—just as it had all those years ago.

But the voices grew louder. Too loud. Like Ethne’s violin once did after Harry Feversham vanished, echoing with a note too sharp, too piercing for comfort. As he touched the locks, knowing he could no longer see the sights, he felt the full weight of what he had lost—not just sight, but the world beyond the door. He replaced the guns, and something stirred within him: a fierce, almost desperate urge to forget his blindness. To pretend it did not exist. To believe so hard that it would cease to be.

And so he fled—not physically, but in mind—into the East.

The scent of spice and dust filled his senses. The clamor of markets, the domes of mosques against a steel-blue sky, the hot breath of the sun on his skin—it all pulled him forward. He walked through Port Said, marching with the endless line of steamers threading the Suez Canal. He heard the chant of Arab coalers, working in chains under the red glow of braziers at night, their naked figures moving like ghosts up and down the planks between barges and deck. He sailed past the red cliffs of Sinai, into the chill waters of the Gulf of Suez. He zigzagged down the Red Sea, watching the Great Bear dip low in the northern sky, while the Southern Cross blazed in the south. He passed Tor and Yambo, saw the white towers of Yeddah rise from the sea, admired the blackened woodwork of their carved windows. He wandered through the shaded bazaars, heart swelling with homesickness turned joy.

From Yeddah, he crossed the coral reefs into Suakin—where the desert stretched westward, vast and silent. He felt its rhythm—the soft padding of camel feet, the sudden stillness beneath rock-cones rising like islands from a motionless ocean, the fleeting blaze of sunset, the wind rustling through twilight, the sky turning pale green in the west, deep blue in the east. Then the planets dropped from nowhere, sweeping down to earth.

He dreamed himself there—free, strong, alive—until the familiar sounds of birds broke through: blackbirds chattering, swallows piping in the garden. Dawn. The world outside was bright with morning light.

He woke from the dream. No more journeys. His affliction had caged him. A chain was welded around his leg. He made his way up the stairs by feel, found his bed, and fell asleep as the sun climbed above the horizon.

* * *

At Dongola, on the great bend of the Nile south of Wadi Halfa, the sun burned already. The town stirred.

Under the few palm trees before the house of Emir Wad El Nejoumi, sport was prepared. A prisoner—white, captured near the wells of El Agia on the Arbain road—had been brought in during the night. Now he waited, bound and bare, for judgment.

Word spread like fire. Men, women, children flocked to the spectacle. Before the palms, an open space led to the emir’s gate. Behind, sand sloped down to the river. In the center stood a gallows—black and stark against the yellow earth.

Harry Feversham stood beneath the trees. Four Ansar soldiers guarded him. His clothes were gone. Only a torn jibbeh clung to his body, a cotton twist on his head. His shoulders and arms were blistered from sun and travel. His ankles were shackled, his wrists bound with rope, an iron collar locked about his neck, linked to a chain held by one of the guards.

Yet he smiled. He laughed. He danced. He seemed mad.

It was a performance. A gamble. If he could keep them guessing—whether he was truly insane or a spy with secrets for the tribes of Kordofan—then perhaps they would hesitate to kill him. Perhaps they would send him to Omdurman instead.

It was exhausting. But he endured.

Inside the house, the emir and his council debated. Outside, the gallows loomed. Time stretched. The sun climbed higher. No one came from the gate. The hours were worse than any fight. For the first time in weeks, he had nothing to do but wait. And fear crept in.

He remembered Berber—the hope of escape, the sudden lantern behind him, the rush of action. Here, there was only time—too much of it. He could anticipate every moment. He could see the vultures perched on his shoulders, circling his eyes. His heart sank. He felt faint—just as he had once, years before, when hounds broke cover and he sat trembling on his horse.

He glanced toward the gallows. But he also thought of Ethne. Of the fourth feather. Of the future he had dared to believe in. That thought steadied him.

He was glad of the collar. The fetters. They made his helplessness real—and thus, less terrifying. He summoned his strength. He laughed again. He mimicked the old woman who danced before him, singing of torture, of hellfire, of dismemberment. He nodded, bowed, mowed at her as if she sang of paradise.

Others blew war-trumpets into his ears. “Do you hear, Kaffir?” a child cried. “Blow louder! Blow louder!”

Feversham clapped his hands. “Good music!” he shouted.

Then came a tall warrior with a long spear. The crowd roared. He stepped forward, swinging the spear like a bowler preparing to bowl. Feversham looked wildly around—no escape. He thrust his chest forward, ready to meet the blow.

But the spear never struck.

One of the guards yanked the chain from behind. Feversham was thrown backward, half-choked, onto the ground. Three times this happened—each time met with laughter and cheers.

Then a soldier appeared at the gate.
“Bring him in!” he called.

The prisoner was dragged across the courtyard into a dark room.

For a moment, darkness. Then his eyes adjusted. He saw a tall man seated on an angareb—the Sudanese bedstead—two others squatting beside him. The man on the bed was the emir.

“You are a spy from Wadi Halfa,” the emir said.

“No,” Feversham replied, laughing. “I am a musician.”

A zither with broken strings was handed to him. He sat on the floor, fingers fumbling, breathing hard. Slowly, he played. The melody wavered—but it was the same tune Durrance had heard in Tewfikieh, the same Ethne had played the night before at Southpool. The only melody he knew.

When he finished, the emir repeated: “You are a spy.”

“I have told you the truth,” Feversham answered, stubborn.

Nejoumi changed tone. Food was brought—raw camel liver, salted, peppered. Feversham had no appetite. But he ate. He knew refusal would be seen as fear—and fear meant death.

As he chewed, Nejoumi asked questions—about Cairo’s fortifications, Assouan’s garrison, rumors of conflict between the Khedive and the Sirdar.

To each, Feversham replied:
“How should a Greek know such things?”

Nejoumi rose. Gave an order.

The soldiers seized Feversham and dragged him back into the sunlight. Water was poured on the rope binding his wrists—so it swelled, bit deeper into his flesh.

“Speak, Kaffir,” Nejoumi said. “You carry promises to Kordofan.”

Silence.

Feversham clung to his plan. He could not alter it—not now, not in fear. He had thought it through. He would not betray it.

A rope was placed around his neck. He was pushed beneath the gallows.

“Speak, Kaffir,” Nejoumi said. “So shall you live.”

Feversham smiled. Grimaced. Shook his head.

He was astonished—he did not fall to his knees. He did not beg. He felt no temptation to crawl. He wondered if the stories were true—that English prisoners went calmly to the scaffold because they were drugged. He was not drugged. Yet he stood firm. His heart pounded—not with terror, but with a strange excitement.

He did not think of Ethne. Not now. The dread of failing her, of the hope dying unfulfilled—none of that mattered. He had a part to play. He played it. That was all.

Nejoumi stared at him for a long moment. Then turned to the men ready to remove the angareb.

“To-morrow,” he said, “the Kaffir goes to Omdurman.”

Only then did Feversham feel the rope burn his wrists.

Mrs. Adair sat quietly in her room, her mind restless with the weight of what she had confessed to Durrance. She could not shake off the uneasiness that followed her words—what would he do now? Would he confront Ethne with the truth? Would he reveal how he had come to understand the depth of Ethne’s feelings for Feversham, and how her own affection for him had been mistaken all along? If he did, it would be impossible to hide her role in his discovery. She would have to face Ethne directly, and the thought of that moment filled her with dread—the quiet gaze, the unblinking honesty in those eyes, the question that would hang in the air: *Why?*

For a time, relief washed over her. Nothing changed between Ethne and Durrance. They met as before, spoke as if nothing had happened—no trace of the night Willoughby arrived at the garden, no echo of the evening Ethne played the Musoline Overture on the violin. The past seemed wiped clean.

But when the immediate fear passed, Mrs. Adair began to feel a different kind of unease. Her interference had done nothing. And then came irritation—then disappointment. Why had Durrance not acted? Why had he not broken through the pretense?

Durrance, however, had made up his mind quickly. Ethne didn’t want him to know. In her sorrow, she clung to the belief that she had brought happiness to someone—someone she truly cared for. That belief was her comfort. And Durrance saw it. He chose not to destroy it—not yet. He needed certainty. He had to know whether Ethne and Feversham were truly separated by misunderstanding or by an irrevocable rift. He had to know what had become of Harry Feversham. Until then, silence was his weapon.

So he feigned ignorance. He dropped his old habit of careful observation. He even let go of his usual attentiveness—because there was no longer any need. Instead, he forced himself into cheerfulness, making light of his blindness, pretending it was no great burden. He smiled often, joked freely, and said things like:

“You see, one can get used to blindness—it becomes the natural thing. But one never gets used to you, Ethne. Every time I meet you, I find something new to delight me. Besides,” he added with a grin, “there’s always the chance of a cure.”

And it worked. Ethne believed him. She saw the effort behind his smile, and it gave her strength. She could now bear her own pain, knowing that he did not suspect her secret grief.

But her pain was deep. For a brief moment, she had tasted the joy she once knew before the three white feathers reached Ramelton. Now, that joy was gone, replaced by a familiar sorrow—sleepless nights, the heavy ache in her head, the endless waiting for dawn. She could no longer pretend Harry Feversham was dead. He was alive—and trapped somewhere in the heat and darkness of Omdurman. She longed to know his fate, yet dreaded the truth.

Once, her impatience broke through. “I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman?” she asked casually, forcing her voice flat.

“Possible? Yes,” Durrance replied easily. “But difficult. It takes time. Attempts have been made to rescue Trench and others—but none have succeeded. The problem is the go-between.”

Ethne turned sharply. “The go-between?”

She hesitated, then whispered, “I think I begin to understand.” Then stopped herself abruptly.

“A man who moves between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier,” Durrance explained. “Usually a Dervish trader or peddler, someone who slips into Wadi Halfa or Assouan. He takes money and promises to deliver messages—or prisoners. But he’s risky. If caught, he’d be killed. So often, he backs out at the last moment. Or worse—he’s a fraud. You pay him. He returns months later, alone, with excuses: bad season, tight guards, suspicion. He asks for more money. You give it. He comes back again—alone.”

Ethne nodded slowly. “Exactly.”

It was a revelation. She had known Harry meant to help Colonel Trench—but how? Now she understood. Harry would be the go-between. He would use his own capture as a means to reach Trench. The idea filled her with hope. He had planned this carefully. He wouldn’t stay behind if he could free Trench. He must return. The second feather would come back to her.

They sat in silence on the terrace. The sunset painted the creek in crimson and gold.

“Life wouldn’t be easy in Omdurman’s prison,” Ethne said, trying to sound indifferent.

“Easy?” Durrance laughed softly. “No. A hovel—crowded, dark, without air or light. The roof barely two feet above your head. Locked in from dusk till dawn. Prisoners standing all night, packed like animals. Imagine it here, on a night like this. Now imagine it in August in the Sudan—suffocating heat, no breath, no water. And if you remember places like this… well, the memory makes it worse.”

Ethne looked out across the garden. At that very moment, Harry might be gasping for air in that foul hole, throat dry, fever burning, dreaming of Ramelton’s green slopes, hearing the murmur of the Lennon River in his ears.

“One would pray for death,” she murmured, “unless—”

She stopped. She almost said, *unless one went there on purpose, with a purpose to fulfill.* But she cut herself off.

Durrance finished the sentence: “Unless there was a chance of escape. And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman.”

He regretted speaking so frankly about the prison. He added quickly, “Of course, what I’ve told you is just hearsay. We don’t really know. The prisoners may not suffer as badly as we think.” Then he changed the subject.

Ethne never mentioned it again.

But sometimes she wondered—how had Durrance known about her sudden departure from the drawing-room the night he told her of meeting Harry in Wadi Halfa? He never referred to it. She decided not to either. His manner had changed—no longer cautious, no longer tense. He seemed to have found a simple explanation. Maybe he thought she had merely fainted, or been startled. It eased her fears.

Still, she puzzled over why he had told her about the meeting at all. Why reveal that Harry had gone south? She found a strange answer—one that satisfied her: perhaps the news wasn’t really his to give. It was a message meant only for her. Durrance was merely the instrument. Some higher force had compelled him to speak.

For the rest of September, they kept up the act. Each morning, Durrance crossed the fields to visit Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair watched them laugh, talk, walk together—as if nothing had ever shaken their bond. And each day, her anger grew. She wanted to speak, to stop the lie. But she held her tongue.

It was a month of tension—for all three. Relief came when Durrance left for London to see his oculist. The visits increased in frequency and length. Even Ethne welcomed them. She could finally rest, shed the mask, and gather strength. There were moments when despair threatened to break through. *Could I keep this up after marriage? When we are always together?* she asked herself. But she pushed the thought away. To look ahead was to risk collapse.

After the third trip, Durrance said:
“Do you remember I once mentioned a famous oculist in Wiesbaden? It seems advisable that I go there.”

“You’re recommended to go?”

“Yes. And alone.”

Ethne looked up, sharp and quick. “You think I’d be dull in Wiesbaden?”

“No fear. I can find some relative to accompany me.”

“No,” he said firmly. “It’s for my own sake. I may have to enter a private home. It’s better to be quiet, unseen.”

“You’re sure?” she asked. “Because I’d hate to think you were doing this because you believed I’d be happier at Glenalla.”

“No,” he answered honestly. He needed space. He too was exhausted by the constant performance. But he couldn’t say it. Not yet. He knew how much Ethne valued her resolve—to protect both their futures. So he stayed silent.

“I’ll return next week,” he added. “Then I’ll know whether I’m going to Wiesbaden.”

He was glad he’d mentioned it—before Calder’s telegram arrived.

That afternoon, the telegraph came. Durrance took it to The Pool that evening and handed it to Ethne. Only four words:

He stepped back instinctively—this new sensitivity born of suffering and reflection. He folded the paper neatly, so that by the time Ethne read it, she was alone on the terrace.

She remembered his description of the prison. Her imagination ran wild. The stillness of a September evening settled over the fields. A mist rose from the creek, creeping across the lawn. Already, in the hot land where the Niles meet, the prison doors were shut.

“He will pay tenfold for his fault,” she whispered, furious at the injustice. “And the fault was not his—his father’s, mine too. Neither of us understood.”

She blamed herself for giving him the fourth feather. She leaned against the stone railing, eyes closed, wondering if Harry would survive the night. Would he live to see another dawn?

The cool stones under her hands felt like reproach.

“Something can now be done,” Durrance said, stepping onto the terrace. “He was my friend. I cannot leave him there. I’ll write to Calder tonight. No expense spared. He is my friend, Ethne. You’ll see. From Suakin or Assouan—something will be done.”

He placed the entire effort at his own feet. He wanted her to believe he was acting purely out of friendship. Not out of love for her, or any lingering connection to Harry.

Ethne turned sharply. “Major Castleton is dead?”

“Castleton?” Durrance blinked. “There was a Castleton in Feversham’s regiment. Is that him?”

“Yes. He is dead?”

“He was killed at Tamai.”

“You’re sure?”

“I was in the square with the Second Brigade. On the edge of the gulley. Osman Digna’s men burst from the ground. Castleton was within the square. I saw him fall.”

“I am glad,” Ethne said simply.

She meant it.

The first feather had returned with Captain Willoughby. Perhaps Colonel Trench would bring the second. Harry had succeeded once—under terrible danger. Now the danger was greater, the task harder. But she believed in him. He had planned carefully. He had resources. And she—she had faith.

Besides, she was relieved. The sender of the third feather could never be approached. And she hated him. That was the end of it.

Durrance stared at her. He was not a man of emotion. He was a soldier—quiet, disciplined, gentle but firm. Yet this cold declaration—gladness at the death of a man she had never met—struck him deeply.

“You disliked Major Castleton so much?” he asked.

“I never knew him.”

“Yet you’re glad he’s dead?”

“I am quite glad,” she insisted.

Another slip. Another clue.

Durrance remembered it. Later, in his gun-room at Guessens, he pondered it. It fitted into the puzzle he was slowly assembling.

Captain Willoughby’s visit had given him the key. A white feather—only one meaning: cowardice. But Durrance had never seen cowardice in Harry Feversham. The charge stunned him. Yet it had happened. Something occurred at the ball at Lennon House. After that, Harry vanished.

Suppose a white feather had been sent to Lennon House—and opened in Ethne’s presence? Or more than one?

She had returned from her talk with Willoughby clutching that feather as though it were priceless. Mrs. Adair had told him that.

So the cowardice had been atoned. That explained her renewed youth, her peace. She treasured the feather not as a symbol of shame, but of redemption.

But Harry had not returned. He remained hidden. Willoughby was not the only one who had accused him. Two others.

One, Durrance already knew. When he suggested Harry might be taken to Omdurman, Ethne had said instantly: *Colonel Trench is in Omdurman.*

She didn’t need explanation. She knew Harry had gone deliberately—to be captured. To reach Trench.

And she had spoken of the unreliable go-between. That helped Durrance. There was a duty on Feversham to aid Trench. Suppose Harry had planned to rescue him—and became the go-between himself?

Then a second feather must have been sent. Trench had sent it.

Now, Durrance connected Castleton to the others. Ethne’s relief at Castleton’s death—why? Because if Castleton had lived, Harry would have owed him the same obligation. The same debt.

So likely—another feather had come. Sent by Castleton.

Durrance pieced it together. It made sense. One man could have told him the truth—but refused. That man would still refuse.

But Durrance intended to make him talk. And to do so, he must arrive with the story complete—no questions, no demands.

“Yes,” he said aloud, “after my next visit to London, I think I’ll call on Lieutenant Sutch.”

Captain Willoughby was a well-known figure at his club—not for charm, but for endurance. He had a peculiar gift: the ability to hold an audience captive with stories so unremarkable they seemed to drain the very air of interest. His tales revolved around people no one in the room had ever heard of, delivered with such solemn conviction that listeners felt compelled to nod along, even as their minds drifted elsewhere. He didn’t listen—he only spoke. And he spoke on, undeterred by silence, indifference, or outright glares. A snub would leave him unruffled, his expression blankly pleasant, while inside he remained lost in a world of his own dullness.

At the supper table or in the smoking-room, he was like a steamroller rolling over conversation—crushing it flat under the weight of his endless monologue. There was no escaping him. Trite anecdotes were sandwiched between stale aphorisms from old books, each delivered with the theatrical surprise of a man who believed himself profoundly wise. If you stayed long enough, you’d lose the will to flee. You’d sit trapped in a web of tedium, your escape blocked by sheer inertia.

Only those who didn’t know him stayed long enough. The others, upon spotting him enter, would rise and vanish into the next room or down the stairs, leaving behind a sudden emptiness where once there had been chatter.

So it was that within half an hour of his arrival, Captain Willoughby found himself alone in a large corner of the club—the same corner that had been bustling moments before. He had made it a habit to select the liveliest group as his audience, not out of desire for attention, but because it gave him the illusion of being wanted.

He sat there one afternoon in early October, enjoying the quiet, when the waiter approached and handed him a card.

Willoughby took it eagerly. He welcomed company—his usual circle had scattered to urgent engagements, leaving him isolated. But as he read the name, his face fell.

"Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head thoughtfully.

Durrance had never visited him before. Why now? It felt suspicious. Had Durrance somehow learned of his trip to Kingsbridge?

"Does Colonel Durrance know I’m here?" he asked.

"Yes, sir," the waiter replied.

"Very well. Show him in."

Durrance’s visit must be about something—probably questions. And questions were dangerous. Willoughby had no intention of getting tangled again in the affairs of Miss Ethne Eustace. Feversham and Durrance could settle their own matters. He wasn’t opposed to diplomacy—but he wasn’t eager to use it here.

As Durrance entered, Willoughby studied him warily. But Durrance showed no signs of inquiry. Instead, he moved with calm assurance.

Willoughby rose and guided him toward the empty corner.

"Will you smoke?" he asked, then paused. "I beg your pardon."

"Oh, I’ll smoke," Durrance said. "It’s not true that a man can’t enjoy tobacco without seeing the smoke. If I let my cigar go out, I’d know it at once. But you’ll see—I won’t let it out."

He lit the cigar slowly, deliberately, and leaned back in his chair.

"I’m lucky to find you, Willough.” he said. “I’m only in town today. I come up from Devonshire every now and then to see my oculist. I’ve been anxious to meet you if I could. On my last visit, Mather told me you were away. You remember Mather? He was with us in Suakin."

"Of course," Willoughby replied warmly. He was glad to talk about Mather—perhaps this would distract Durrance from other things.

"We’re both curious," Durrance continued, "and you might clear something up. Have you ever met an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby repeated slowly. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No—a Kabbabish man."

"Abou Fatma?" he echoed, as though hearing the name for the first time. "No, I never came across him." Then he stopped.

Durrance sensed the pause was unnatural. Normally, Willoughby would have asked why he was inquiring. But he said nothing.

In truth, Willoughby was wondering how Durrance had even heard of Abou Fatma. The name had surfaced only once—on the veranda of the palace in Suakin, a year ago. He hadn’t spoken of it since.

But he had spoken the truth—he hadn’t met Abou Fatma. Though Feversham had mentioned him.

"That makes me more curious," Durrance said. "Mather and I were together during the last reconnaissance in '84. We found Abou Fatma hiding near the Sinkat fort. He told us about the Gordon letters—how he’d hidden them in Berber. Ah! You remember his name now."

"I was just getting my pipe out," Willoughby said, hoping to cover the delay. "But yes—I remember now, thanks to the letters."

"They were brought to you in Suakin, fifteen months ago," Durrance said. "Mather showed me the article in the *Evening Standard*. I’ve been wondering—did Abou Fatma return to Berber and recover them?"

"Since I never met him," Willoughby replied awkwardly, "it seems he wasn’t the one."

He regretted denying knowledge so quickly. Now he feared the next question.

But Durrance didn’t ask it. Instead, he said slowly, "I wondered—Abou Fatma wouldn’t likely return to Berber. After all, whoever retrieved the letters did so at great risk. And since Gordon was dead… what reason would there be?"

"Quite so," Willoughby said, relieved. "No obvious reason."

"For no obvious reason," Durrance corrected gently.

Willoughby turned, eyes narrowing. Was Durrance aware of his trip to Kingsbridge? Of his meeting with Ethne?

But Durrance only smoked, his head tilted back, eyes closed. He seemed satisfied. No further questions.

Yet in his mind, Durrance was piecing together the puzzle. He knew Harry Feversham had disappeared after a moment of disgrace—some act of cowardice that had earned him three white feathers. But Feversham had vanished quietly, without explanation. And now, five years later, he was in the Sudan—broken, destitute.

Durrance suspected the redemption came through the Gordon letters. That someone had retrieved them from Berber—and that Feversham had done it. Not for glory, but for atonement.

And the only way that story made sense was if Feversham had heard of Abou Fatma’s tale—heard it in Suakin, perhaps, from Willoughby himself.

Willoughby’s silence confirmed it. His hesitation, his careful speech—Durrance recognized the signs. Just like Ethne used to speak—guarded, wary, full of unspoken meaning.

And then came the trap: "For no obvious reason," Durrance had said. A tiny flicker in Willoughby’s eyes—then a sharp turn of the head. Caught.

But Durrance didn’t press. He had no wish to provoke Willoughby into warning Ethne. So he changed tack.

"I wanted to tell you why I sought you out," he said. "It’s about Harry Feversham."

Willoughby stiffened.

"Something happened to him," Durrance continued. "More than five years ago. He did something—or failed to do something. The secret has been kept tight. He dropped out of sight. Disappeared."

"You're going back to the Sudan?" Durrance asked.

"Yes—in a week."

"Then I need you to look for him," Durrance said, leaning forward. "He’s there. At Wadi Halfa this spring, I ran into him. He’d fallen hard. I’ll tell you what happened."

He recounted their meeting outside Tewfikieh café—Feversham thin, worn, almost unrecognizable.

"It’s strange," Durrance said. "A man you knew well—gone in a second. Vanished like smoke. I want you to help him, Willoughby. If you find him, pull him back. Let me know. Harry Feversham was a friend of mine—one of the few real ones."

"All right," Willoughby said cheerfully. "I’ll keep an eye out. I remember he was a good friend of yours."

He reached for the matches.

Durrance watched as the phosphorus scraped and the match flared. Willoughby lit his pipe—a seasoned briar that bubbled as he sucked at it.

"Yes," Durrance said, "a great friend. You and I dined with him in his flat above St. James’s Park—just before we left England."

At that, the pipe stopped bubbling.

Silence.

Then—*swear!*—and a sharp stamp on the carpet.

Durrance smiled inwardly. He saw the scene clearly now: Willoughby lighting his pipe, frozen mid-action by a careless remark, staring at Durrance’s face, forgetting the match until it burned his fingers.

And then the dinner party came back to him.

"Feversham had just returned from Dublin," Durrance said. "He asked us to dinner to tell us he was engaged to Miss Eustace. That was almost the last time we saw him."

He took a slow draw of his cigar.

"And there was a third man present."

"Was there?" Willoughby asked. "It’s so long ago."

"Trench."

"To be sure—Trench was there. Poor old Trench—we may never dine together again."

His voice was light, casual. He struck another match and lit his pipe.

As he did, Durrance placed his cigar on the edge of the table.

"And we shall never dine with Castleton again," he said slowly.

"Castleton wasn't there!" Willoughby snapped—too fast.

"Not even expected," Durrance corrected. "He was dining elsewhere. He sent the telegram, you remember."

"Ah, yes," Durrance said. "A telegram came."

Now Durrance was certain. That dinner party held the key.

Willoughby, Trench, Castleton—three men whose actions had led to Feversham’s downfall.

He remembered standing by the window, watching the park, hearing the tattoo from Wellington Barracks—then the telegram arriving.

The pieces clicked.

Castleton had dined with a high official at the War Office that night. And yet—had sent a telegram.

To whom? To Feversham.

Feversham had read it silently, crumpled it, thrown it in the fire.

No word spoken.

But now Willoughby confirmed it: Castleton had sent it.

And the news? That Feversham’s regiment was ordered to active service.

That was it.

The moment Feversham received that message—engaged to Ethne, proud, strong—his courage broke. He resigned. He fled.

Not because he was weak—but because he was human.

And now, five years later, he was paying for it.

Durrance stood.

"Well," he said, "I can count on you, Willoughby. If you find him, help him. Draw on me for money."

"I’ll do my best," Willoughby said.

"You’re going?" Durrance asked.

"I could have won a bet this afternoon."

"How?"

"You said you never let your cigars go out. This one’s stone cold."

"I forgot," Durrance said. "I was thinking of Feversham."

He stepped out, hailed a cab, and drove away.

Willoughby exhaled. Relief washed over him. He had managed it—kept the secret. He’d outmaneuvered Durrance.

Or so he thought.

Durrance, however, had seen everything.

The final piece had fallen into place.

The telegram had come from Castleton. Castleton had been with a War Office official. The news was service—immediate, unavoidable.

Feversham had known. And panicked.

Durrance understood now. The cowardice wasn’t born of fear—it was born of weakness in a moment of triumph.

But the redemption? That was real.

Harry Feversham was atoning.

With every step, every hardship, every silent day in the Sudan—he was making amends.

Durrance chuckled softly.

"I’ll astonish old Sutch," he murmured.

That evening, he boarded the night mail. By midday the next day, he was home in Devonshire—quiet, content, and finally knowing the truth.

Inside the drawing-room at The Pool, Durrance said farewell to Ethne. He had arranged their parting with precision—no lingering, no drawn-out goodbyes. Outside, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage strapped securely to the roof, his servant standing by the door. Ethne stepped out onto the terrace beside him, where Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone staircase.

Durrance reached for her hand, but she turned instead to Ethne and said quietly, “I need a moment with Colonel Durrance before he leaves.”

“Of course,” Ethne replied. Then, turning to him, she added, “Then we’ll say our goodbyes here. You’ll write from Wiesbaden? Soon, please?”

“The moment I arrive,” he promised.

He descended the steps with Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne alone on the terrace. The final act of pretense was over. Months of tension, silence, and careful observation had ended. Both were relieved—free now to breathe without constraint. Durrance walked briskly beside her, his step light with release. But Mrs. Adair lagged, her voice heavy when she finally spoke.

“So you’re going,” she said. “In two days you’ll be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne will be at Glenalla. We’ll all be scattered. It will be lonely here.”

She had achieved what she set out to do—separating Ethne and Durrance, at least for a time. She would no longer have to endure their presence, their voices, their quiet intimacy. Yet now that it was done, she felt nothing but emptiness. Her triumph tasted bitter.

“The house will seem very empty once you’re all gone,” she murmured, stepping down into the garden beside him.

“We’ll return,” Durrance said gently, trying to reassure her.

Mrs. Adair looked around. The flowers were gone, the sunlight faded. Clouds covered the sky, the grass beneath their feet dull green, the stream running gray between the trees. Leaves, russet and yellow, littered the lawns like fallen embers.

“How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?” she asked.

“I can’t say for sure. As long as it’s necessary.”

“That tells me nothing at all,” she replied sharply. “I suppose it was meant not to tell me anything.”

He didn’t answer. The silence stung her. She knew nothing of his plans—not whether he intended to honor his engagement to Ethne or break it entirely. Curiosity gnawed at her. It might be years before she saw him again, and those years would be filled with uncertainty.

“You distrust me?” she challenged, her voice rising with anger.

Durrance answered softly, “Have I no reason to distrust you? Why did you tell me about Captain Willoughby’s arrival? Why did you interfere?”

“I thought you should know,” she said.

“But Ethne wanted it kept secret. I’m glad I learned it—very glad. Still, you told me. And you were her friend.”

“Yours too, I hope,” she countered, then broke into an exclamation: “How could I stay silent? Don’t you understand?”

“No,” he said simply.

He might have understood—but he hadn’t given much thought to her. And she knew it. That knowledge festered within her. His single word—"No"—cut deeper than any accusation. It made her reckless.

“I spoke brutally,” she said. “But I told you the truth, as brutally as I could. Doesn’t that help you see?”

Again, he said only, “No.”

That one syllable drove her beyond caution. Suddenly, she was speaking without control—words spilling out, raw and unfiltered. She stood apart from herself, watching the madness unfold, yet unable to stop.

“I told you the truth brutally on purpose,” she confessed. “I was so hurt because you wouldn’t see what was plain—had you only the mind to see. I wanted to wound you. I am a bad woman, I suppose. There you were, talking together in the dark, and I alone on the terrace. It was the same today. You and Ethne in the room—I outside. I wonder if it will always be this way. But you won’t say it—you won’t say it!”

She clapped her hands together in despair. Durrance said nothing. He walked silently toward the stile, quickening his pace slightly, forcing her to hurry to keep up.

Her words followed him. “I don’t think I’d have minded so much,” she continued, “if Ethne had truly cared for you. But she never did—only as a friend. Just a friend. And what is friendship worth?” she sneered.

“Something,” Durrance said quietly.

“She shrinks from you!” she cried. “Why? Because you’re blind. She fears what your life might mean—how it might limit hers.” Her voice dropped to a whisper, edged with scorn. “But I—*I* am glad. When the news came from Wadi Halfa that you were blind, I was glad. When I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad. Ever since, I’ve been glad—*quite* glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the start, she shrank, afraid of how her life would be chained and burdened.”

She repeated, over and over, “I am not afraid. I am not afraid. I am not afraid.”

To Durrance, this confession was more horrifying than anything he’d ever imagined—the woman who had been Ethne’s friend, unraveling before him in a storm of jealousy and pain.

“Ethne wrote to you from Wadi Halfa out of pity,” Mrs. Adair went on. “That was all. She wrote from pity, then feared what she’d done. And fearing, she lacked the courage to admit it. You wouldn’t have blamed her, if she’d been honest. You would have remained her friend. But she couldn’t face it.”

Durrance knew another explanation—one true and clear. But tomorrow he would be gone from the Salcombe estuary, Ethne en route to Ireland. Arguing against her accusations was pointless. Besides, they were nearing the stile—the boundary between The Pool’s garden and the open fields. Once across, he would be free.

He said only, “You are not just to Ethne.”

At those words, the storm in Mrs. Adair subsided. She saw the futility of her words, her boasts, her attacks. They could change nothing. Durrance was always with Ethne, never with her. She felt the shame of her outburst, and began to justify herself.

“I am a bad woman, I suppose,” she whispered. “But I haven’t had the happiest of lives. Perhaps there’s something to be said for me.”

It sounded weak, even to her. But they had reached the stile, and Durrance turned to face her. His sternness softened. He remembered her from the past—when he could still see—graceful, composed, dignified in speech and bearing. This woman seemed almost a stranger. The contrast struck him deeply, making him willing to listen.

“Will you tell me?” he asked gently.

“I was married almost straight from school,” she began. “A child, really. I knew nothing. I married a man I barely knew—my mother’s choice. She thought she was doing what was best. She secured me a position, comfort, safety from poverty. I accepted blindly. I couldn’t refuse—she was a strong woman, and I was trained to obey. I married dutifully, as she wished. It’s a common story, perhaps, but its repetition doesn’t make it easier to bear.”

“But Mr. Adair?” Durrance asked.

“He was kind,” she said. “And he cared for me. That was the only thread holding me—knowing he loved me. At first, I was content. I had houses in town and here. But it was dull.” She spread her arms wide. “Oh, how dull it was! Do you know those narrow back streets in industrial towns? Rows of identical houses, same windows, same doors, same steps. Smoke above, everything blackened. Even the plants in the windows were dirty. Any preacher promising color to their gray lives could draw crowds. When I thought of my own life, that street came to mind. There are women who find joy in managing large homes, London seasons, endless visits. I was not one of them. Dull—so terribly dull. And you, with your thousand tasks, cannot imagine how crushing it was. But it wasn’t all.”

She hesitated, but could not stop. “I married knowing nothing of what mattered. At first, I believed this was the fate of every woman. But soon, doubts crept in. I began to learn that life offered more—more than mere dullness. Not for me, perhaps, but for others. I’d pass a couple riding together, catch a glance of the woman’s face—and see joy. Or talk briefly with someone alone, hear the warmth in her voice, and know instantly there was *more*. Only the chance of that more—my mother denied me.”

All hardness had left Durrance’s face now. Mrs. Adair spoke plainly, without appeal or plea. No trace of earlier rage remained. She was simply telling her truth.

“And then you came,” she continued. “I met you, and met you again. You left for duty, returned. And I learned—didn’t just know, but *knew*—what that ‘more’ was. But it was still denied to me. Still. Yet, despite that, I felt happier. I thought I could be content to watch your progress, take pride in your path. But then—Ethne came. And you turned to her. At once. Oh, at once! If you had only been slower… In no time, I was sorrowful that you had entered my life.”

“I never knew this,” Durrance said. “I never suspected. I’m sorry.”

“I took care you wouldn’t,” she replied. “But I tried to keep you. With all my strength, I worked to hold you. No matchmaker ever labored harder to unite a couple than I did to bring Ethne and Feversham together. And I succeeded.”

The revelation struck Durrance like a blow. He leaned back against the stile, nearly laughing. So this was the root of it all—the beginning of a chain that led to Feversham’s downfall, Ethne’s suffering, the months of deception. How small the seeds of such great consequences!

“I succeeded,” she continued. “You told me so one morning in the Row. I was so happy. You didn’t notice, I’m sure. Then, in the next breath, you told me you were going to the Soudan. You were away three years. They weren’t happy years for me. You returned. My husband was dead. Ethne was free. She refused you. Then you became blind, and she claimed you. You can see the ups and downs of my life. But these last months—these have been the worst.”

“I’m very sorry,” Durrance said.

She was right—he thought. There *was* something to be said for her. The world had treated her unfairly. He could feel her pain, because he was enduring something similar himself. Now he understood why she had revealed Ethne’s secret that night. He could not be harsh with her.

“I’m very sorry, Mrs. Adair,” he repeated, lamely. He had no other words. He held out his hand.

“Good-bye,” she said.

He climbed over the stile and walked across the fields toward his home.

Mrs. Adair stood at the stile long after he was gone. She had spoken her truth—and harmed no one but herself and the man she secretly cared for. She saw that clearly. And she foresaw that if Durrance did not marry Ethne, he would return to Guessens. Then their meeting would be fraught with awkwardness, their friendship forever shadowed by what had passed on this afternoon.

She had silenced her own longing, but not her regret. The price of honesty was isolation. And she had paid it fully.

The land stretched beneath Calder like a wound—dry, cracked, and unyielding. As the steamer chugged down the Nile from Wadi Halfa toward Assouan, he leaned against the railing of the upper deck, watching the world unfold in shades of dust and sun. The river was narrow here, hemmed in by barren rock and sand, shaped into crude pyramids by time and wind. Only a thin ribbon of green clung to the banks—a fragile defiance against the heat and emptiness. It was a harsh place, ruled by silence and indifference.

Below, lashed to the side of the steamer, a barge bobbed gently. On its lower deck, amid a cluster of native passengers, stood an angareb—a simple bed of woven matting over four legs. Upon it lay a figure, wrapped tightly in a black veil, motionless as stone. Two Arab men sat near the stern, laughing and talking, their eyes never lingering on the still form. To them, it might have been death or merely deep sleep—no matter. They had no care for it.

Calder watched. He could not tell if the body beneath the veil was man or woman. The fabric clung close around the face, tracing the hollows of the eyes, the curve of the nose, the line of the mouth. But nothing revealed gender. Not a breath stirred the cloth. Not a limb twitched. Yet the sun crept forward, relentless, crawling up the dark shape until it blazed upon the face. Still, there was no movement. No flicker. No sign of life.

He shouted across the gap. “Move that bed into the shade! Now!”

The two men glanced up, grumbled, then obeyed. One of them stepped forward, dragging the angareb into the shadow cast by the upper deck.

“Is it a man or a woman?” Calder asked.

“A man,” one replied. “We’re taking him to the hospital in Assouan. But we don’t think he’ll make it. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago.”

“You give him nothing to eat or drink?”

“He’s too ill.”

It was a familiar story—the kind that echoed through this land where fate was written in the stars and suffering seen as inevitable. The man likely began with only a bruise, something easily healed. But no one touched him. No water. No food. No cleansing. The wound festered. Gangrene set in. And only when the local Mudir discovered him did he finally get moved—on this steamer, to die slowly under the sun.

Yet Calder could not let it go. There was something about the stillness—so complete, so final—that gripped him. When a strong wind sprang up at dusk, blowing upstream and rattling the rigging, he felt a strange relief. At least the air would cool the man’s skin. Maybe, just maybe, it would ease his pain.

That evening, at dinner, a man with a German accent spoke beside him. Calder interrupted abruptly.

“You’re not a doctor, by any chance?”

“No,” the man said, smiling behind his spectacles. “I’m a medical student from Bonn. I came from Cairo to see the Second Cataract—but they wouldn’t let me past Wadi Halfa.”

Calder didn’t hesitate. “Then I’ll borrow your holiday. I need your help.”

“For yourself? With pleasure,” the student replied. “Though I’d never have guessed you were ill.”

“I’m not. It’s the man on the angareb.”

“The one on the barge?”

“Yes. If you’re willing. I warn you—he’s been hurt for three weeks. These people don’t touch him once he’s injured. You won’t like what you see. This isn’t a place for neglected wounds.”

The student shrugged. “All experience is good.” And together they rose and stepped onto the upper deck.

The wind had strengthened, whipping waves against the hull. The steamer rocked. Spray flew like snow, striking the deck with sharp cracks. A single lantern hung from the center of the upper deck, casting weak light in a small circle. Beyond it, darkness swallowed everything—except at the bow, where the breaking water flared white in the night.

“The man’s not here,” the student said, peering down.

“Probably moved,” Calder replied. “No one’s near the front.”

He stared into the blackness, silent for a moment. Then: “I think he’s there.”

They hurried down to the lower deck. Now they could see clearly. The angareb stood exactly where it had been placed earlier—washed by the spray, drenched by the sea. And on it, the figure remained untouched, unmoving, as if carved from stone. Cold water splashed across the veil, but still no breath stirred.

“I knew it,” Calder murmured.

He fetched a lantern and climbed across to the barge, followed by the student. He called the two Arabs.

“Move the bed away from the bows,” he ordered. When they obeyed, he added, “Now uncover him. My friend wants to examine the wound.”

They hesitated. One of them raised his chin defiantly. “There are doctors in Assouan. That’s where we’re taking him.”

Calder lifted the lantern. With his own hand, he pulled back the veil.

“Now,” he said.

The student knelt, examining the thigh. It was a gruesome sight. The wound oozed, crawling with infection. Flies hovered. The smell was thick in the air.

“I can do nothing,” the student said, pulling the cloth back over the wound. “Not here. Not without clean water, dressings, proper care. Perhaps in a hospital—there might be hope. But here? Nothing. I couldn’t even begin.”

“Do they understand English?” Calder asked.

“No.”

“Then listen. This wasn’t caused by a fall from a palm tree. That’s a lie. This wound was made by a spear—or something like it.”

“Are you sure?”

“Absolutely.”

Calder leaned close to the man’s face. Though he didn’t move, his eyes followed Calder’s words. The man was conscious.

“You understand English?” Calder asked.

The lips tried to speak. Failed. But the eyes—wide, desperate—answered. Yes.

“Where are you from?” Calder asked.

The lips trembled. No sound. But the eyes burned with urgency.

Calder dropped to his knee, holding the lantern close. He spoke slowly, naming towns.

“Dongola?”

No response.

“Metemneh? Berber? Omdurman?”

At the last name, the man blinked once—and closed his eyes.

Calder pressed on. “You were wounded there?”

No.

“At Berber?”

Yes.

“You were in prison at Omdurman and escaped?”

No.

“But you were wounded.”

The man nodded faintly.

Calder leaned closer. “You helped someone escape?”

Yes.

“Who? Trench?”

No.

He named others—white captives known to be held in Omdurman. Each time, the eyes denied.

“Feversham?” Calder whispered.

The eyes opened wide. A slow, clear nod.

It was all he could get. The man could not speak. Could not say his name. His companions refused to talk. They claimed they were only doing a favor for relatives in Korosko who had asked them to carry the stranger. They knew nothing more.

But Calder now knew enough.

Harry Feversham was alive. He had been rescued. Or at least, he had tried.

And yet—this man, Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe, had been left to die on a riverboat, wounded and helpless. His presence meant that Feversham’s plan—his careful, secret effort to free Colonel Trench—had failed.

Calder stood beside the angareb, watching the broken man breathe through the veil. He was eager to speak, to tell his tale. But weakness had sealed his lips. All Calder could do was ensure he reached the hospital in Assouan.

When the doctors examined him, they shook their heads. Recovery was possible—just barely. But slow. Painful. And uncertain.

Calder continued his journey to Cairo, then Europe. An opportunity had slipped through his fingers. The rescue had failed. The man who might have told the truth was dying in silence. And somewhere out there, Harry Feversham was still trapped—waiting, perhaps, for news that would never come.

At the moment when Calder, frustrated in his search for news of Feversham from the only man who could provide it, stepped into a train carriage at Assouan, Lieutenant Sutch was driving along a sun-bleached road through Hampshire, crossing a moorland carpeted with heather and gorse. Like many who live in solitude, Sutch had grown accustomed to speaking aloud to himself, and as he urged his pony forward with reluctance, he murmured again and again: “I knew trouble would come. From the very beginning, I foresaw it.”

The road dipped sharply into a valley, revealing a narrow track descending between dense pine forests toward a small railway station. The gleaming rails caught the afternoon light, and the telegraph poles stretched straight into the distance, vanishing like sentinels in the haze. The sight unsettled Sutch. He pulled back on the reins and sat still, frowning at the red-tiled roof of the station.

“I promised Harry I’d say nothing,” he muttered, finding comfort in the repetition. “I swore it faithfully in the Criterion grill-room.”

A distant engine whistle pierced the quiet, sharp and clear. It broke through his dark thoughts. A plume of white smoke appeared in the distance, curling like a ribbon across the sky.

“I wonder what brings him,” Sutch said, uncertain. Then, gathering courage, “Well, there’s no use avoiding it.” He flicked the pony and drove down the hill. As he reached the platform, the train slowed to a stop. Only two passengers emerged—Durrance and his servant—and they stepped immediately onto the road. Sutch called out, and Durrance approached the trap.

“You got my telegram in time?” Durrance asked.

“Luckily, I was home.”

“I’ve brought a bag. May I stay with you for one night?”

“Of course,” Sutch replied, though his voice betrayed hesitation. Durrance heard it clearly, but he expected no warmth. He had deliberately sent the message late, knowing that if he’d given an address, he might have been refused. And indeed, his suspicion was correct. Though the telegram had merely announced his arrival, its timing warned Sutch that something serious had happened—something troubling between Ethne Eustace and Durrance. Durrance had come, no doubt, to ask about Harry Feversham—the very questions Sutch had vowed never to answer. But then Sutch saw Durrance testing the step of the trap with his foot, and realized: the man was blind.

He reached out, grasped Durrance’s arm, and helped him climb in. In that instant, a wave of relief washed over him. It wouldn’t be hard to deceive a blind man. Ethne herself had thought the same thing, felt the same ease. Sutch even found room for pity.

“I’m truly sorry to hear about your misfortune,” he said as they moved up the hill. “I know how it feels—to be suddenly halted just when things are going well. My own wound is nothing compared to your blindness. I won’t talk about patience or compensation. That’s what people say who haven’t suffered. We know better. For a young man, active and rising in a career that demands motion—when that career is cut short through no fault of his own—there are no compensations.”

“Through no fault of his,” Durrance echoed slowly. “I agree. Only the man whose fall comes from his own weakness finds comfort afterward.”

Sutch glanced at him sharply. Was this a reference to Harry Feversham? Did Durrance know more than he let on?

“What kind of compensation?” Sutch asked, uneasy.

“The chance to know oneself—above all else,” Durrance said. “He is stopped. His path ends. Perhaps disgraced. But the shock itself is his opportunity. Don’t you see? It’s the moment he finally sees who he really is. Before, life was illusion. He believed he was one man, but he wasn’t. Now, at last, he knows. Once he knows, he can begin to mend. Yes, there are compensations. You and I both know such a case.”

Now Sutch was certain. Durrance was speaking of Feversham. He didn’t know everything—but enough to be dangerous. And what he did know, though incomplete, wronged Harry. That was why Sutch didn’t pretend ignorance. Years had done nothing to dim his deep affection for Harry. He cared for him with a tenderness akin to maternal love, and he could not bear any insult to his memory.

“That case isn’t quite right,” Sutch argued. “You’re thinking of Harry Feversham.”

“Who believed himself a coward, and wasn’t. He made the mistake that ended his career. He discovered the truth. He set out to redeem himself. Isn’t that exactly the case?”

“Yes,” Sutch admitted. “But there’s another way to see it—one I know is wrong, yet I thought you might believe it: that Harry thought himself brave, only to find he was a coward. But how did you learn all this? No one but me knew the full story.”

“I’m engaged to Miss Eustace,” Durrance said simply.

“She didn’t know everything. She knew of the disgrace, but not of the resolve to fix it.”

“She knows now,” Durrance replied, and added, with quiet force, “And you’re glad of that—very glad.”

Sutch hadn’t shown it, but his face must have revealed it. Durrance couldn’t see, but he sensed it.

“It’s true,” Sutch said firmly. “I am glad. I understand why you’d think it better if she didn’t know. But I can’t help it. I am glad.”

Durrance laughed softly. “I like you more for being glad.”

“But how did she learn?” Sutch pressed. “Who told her? I didn’t. There’s no one else.”

“You’re mistaken. Captain Willoughby came to Devonshire six weeks ago. He brought a white feather—and gave it to Miss Eustace as proof he withdrew his accusation against Harry.”

Sutch pulled the pony to a halt in the middle of the road. Joy surged through him, so strong he forgot Durrance beside him. He sat silent, radiant, filled with a happiness he hadn’t known since youth. At sixty-five, his blood ran slow, his joys were muted—but now, a flood of pure joy lifted his heart. Five years earlier, standing on Dover’s pier, he had watched a mail packet vanish into rain and darkness, praying he might live long enough to witness this moment. And now it had come.

His guilt—burdened since the night Harry had touched his arm outside the War Office, confessing his despair—was lifted. Harry was Muriel Feversham’s son, and Sutch, by right, should have guided him, protected him, raised him after her death. He had failed. He had carried that failure like a weight, imagining her ghostly eyes watching him, whispering in dreams: *Since I died, since I could no longer help, surely you could have helped—just for my sake.* Now, thanks to Harry’s own courage, that guilt was gone.

“I knew,” Sutch breathed. “I knew he wouldn’t fail. Oh, I’m so glad you came today, Colonel Durrance. Part of it was my fault—Harry’s charge of cowardice. I could have spoken. There was a night in Crimea at Broad Place… a word might have mattered. But I stayed silent. I’ve blamed myself ever since. Thank you for this news. Do you know the details? Was Willoughby in danger, and did Harry save him?”

“No,” Durrance said. “Not exactly.”

“Tell me! Tell me!”

Sutch leaned forward, afraid to miss a word. Durrance recounted the tale of the Gordon letters, their recovery by Feversham. It was too brief for Sutch’s longing.

“Oh, I’m so glad you came!” he exclaimed.

“You understand,” Durrance said, “that I didn’t come to repeat the questions I asked in the club courtyard. On the contrary—I’ve come to give you news.”

Sutch urged the pony forward. He had said nothing to betray his fear that Durrance had come seeking answers. Yet he was startled by the accuracy of Durrance’s guess. But the joy of the news overwhelmed his curiosity.

“So Miss Eustace told you the story? Showed you the feather?”

“No,” Durrance replied. “She said nothing. Never showed me the feather. She forbade Willoughby to speak of it. She sent him away before I knew he’d arrived. You’re disappointed?”

Sutch was. He was jealous. He wanted Harry to remain first in Ethne’s heart. She had loved him—had risked everything for him. Sutch wished she would remember him as he remembered her. So he was hurt that she hadn’t rushed to Durrance with the news, broken off the engagement.

“How did you learn then?” Sutch asked.

“Someone else told me. I heard Willoughby had come, that he brought a white feather, and that Ethne took it. That gave me a clue. I waited for him in London. He’s not clever—he tried to obey her silence, but I found out what I needed. The rest, I pieced together. Ethne sometimes slipped. You’re surprised I managed it?”

Sutch jumped in his seat. “I said nothing to suggest surprise.”

“That’s true,” Durrance said, smiling. “But you are. You don’t know that it’s only since I’ve been blind that I’ve begun to see. Let me prove it. This is the first time I’ve come here. Yet I know you’ve driven me up a hill between pines, and now across open heather.”

Sutch turned sharply. “The hill, yes. But the pines?”

“The air was thick. Trees nearby. I guessed pines.”

“And the heather?”

“The wind blows freely. There’s a dry, stiff rustle. I’ve never heard that sound except over heather.”

They returned to Harry. Durrance spoke of his disappearance, but not of the fourth feather—Ethne’s own. Yet he knew every detail of the three sent to Ramelton.

“I knew the men who sent them,” Durrance said. “Trench, Castleton, Willoughby. I met them daily in Suakin—ordinary officers. One shrewd, one dull, one foolish. They went about their duties quietly. It seems strange now. They should have looked different—marked as messengers of fate. But they didn’t. Just plain soldiers. Doesn’t it seem odd? Men who could destroy lives, break hearts, bring years of suffering—without saying a word. And we never noticed them until later, when consequences rose up and struck us down.”

“Yes,” Sutch said. “That thought has crossed my mind.” He wondered again why Durrance had come. But Durrance remained silent.

They reached Sutch’s house—a solitary dwelling by the roadside, overlooking rolling downs. Sutch showed him the stable, the horses, the garden. Durrance listened intently, asking questions about flowers, soil, seasons. Not all pretense. Both men shared a common sorrow—the sudden end of promising lives. One old, one young, but both facing long, quiet years ahead. Durrance wanted to know how Sutch had endured.

“You don’t live near the sea,” Durrance said, standing at the door after the tour.

“No,” Sutch replied. “I dare not.”

Durrance nodded. “I understand. You love it too much. You’d see your loss every day.”

Inside, they dined and drank wine. Still Durrance said nothing of his purpose. Finally, Sutch broke the silence.

“Harry Feversham must return to England. He’s done enough to restore his honor.”

It might be awkward for Durrance, and Sutch blurted it out awkwardly. But Durrance answered at once.

“I was waiting for you to say that. I wanted you to realize it without my prompting. That’s why I came.”

Sutch felt immense relief. He’d expected resistance, at best reluctant agreement. Overwhelmed, he continued.

“His return won’t really affect you or your wife. Since Miss Eustace has forgotten him…”

Durrance shook his head.

“She hasn’t forgotten him.”

“But she said nothing. Even after Willoughby brought the feather. She forbade him to tell you.”

“She is loyal,” Durrance said. “She pledged herself to me. Nothing—not happiness, not memories of Harry—would make her break that vow. I know her. But I also know she only promised because of pity—for my blindness. She hasn’t forgotten Harry.”

Sutch leaned back, smiling. He could have laughed. He didn’t doubt Durrance. He swelled with pride. Harry, despite disgrace and absence, still held Ethne’s heart. Sutch imagined her virtues—her nobility, her strength. The greater she was, the prouder he felt that Harry had earned her love. He reveled in it. It wasn’t surprising, really—anyone who knew Harry would understand. Durrance would have to step aside. But then, he shouldn’t have crossed Harry’s path at all.

“You’re glad,” Durrance said quietly. “Glad she hasn’t forgotten him. It’s hard on me, perhaps. I have little left. If you’d told me two years ago, when I asked that summer evening in the club courtyard…”

Sutch felt a pang of guilt. Durrance’s gentle tone, the weariness in his voice, reminded him of his own cruelty. He was right—had Sutch spoken then, Durrance would have been spared pain.

“I couldn’t!” Sutch insisted. “I promised Harry, solemnly, that I’d tell no one until he returned. I was tempted—but I gave my word. Even if he died, I could only tell his father—and not all the truth.”

He rose and walked to the window. “It’s hot in here. Mind if I open it?” Without waiting, he raised the sash. He stood there, silent, torn. Durrance didn’t know of the fourth feather—the one Ethne had broken from her fan. He hadn’t heard Harry’s words in the Criterion. But some of those words—spoken in confidence—should now be heard. Compassion urged Sutch to speak. Love demanded silence. He knew Harry would want them told. He knew silence had already caused harm.

In the end, love yielded to duty. He returned to the table.

“There’s something you should know,” he said. “When Harry left to redeem his honor, he had no hope—no wish—that Miss Eustace would wait for him. She was the spur, but she didn’t know it. He didn’t want her to know. He had no claim on her. Not even a hope that she might become his friend—in this life, at least. When he left Ramelton, he parted from her—forever, in his mind. You should know that. Miss Eustace, you say, won’t break her promise. Well, I repeat what I said that night at the club: there will be no disloyalty if you marry her.”

It was hard to say. But he was relieved when it was done. Whatever Durrance said, the truth had to be spoken. He waited, tense.

“It’s kind of you to tell me,” Durrance said, smiling warmly. “I can guess what it cost you. But you’ve done Harry no harm. Because, as I said, Ethne hasn’t forgotten him. And I have my own view. Marriage between a blind man like me and any woman—especially Ethne—can only be fair if there’s more than friendship on both sides. Harry must return to England. He must return to Ethne. You must go to Egypt and bring him back.”

Sutch’s suspense ended. He had followed his conscience—and honored Harry.

“I’ll leave tomorrow,” he said. “Is Harry still in the Sudan?”

“Of course.”

“Why ‘of course’?”

“Willoughby withdrew his accusation. Castleton is dead—killed at Tamai. Trench is a prisoner in Omdurman.”

“So is Harry Feversham.”

Sutch stared. For a moment, he didn’t grasp it. Then understanding hit—then disbelief. But the truth was undeniable. He sank into his chair, stunned. Silence fell, heavy and long.

“What am I to do?” he asked at last.

“I’ve thought it through,” Durrance said. “Go to Suakin. I’ll give you a letter to Willoughby—he’s Deputy-Governor—and another to a Greek merchant I know. You can draw on him for whatever money you need.”

“That’s generous, Durrance,” Sutch said, forgetting the man was blind. He reached across the table, hand outstretched. “I wouldn’t take a penny if I could help it. But I’m poor. Honestly, it’s very kind.”

Lieutenant Sutch, though he retired late, rose with the dawn. He stirred the entire household into motion, packing and unpacking his belongings with frantic energy. The house, which had known thirty years of quiet routine under his roof, now echoed with chaos. Servants darted through hallways, breathless in their search for long-forgotten garments from his old travel kit, but no matter how swiftly they moved, Sutch remained unsatisfied. His agitation was unmistakable—childlike, almost feverish.

It was no wonder. For three decades, he had lived on half-pay, a man adrift from purpose. Now, miraculously, fate had thrust something vital into his hands—something demanding courage, cunning, and resolve. Lieutenant Sutch was to be employed again. And the thought of delay sent tremors through him. He dreaded every moment before departure, fearing some unseen obstacle might snatch this chance away and return him to the silence of inactivity.

"I’ll be ready this afternoon," he declared briskly to Durrance over breakfast. "I’ll catch the night mail to the Continent. We could go up to London together—you’re heading that way to Wiesbaden."

"No," Durrance replied. "I have one more stop to make in England. It didn’t occur to me until last night—your words put it in my mind."

"Ah?" Sutch raised an eyebrow. "And who is it you intend to visit?"

"General Feversham," said Durrance.

Sutch set down his cutlery and stared. "Why on earth would you want to see him?"

"I want to tell him how Harry has redeemed himself—how he’s still doing so. You told me last night you were bound by a promise not to reveal anything about his son’s intentions or success until Harry returned. But I am not bound by such a vow. I believe that pledge is unfair to the general. Nothing could wound him more deeply than knowing his son was a coward. Even if Harry had stolen or killed, the old man would have preferred it. But this—he’d rather believe his son was a thief than a coward. So I’m going to Broad Place to tell him plainly: Harry never was a coward."

Sutch shook his head slowly. "He won’t understand. He’ll thank you, of course. He’ll be glad the family name is cleared. But he’ll never grasp why Harry fell so low in the first place. In truth, he’ll only care because the honour is restored."

"Not so," Durrance countered. "I think the old man loves his son, even if he’d never admit it. I’ve always liked General Feversham."

Sutch had seen little of the general in recent years. He bore a grudge—justified, in his view—for the role Feversham played in Harry’s downfall. Had the general truly understood his son, the white feathers would never have reached Ramelton. Sutch imagined the old man seated stiffly on his terrace at Broad Place, unaware of his own guilt, perhaps even playing the martyr—blaming his son for shaming the lineage immortalized in portraits that lined the hall like silent judges.

Sutch knew he could never speak patiently with Feversham. He doubted any argument would sway the man’s rigid beliefs. And he hadn’t given thought to whether Durrance should deliver this news at all.

"You’re very considerate," Sutch said.

"Only out of self-preservation," Durrance replied. "Selfishness is the natural flaw of the blind. I know that, so I guard against it."

That morning, Durrance traveled by rail from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place in the golden light of late afternoon.

General Feversham was nearing eighty. Though his posture remained as upright as ever—just as it had been when he once introduced young Harry to his Crimean comrades—he had shrunk in stature. His face seemed smaller, drawn tight over bone. Durrance had walked with him on the terrace just two years prior, and even then, blindness had not dimmed his perception of change. The general moved with heavier steps now; a childish tone had crept into his voice.

"You’ve joined the veterans early, Durrance," he said. "I read about it in a paper. I’d have written—if I’d known where."

No hint of suspicion crossed his features. He rang for tea, which was brought into the great hall where ancestral portraits loomed like shadows. He asked after officers in the Sudan, lamented the War Office’s incompetence, and muttered that the nation was slipping into ruin.

"Everything goes to the devil through bad luck or worse management," he snapped. "Even you, Durrance—you’re not the same man who walked with me two years ago."

Feversham had never been known for diplomacy, and solitude had done nothing to improve him. Durrance could have retorted with a *tu quoque*, but he held back.

"But I come on the same business," he said.

Feversham sat straighter. "And I give you the same answer. I have nothing to say about Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him."

His voice was flat, unemotional—as though speaking of a stranger. Not a trace of sorrow tinged the name.

Durrance hesitated. Was affection entirely dead within the general?

"Would it please you to know where Harry has been these past five years? How he lived?"

A pause. Not long—but long enough.

"Not in the least, Colonel Durrance."

The refusal was absolute. Yet Durrance noticed the hesitation.

"And what business he’s pursued?"

"I’m not interested. I don’t wish him to starve. My solicitor tells me he receives his allowance. That’s enough for me."

"I’ll risk your anger, General," Durrance said quietly. "There are times when disobedience is wise. This is one. Of course, you can throw me out. Otherwise, I shall tell you everything."

Feversham laughed—a dry, brittle sound. "Of course, I can’t throw you out. But I warn you—you’re taking unfair advantage of being my guest."

"True," Durrance said calmly—and began his tale: the recovery of the Gordon letters from Berber, his meeting with Harry at Wadi Halfa, Harry’s imprisonment in Omdurman. He carried the story to the present day, ending with Lieutenant Sutch’s departure for Suakin.

Feversham listened without interruption, without shifting in his chair. Durrance couldn’t read his expression—but the fact that he listened at all offered some comfort.

For a time, silence filled the hall. Then, slowly, the general raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his eyes as though seeing were possible. He remained that way, motionless.

When he finally spoke, his hand did not move.

"What I don’t understand," he said slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I couldn’t understand it before. Now, after hearing of his bravery, I understand it even less. It’s one of those inexplicable things. They happen. That’s all there is to say. But I’m glad you made me listen, Durrance."

"I came with a purpose," Durrance said. "It’s yours to decide—but I see no reason why Harry shouldn’t return home and reclaim what he lost."

"He cannot regain everything," Feversham said. "It wouldn’t be right. He committed the sin. He must pay. He can’t reclaim his career."

"True," Durrance agreed. "But he can find another. He’s not too old yet. That’s all he loses."

Now, Feversham lowered his hand and shifted in his seat. He looked quickly at Durrance, opened his mouth—then closed it.

"Well," he said, as though the matter were trivial, "if Sutch can manage Harry’s escape from Omdurman, I see no reason why he shouldn’t come home."

Durrance stood.

"Thank you, General. If you could send me to the station, I can catch the six o’clock train."

"But you’ll stay the night!" Feversham insisted.

"It’s impossible. I leave for Wiesbaden early tomorrow."

Feversham rang the bell and ordered a carriage.

"I’d have been glad if you stayed," he said. "I see few people now. To be honest, I don’t much care to see many. One grows old—and becomes a creature of habit."

"But you have your Crimean nights," Durrance said cheerfully.

Feversham shook his head. "None since Harry left. I had no heart for them."

For a fleeting second, the mask slipped. His stern face softened—just enough to reveal the depth of his grief. These five years had been lonely, painful. No one had ever guessed. He had kept his pride intact, refusing to let anyone see him broken. But now, in these quiet words, he laid bare the truth: the memories of war—once cherished—had become unbearable. A story of valor would sting as sharply as one of cowardice. The silence of Broad Place was his punishment.

The carriage wheels rolled over gravel.

"Good-bye," Durrance said, extending his hand.

"By the way," Feversham added, "organizing this escape will cost a great deal. Sutch isn’t wealthy. Who is paying?"

"I am."

Feversham gripped his hand firmly. "It’s my right, of course. I’ll repay you. Let me know the sum."

"Thank you."

He escorted Durrance to the door. A question formed—delicate, personal.

"Didn’t I hear," he said casually, "that you were engaged to Miss Eustace?"

"I think I said Harry would regain all he lost—except his career," Durrance replied.

He stepped into the carriage and drove off.

His work was done. All that remained was waiting in Wiesbaden—and praying that Sutch succeeded.

General Feversham stood on the steps, watching until the carriage vanished among the pines. Then he turned slowly toward the house.

"There is no reason why he should not come back," he murmured.

He looked up at the portraits—the dead Fevershams in their uniforms. They would not be disgraced.

"No reason in the world," he repeated. "And please God, he will come back soon."

The dangers of escaping from Omdurman weighed heavily upon him. He admitted to himself how tired he felt—how old. That night, many times, he sat on the bench once favored by his wife, gazing across the moonlit land toward the Sussex Downs, whispering the same prayer:

"Please God, Harry will come back soon."

These were the days before the great mud wall rose around the House of Stone in Omdurman. Then, only a thorn fence—thin and brittle—surrounded the prison and its grim courtyard. It stood on the eastern edge of the town, a place so foul and desolate it seemed to mock the very idea of civilization. Not a single flower bloomed within its walls. No grass grew, no tree offered shade. Only a barren, sun-baked plain stretched beyond, scorched by the relentless heat. Upon it sprawled a ragged city of hovels, crawling with disease and vermin, breathing out decay.

Between the prison and the Nile, no buildings stood. During daylight hours, prisoners were allowed to shuffle in chains down the half-mile slope to the riverbank—to fetch water, to wash, to endure their daily rites. For natives or blacks, escape was not impossible. Along the shore, dhows bobbed at anchor; river traffic had its harbor there, and the broad foreshore served as a bustling market. The space between the river and the prison teemed with life—crowds swarmed, friends met, plans were whispered, and sometimes a captive slipped into the throng and vanished toward the blacksmith’s shop, where iron could be bought for a price, and risk meant little.

Chains were common in Omdurman. Slaves wore them like clothing. The clink of iron echoed through every street of that long, brown, treeless city. But for a European prisoner, escape was another matter entirely. There were few white men in captivity, and each was marked, watched, hunted. To flee required camels hidden across the desert, money, months of secret planning—and above all, a native willing to risk his life. Camels might be arranged, but drivers often deserted. Preparations could be ruined by a sudden flogging—just suspicion of hidden wealth enough to bring the lash down. And even the most loyal servant might falter when the moment came.

Colonel Trench began to lose hope. He knew his friends were trying. Sometimes the boy who brought his food would nod silently, or whisper: *Be ready.* At times, during parades of the Khalifa’s troops, when he was paraded as a trophy of conquest, a man would brush against his camel and murmur encouragement. But nothing ever came of it. Each day, he watched the sun rise over the bend of the Nile behind the tall palms of Khartum, burning across the sky, and the months passed like dust in the wind.

One evening in late August—just after Durrance returned from the Sudan, blind and broken—Trench sat in the corner of the enclosure, staring westward as the sun sank toward the plain. His heart pounded with anticipation, though not of freedom. It was the dread of nightfall that gnawed at him. For however unbearable the heat and weight of the day, it was nothing compared to what came after dark.

As twilight fell, Idris es Saier—the towering negro of the Gawaamah tribe—appeared with his guards.

“Into the House of Stone!” he bellowed.

With curses and prayers, the prisoners surged forward, driven by whips that cracked without mercy over the backs of those lagging behind. They jostled and stumbled toward the narrow entrance of the prison. Inside, thirty captives already lay on the wet mud floor—some dying, others barely conscious. Two hundred more would be forced in, packed like cattle until morning. The room was thirty feet square, with a solid pillar at its center supporting the roof. No windows existed—only a few tiny holes near the top, pretending to let air in. Into this stinking, suffocating chamber they were herded, screaming, fighting, clawing at one another. The door slammed shut. Darkness swallowed everything. A man could not see the shape of the head beside him.

Trench fought like a beast. One corner near the door was his target—a spot that might shield him from blows, from trampling feet, from the grinding weight of shackles. If he could hold it, he might survive the ten endless hours of darkness and suffocation. But there was a constant fear in his mind, a poison that twisted his thoughts:

He had seen it happen. Once a man went down in the crush, he never rose again. He was crushed beneath hundreds of feet, flattened like clay. Trench was small. He fought with his elbows, his knees, his teeth. He kicked with his fetters, tore at clothes, dug under arms, burrowed between bodies. He used his nails, his fists, even swung the chain from the ring around his neck. At last, he reached the corner, drenched in sweat, gasping. He would defend it now, hour after hour.

“If I were to fall!” he choked. “O God, if I were to fall!” He shouted into the din—no less than a shout could be heard—“Is it you, Ibrahim?”

“Yes, Effendi,” came the reply.

Relief washed over him. Ibrahim, a tall Arab from the Hadendoas, was his companion in suffering. Between them, a bond had grown—not of friendship, perhaps, but of necessity. There were no rations in Omdurman. Each prisoner lived on charity or the money sent by friends outside. Trench received occasional sums, smuggled in by a native from Assouan or Suakin. But often, nothing came. Then he ate scraps from Greek converts who claimed faith in the Mahdi—or starved in silence. Ibrahim, too, sometimes had no one to send him food. So they helped each other. At night, they stood side by side against the wall.

“Yes, Effendi,” Ibrahim said, groping in the dark, steadying Trench with a hand.

A violent brawl erupted in a far corner. The crowd swayed with each lunge and retreat, like waves in a storm. Men fought with their teeth, their hands, their chains. Above the shrieks, the clank of iron, and the curses, came sudden sobs—then stifled screams—signs that someone had fallen. Dirt was thrown across the room, flung blindly. Heads collided in the effort to dodge. All of it happened in absolute blackness.

For two hours, Trench stood in that infernal place—ringed by noise, choked by heat. Eight more hours remained before dawn. He stood on tiptoe, lifting his head above the press, but still the air was thick, sour, moist. His throat burned. His tongue felt swollen, dry as a fig. He thought: *No hell could be worse than this—unless fire were added. But fire is already here.*

“If I were to fall!” he cried—just as the door opened.

Idris es Saier stood in the frame.

“Make room!” he roared. “Make room!”

And he hurled flaming tufts of dried grass into the darkness. The flames landed on backs, shoulders, heads. The prisoners couldn’t move. They couldn’t raise their hands. The fire burned them.

“Make room!” Idris repeated. Whips cracked. A path was cleared. A man was thrown inside. The door slammed shut.

Trench stood near the entrance. In the dim light from the doorway, he saw the new prisoner—a slight figure, bent under pain, bound heavily in iron.

“He will fall,” Trench muttered. “He’ll fall tonight. God! If I were to—”

Then the crowd surged again. Curses rose higher.

The newcomer clung to the door, pressing his face against the wood—any breath of air through the cracks was precious. Those behind pulled him away, shoved him back. He became a wedge, driven between men like a hammer drives steel. Finally, he was thrown directly into Trench.

In that prison, kindness died. By day, outside, prisoners shared misery, sometimes aid. But in the dark, survival was law. Pity was weakness. Trench was no different. His only thought was to live—long enough to taste water at dawn, to breathe clean air.

“Back!” he shouted. “Back, or I strike!”

As he raised his arm, he heard the man who had been thrown into him—muttering English.

“Don’t fall,” Trench said, grabbing his arm. “Ibrahim! Help! God, if he were to fall!”

And in the chaos, he caught the sound—his own language. After months of silence, the voice of home. It stirred something deep within him, like the touch of a woman’s hand. It calmed him. It reminded him of things lost.

He couldn’t hear the words clearly—the noise was too loud. But fragments drifted through the din: familiar phrases, once spoken in comfort, now twisted by fever. Images flooded his mind: green meadows, cool skies, rivers sparkling under sunlight. For a moment, he forgot the stench, the heat, the darkness.

Then he felt the man waver. “If he were to fall!” Trench cried.

Ibrahim stepped in. Together, they fought the crowd, clearing space. “Shaitan! They are mad!” the others yelled.

They set the Englishman down. Ibrahim guarded him. Trench crouched beside him.

Now, in a quiet moment, Trench could hear the words.

“They’re telling me about the sea,” the man murmured. “I saw the riding lights of yachts—how they shimmered, shortened, lengthened… there was music, too, as we passed the pier. What was it playing? Not the overture… I can’t remember any tune…” He laughed, crazily. “I’ve always been bad at music, haven’t I? Except when you played…”

He spoke of hills, woods, Bray—“a fairyland of lights”—and Dublin Bay. He remembered lunching there, just the two of them, before everything changed. “It seemed strange to leave you behind… among the hills… strange and not quite right…”

His voice trailed off. He lifted an elbow, fumbled in his breast pocket.

“Yes, the things to be done,” he mumbled.

Trench held him up. The heat was unbearable. The noise continued—shouts, moans, songs sung in madness, men dancing in chains. But beside Trench, a man who had sailed from Dublin Bay now lay dying in a foreign hell.

The memory of the sea, the salt wind, the ship’s bow cutting through water—it overwhelmed Trench. Practical, unimaginative as he was, he nearly wept.

Then the stranger spoke again.

“It’s funny—those three faces. Always the same. The man in the tent with the lancet… the man in Piccadilly… and mine. Funny. Not quite right. They grow big when I’m falling asleep… come close… won’t go away… frighten me…”

He gripped Trench’s arm, trembling.

“It’s all right, old man,” Trench said, gently. “It’s all right.”

The man relaxed. Then he began speaking again—this time of the future.

“Will you take it back?” he asked, hesitantly. “Really? The others have—all except the man who died at Tamai. And you will too!”

He sounded amazed, as if some miracle had occurred.

“Oh, it hasn’t been the best of times, of course. But I didn’t expect it to be. And at worst… I always had the afterwards to look forward to… supposing I didn’t run…”

Trench listened, intrigued. This was no ordinary delirium. It was a story unfolding—a life, perhaps a tragedy. And the word “afterwards” kept returning—like a theme in music. Was it the key?

The heat intensified. Darkness pressed heavier. The cries softened. Exhaustion took hold.

Trench leaned closer.

“I saw your light that morning,” the man whispered. “You put it out suddenly… did you hear my step on the gravel? I thought you did. It hurt…”

Then he protested fiercely: “No, no—I had no idea you’d wait. I didn’t want you to. Afterwards, maybe… but nothing more. Sutch was wrong…”

Trench frowned. “Feather.” He heard the word. But what did it mean?

Then came the name.

“Durrance.”

Trench froze.

This man knew Durrance!

Not just a fellow countryman—but someone who had known him, fought beside him, perhaps at Tokar or Tamai. A link formed between them.

Trench strained to hear more.

“Durrance stood at the window,” the man said, “after I told them about you, Ethne.”

Trench repeated the name. A woman—Ethne. He didn’t know her. But the voice went on.

“All the time I was thinking of sending in my papers… after the telegram… he stood at the window, back to me, looking across the park. I thought he blamed me. But now I think… he was making up his mind to lose you…”

Trench gasped. His blood ran cold.

He remembered—clearly—Durrance standing at a window, back turned. A telegram arrived—read slowly, painfully. Suspense filled the room. A man spoke of betrothal, of resigning. But surely—surely this wasn’t the same man?

Yet the name Ethne… Donegal… Dublin Bay… the feather…

“Good God,” Trench whispered. “Was it Ethne? Was it?”

Silence. Then the man spoke of a city of mud, a sun that burned, a man lying under a linen robe, drawing a face across three thousand miles—until sunset, when he gathered courage and walked to the gate.

Then—four words cut through the dark.

“Three little white feathers.”

Trench recoiled.

He had written those words. He had devised that message.

“Three little white feathers,” the voice repeated. “This afternoon we were under the elms by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then came three little white feathers. And the world’s at an end.”

Trench knew.

He knew the girl—Ethne. He knew the place. He knew the name.

“Feversham!” he cried. “Feversham!”

He shook the man. “Under the elms by the Lennon River—”

Images flashed—green shade, golden sunlight flickering through leaves—a mirage in the desert.

Feversham had been there. Under the elms. On the day the feathers came.

And now he was here—in the House of Stone.

But why?

Trench didn’t need to ask.

He knew.

For three days, Feversham wandered in and out of consciousness, his mind drifting like a leaf on a stormy river. During that time, Trench brought him water from the Nile, shared what little food he had, and watched over him with quiet devotion. Each night, Trench stood guard beside him, shielding him from danger within the prison’s shadowed walls—always at the front, where the risk was greatest, as if guarding not just a man, but a hope.

On the fourth morning, Feversham stirred. His eyes opened slowly, blinking against the harsh light of day. And there, bending over him, was Trench’s face.

At first, it seemed a vision—a nightmare figure from childhood dreams, looming too close, too real. Feversham flinched, pushing weakly at the form with one trembling hand. But then he looked around. He saw the sun-baked earth beneath him, the stone wall of the prison rising behind, the scattered figures of other prisoners—some dragging chains, others lying still in sickness or despair. The air was dry and sharp with dust. This was no dream.

He turned to Trench, clutching at his arm as if afraid he might vanish into thin air. Then, a smile broke across his lips.

“I’m here,” he whispered. “In Omdurman. In the House of Stone. Umm Hagar. I’ve made it.”

His voice held relief, almost joy. To Trench, it sounded strange—almost mocking. A man who claimed to feel nothing, who wore indifference like armor, might speak so casually of being imprisoned. But Feversham wasn’t that man. His fevered ramblings had proved otherwise. This satisfaction was real. The danger of Dongola was behind him. He’d found Trench. He was where he had always meant to be.

“You’ve been here a long time,” Feversham said.

“Three years,” Trench replied.

Feversham stared at the zareeba—the thorn-walled enclosure that held them all. “Three years,” he murmured. “I feared you wouldn’t be alive.”

Trench nodded. “The nights are the worst. They’re longer than any man should endure. Yet I’ve lived through a thousand of them.” He paused, as if astonished by the thought himself. “A thousand nights in this place.”

“But we can go down to the Nile during the day,” Feversham said, suddenly sitting up. His eyes darted toward the thorny fence. “Surely we’re allowed that. I was told so. An Arab at Wadi Halfa said we could.”

“And it’s true,” Trench answered, pointing to a clay bowl beside him. “I filled that from the Nile this morning.”

“I must go,” Feversham insisted, pushing himself upright. “Now. Today.”

But Trench hushed him. “Hush. There are many prisoners here—and many who betray.”

Feversham sank back, more from weakness than obedience. “They don’t understand us,” he argued, his voice now calm.

“They may not,” Trench said, “but they see us talking. Idris would know in an hour. The Khalifa by sunset. And then—more chains, the courbatch. Lie still. You’re weak. I am tired too. Let’s sleep. We’ll go later.”

Trench lay down beside him and was asleep almost instantly. Feversham watched him, studying his face now unguarded. The lines were deep, the skin pale, the eyes hollow. Three years had carved their mark.

It was near noon when he woke. “No one brings you food?” he asked.

“A boy will come,” Trench said. “And news.”

They waited. When the gate opened, the prisoners surged forward like wolves. Guards with rhinoceros-hide whips stood ready, but even they couldn’t control the frenzy. The "aseeda"—a thick, sour paste of crushed grain—was barely enough for survival. Men fought for scraps. One fell, too weak to rise, and stayed there, defeated before the meal began.

Among the crowd, a boy hesitated, hidden behind others. He was spotted quickly. Knocked down, his food snatched. But he screamed—loudly—and soon Idris-es-Saier arrived, thrusting aside the attackers.

“For whom do you come?” Idris demanded.

“For Joseppi, the Greek,” the boy replied.

Idris pointed to Feversham’s corner. The boy approached, hands empty, explaining something. Then he slipped a gazelle skin from beneath his robe and dropped it beside Feversham. Inside: a chicken.

That single bird became their breakfast, lunch, and supper.

Later, they were allowed to leave the zareeba and walk to the Nile. They moved slowly, halting often. At one pause, Trench leaned forward.

“We can talk here.”

Below, prisoners unloaded dhows, splashed in the muddy water. The shore was noisy, chaotic. Guards were nearby—but not close enough to hear.

“Yes,” Feversham said. “Why have you come?”

“I was captured in the desert,” Feversham said slowly. “On the Arbain road. Masquerading as a mad musician who’d wandered from Wadi Halfa with a zither.”

“I know,” Trench said. “But you didn’t escape by accident. You came here deliberately. To join me.”

“How do you know?” Feversham asked.

“You told me,” Trench said. “Over the last three days. You spoke much.”

Feversham looked around nervously.

“You came because five years ago,” Trench continued, “I sent you a white feather.”

“And was that all I said?” Feversham asked, anxious.

“No,” Trench replied, drawing out the word. He sat up, head in hands, staring at the river, avoiding Feversham’s gaze. “You spoke of a girl. The same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now—her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers came. She gave you a fourth feather. I didn’t think of that possibility. I’m sorry.”

Silence stretched between them. Then Feversham spoke, slowly.

“I’m not sorry. Not about her. I’m glad she was there. That fourth feather—snapped from her fan—it kept me going. Without it, I might have given up. I saw you—Durrance, Willoughby—walking your lives as I once did. The routine of regimental life… it called to me. I could have run. I could have slipped onto a boat and gone back to Suez. But the chance never came. For three years.”

“You saw us,” Trench said.

“And you saw me?”

“How would you have taken it if I had?” Trench asked.

Trench remained silent.

“I saw you,” Feversham said. “But I made sure you didn’t see me. I needed that memory—of Ramelton, of that night. I needed the feel of that fourth feather. Without it, I never would have gone down from Obak to Berber. I never would have come here.”

Trench turned sharply. “She would be glad to hear you say that,” he said. “I’m sure she regrets her feather. As I regret mine.”

“There’s no reason to regret,” Feversham said. “I don’t blame you. Or her.”

He fell silent, gazing at the river. The air rang with shouts. The shore teemed with men in blue and yellow robes, unloading boats. Across the Nile, palm trees rose against a cloudless sky. The sun was sinking.

Soon, the horrors of the House of Stone would return.

But both men were thinking of another place—of elms along the Lennon River, of a hall open to the cool night, echoing with waltz music. A girl and a man stood together, three white feathers fallen between them. One remembered. The other imagined. But both saw it clearly.

Feversham smiled finally.

“Perhaps she’s seen Willoughby now. Perhaps she’s taken his feather.”

Trench reached out, offering his hand.

“I’ll take mine back now.”

Feversham shook his head. “Not yet.”

Trench’s face lit up. A hope he’d buried—fearful it might be false—rose again.

“Then you *have* a plan,” he said.

Feversham’s expression darkened. “I said nothing of it. Tell me—when I was delirious, did I mention it? Did I breathe a word about escaping?”

“Not a single word,” Trench said. “So I dared not believe.”

Feversham exhaled, relieved. “If I’d spoken of it in the prison, I might have spoken in Dongola. I was delirious there too. But you say I didn’t here—so perhaps not there either.”

He shuddered. “There was a woman in Dongola. She spoke some English. Not much—but enough. She’d been in Khartum under Gordon. She questioned me. It was terrible.”

Trench interrupted softly. “I know. You told me things that made me shiver.” He pulled back Feversham’s sleeve. The scars on his wrists confirmed the tale.

“I felt myself slipping,” Feversham went on. “So I forced myself to think of something else. Of Ethne. That’s why you heard me talk of her.”

Trench sat quietly, knees drawn up, staring ahead. He dared now to believe.

“So it’s true,” he whispered. “There will be a morning when we don’t drag ourselves from the House of Stone. There will be nights when we sleep in beds. Actual beds. And—” He hesitated, shyly. “There will be something more.”

He stood up abruptly. “We’ve sat too long. Let’s move.”

They walked another hundred yards, then stopped again.

“You have more than a hope,” Trench said. “You have a plan?”

“More than a plan,” Feversham replied. “Preparations are complete. Camels wait ten miles west of Omdurman. Rifles and ammunition are buried nearby. Provisions and water are ready. We travel by Metemneh—fresh camels await there. From Metemneh to Berber. Cross the Nile. More camels five miles from Berber. Then ride over the Kokreb pass to Suakin.”

“Now?” Trench asked.

“Now,” Feversham said. “When I can sit a horse for ten miles and a camel for a week.”

“And how soon will that be?”

“Not long,” Feversham promised. “I swear it.”

He rose. “As you stand, look behind. See the man in blue linen? Loitering between us and the gaol? He signaled to me earlier. I didn’t reply. But on the day we escape—I will.”

“He’ll wait?”

“For a month. We must escape in one night. We can signal him. A passage could be made through the wall—stones are loosely built.”

They walked farther, reaching the water’s edge. Amidst the noise, they spoke again—this time with humor.

“There’s a better way than breaking through the wall,” Trench said, laughing. He pointed to a prisoner who had collapsed into the water, weighed down by chains and load, struggling helplessly.

“There is a better way,” Trench repeated. “Do you have money?”

“Ah! Ai!” Feversham roared with laughter as the prisoner sank again. “I have some hidden. Idris took what I didn’t hide.”

“Good,” Trench said. “Idris will come to you today or tomorrow. He’ll preach about Allah’s mercy, the wickedness of the world, the peril of your soul. He’ll tell you of his starving children. If you give him fifteen dollars—out of what he took—you may earn permission to sleep outside the prison. Accept it. For a few nights. Then he’ll come again. Give him more. Ask for me to sleep outside too.”

“Come,” Trench said. “There’s Idris, shepherding us home.”

It happened exactly as predicted. That afternoon, Idris delivered a long sermon. Feversham learned God loved him. He heard of Hicks Pasha’s army destroyed—not by soldiers, but by angels. Spears flew without hands, piercing unbelievers. He met Nebbi Khiddr, the Khalifa’s invisible spy, who reported every whisper in the jail.

Then came the plea: starving children.

Feversham offered fifteen dollars.

Trench’s plan worked.

That night, Feversham slept outside. Two nights later, Trench joined him.

Above them, stars blazed in a clear sky.

“Only three more days,” Feversham said.

Trench drew in a long breath.

After a while, he spoke. “Are you awake?”

“Yes.”

“Well…” He hesitated. “Each man has his weak spot. Mine isn’t love. I’m not marrying. But I want to die at home. Not just in my country—but in my village. Buried under the trees I know. Near the church. By the trout stream where I fished as a boy. I’ve been terrified I’d die here. You’ll laugh.”

Feversham didn’t laugh. The words echoed familiarly. Not spoken to him—yet they might have been. By Ethne Eustace.

“No,” he said warmly. “I understand.”

And something shifted between them. A bond formed, born in that starlit moment.

They lay side by side, breathing the cool air, watching the stars.

“It will be strange to see them dim again,” Trench said.

“There will be compensations,” Feversham laughed.

They talked of England—hunting, fishing, theatres, books. Trench heard the reel of his rod again, winding line in the quiet stream. They imagined walking London streets, returning to old haunts.

“The Great Bear,” Trench said suddenly. “It’s late.”

The constellation dipped behind the thorn hedge.

They turned over.

“Three more days,” Trench said.

“Only three more,” Feversham replied.

And in a minute, they were neither in Omdurman nor England. The stars marched silently overhead. The desert wind sighed. And they drifted into sleep—two men dreaming of freedom, already half there.

"Three more days." The words lingered between them as they fell into sleep. But dawn brought no relief—only fever. Trench awoke shivering, his body burning with an illness that grew stronger by the hour. By afternoon, he was delirious, and the roles reversed: Feversham now tended him, just as Trench had once cared for him. The House of Stone, that ancient prison, had claimed its toll. Yet it was no mere chance that struck down Trench at this moment—when freedom, after years of torment, seemed within reach. The sudden surge of hope, the joy of imminent escape, had overwhelmed his weakened frame. His body could not endure the weight of deliverance.

"It’s nothing," Feversham insisted, though his voice trembled. "He’ll be well in a few days."

But Ibrahim shook his head gravely. "It is _Umm Sabbah_," he said—the dreaded typhus that had swept through the prison, claiming lives on the seventh day.

Feversham refused to believe it. "No," he repeated, stubbornly, though fear gnawed at his thoughts. *Will the camel men wait?* Each morning, he walked to the Nile, hoping to see Abou Fatma in his blue robe—always there, always silent. Each time, the man made his sign, but Feversham gave no answer. He could not risk it.

With Ibrahim’s help, he nursed Trench. The boy came daily with food, fetching tamarinds, dates, roots—anything to make cooling drinks. Together, they moved Trench from shade to shade as the sun crossed the zareeba. Some aid was given to Idris’s starving family, and the forty-pound chains were removed. Trench was fed salt-soaked vegetable marrow, his mouth packed with butter, his body oiled and wrapped tightly in camel cloths. The fever ran its course. On the seventh day, Ibrahim whispered: "This is the last. Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham said fiercely. "Not here. Not like this. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.' Not here."

His mind had cleared of everything else—the man in blue, the camels, the third feather, Ethne’s fourth—gone. Only Trench mattered. Only the words spoken under the stars on their first night together. "He must not die here," he said again, and stayed by Trench’s side through the long battle between life and death.

At times, the prison seemed to win. At others, Trench’s strong body fought back. That night, life prevailed. The seventh day passed. Even Ibrahim began to hope. On the thirteenth, Trench slept without rambling. When he woke, his head was clear. He found himself swathed in cloths, unable to move, but the heat had passed. The shadow of the House of Stone stretched across the sand. He had no desire to stir—just to lie still, in peace.

Then came shouts from the gate. Prisoners flooded in. Feversham was among them, collapsing beside Trench, exhausted.

"How long have I been ill?" Trench asked.

"Thirteen days."

"I can’t travel yet. You must go. Now. Before it’s too late. Perhaps you can do something for me when you reach Assouan. But I can’t move. Will you go tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham replied. "I wouldn’t leave without you. And it’s too late."

"Too late?" Trench murmured, slow to grasp. He wanted only silence, coolness, rest. But the truth settled in: Abou Fatma had gone.

"The camel men waited no longer," Feversham said. "They feared discovery. They’d return whether we went or not."

"You should have gone," Trench said quietly. For himself, he cared little—so long as he could rest. "Our one chance is lost."

"Not lost," Feversham corrected. "Only delayed. Abou Fatma left me paper, pen, soot mixed with water. He dropped them near me by the river. I hid them. Last night—there was a moon—I wrote to a Greek merchant at Wadi Halfa. He took the letter. He’ll deliver it, get paid. In six months, a year at most, he’ll return to Omdurman."

"Six months?" Trench scoffed. "He’ll ask for another letter. More money. Then say, 'Next year.' I know these people."

"You don’t know Abou Fatma," Feversham said. "He was Gordon’s servant before Khartum fell. He’s been mine since. He waited in Obak while I went to Berber. He risked his life coming here. He’ll come back. Within six months, I swear it."

Trench said nothing. His eyes drifted to a pile of fresh earth in the corner. "What are they digging?"

"A well," Feversham answered.

"A well?" Trench frowned. "So close to the Nile? Why?"

"I don’t know," Feversham admitted. But he did know. Deep inside, he feared the reason. A wall was being built around the prison—high, smooth, unclimbable. Too many had escaped along the Nile bank. Now, they would be locked in forever. The prisoners built it themselves—mud bricks dried in the sun. Feversham helped. Trench joined him as soon as he could stand.

"Our last hope is gone," Trench said.

Feversham didn’t argue aloud. But his heart sank. Day by day, the wall rose—a foot, then two. Twelve feet high, seamless, unbreakable. It stood like a coffin. The palm trees of Khartum vanished from view. Their world was now a square of sky—blue by day, dark and star-strewn by night.

Trench covered his face. "I daren’t look at it," he whispered. "We’ve built our own tomb, Feversham. That’s the truth."

Then he raised his arms and cried out: "Will they never come up the Nile—the gunboats, the soldiers? Have they forgotten us? Good God—have they forgotten us?"

"Hush," Feversham said. "We’ll find a way. We must wait six months. We’ve waited years. Six months—what are they?"

But even as he spoke, he felt the weight of despair.

The next six months passed in silence. No news reached them. The prison was a living hell—scorpions stung at night, guards beat them by day. They lived at the mercy of Idris-es-Saier and Nebbi Khiddr, who always betrayed them when Idris needed money. Religious men came to convert them, and theological debates became their only excitement. One day they’d be freed from shackles; the next, thrown back into darkness.

The sixth month ended. The seventh began. Then the eighth. Still, no word from Abou Fatma.

"He will never come," Trench said.

"Surely he will—if he’s alive," Feversham said.

"But is he?"

One morning, two of the Khalifa’s bodyguards arrived. Idris approached the prisoners.

"Verily God is good to you," he said. "You are to meet the Khalifa. How happy you should be!"

Trench and Feversham rose—not with joy, but dread.

"What does he want?" they thought.

They followed the guards through the streets to the Khalifa’s house. Outside, a white man sat waiting. Inside, the Khalifa sat on a throne, a grey-bearded Greek at his side.

"You are to work in the powder factory," the Khalifa declared. "You will help make gunpowder. Soon, the armies of the Turks will fall."

Feversham opened his mouth to deny it—but Trench stepped forward, speaking fluent Arabic: "There is nothing about gunpowder I do not know."

And so they were assigned to the factory, under the Greek’s supervision.

That man—though unknown to them—would become their savior. Out of pity, he suggested their employment. And when they confessed ignorance, he covered for them.

"I know nothing," Trench admitted. "Except that crystals are used."

"But we leave the prison each day," Feversham said. "That’s something. Who knows when escape might come?"

The factory lay north of the city, on the Nile bank beyond the mud wall, behind the slave market. Every morning, they walked out of the prison, past the storehouses, into the factory. Every evening, they returned—no guard, no escort. Each journey, they searched for Abou Fatma.

But May came, and summer. Still no sign.

"Something has happened to Abou Fatma," Feversham said. "Perhaps he was caught at Berber."

"He will not come," Trench said.

Feversham could no longer pretend otherwise. He did not know that Abou Fatma had been wounded—a sword thrust in the thigh as he fled. He had been recognized by an old gaoler, barely escaped, and spent months recovering in Assouan’s hospital.

Yet hope stirred again. One dusk, as they passed the storehouses, a shadowy figure whispered: "Stop."

Trench feigned injury, kneeling to examine his foot. The man walked past, dropping a slip of paper at their feet. A Suakin merchant—Mohammed Ali—had delivered it.

Trench picked it up, hid it, limped on. Once clear, he read aloud. His voice shook.

"A man will bring you a box of matches. When he comes, trust him.—Sutch."

"Who is Sutch?" Trench asked.

"A great friend," Feversham said. "He’s in Egypt. Probably Suakin."

A man with matches! They might meet him tonight!

But it was a month later when, on the riverbank, an Arab pushed past and said: "I am the man with the matches. Tomorrow, same place, same hour."

He dropped a box of colored matches. Feversham stooped—but Trench stopped him.

"Don’t touch them," he said, pressing the box into the ground.

"Sutch!" Feversham breathed. "He comes to help! How did he know?"

Trench trembled with excitement, but dared not speak. Both men slept fitfully, waking with the feeling that something immense had changed.

Feversham, gazing at the stars, imagined he was in the garden of Broad Place, Surrey—the pines, the house, the hills. He fell asleep at dawn—only to be shaken awake by Trench.

"Suppose they keep us in today," Trench whispered, terrified. "Suppose they do?"

"Why would they?" Feversham asked—but the fear gripped him too.

Idris crossed the yard, unbolted the door. The prisoners surged out—some screaming, some fighting. One man ran, foaming at the mouth, smashing his head against the wall.

"He’s mad!" Trench gasped.

And suddenly, he spoke of madness—his own fear of losing reason. To die was bearable. But to go mad—foaming, raving, cast aside—was horror.

"If this man with the matches fails," he said, "I’ll be near it—very near it."

He buried his face in his hands.

Later, at the factory, a man named Abdul Kader appeared.

"I am here to arrange your escape," he said. "But flight is impossible."

"Impossible?" Feversham asked.

"Yes. I brought three camels—two died. The Effendi at Suakin gave me money—but not enough. If you write a letter asking for two hundred pounds, I’ll return in three months."

Trench turned away. All hope drained from him. Abdul Kader wasn’t risking his life—he was a courier, shuttling between Omdurman and Suakin for money. No escape.

"I have nothing to write with," Feversham said.

Abdul produced paper and pen. "Write quickly," he urged.

Feversham wrote—but knew it was useless.

He handed the letter over, took Trench’s arm, and walked away.

They reached the mud wall at sunset. The Nile shimmered—olive, pink, green. Stars emerged. Drums beat in the town.

They walked slowly, heads down, hearts broken. Hope was dead. Despair had taken root.

Then, an Arab stepped beside them.

"The camels are ready—ten miles west," he whispered.

Feversham looked up. Slowly, the meaning sank in.

"Abou Fatma!" he breathed.

"Hoosh," Abou Fatma said. "The camels are ready. Now."

Trench leaned against the wall, pale, weak. He seemed about to faint.

"Is it true?" he asked faintly.

Before Feversham could answer, Abou Fatma spoke: "Walk slowly. Before you reach the end of the wall, it will be dusk. Cover your heads, wrap rags around your chains—so they don’t rattle. Turn back, go near the water beyond the storehouses. I’ll be there with a blacksmith to remove your chains. Keep your faces hidden. Do not stop. He’ll think you’re slaves."

He handed them rags, held his hands behind his back.

They walked forward slowly. Dusk crept across the river, climbed the sand, enveloped them. They sat, wrapped the rags around their chains, secured them tight.

The sunset faded. Darkness gathered. They turned, walked back.

By the water’s edge, Abou Fatma waited with the blacksmith. Without a word, the chains were knocked off.

"Come," Abou said. "No moon tonight. How long before they notice?"

"Who knows?" Feversham said. "Maybe already. Maybe not till morning. There are many prisoners."

They ran—up the sand slope, through tribal quarters, across the city, through the cemetery.

On the far side stood a disused house. A man rose in the doorway. Abou Fatma entered, returned moments later—each leading a camel.

"Mount," Abou said. "Bring the camel’s head around. Hold it as you climb."

"I know the trick," Trench said.

Feversham climbed behind him. The two Arabs mounted the second camel.

"Ten miles to the west," Abou Fatma said—and struck the camel on the flanks.

Behind them, the lights dimmed. The drums faded.

The wind cut sharp and cold from the north, carrying with it the scent of distant sand and ancient stone. The camels, refreshed by the breeze, picked up speed, their hooves thudding rhythmically against the earth.

"Quicker," Trench muttered through clenched teeth. "Idris might already have missed us."

"Even if he has," Feversham replied, voice steady despite the tension coiling in his chest, "it will take time to gather men, to bring camels—by now it’s dark. They won’t catch us tonight."

Yet even as he spoke, he turned again toward Omdurman, where the sky still glowed faintly above the city. The drums were silent—thank God for that—but silence was no comfort. In this land, a man could vanish into the night without a sound. No thundering hooves would warn them of pursuit. Perhaps at that very moment, Ansar soldiers were riding within thirty paces behind them. Feversham strained his eyes into the blackness, searching for the glint of a white turban.

Trench did not look back. His jaw was set, his gaze fixed ahead. But fear gnawed at him just as fiercely, perhaps more so—because he dared not turn. His mind replayed the narrow streets of Omdurman, the scattered fires flickering at street corners, the shadows darting between houses, hunting two fugitives who had slipped from the House of Stone.

A word from Feversham broke his trance.

"What is it?"

"I can't see the fires of Omdurman anymore."

"The golden blot," Trench murmured, half to himself, "very low down."

Feversham didn’t press for meaning. He knew better than to ask. The words had come unbidden, stirred by memory—by the image that had been the last thing Feversham saw when he left Omdurman on his mission, and now, strangely, the first thing returning to him as he neared its end.

They rode on in silence until two figures emerged from the darkness, rising like phantoms at the feet of their camels. Abou Fatma whispered, “Instanna.”

They halted, guiding the beasts to kneel.

"The new camels are here?" Abou Fatma asked. Two men vanished briefly and returned with four fresh animals. Saddles were removed from the tired camels, replaced upon the new ones.

"Good camels?" Feversham asked, helping to secure the gear.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma said. "Fast. Strong." He glanced eastward, listening.

"The arms?" Trench demanded, voice urgent. "You’ve got them? Where?" He leaned forward, scanning the ground.

"In a moment," Abou Fatma said. But Trench could barely wait. His hands trembled with need.

"There’s ammunition?" he asked, breathless.

"Yes. Rifles. Revolvers. Ammunition."

Abou Fatma led them twenty yards away, to a patch of desert grass swaying gently in the wind. He knelt, digging into the soft sand with his bare hands.

"Here," he said.

Trench dropped to his knees beside him, clawing at the earth with frantic speed. A low whimper escaped his lips—a guttural sound, almost animal. To Feversham, it was chilling: the man reduced to instinct, driven by survival.

Then fingers brushed metal—the lock of a rifle. Instantly, Trench straightened, calm returning. He lifted the weapon, cradling it like a child.

"Now," he said, laughing—a sudden, wild joy breaking through the strain. "Now I don’t care. Let them chase us from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I’ll never go back. Not even if they catch us." He caressed the rifle as though it were alive, speaking to it in quiet reverence.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and rode slowly back toward Omdurman. Abou Fatma and one other stayed with the fugitives. They mounted and began moving northeast.

Less than fifteen minutes had passed since their halt. That night, they rode through halfa grass and mimosa trees, progress slow and cautious. At sunrise, they reached flat, barren land dotted with small hills.

"Are the Effendi tired?" Abou Fatma asked. "Would you rest and eat? There is food on each camel's saddle."

"No," Feversham said. "We can eat as we ride."

Dates, bread, water from a zamsheyeh—this was their meal, consumed while seated atop their camels. Now freed from the thick grass, the beasts moved swiftly. By sunset, they stopped for an hour’s rest.

That night and the next day, they pushed onward—over rocky ridges, through valleys, across honey-colored sands. The pace felt interminable, like a funeral procession. A mountain rose at dawn, standing motionless on the horizon, unchanged all day.

At times, farmers tending sorghum fields sent their hearts racing. They veered wide. Caravans spotted far off by Abou Fatma’s sharp eyes forced them to kneel behind rocks, rifles ready.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, fresh camels awaited them. They switched mounts and traveled west of the Nile, keeping clear of known routes. Through the Ababdeh desert, they caught sight of a broad gray road stretching across their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma said. "We cross it tonight. If God wills, tomorrow evening we cross the Nile."

"If God wills," Trench echoed, voice heavy with dread. "If only He wills."

They were now in caravan country—no longer safe by daylight. They dismounted and hid behind shrubs on high ground, watching the road like hawks. People moved along it like specks. When night fell, they crossed quietly, then pressed hard toward the river.

At dawn, Abou Fatma called a halt. They were in open, desolate terrain—no shelter, no cover.

"We must build a circle of stones," he said. "Lie close within it. I’ll go to the river. I’ll check the boat, ensure our friends are ready. I’ll return after dark."

They gathered stones quickly, forming a low wall about a foot high. Inside, Feversham and Trench lay flat, rifles and water-skins at hand.

"You have dates?" Abou Fatma asked.

"Yes."

"Then do not move until I return. I’ll take your camels and bring fresh ones tonight."

He rode off with his companion, leaving the two men alone in the sun.

They dug out the sand beneath the stones, lying still, staring through gaps in the wall. For both, this was perhaps the longest day of their lives. So near to safety—and yet not safe.

To Trench, it felt longer than any night in the House of Stone. To Feversham, longer than those days six years ago, sitting in his rooms above St. James’s Park, waiting for darkness before stepping outside.

They had no shade. The sun beat down mercilessly. Within the stone ring, there was no room to shift. They spoke little.

But sunset came at last. Darkness crept over the desert, cool winds rustling through the dunes.

"Listen!" Trench whispered.

Both men strained their ears. Soft padding—camels approaching. A low whistle.

"We’re here," Feversham said calmly.

"God be thanked!" Abou Fatma exclaimed. "Good news and bad. The boat is ready. Friends await. Fresh camels are prepared by the riverbank near Abu Hamed. But your escape is known. Roads and ferries are watched. Before sunrise, we must strike inland from the eastern bank."

They crossed the Nile cautiously around one o’clock. The boat was sunk on the far side. Camels waited. They traveled inland, slower than they wished—boulders littered the ground, forcing the beasts to walk.

The next day, they hid again in a stone ring while the camels grazed on higher ground. But that night, they made good progress. After two days, they reached the groves of Abu Hamed, rested twelve hours, and took fresh camels.

From there, their path led across the great Nubian Desert. Today, travelers pass through 240 miles of coal-black rock and yellow sand aboard trains, stopping at stations marked by signs and telegraph poles stretching endlessly into the sky. But in those days, movement was harder.

They kept east of the modern railway, following the line of wells among the hills.

On the second night of this leg, Trench shook Feversham awake.

"Look," he said, pointing south. "There’s no Southern Cross tonight."

His voice cracked with emotion. "For six years—every night for six years—I’ve seen it. How many times have I lain awake, watching those four slanting stars, wondering if this night would be the last? I tell you, Feversham, this is the first time I’ve truly dared believe we’d make it."

They sat up, gazing at the southern sky, hearts full of gratitude. When sleep came, it was restless—each waking to fear the constellation might still blaze low on the horizon, only to fall back into dreams of freedom.

After seven days, they reached Shof-el-Ain—a tiny well in a barren valley between featureless ridges. Here, they camped.

"We are safe," Abou Fatma declared. "God is good. North to Assouan, west to Wadi Halfa—we are free!"

He spread a cloth before the kneeling camels and poured dhurra for them. In his joy, he patted one on the neck. The camel turned and snarled.

Trench reached out to Feversham. "Thank you," he said simply.

"No thanks needed," Feversham replied, not taking the hand. "I served myself from the start."

"You’ve learned the churlishness of a camel," Trench laughed. "It carries you wherever you want, till it drops. Yet if you show gratitude, it bites. Hang it all, Feversham—there’s my hand."

Feversham untied a knot in his jibbeh and pulled out three white feathers—two small, heron feathers; one large, an ostrich feather broken from a fan.

"Will you take yours back?"

"Yes."

"You know what to do with it."

"Yes. There shall be no delay."

Feversham carefully wrapped the remaining feathers and tucked them deep into his ragged jibbeh, tying them securely.

"We shake hands, then," he said. And as their hands met, he added, "Tomorrow morning, we part company."

"Part company?" Trench protested. "After a year in Omdurman, weeks of flight? Why?"

"There’s nothing left to do. Castleton’s dead. You keep the feather he sent—but he’s gone. You can’t use it. You must go home."

"Yes," Feversham said. "But not with you. You go to Assouan, then Cairo. Friends await you. I won’t go with you."

Trench was silent. He understood. Feversham wanted to tell Ethne Eustace first—alone. Without him.

"I ought to tell you—no one knows why you resigned your commission, or about the feathers. We never spoke of it. Agreed never to speak—for the honor of the regiment. I’m glad we kept that vow."

"Perhaps you’ll see Durrance," Feversham said. "If you do, give him a message. Tell him the next time he invites me to visit—whether in England or Wadi Halfa—I’ll accept."

"And which way will you go?"

"To Wadi Halfa," Feversham said, pointing west. "I’ll take Abou Fatma. We’ll travel slowly down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you to Assouan."

They slept that night in peace beside the well.

At dawn, they parted.

Trench rode off first. As his camel rose, he leaned down. Feversham handed him the nose rein.

"Ramelton—that was the name? I won’t forget."

"Yes," Feversham said. "Ramelton. There’s a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. Drive twelve miles to Ramelton. But she may not be there."

"If not there," Trench said, "I’ll find her somewhere else. Make no mistake, Feversham—I’ll find her."

And he rode forward, alone with his guide.

More than once, he looked back—Feversham still standing by the well. He longed to turn back, to ride to him, but only waved instead. The wave went unanswered.

Feversham had no eyes for his companion. His six years of trial, of purpose, had ended. And with it, a hollow emptiness settled in.

For six years, his mission had given him strength. Now, it was over.

Ethne? She was likely married by now. A wave of bitter despair washed over him—the folly of that night six years ago, the mistaken choice that had shattered everything.

He saw again the room in London, the quiet park beyond the window, the knock on the door, the telegram in his hand.

Then he remembered—his father. Probably reading the *Times* now, on the terrace at Broad Place, among the pine trees of Surrey. He must visit him. He must return the fourth feather to Ramelton.

And there was a telegram to send—to Lieutenant Sutch at Suakin.

He mounted his camel and rode west with Abou Fatma, slowly toward Wadi Halfa.

But the weight of loss did not lift. Nor did the anger at the foolish act that had brought him down.

Across the shimmering heat, the wooded slopes of Ramelton were visible.

And for the first time in his life, Harry Feversham doubted the truth of the "afterwards."

On a bright August morning, Harry Feversham rode across the Lennon bridge into Ramelton. The suns of the Sudan had bronzed his skin, and the years of trial had etched their marks upon him. He moved through the narrow streets unremarked, a stranger in his own past. At the crest of the hill, he turned onto the broad highway that wound through valleys and over ridges toward Letterkenny. He rode swiftly, yet not alone—accompanied by ghosts: echoes of people he once knew, memories of laughter and silence, words spoken long ago but still echoing in his mind.

The years between then and now had slipped away during his journey across Egypt and Europe. They were no longer real, only shadows. He did not dwell on what might have been, nor on the weight of his failure. What mattered was what had been—this quiet Irish town where joy and sorrow had both lived in him. Here, he had known happiness; here, he had suffered his deepest pain. Here, his presence had brought comfort—and harm.

He paused briefly opposite the church, perched high above the road. A sudden question stirred within him: Was Ethne still here? Was old Dermod alive? What would she say to see him again?

But then he came back to himself—sitting on his horse, the morning still and golden. Larks sang overhead, a landrail called from the meadow, and a rooster crowed from the valley below. He looked around, took a breath, and urged his horse forward.

Again, he rode with phantoms—the faces of friends, lovers, enemies, all walking beside him in memory. He passed a grove of trees, a broken fence, an open gate. Without hesitation, he turned in, riding down a weed-choked drive that led to a ruined house. The crumbling walls and roofless rooms jolted him from his reverie.

He dismounted, tied his horse to a tree, and hurried inside. “Ethne!” he called. No answer. Room after room stood empty. He descended into the garden, but found no one. The overgrown lawn, the tangled flowerbeds—everything spoke of abandonment. He mounted again and rode back at a brisk pace.

In Ramelton, he stopped at the inn, handed his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. As the landlady served him, he asked quietly, “So Lennon House burned down?”

“Five years ago,” she replied. “This very summer.” And without being prompted, she launched into a detailed tale: the fire, the fall of the Eustace family, Bastable’s drunkenness, Dermod’s death at Glenalla. “But we hope it’ll be rebuilt,” she added. “They say Miss Eustace will marry soon—then the house will rise again.”

Feversham said nothing.

“And where does Miss Eustace live now?” he asked.

“At Glenalla,” she answered. “Halfway to Rathmullen, there’s a track leading up to your left. It’s a poor mountain village—not fit for her at all. You’d like to see her?”

“Yes,” he said. “Please have my horse brought round.”

As he waited, he glanced through the hotel register and saw, under late July, the name: *Colonel Trench*.

“You’ll come back tonight, sir?” the landlady asked as he mounted.

“No,” he replied. “I don’t think I’ll return to Ramelton.”

He rode down the hill, crossed the Lennon bridge once more, and continued four miles until he reached the track near a small bay of the lake. Turning in, he passed a few white cottages and climbed into the purple hills.

It was about five o’clock when he arrived at the scattered village of Glenalla. The place seemed quiet, unplanned—cottages huddled together, then fields, then a stand of larches and a lone house beyond. After that, another gap revealed the glimmering water of the lough, gulls wheeling above it. Then, ahead, a small grey church stood bare on its rocky plateau.

A path of white shell-dust led from the church door to a wooden gate. As he approached, a collie dog barked from behind it.

He stopped. The dog was old—its muzzle grey, its eyes sharp. He recognized its markings. He looked toward the church. The door stood open.

He dismounted, tied his horse, and stepped into the churchyard. The dog sniffed at his knee, then erupted into joyful chaos—barking, leaping, whirling, screaming with delight. It ran to the door, barked fiercely, then dashed back to him, jumping up with wagging tail.

Feversham caught it gently, patted it, and laughed.

Then he froze.

At the church doorway stood Ethne Eustace.

He set the dog down and walked slowly along the path. She stood motionless, silent, watching him. Not a word passed between them until he drew near.

“Harry,” she said.

Just one word. But it shattered every ghost that had haunted him all day. Six years had passed since he last stood beneath her window on a June dawn. Now they faced each other, seeing changes invisible to others.

Feversham was thinner, worn—his eyes sunken, his face aged beyond his years. The hardships of the House of Stone had left their mark. Yet Ethne saw more than just weariness. She saw strength. The timidity that once clung to him—the fear of judgment—was gone. In its place was calm self-assurance. He had endured a test, and he knew he had passed.

She felt a surge of sorrow for the suffering he had borne—but also something deeper. She saw the man he had become. And though she stood still, her heart stirred with pride.

“It is not all harm,” she said softly. “These years were not wasted.”

He thought of her solitude in this remote village—of how she had chosen this life, even if he didn’t know it. He looked into her face. Her eyes were still clear, her cheeks still flushed with color. But something had changed. There was depth now—character shaped by hardship, wisdom earned through loss.

“I am sorry,” he said. “I wronged you six years ago. I needn’t have.”

She held out her hand. “Will you give it to me, please?”

He hesitated. “What?”

“That fourth feather,” she said.

He reached into his coat, pulled out his letter-case, and shook two feathers into his palm. One was large—an ostrich feather. He offered it to her.

“Both,” she said.

He hesitated no longer. He gave her both feathers. She clasped them tightly, pressing them against her chest with a quiet smile.

“I have all four now,” she said.

“Yes,” he replied. “All four. What will you do with them?”

She laughed—a light, defiant sound. “Do with them? Nothing. I’ll keep them. I’m proud to hold them.”

She kept them—as she once kept his portrait. Perhaps Durrance was right: women remember, cherish, build their lives from what has passed. Men let go. Feversham would have thrown the feathers away, crushed them underfoot. They had served their purpose. They could no longer shame or inspire. They were dead.

But Ethne held them close. To her, they were alive.

“Colonel Trench was here two weeks ago,” she said. “He told me you were coming.”

“He didn’t know about the fourth feather,” Feversham said. “I never told anyone.”

“Yes,” she replied. “You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone. He told me. And I no longer hate him.”

She said it plainly, seriously—as if it were a truth worth stating.

“I’m glad,” Feversham said. “He’s a great friend.”

She was silent for a moment. Then:

“I wonder if you’ve forgotten our ride from Ramelton to the house—when I came to fetch you from the quay? We were alone in the dog-cart. We talked—”

“Of friends whom one knows instantly,” he interrupted. “People one recognizes, even if never seen before.”

“Indeed,” she said. “And whom one never loses—whether absent or dead.”

“You said one could always trust such friends,” he continued.

“And you said one could make mistakes,” she finished.

“I did,” he admitted.

“But I disagreed,” she said. “One may seem to err, and believe so for a time. But in the end, the mistake is not real. I’ve thought of those words often. When Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather—I remembered them, full of remorse. And now… I remember them again. Though I have no room for regret. I was right. I should have held fast to my belief. But I didn’t.”

Her voice trembled slightly. “I was young. I knew little. I judged too quickly. But now I understand.”

She opened her hand and looked at the white feathers. Then she turned and walked into the church.

Feversham followed.

Ethne sat in the corner of a pew, near the aisle, her hands folded quietly in her lap. Harry Feversham stood beside her, silent and still. The little church was hushed, bathed in the soft glow of afternoon light that streamed through the high windows, painting golden patterns across the stone roof. Outside, the gentle hum of summer—birds singing, leaves rustling, distant voices—drifted in through the open door, as if nature itself were listening.

She spoke first, her voice calm, measured, yet carrying a weight that seemed to fill every quiet corner of the space. “I’m glad you remembered our ride and what we said,” she began. “It matters to me that you do. Because though I’ve brought you back after all these years—after so much pain, so much waiting—I must send you away again. You’ll be one of those absent friends I won’t lose, even when you’re gone.”

Her eyes stayed fixed straight ahead, steady and unshaken. It had been hard to say these words—harder than she’d imagined—but she had thought them through for days, rehearsed them in silence, until they came now like a breath of truth. At first sight of him, returned from exile, she had nearly broken down. But she had gathered herself, stepped back into the church, and waited. She knew he would understand. He would know how deeply this cost her. And she wanted him to know—wanted him to feel the full meaning of it: that none of his six years had been wasted, that every moment of suffering, every silent night, had shaped something real between them.

“It’s not easy,” she continued, “but there’s no other way.” She paused slightly, letting the silence settle around them like dust. “You’re home now. That’s a comfort. But there are greater things to hold onto. Colonel Trench told me enough about your captivity—your courage, your endurance—to know we see the same truth. This parting… it’s nothing compared to the first. That one felt final. Back then, I believed we were forever separated. We wouldn’t meet again—not here, not beyond. But you broke that wall. With everything you endured, with every wound you carried, you made it possible for us to have an ‘afterwards.’ I’m certain of it. And I believe you are too.”

She stopped. For a long while, no sound but the faint creak of the church door and the whisper of wind outside. To Feversham, her words were like water falling on parched earth—life-giving, healing. They lifted him up. The six years of shame, of hiding, of loneliness, of physical torment—all of it faded into insignificance. He had dreamed of hearing such words, lying awake in Suakin, in desert camps, under stars that burned too bright. He had never dared hope they would come. Now they had.

And he stood there, motionless, still hearing her voice even after it had ceased.

He remembered once telling Sutch that he believed he would die with her reproaches echoing in his ears—those cruel words she’d spoken long ago. But now he knew that wasn’t true. The words he would carry to the grave were these—gentle, clear, full of grace.

He had expected her to speak of her engagement. He had heard rumors, and he didn’t argue. But he sensed there was more. And there was.

She hesitated before speaking again, stretching out the moment, prolonging their time together. This was the last time she would see him. Once he left, once his shadow passed through the church door, she would never speak to him again, never look upon him, until her life ended. So she held back, drawing out each word, each pause, as if clinging to seconds that might never return.

“I’m going to be married,” she said at last. “Soon. To Colonel Durrance—someone you know.”

Feversham nodded slowly. “He’s cared for you for a long time. I didn’t realize it until I left, but I suspected. After thinking over everything, I became sure.”

“He’s blind,” Ethne said softly.

“Blind?” Feversham echoed, stunned. “Him? Of all men?”

“Yes,” she replied. “Exactly. His blindness explains everything—why I marry him, why I send you away. It happened after he lost his sight. Before Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather. Between those two events.”

She looked at him then, for the first time since taking her seat. Her eyes were clear, resolute.

“I thought carefully after you left,” she said. “I lay awake many nights, trying to sort things out. I decided no man’s life should be ruined because of me.”

“You weren’t ruined,” Feversham interrupted. “Please believe that.”

“Partly you were,” she said gently. “You wouldn’t admit it—for my sake. But it was true. And I swore no second man would suffer that fate. When Durrance went blind—when I saw what it meant to him, the loss of everything he loved—”

“Except you,” Feversham murmured.

“Except me,” she agreed. “So I promised myself I would be his. But he has grown so sharp, so perceptive. A glance, a tone, and he knows what lies beneath. Right now, he doesn’t know about the four feathers.”

Feversham frowned. “Are you sure?”

“Yes,” she said. “Why?”

“Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin when I was in Omdurman. He knew I was a prisoner. He tried to help me escape.”

Ethne blinked. “Oh. Then Durrance must have known you were in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa. He heard you went south. He was worried. He asked someone to find news—and they told him you were captured. He told me himself. And yes—he said he’d try to arrange your rescue. Probably through Sutch. He’s been in Wiesbaden with an eye doctor—only just returned. Otherwise, he would have told me. He may have sent Sutch to Suakin. But he knows nothing of the feathers. Only that our engagement ended abruptly. He thinks I’ve forgotten you entirely.”

She paused. “But if you came back—if we met, even calmly, even briefly—he would guess. He’s too quick. And he would guess right.”

Feversham watched her face. A flush rose in her cheeks. He did not move. Did not lean in. Did not speak with emotion. He kept his distance, knowing that any sign of feeling would make the farewell harder.

“Yes,” he said quietly. “And he must not guess.”

“No,” she whispered. “He must not. I’m so glad you understand, Harry. The only thing we can do is keep it simple, honest. He must never know. Because—as you said—he has nothing left but me.”

“Is Durrance here?” Feversham asked.

“At the vicarage.”

“Then I’ll go to him,” Feversham said. “I owe it to him. But I want you to know—I never expected you to wait. I never wanted you to. I had no right to expect it. When you gave me that fourth feather in the little room at Ramelton, with music drifting through the door, I understood. It was final. There would be no friendship, no lingering. No half-measures.”

He paused. “That’s why I never told you about my plan—the one that formed the moment you handed me the feathers. I might have failed. I might have died. I might have run. So I waited. If I came back, I would speak then. That was all I hoped for—just to come home, and for you to take back your feather. Not to renew anything here, but to meet again… afterwards.”

Ethne smiled. A small, quiet smile. “Then there will be no parting.”

She said it simply, without sigh or tremor. But her eyes held his, and he saw the depth of what it cost her. And suddenly, with a clarity he’d never known before, he understood—fully, completely—what it had meant six years ago when she was left alone, her heart shattered, her pride wounded. He had always carried guilt, but now he felt its full weight.

“You suffered so much,” he said, voice thick.

“Not alone,” she replied. “I didn’t spend nights in the House of Stone.”

“But it was my fault,” he insisted. “Do you remember what you said when morning came through the blinds? ‘It’s not right that one should suffer so much pain.’ It wasn’t right.”

“I forgot those words,” she said softly. “Until Colonel Trench reminded me. I shouldn’t have said them. I didn’t mean them to live in your memory. I’m sorry.”

“Oh, they were enough,” Feversham said, laughing lightly. “I used to think they’d be the last words I ever heard. But today—you’ve given me others to replace them.”

“Thank you,” she said, quietly.

There was nothing else to say. Feversham wondered why she hadn’t risen. He didn’t ask about his journey, his trials. The moment was too sacred for talk of adventure. They were deciding the shape of their lives—now and beyond.

Yet she lingered. Still seated, still composed, her heart aching with longing. Just a little longer, she thought. The sunlight was fading from the walls. She watched the golden patch shrink, inch by inch. When it vanished, she would let him go.

“I’m glad you escaped Omdurman without help,” she said. “I wanted it to be yours alone—no interference, no rescuers.”

Silence followed. She glanced toward the wall. The light was thinner now.

“You suffered terribly at Dongola,” she whispered. “Colonel Trench told me.”

“What does it matter now?” Feversham replied. “It feels far away.”

“Did you have anything of mine?” she asked.

“My white feather,” he answered.

“Anything else? Something I gave you long ago?”

“Nothing.”

“I kept your photograph,” she said. “I saved it.”

Feversham leaned forward, startled. “You did?”

She nodded. “The moment you left that night, I packed your gifts and sent them to your rooms. But I took your photo first. I kept it. I burned all your letters—after I addressed the parcel and carried it to the hall. I’d just finished when I heard your footsteps outside. But the photo was already safe. I still have it. I’ll keep it with the feathers.”

After a moment, she added, “I wish you’d had something of mine with you.”

“I had no right to anything,” he said.

The last sliver of gold light vanished. The church was filled with cool evening light—clear, but colorless.

“What will you do now?” she asked.

“Go home. See my father. How we meet will decide what comes next.”

“You’ll tell Durrance. I’d like to hear about it.”

“I’ll write.”

They walked out together, side by side. She leaned slightly toward him, their arms brushing. He mounted his horse. As his foot touched the stirrup, she pulled her dog close.

“Good-bye,” she said.

No smile now. Just her hand, offered steadily. He took it, bent low from the saddle, and held it a moment longer than needed. Her eyes met his—brimming with tears, but unwavering.

“Good-bye,” he said.

He released her hand and rode down the hill. He rode a hundred yards, then stopped. Looked back.

She stood where he left her, facing him, unmoving. No wave. No gesture. Just stillness. Then she turned and walked slowly up the village street, alone.

Feversham watched until she reached the gate. By then, she was blurred, fading into the dusk. He saw her enter, but she did not turn again.

He rode on.

The wrong he had done so long ago—though he had labored for six years to atone—could not be erased. Its consequences would live on, bringing sorrow not to himself, but to another. That she bore it with dignity, without complaint, only deepened his remorse. She deserved no pain. Not this. Not ever.

For Ethne was not one to love lightly, nor forget easily. If she loved, she gave her whole heart. And now, though she would marry, she would be alone—truly alone—until the very end.

That evening, the words that could not be believed were spoken.

Ethne sat in the parlour of her farmhouse, wrapped in silence. Though it was summer, a chill had settled over her, and she lit the fire. She stared into the glowing embers, her stillness betraying the storm within. The moment she had longed for—yet feared—had passed. Harry Feversham was gone. And now, alone in this remote village nestled among the hills, she felt more isolated than ever since Willoughby had sailed away from Salcombe on that August morning.

From the day Willoughby arrived, she had lived for one brief half-hour: the time when Harry would come to see her. That half-hour had come and vanished. Now she understood how deeply she had counted on it, how her very life had been shaped around its arrival. Without it, the world seemed hollow.

Yet she had known this emptiness would come. She had foreseen the ache that would follow his departure—the desperate urge to call him back. But she had also known she must endure it. So she sat by the fire, shivering not just from cold, but from sorrow, reminding herself that tomorrow would bring clarity. Tomorrow, she would compare today’s farewell with the night of the ball at Lennon House—and realize how much smaller this parting truly was.

She wondered what Harry would do now. Would he return to duty? Rise again in rank? Build a name for himself? As she imagined his future, Dermod’s old collie dog nudged her hand, sensing her distress. He was ancient, she thought—soon he would die, leaving her entirely alone. And then, perhaps years would pass before she lay down in her bed and knew the final moment had come.

A knock at the door. A servant announced Colonel Durrance was waiting.

“Yes,” she said softly. When he entered, she rose to meet him—not with hesitation, but with quiet resolve. She stepped out of her private grief, as she had promised herself she would. She spoke to him naturally, discussing their marriage, the rebuilding of Lennon House—anything to keep the world spinning forward. It was hard, but she had grown accustomed to bearing burdens.

Only that night did Durrance make her path heavier. After tea, he asked her to play the Musoline Overture on her violin.

"Not tonight," she replied. "I’m rather tired."

But even as the words left her lips, she changed her mind. She had vowed not to shrink from any task—great or small. The little things, especially, demanded care. They revealed the truth most clearly.

"Still," she said, smiling faintly, "I think I can play it." She took down her violin.

She played through the entire overture—from beginning to end. Durrance stood by the window, facing away, until the last note faded. Then he turned and walked beside her.

"I was rather a brute," he said quietly, "to ask you to play that tonight."

"I wasn’t anxious to," she answered, setting the instrument aside.

"I know. But I wanted to find out something. And I knew no other way."

Her face turned toward him, startled. "What do you mean?"

"You’re so rarely off your guard," he said. "Only when you play—only then do you let go. Once before, when you played this overture, you were unguarded. I remembered that. I thought—if I could get you to play it again tonight, the same piece once heard in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—I might catch you off balance."

The breath left her. Her cheeks drained of colour. He couldn’t know. It was impossible. He didn’t know.

But Durrance continued, calm and steady.
"Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

To Ethne, these were the unthinkable words. She stared at him, stunned. Had he really said them? For a moment, she thought her senses had betrayed her. But he repeated the question. There was no escape from the truth.

"Who told you about a fourth feather?" she whispered.

"Trench," he replied. "I met him at Dover. But he only told me of the fourth. I already knew of the three. Trench wouldn’t have mentioned the fourth if I hadn’t known the others. If I hadn’t asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' And I knew because Captain Willoughby came to The Pool with the first feather. That was enough."

"How do you know?" she cried, voice breaking. He stepped close and gently took her arm.

"But since I know," he said, "what does it matter how?"

He had known for months—ever since Willoughby brought the first feather. He had waited to tell her until Harry returned. And Harry had come today.

Ethne sank back into her chair. The shock was overwhelming. She had guarded her secret so fiercely, believing it untouched. To learn that it had been known all along—known for a year—was like a blow to the heart.

Yet even in confusion, she knew she needed time to gather herself. So she asked the trivial, to buy moments.

"You were in the church, then? Or did you hear us on the steps? Or did you meet him as he rode away?"

"Not one of those," Durrance said, smiling.

She had chosen the right distraction. He had vanities, like any man—especially one who had lost his sight. Since becoming blind, he had taken pride in his sharp perception. It pleased him to uncover truths others expected him to miss, to reveal them with quiet certainty.

"Not one of your guesses is right, Ethne," he said, inviting her to press further.

"So how did you find out?" she asked.

"I knew from Trench that Harry would return soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie barked at me—so I knew you were inside. But there was a saddled horse tied to the gate. Someone else was with you—someone not from the village. Then I asked you to play. And that told me who rode it."

"Yes," she murmured, barely listening. "Yes, I see."

Then, in a voice clear and composed, she said:

"You went to Wiesbaden for a year. You left just after Captain Willoughby came. Was that why?"

"I went," he said, "because neither of us could keep up the pretense any longer. You pretended not to care whether Harry lived or died. I pretended not to see that you cared more than anything. One of us would have broken eventually. I couldn’t fail. Nor could you. I couldn’t let you live a year believing two lives had been ruined for my sake—when you’d said, 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me.' And you couldn’t let me, who said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship,' know that on one side there was only friendship. We were too close to failing. So I left."

"You didn’t fail," she said quietly. "It was only I who failed."

She blamed herself bitterly. She had sworn to protect him from knowing the truth—that he was being married out of pity. She had failed. He had protected her from knowing she had failed. And that was worse.

It wasn’t shame that cut her—it was guilt. Because by failing, she had robbed him of his last chance at happiness.

"But it wasn’t your fault," he said. "You were off guard a few times—once, when you played the overture. But the real clues weren’t found that way. That night, when Willoughby brought news, your music carried joy. I felt it. But the truth came slowly, through signs, not emotions. You should be glad I found out."

"Glad?" she echoed.

"For my sake," he said. "Two lives shouldn’t be spoiled because of you. But if I hadn’t discovered the truth, not two—but three—lives would have been ruined. Yours. Harry’s. Mine."

"Three?"

"Yes. Yours. Because you would have sacrificed your own happiness to spare me. Harry’s, because he would have watched you marry a man you didn’t love. And mine—because I would have lived knowing I was loved only out of duty. A fine compensation—sitting across from you, knowing you married a cripple out of pity, and that in doing so, you crippled yourself. But now—"

"Whereas now?" she asked.

"I remain your friend," he said gently. "And I would rather be that than your unloved husband."

She said nothing. The decision had been made—for her, by him.

"You sent Harry away this afternoon," Durrance said. "You said goodbye twice."

At "twice," she looked up sharply.

"Once in the church," he explained. "Again—on your violin."

He picked up the instrument from the chair where she had placed it.

"It has been a good friend," he said. "A true friend to all of us. Soon, Ethne, you’ll understand. I stood at the window while you played. I’ve never heard anything so sad—and yet so noble. True music doesn’t complain."

He laid the violin back down.

"I’m sending a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly tonight. But he will return tomorrow."

It had been a day of unbearable emotion. As Durrance bent toward her, he saw tears tracing silent paths down her cheeks. For once, they were allowed to fall.

He took his cap and moved quietly toward the door.

"Don’t go yet," she said, rising from the hearth and stepping into the center of the room. "The oculist in Wiesbaden... did he give you hope?"

Durrance paused, weighing the truth against the lie.

"No," he said at last. "There is no hope. But I am not helpless. I can move about. Perhaps one day I’ll journey far—among strange lands, strange people."

He left the house, carrying his purpose. He had learned his lesson long ago—and the violin had taught it. That afternoon, it had spoken again. Not with the same voice, but with the same message.

True music does not complain.

In the soft light of early summer, two elderly men sat on the terrace of Broad Place, sipping tea and reading their newspapers after breakfast. The air was still and warm, thick with the scent of roses and cut grass. The older of the two turned a page slowly.

"I see Osman Digna’s back at Suakin," he remarked. "Looks like there’ll be some trouble."

"Oh?" said the other, setting down his paper. "He won’t amount to much." And yet, as he spoke, his mind slipped away from the English countryside. Before him now stood the white-washed city by the Red Sea, shimmering under a relentless sun, surrounded by dusty plains tangled with halfa grass, and beyond, the distant hills of Khor Gwob.

"A stifling place, Suakin, eh, Sutch?" General Feversham observed.

"Appallingly so," replied Lieutenant Sutch. "I heard about an officer who collapsed during morning parade—sunstroke right through his helmet. A damp, oppressive town. But I was glad to be there. Very glad," he added, with quiet conviction.

"Yes," said Feversham briskly. "Ibex, eh?"

"No," Sutch answered. "All the ibex were shot out by the garrison for miles around."

"Not even that? Then what kept you occupied?"

"That’s it," Sutch said simply. "Something to do."

And both men returned to their papers. But soon a footman arrived bearing letters. Feversham scanned his envelopes swiftly, selected one, and gave a low grunt of satisfaction. He took out his spectacles, placed them carefully on his nose, and lit a cigar with deliberate care.

"From Ramelton?" asked Sutch, laying down his paper.

"From Ramelton," Feversham confirmed. "Let me have a moment first."

He set the letter on the garden table between them, opened his case, and began preparing his cigar. He was no longer in a hurry; this was a ritual. A letter from Ramelton was not just news—it was an event, to be savored slowly, with comfort and ceremony. He settled into his chair, stretched his legs, and smoked long enough to ensure the cigar burned well before picking up the letter again.

"From him?" Sutch asked.

"No," Feversham replied. "From her."

"Ah," said Sutch, watching closely.

Feversham read slowly, then turned back to the beginning and started over. When he finished, he looked up.

"They’re pleased with the house now that it’s rebuilt," he said.

"Anything else?"

"Yes. Harry’s completed the sixth chapter of his history of the war."

"Good!" Sutch exclaimed. "He’ll do well. He has imagination, knows the ground, was there when it all happened. And he was in the bazaars—he saw the real side of things."

"True," Feversham agreed. "But you and I won’t read it, Sutch."

"No," Sutch corrected gently. "Not us. But you might. You’ve got years ahead of me."

Feversham paused, then went back to the letter. Sutch leaned forward slightly.

"Anything more?"

"Yes. They’re coming here in two weeks."

"Excellent," said Sutch. "I’ll stay."

He walked along the terrace, then returned to find Feversham sitting motionless, the letter folded on his lap, his brow furrowed deeply.

"You know," Feversham said quietly, "I never understood."

"Did you?" Sutch asked.

"Yes. I think I did."

Sutch said nothing. It was better this way—better that Feversham never grasped the full truth. For understanding would mean regret, and regret was something neither man wished to carry.

"Have you seen Durrance lately?" Feversham asked suddenly.

"Yes. He’s abroad now."

Feversham turned toward him. "He came to Broad Place when you were at Suakin. Spoke to me for half an hour. He was Harry’s best man. That too—I never understood. Did you?"

"Yes," Sutch said. "I did."

"Oh," Feversham murmured. He said no more, but as always, he tucked the mystery away, untouched, unexamined.

He didn’t return to his letters. Instead, he sat smoking, looking across the golden fields, listening to the rustle of wheat and the distant hum of farm life.

Sutch had finished his own mail. After a long silence, Feversham spoke again.

"I’ve been thinking," he said. "Have you noticed the date?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes. Next week is the anniversary of our attack on the Redan—and Harry’s birthday."

"Exactly," Feversham said. "Why shouldn’t we revive the Crimean nights?"

Sutch jumped to his feet. "Splendid! Can we gather a full table?"

"Let’s see," Feversham said, ringing the bell. The servant brought the Army List. The two old soldiers bent over it, planning their reunion.

But there was one more figure to whom the story must give its final word.

That night, after the invitations had been sent and the lights of Broad Place had gone out, a man stood on the rail of a steamer anchored at Port Said. He listened to the rhythmic chant of Arab coolies carrying coal baskets between the barges and the ship’s side. The noise of the city drifted across the water—the clatter of carts, the cries of vendors, the glow of braziers on the quays, the dark funnels of ships rising like ghosts in the twilight.

Accompanied by a servant, he had returned to the East. Early the next morning, the steamer slid into the Suez Canal. By sunset, it emerged into the cool waters of the Gulf of Suez.

Kassassin. Tel-el-Kebir. Tamai. Tamanieb. The attack on McNeil’s zareeba. One by one, the memories surged back—Durrance relived the days of action, the years of purpose, the fierce pride of service.

To the west, preparations moved forward steadily—plans that would one day bring down the Dervish Empire and crush it beneath the weight of British resolve. On the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had once vowed to take part in that great work. But the desert, which he loved, had struck him down and cast him out.

Yet now, the boat sailed southward into the Red Sea. Three more nights, and though he would not see it, the Southern Cross would rise slantwise into the sky—its ancient stars marking the end of one journey and the beginning of another.

By A. E. W. Mason

Being a record of the growth of an English gentleman during the years 1685–1687, under strange and difficult circumstances, written some time afterward in his own hand, and now edited by A. E. W. MASON.

*Philadelphia Evening Bulletin:* In spirit and color, it recalls the remarkable works of Mr. Conan Doyle. The author has captured the diction of the seventeenth century with charm, and the adventures are perilous enough to satisfy even the most heroic reader.

*Boston Courier:* An ingeniously crafted tale filled with varied and thrilling episodes. The incidents are gripping and never drawn out.

*The Outlook:* "The Courtship of Maurice Buckler" is rich in action, curiosity, and originality. While its narrative vigor may resemble "The Prisoner of Zenda," it stands apart in its authenticity and flair. The prose is excellent, the dialogue sharp, the characters vivid, and the atmosphere of the era perfectly preserved.
