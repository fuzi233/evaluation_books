CHAPTER XLI. “This, too, is probable, according to that saying of Agathon: ‘It is a part of probability that many improbable things will happen.’” —ARISTOTLE: _Poetics_.

Imagine the turmoil within a mind like Deronda’s—deeply feeling, yet relentlessly questioning—on the evening after his encounter with Mordecai. For a man less sensitive, such an experience might have stirred curiosity or surprise; but for Deronda, it had struck at the very core of his being. The intensity of the moment left him unsettled, and as was his nature, he turned inward, dissecting his emotions with the precision of reason. He felt, with unease, how much of his reaction had been shaped by Mordecai’s fervor—the strength of his belief, the fire in his voice, the certainty that seemed to radiate from every word. And this awareness sparked a quiet alarm.

Deronda had always resisted what he saw as moral complacency—the tendency to dismiss what is close at hand simply because it lacks grand historical weight. If he had read of such a meeting centuries ago—between a restless youth and a dying visionary in ancient Rome or Jerusalem—it would have seemed perfectly natural that the young man would be moved. In his imagination, the scene would unfold with all the gravity of legend: robes, torchlight, prophetic gestures. Why, then, should he feel ashamed of his own stirring, merely because he wore a white tie and dined among people who would scoff at any serious reflection on life’s deeper currents? Was not that mockery—the pretense that emotional depth is mere vanity—a symptom of spiritual poverty?

He refused to yield to such cowardice. Yet he also feared yielding to impulse without thought. He could not allow himself to be swept along by reverence alone, nor let pity for another’s struggle blind him to the path ahead. What, truly, had occurred between them?

He heard clearly the voice of Sir Hugo, if not in words, then in tone: *“A dying Jew, consumed by a fanaticism sharpened by death and resistance, has chosen you as the living image of a dream—half hope, half despair. His belief is intense, yes, but it is not unique. Fanaticism may not be as common as financial ruin, but it flourishes in many forms. While one man waits on a bridge for divine fulfillment, another insists he has cracked the universe’s code, accusing physicists of conspiracy. Another claims to hold the metaphysical key, differing only by a hair’s breadth from truth. These men are scattered across the world, each convinced they must be heard. Some write books no one wants to print. This Mordecai is more poetic than most—more passionate, more compelling—but still, he belongs to the same breed. Kindness is due, but what chance is there that his ideas carry the weight he assigns them? A man of the world knows better.”*

Deronda heard these thoughts—not as others’ opinions, but as his own inner caution. He repeated them aloud in silence. It was not the first time he had faced this question: the strange kinship among those driven by passion—prophets, visionaries, reformers, inventors of impossible machines. The same hunger animates them all. The same human drama plays out again and again. Even error has its martyrs. Reduce the greatest minds to abstract qualities, and they begin to resemble fools. Say that Copernicus stood firm against ridicule—and so does the inventor of perpetual motion. We cannot judge spirits fairly by such tests. To avoid punishing the innocent, we must understand their subject matter, and share in the burden of human striving—both near and far.

So, could he say: “Let time decide?” But time begins now. And justice begins in individual hearts—separate yet linked. Even steam engines wouldn’t exist without that connection. They were born not just in Watt’s mind, but in the shared understanding of others.

This line of thought was familiar to Deronda. It kept him from dismissing Mordecai outright—even if their exchange had carried with it a claim that felt almost supernatural. That claim—*You must believe what I believe. Feel what I feel. See what I see.*—was, in rational terms, absurd. To accept it directly would be folly; to pretend to do so would be dishonesty. And yet, in the midst of his compassion, Deronda felt relief that he had not surrendered to false promises.

But the claim rested on a fragile assumption: that he, Deronda, was of Jewish blood. How hypothetical was that! Yet since the age of thirteen, he had lived with another hypothesis—equally unproven, yet deeply felt—that Sir Hugo was his father. That belief had shaped his emotions, fueled his struggles, given meaning to his love and resentment. He had long practiced living with possibility, even when it might be disproved. He had prepared himself for revelations that might alter his sense of duty.

To live in suspense, yet emotionally engaged, was not foreign to him. And now, suppose this imagined Jewish heritage—and Mordecai’s desperate call to discipleship—were not illusion, but the prelude to truth? Suppose Mordecai’s beliefs actually took root in him? Could it be that, as Mordecai found renewal through Deronda’s presence, so Deronda might find form in Mordecai’s vision? Might he discover, in that other’s mind, the complete shape of a duty that had long lain fragmented in his own thoughts—like sculptures waiting to be joined?

The idea was dreamlike. And yet, he began to defend it. If the influence had come from a celebrated scholar, a philosopher, a revered teacher—would receptiveness be mocked? Only by those who fear dependence on others’ ideas, who prefer to imagine they’ve always held the truth in silent superiority.

And what of Mordecai’s poverty? Was that enough to dismiss his spirit? There was a tale of Emperor Domitian, who, hearing of a Jewish family descended from David, summoned them in fear—only to release them upon seeing their work-worn hands. He judged them unworthy. But the Rabbi who waited at Rome’s gate believed the Messiah would come among the poor. Both were wrong. Outer signs mean nothing. Poverty does not prove inspiration—but it has often walked beside it. To reject discipleship because of it would be dullness, not wisdom.

A stronger objection: Mordecai’s mind was fevered, his visions overpowering. Did such passion allow for sound judgment? Could someone who sees the world through dreams be trusted to assess consequences? Perhaps not. But perhaps—just perhaps—Mordecai was one of those rare souls where passion and foresight are fused. Where belief shapes reality not through delusion, but through force of will.

History shows us that great discoveries have begun this way. Science itself depends on the courage to believe before proof arrives. Passion, in human affairs, is even more potent. Enthusiasm can be proof. One soul’s fire may become tomorrow’s light.

So, Mordecai’s excitement was not, in itself, a reason to dismiss him. Suppose he had presented himself as a cold logician. Would that make him free from error? Even the driest argument can fall into fantasy—concluding too hastily that it has finally grasped the universe. Men dream in proofs. They build illusory worlds from axioms and conclusions, ending with a neat Q.E.D. No formula protects us from error. The unemotional mind may wander into mathematical dreams where nothing exists except what never did. Perhaps the emotional mind, though, sees truths ahead—truths that will one day be real, as the artist sees patterns science later explains.

At any rate, assumptions must not be trusted. We must endure our imperfect thinking—whether in whole history or in single minds. Columbus held beliefs we call superstitions, used arguments we now scorn. Yet he also had accurate physical insights and the genius to persist. The world laughed at those who ignored him. “My contempt for them,” Deronda said to himself, “binds me to avoid repeating their mistake on a smaller scale—to refuse to deafen myself to the possibility that this man and I may be connected in ways I cannot yet see.”

What mattered was not how Mordecai came to believe, but what might come of their meeting. If he were truly of Jewish descent, and if he came to embrace Mordecai’s faith—then the encounter would be transformative. But if not? Then he would be the cause of deep sorrow. He would have to deliver a refusal that might crush Mordecai’s last hope. And though tenderness could soften the blow, it could not erase the pain.

Would it be worse to disappoint him—or to deny himself the chance of something greater? Here, Deronda hesitated.

Late in life, he had begun to feel differently about his possible Jewish roots. The old reluctance had softened. And yes—he was romantic. The same youthful energy that drives countless adventurers to seek hidden origins stirred in him. The idea that he might be walking a path of discovery—of identity, purpose, belonging—excited him. Not because he sought glory, but because it touched something deeper: the longing to know oneself.

Yet he could not call it anything more than a possibility. His belief that Sir Hugo was his father remained strong. To imagine otherwise felt like betrayal—not just of memory, but of love. He loved Sir Hugo fiercely, beyond anger or doubt. To entertain the thought that this bond was a lie was painful beyond words.

Still, he repeated to himself what he had told Mordecai: *I cannot hasten the truth without clearer grounds.* And now, he found himself tempted to keep uncertainty alive—not as evasion, but as protection. If future meetings revealed only illusion, then the lack of proof might spare Mordecai the worst wound—the denial of kinship. Perhaps even, it could be used gently to encourage friendship, to offer comfort while preserving dignity.

These were the thoughts that filled Deronda’s mind during the four days before he could keep his promise to visit Mordecai at Ezra Cohen’s. Sir Hugo’s demands often stretched late into the night, making any evening journey to Holborn impossible. But the silence of those days was thick with questions—questions not just of blood, but of belief, of duty, of the strange, trembling possibility that a man might find himself not in the mirror of society, but in the eyes of a stranger who saw him as a chosen one.
