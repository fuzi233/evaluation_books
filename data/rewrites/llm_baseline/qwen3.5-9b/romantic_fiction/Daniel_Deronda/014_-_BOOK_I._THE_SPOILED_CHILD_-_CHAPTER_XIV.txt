"I will not clothe myself in wreckage—wear jewels sawn from the crushed bones of drowned women; feel the chilly, vaporous hands of angry ghosts clutching my necklace; trick my maiden bosom with the inheritance of orphans. Let your dead love marry its dead."

By the next morning, Gwendolen looked lovely and vigorous, like a tall lily freshly opened. A reaction of youthful energy surged through her; yesterday's self-distrust seemed no more than a fleeting shiver upon the surface of a full stream. The upcoming archery match at Cardell Chase was a delightful prospect in itself. She pictured herself moving beneath the beeches like a wood-nymph, surrounded by appreciative company. This imagined scene added a charming allure to Grandcourt's anticipated advances. Today, she foresaw him making slow, conversational moves toward a declaration, and she prepared to await and encourage it, following the rational conclusion she had previously laid out for her uncle.

Descending to breakfast, after everyone had departed except Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen found letters on her plate. She read one with a gathering smile, then handed it to her mother. Upon receiving it back, Mrs. Davilow also smiled, finding renewed cheer in her daughter's evident good spirits.

"You don't feel inclined to go a thousand miles away?"

"Not exactly so far."

"It was a sad omission not to have written again before now. Can't you write now, before we set out this morning?"

"It isn't so pressing. Tomorrow will suffice. You see, they are leaving town today. I must write to Dover; they'll be there until Monday."

"Shall I write for you, dear, if it bothers you?"

Gwendolen paused, sipped her coffee, and then answered brusquely, "Oh, no, let it be. I will write tomorrow." Feeling a sudden pang of guilt, she looked up and said with playful tenderness, "Dear, old, beautiful mamma!"

"Old, child, truly."

"Please don't, mamma! I meant 'old' for 'darling.' You aren't twenty-five years older than I am. When you talk like that, my life seems to shrivel up before me."

"One can find great happiness in twenty-five years, my dear."

"I must lose no time in beginning," Gwendolen said merrily. "The sooner I secure my palaces and coaches, the better."

"And a good husband who adores you, Gwen," Mrs. Davilow encouraged.

Gwendolen pouted saucily and said nothing.

A slight drawback to her joy was that the rector was detained by magistrate's business and likely wouldn't reach Cardell Chase that day. She cared little that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna chose not to go without him, but her uncle's presence would have made the execution of their decision seem inevitable. Decision itself had become formidable. Having come close to accepting Grandcourt, Gwendolen felt this unexpected abundance of options rounding itself into something too definitive. When we begin wishing greatly for ourselves, whatever we obtain soon transforms into mere limitation and exclusion. Yet, there remained the comforting notion that marriage would serve as a gateway to greater freedom.

The meeting place was a grassy spot known as Green Arbor, where a cluster of hanging branches formed a sheltering amphitheater. Here, the coachload of servants with provisions prepared the picnic meal. The warden of the Chase was tasked with guiding the roaming archers, ensuring they stayed within the prescribed distance from this center and prevented them from wandering beyond the fixed limits—a curve passing through landmarks like the Double Oak, the Whispering Stones, and the High Cross. The plan called for a brief stroll before lunch, saving the main expedition for the exquisite light of the afternoon. The muster was swift, sparing anyone dull moments of waiting. As groups scattered through the dappled light and shadow cast by neighboring beeches and rarer oaks, one might imagine a painter delighted by the scene. This form of roaming archery was far more picturesque than the stationary game, though success at variable targets was less predictable, distributing hits unevenly among the volunteers. Perhaps for this reason, combined with the dual distraction of being preoccupied and unwilling to reveal it, Gwendolen did not excel in these initial trials, unless it was for the lively grace with which she accepted her comparative failures. She wore white and green, as she had on the day she was introduced to Grandcourt—an event that marked an epoch for her. He was constantly by her side now, yet it would have been difficult to judge from mere appearance that their relationship had altered since their first conversation. Still, other signs led most observers to conclude they were either already engaged or on the verge of it. Gwendolen believed this herself.

As the groups returned toward Green Arbor in divergent clusters, chatting rather than aiming, words were exchanged that seemed the true beginning of that ending—the start of her acceptance. Grandcourt remarked, "Do you know how long it has been since I first saw you in this dress?"

"The archery meeting was on the 25th, and this is the 13th," Gwendolen laughed. "I'm not good at calculations, but I'd venture to say it's nearly three weeks."

A brief pause, then he said, "That is a great loss of time."

"That your knowing me has caused?" she retorted playfully. "Pray, don't be uncomplimentary; I dislike it."

Another pause. "It is because of the gain that I feel the loss."

Here, Gwendolen allowed a silence to stretch. She was thinking, *He is truly ingenious. He never speaks stupidly.* Her unusual quietude seemed the strongest possible affirmative answer, prompting him to continue: "The gain of knowing you makes me feel the time I lose in uncertainty. Do *you* like uncertainty?"

"I think I do, rather," Gwendolen said suddenly, beaming at him with a playful smile. "There is more in it."

Grandcourt met her laughing eyes with a slow, steady gaze that seemed like abstract vision, then asked, "Do you mean more torment for me?"

Something so strange occurred to Gwendolen in that moment that she was shaken out of her usual self-consciousness. Blushing and averting her eyes, she replied, "No, that would make me sorry."

Grandcourt might have pressed this answer, which her shift in demeanor rendered decisively favorable, but he was not so overcome as to ignore that they were now descending into Green Arbor, visible to everyone, at an ill-chosen point where the path grew inconveniently steep. This offered a literal reason for him to offer his hand; she took it, and they descended in silence, much observed by those already on the level—including Mrs. Arrowpoint, who stood with Mrs. Davilow. That lady had decided Grandcourt's merits were insufficient to induce Catherine to accept him, given Catherine's high standards, evidenced by her refusal of Lord Slogan. Thus, she regarded the tenant of Diplow with dispassionate eyes.

"Mr. Grandcourt is not equal as a man to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger—too languid. Granted, Mr. Grandcourt is younger, but I shouldn't be surprised if Sir Hugo outlives him despite the age gap. It is ill-calculating regarding successions," Mrs. Arrowpoint concluded, speaking somewhat too loudly.

"It is indeed," Mrs. Davilow agreed with quiet cheerfulness. She was so satisfied with the current state of affairs that her habitual melancholy regarding life's general unsatisfactoriness was entirely in abeyance.

I need not detail the finest food consumed in that green refectory nor dwell on the tales of forest scenery spreading beyond the hollow's edge. I am bound instead to recount a story of life at a stage when the blissful beauty of earth and sky entered consciousness only through narrow, oblique channels, while the mind was busy with a small social drama, almost as little aware of wider relations as if it were a puppet show. Understand that the food and champagne were of the best; the talk and laughter belonged to the highest society, where no one made invidious displays, and the advantages of the world were taken with the high-bred depreciation born of familiarity. Some gentlemen strolled and smoked cigars during the interval before four o'clock, the time to resume roaming. Strangely, Grandcourt was among them, unlike Mr. Lush, who seemed to enjoy the day generously by making himself particularly useful, ordering things for everyone. By this activity, he became more of a blot on the scene for Gwendolen, though he kept amiably aloof, never even looking at her openly.

When the group prepared to depart, it appeared the bows were under the charge of Lord Brackenshaw's valet. Mr. Lush sought to spare ladies the trouble of retrieving theirs from the carriage. He did not intend to fetch Gwendolen's, but fearing he might, she hurried to get it herself. The valet, seeing her approach, met her with the bow and simultaneously handed her a letter. She asked no questions, instantly recognizing the address was in a lady's delicate handwriting—the kind formerly esteemed feminine before the modern uncial style. Moving away with her bow, she saw Mr. Lush approaching to fetch others. To avoid meeting him, she turned aside, walking with her back toward the carriage stand, and opened the letter. It read:
