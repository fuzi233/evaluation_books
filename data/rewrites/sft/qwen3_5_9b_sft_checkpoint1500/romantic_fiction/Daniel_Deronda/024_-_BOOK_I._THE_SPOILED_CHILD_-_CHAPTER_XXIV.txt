"I question things but do not find
One that will answer to my mind:
And all the world appears unkind."

Gwendolen breathed a sigh of relief that the interview with Klesmer was concluded before she faced her uncle and aunt. With a resolve forged in anticipation, she had convinced herself that whatever horrors awaited her lay ahead, and she felt a grim, dogged calm settle over her, ready to weather any humiliation.

The meeting did not occur until Monday. Gwendolen accompanied her mother to the rectory, having stopped first at Sawyer's Cottage by the way. They had inspected every inch of the narrow rooms bathed in a harsh midday sun, unfiltered by blinds or curtains, for the furnishings meant to be scavenged from the rectory had not yet arrived.

"How *shall* you endure it, mamma?" Gwendolen asked as they walked away, her voice tight. She had remained silent during the tour of the bare walls, the scrubbed floors, and the tiny garden choked with cabbage stalks and a yew arbor thick with dust and cobwebs. "You and the four girls squeezed into that closet of a room, with that garish green and yellow wallpaper staring you in the eye? And without me?"

"It will be a comfort, dear, that you do not have to share that burden."

"If it weren't for the money I must earn, I'd sooner be there than go to be a governess."

"Do not set yourself against it in advance, Gwendolen. If you go to the palace, you will have every luxury surrounding you. You know how deeply you have always cherished that. You won't find it nearly as hard as climbing those steep, narrow stairs, listening to crockery clatter through the house, and overhearing the girls talking."

"It feels like a bad dream," Gwendolen exclaimed impulsively. "I cannot believe Uncle will let you go to such a place. He should have taken other steps."

"Don't be unreasonable, dear child. What else could he have done?"

"That was for him to figure out. This world seems extraordinarily strange to me if people in our position must sink like this all at once," Gwendolen said, her mind drifting to other worlds she knew, constructed with a perfect logic that arranged her future agreeably.

It was her temperament that shaped her sentences under this crushing new pressure. She could have spoken more wisely of vicissitudes affecting others, though it was never her aspiration to sound virtuous so much as clever—a flaw that must be considered when judging her words, which were often worse than her intentions. Despite the sharp sting of her own humiliation, she felt a flicker of guilt when her uncle and aunt received her with a warmth and affection they had rarely shown before. She was struck by the dignified cheerfulness with which they discussed the necessary economies in their living arrangements and the education of the boys. Mr. Gascoigne's inherent worth of character, slightly veiled by worldly distractions but shining brightly now, revealed itself under this sudden financial reduction. Prompt and methodical, he had already dismantled his carriage, scrutinized his worn suits, eliminated breakfast meat, abandoned periodicals, withdrawn Edwy from school to manage the boys' studies personally, and ordered the entire household onto the sparest footing imaginable. For healthy souls, economy holds its pleasures; the rector's spirit had permeated the house. Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who always modeled their behavior on their father, genuinely missed nothing they truly cared for, and sincerely believed the saddest part of their losses was the displacement of Mrs. Davilow and her children.

For the first time, Anna could channel her resentment regarding Rex into sympathy for Gwendolen. Mrs. Gascoigne hoped trouble might prove salutary for her niece, refusing to add any bitterness to the remedy. Both women had been busily devising ways to procure blinds and curtains for the cottage from the household stores, yet with delicate tact, they kept these practicalities in the background. Instead, they focused initially on Gwendolen's journey and the comfort it brought her mother to have her back home.

There was nothing for Gwendolen to latch onto that justified extending her discontent to the people around her. She felt shaken into a hyper-alert state, as if summoned to a drill everyone else was obediently following, when her uncle began, in a voice of firm kindness, to outline the efforts he had made to secure her a situation offering maximum advantage. Mr. Gascoigne had not forgotten Grandcourt, but the possibility of further advances from that quarter was too vague for a man of his good sense to rely upon; uncertainties of that nature could not paralyze his actions in securing immediate aid for his niece under current conditions.

"I felt there was no time to lose, Gwendolen," the rector continued. "A position in a good family where you will receive some consideration cannot be obtained at a moment's notice. However long we wait, we could hardly find one where you would be better off than at Bishop Mompert's. I am known to both him and Mrs. Mompert, which is an obvious advantage. Our correspondence has proceeded favorably, but I cannot be surprised that Mrs. Mompert wishes to see you before making an absolute engagement. She intends to arrange for you to meet her at Wanchester when she travels to town. I daresay you will find the interview somewhat trying, my dear, but you will have a little time to prepare your mind."

"Do you know *why* she wants to see me, uncle?" Gwendolen asked, her mind rapidly cycling through various reasons an imaginary Mrs. Mompert with three daughters might entertain—all reasons of a distinctly disagreeable nature for someone presenting herself for inspection.

The rector smiled gently. "Do not be alarmed, my dear. She desires a more precise impression of you than my report can convey. A mother is naturally scrupulous about choosing a companion for her daughters. I have told her you are very young. However, she exercises close supervision over her daughters' education, which makes her less anxious regarding age. She is a woman of taste and strict principle, and she objects to having a French person in the house. I am certain she will judge your manners and accomplishments as good as she is likely to find, and regarding the religious and moral tone of the education, she, and indeed the bishop himself, will preside."

Gwendolen dared not answer, but the suppression of her decided dislike for the entire prospect sent a deep, unusual flush over her face and neck, receding as swiftly as it arrived. Anna, filled with tender fears, interlaced her little hand with her cousin's, and Mr. Gascoigne, a kindly man, could not fail to perceive the trial this sudden change entailed for a girl like Gwendolen. Determined to present a cheerful view of affairs, he continued in an easy, conversational tone, as if addressing no objections, "I hold this position in such high regard that I should have been tempted to strive for it for Anna, had she been at all likely to meet Mrs. Mompert's expectations. It is truly a home, with a continuation of education in the highest sense: 'governess' is a misnomer. The bishop's views are decidedly Low Church compared to my own—he is a close friend of Lord Grampian's—but though privately strict, he is not narrow-minded in public matters. Indeed, he has aroused as little dislike in his diocese as any bishop on the bench. He has remained friendly to me, though before his promotion, when he was an incumbent of this diocese, we had a minor controversy concerning the Bible Society."

The rector's words were laden with satisfactory meaning for himself, leading him to assume they would produce similar effects in his niece's mind. "Continuation of education"—"bishop's views"—"privately strict"—"Bible Society"—these phrases sounded to him like a few harmless snakes released for the instruction of ladies who viewed all reptiles as equally equipped with poison sacs, stinging or biting merely for convenience. To Gwendolen, already recoiling from the opening prospect, such phrases resembled the intensifying heat of a burning glass—not at all the links of reflective persuasion they formed for the benevolent uncle. Desperately, she began to search for an alternative.

"There was another situation, I think, mamma mentioned?" she said, exercising determined self-mastery.

"Yes," the rector replied in a deprecating tone. "But that is in a school. I would not derive the same satisfaction from your taking that post. It would involve much harder work, as you are aware, and offer fewer benefits in other respects. Furthermore, you do not have an equal chance of obtaining it."

"Oh, dear no," said Mrs. Gascoigne. "It would be much harder for you, my dear—and much less appropriate. You might not even have a bedroom to yourself." Gwendolen's memories of school life conjured other particularities that forced her to admit internally that this alternative offered no relief. Turning back to her uncle, she feigned acceptance of his ideas, "When is Mrs. Mompert likely to send for me?"

"That is somewhat uncertain, but she has promised not to entertain any other proposal until she has seen you. She has entered deeply into your position. It will likely be within the next fortnight. But I must depart now. I am going to lease part of my glebe."

The rector ended with great cheerfulness, leaving the room with the satisfying conviction that Gwendolen was adapting herself to circumstances like a sensible girl.
