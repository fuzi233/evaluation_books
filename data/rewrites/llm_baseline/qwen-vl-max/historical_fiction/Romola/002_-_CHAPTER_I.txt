To Dante’s ears, these same streets had once echoed with the clash of swords and shouts of rival families locked in deadly feud. But in the fifteenth century, their noise was less about blood and more about woolcarders from San Martino and Garbo, trading insults as freely as they traded cloth.

On the morning of April 9th, 1492, two men stood facing each other beneath the loggia: one bent slightly forward, peering down with sharp curiosity; the other sprawled on the pavement, blinking up like a sleeper startled from dreams.

The standing man spoke first. He was broad-shouldered and gray-haired, shaped by labor and hardened by time—a man who, in Tuscan terms, had been "molded with the fist and polished with the pickaxe." Yet his face bore lines of self-importance, as though he meant to compensate for any roughness in his appearance. At his feet lay a large leather sack, bulging with goods, and beside it hung a peddler’s basket filled with small wares—thread, pins, shards of glass likely exchanged for such things.

“Young man,” he said, pointing to a ring on the reclining figure’s finger, “when your chin has grown a stiffer beard, you’ll know better than to sleep in street corners with a jewel like that on your hand. By the holy gospels! If it had been anyone but me watching over you just now—but Bratti Ferravecchi is no thief. A cat won’t eat her mouse unless she catches it alive, and Bratti won’t taste gain unless it comes with a fair bargain. Three years ago, on Saint John’s Day, the saint sent me a dead body—a blind beggar, his cap lined with coins. But I turned my stomach against the money, until I remembered that Saint John owed me those pieces for my yearly offering at the festival. Then I buried the man and paid for a mass. That made it a fair trade. But what brings a young fellow like you, with the face of Messer San Michele, sleeping on stone with only the wind for a curtain?”

The man’s voice was deep and guttural, hard for the newly awakened listener to follow. But he understood the gesture toward the ring. He glanced down, then without thought pulled it off and slipped it into his doublet, rising as he did so and stretching his arms.

“Your tunic and hose don’t match that jewel,” Bratti observed slowly. “Anyone might say the saints have sent *you* a dead body. But if you took the jewels, I hope you buried him—and you can afford a few masses in his name.”

A sudden tension passed through the younger man. His careless stretch froze. For a moment, he frowned sharply at Bratti. Then, as quickly, he regained composure. He removed the red Levantine cap hanging low over his left ear, pushed back his long dark curls, and looked down at his clothes, smiling.

“You speak truth, friend,” he said. “My garments are stained like an old sail—not because they’re ancient, but because they’ve seen both sea and rain. The fact is, I’m a stranger in Florence. Last night, footsore and weary, I chose this porch over searching for lodgings that might be worse than a den of wolves.”

“A stranger, indeed,” Bratti replied. “Your words flow sweetly, so smooth that even a Florentine Christian can’t tell a hook from a hanger. Not from Genoa? More likely Venice, by the cut of your clothes.”

“At this moment,” the stranger answered, still smiling, “it matters less where I came from than where I can go for breakfast. This city gives me a cold look. Can you show me a livelier quarter where I might find food and shelter?”

“That I can,” Bratti said. “And it’s your luck that I happened to walk in from Rovezzano this morning, stopping at Mercato Vecchio to say an Ave at the Badia. That’s your good fortune. But let’s see what profit I make from it. Nothing for nothing, young man. If I show you the way to Mercato Vecchio, you’ll swear by your patron saint to let me have the first bid on your worn suit when you buy something better—as you surely will.”

“Agreed, by San Niccolò,” the stranger laughed. “But let us go now. I feel the need of something warmer inside this doublet you seem so eager to claim.”

“Claim? Not at all,” Bratti said, slinging his bag over his shoulder and setting off. But he broke off mid-sentence, letting out a loud, grating cry like the squeal of a cartwheel:

(Who wants to exchange rags, broken glass, or old iron?)

“It’s not much,” he added, returning to normal tone. “Your clothes are worth little. Still, if you want a lute worth more than any new one, or a sword once carried by a Ridolfi, or a paternoster of the finest sort, I could give you a great deal—if you let me take your clothes as part payment. I may stand here simply, but I run the best shop in Ferravecchi, right near the Mercato. Praise be to the Virgin—I carry no pumpkin on my back. And I don’t sit cooped up all day. I’ve a wife and a raven to watch the stock. _Abbaratta, baratta, chi abbaratta?_”

“And now,” he continued, “where do you come from, and what brings you to Florence?”

“I thought you liked nothing unless it came with a price,” the stranger said. “You’ve offered me nothing yet in return for that information.”

“Well,” Bratti replied, “a Florentine knows how to pay fair value for news—even if it doesn’t fill his pockets. If I take you to the prettiest girl in the Mercato for a cup of milk—that’ll be a fair trade.”

“No,” the stranger said. “I can find her myself, if she truly is there. Pretty faces often peek from doors and windows. Besides, a sharp trader like you should know that someone bidding for nuts and news might find them hollow.”

“Ah!” Bratti said, glancing sideways with a hint of admiration. “You weren’t born on a Sunday—the salt-shops were open when you came into the world. You’re not a Hebrew, then? From Spain or Naples? Let me tell you—those Frati Minori are trying to heat Florence as hot as Spain for those dogs of hell who want to hoard all the profits of usury for themselves, leaving none for Christians. And if you walk Calimara with a yellow cloth in your cap, it will ruin your beauty more than a sword-cut across that smooth olive cheek of yours. _Abbaratta, baratta, chi abbaratta?_ I tell you, grey cloth fights yellow cloth. There’s enough grey cloth in Florence to make a gown and cowl for the Duomo. But not enough yellow to make hose for Saint Christopher—blessed be his name, and send me a sight of him today! _Abbaratta, baratta, b’ratta?_”

“All very amusing,” the stranger said, somewhat scornfully. “But it concerns me not. I am no Hebrew.”

“See?” Bratti said triumphantly. “I’ve made a bargain with mere words. I’ve made you tell me something, though you’re as slippery as a lamprey. San Giovanni be praised! A blind Florentine beats two one-eyed men. But here we are—at the Mercato.”

They stepped from the narrow alleys into the wide piazza known to older writers as Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. Though it had served as a marketplace since ancient times—perhaps even the spot where the ancestors of the Florentines descended from their high hills to trade with valley farmers—it had never been shunned by wealth. In the early decades of the fifteenth century, the Medici and other powerful *popolani grassi*, the commercial nobility, had homes here. They didn’t mind the roar of dialects or the butcher stalls, which the poet Antonio Pucci once called a chief glory—*dignita*—of a market that, in his view, surpassed all others. But now, Lent was not yet over. The proud guild of butchers was quiet. Instead, the season belonged to market gardeners, cheesemongers, sellers of macaroni, corn, eggs, milk, and dried fruits. Women’s voices dominated the chatter. Yet every season brought its own sounds: pots clanging, money changers clicking coins, vendors shouting bargains, dicers challenging passersby, linen and wool being vaunted, kettles and pans displayed. Mules and carts choked the narrow entries, and insults flew—much like those used by modern women under similar provocation.

Ladies and gentlemen visiting the market witnessed more amateur fighting than they’d see today, and far more ragged poverty and disorder than modern households could imagine. As the day advanced, the gaming houses revealed their grim drama: trembling hopes, despairing groans, curses, blows.

Yet there were brighter sights too: brood rabbits, innocent as ever; doves and singing birds for children’s gifts; kittens for sale, even handsome *gattuccios* famed for catching mice. And above all, fresh young faces—soft cheeks and bright eyes, glowing from the early morning journey from distant castelli—not to mention older ones, warm with honest kindness, always present in scenes of human toil.

At the center of the square, atop a venerable column taken from San Giovanni’s church, stood Donatello’s statue of Plenty. Nearby, a fountain bubbled where, according to Pucci, good wives washed their utensils—and their throats—not from lack of wine, but to save money for their husbands.

But on this morning, something had changed. The stalls were set up, buyers gathered, ready for the best vegetables and butter. Yet something else held everyone’s attention. Traders turned from their wares, joining clusters of talkers scattered across the square. A secondhand cloth seller, dangling hose, accidentally looped them around his neck in haste to join a group. A cheesemonger, knife in one hand, cheese in the other, scribbled notes on his prize piece of *marzolino*. Elderly women, baskets askew, wailed in unison.

The Florentine boys, ever present and especially mischievous—like sour crabs—saw their chance. Some darted for nuts and figs; others for cooked treats. Dogs, trained to enjoy Lenten fare, sniffed and vanished beneath stalls. Mules, tangled in baskets, stretched their muzzles toward aromatic greens.

“Diavolo!” Bratti exclaimed as they entered unnoticed. “The Mercato has gone mad—as if the Holy Father had excommunicated us again. I must know what’s happening. But don’t worry: it seems a thousand years before you see Tessa and get your milk. But hold fast to me—I’ll keep my end of the bargain. Remember: I get the first bid on your clothes, especially the hose. Stained as they are, they’re the finest *panno di garbo*—ruined, yes, but still good.”

“Ola, Monna Trecca!” Bratti shouted, turning to an old woman near the nearest group. “Your mules are trampling your parsley—has the world ended?”

“Monna Trecca” (Dame Greengrocer) spun around, half-furious, half-dazed—first at Bratti, then at her ruined goods, then back at him.

“May you have a bad Easter and a bad year—and die by the sword!” she shrieked, lunging for her stall. But Bratti ignored the curse, slipping past her to hear the story gripping her. He signaled the stranger to stay close.

“I saw it myself,” said a fat man clutching leeks. “I was at Santa Maria Novella. I saw it. The woman jumped up, threw her arms out, screamed—she saw a great bull with fiery horns charging the church to crush it. I saw it!”

“Saw what, Goro?” asked a slim man with a razor at his belt, wearing a jerkin and skullcap tilted carelessly. “The bull—or just the woman?”

“The woman, of course,” Goro replied, annoyed. “But it’s all the same—*mi pare*. Doesn’t change the meaning—*va!*”

“Meaning?” several voices chimed in. Then chaos erupted: “Lights flashing over San Lorenzo for three nights!” “Thunder in clear starlight!” “Duomo’s lantern struck by Saint Michael’s sword!” “Medici *palle*—all smashed!” “Lions tearing each other apart!” “Ah! They might well!” “*Boto* fell in Santissima Nunziata!” “Died like a true Christian!” “God will forgive him!”

Only Bratti, the barber, and a thin-lipped man with spectacles and a pen-case remained silent—Bratti piecing together the fragments, the barber listening, the scholar waiting.

“*Ebbene*, Nello,” Bratti said, sidling near the barber. “So the Magnifico is dead—may his soul rest. And the price of wax will rise?”

“Exactly,” Nello replied, then added gravely, swiftly: “And his waxen image in the Nunziata fell at the same moment—though perhaps later, whenever the Frati Serviti choose. Several cows and women have borne stillborn calves this Lent. And since Carnival, no one’s counted the rotten eggs. Ah! A great man—a great politician—a greater poet than Dante. Yet the dome didn’t fall—only the lantern. *Che miracolo!*”

A sharp, prolonged “Pst!” sliced through the din. It came from the pale man in spectacles. Instantly, silence fell. All eyes turned to him.

“You Florentines are blind,” he began, voice sharp and clear. “It seems you need only hay to become cattle. Do you think Lorenzo’s death is God’s scourge for Florence? Go! You’re sparrows praising a dead hawk. What! A man who tried to slip a noose around every neck in the Republic, tightening it at his pleasure? You love that—you like magistrates chosen in secret, citizens denied rights unless they’re Medicean. That’s what *netto di specchio* means now—not paying your dues, but turning a blind eye to robbery—of public funds, of daughters’ dowries. Playing chamberlain one day, philosopher the next—listening to bawdy songs at Carnival and crying ‘Bellissimi!’—then sacred hymns and crying ‘Bellissimi!’ again. That’s what you love. You rage over your *quattrini bianchi*, but ignore the hole in the treasury draining gold into Lorenzo’s pockets. You pay for footmen to march before a citizen, so he may seem noble and kind. ‘Look,’ you say, ‘what a tall Pisan we keep, flashing his sword before him!—and yet Lorenzo smiles. How generous!’ And you think his death is God’s warning—portents everywhere. But I tell you: there’s another scourge in the air.”

“Nay, Ser Cioni,” Nello said. “Stay politics, not prophecy. Politics is the better horse.”

“But if you speak of portents,” another man said, “what greater sign than a pious notary? Balaam’s ass was nothing.”

“A notary without work, inkwell dry,” a third added. “Better wear a cowl, Ser Cioni. Everyone will believe your fasting.”

The notary turned and left with a look of contempt, revealing a short, sallow man behind him—bent shoulders suggesting a life of deskwork.

“By San Giovanni,” said Goro, suddenly uncertain, “perhaps Ser Cioni speaks truth. I’ve reason to complain about those *quattrini bianchi* myself. Grumble? Suffocation! We do grumble. And if anyone says the word, I’ll join the crowd—sooner than let our money be tampered with as if magistrates were necromancers. And Lorenzo could have stopped such things—if he’d wanted. As for the bull with flaming horns—yes, Ser Cioni may be right: it could mean many things. Perhaps more than taxes. When God sends a sign, it’s not one meaning. It’s many.”

“Spoken like an oracle, Goro!” Nello said. “When we mortals pack two meanings into one sentence, it’s blasphemous not to believe the bull means everything anyone in Florence wants it to.”

“You scoff, Nello,” said the sallow man, now visible. “But it’s true: every revelation—vision, dream, portent, scripture—has many meanings. Only the enlightened can unfold them.”

“Assuredly,” Nello replied. “I’ve heard the Frate in San Lorenzo. But I’ve also heard Fra Menico in the Duomo. According to him, your Fra Girolamo runs after the wind of Mongibello, and those who follow him are like swine rushing into the sea—or worse. With San Domenico roaring *è vero* in one ear, and San Francisco screaming *è falso* in the other—what is a poor barber to do? Unless he’s enlightened? But clearly, Goro is beginning to see—he already thinks the bull means first himself, then all aggrieved taxpayers, determined to gore the magistracy at the first chance.”

“Goro is a fool!” a deep bass voice thundered, dropping like a bell amid the chime. “Let him take his leeks home and shake his flanks over his wool-beating. He’ll mend matters more that way than by parading his barrel-shaped body in the square, as if everyone could measure his grievances by the size of his belly. The burdens that harm him most are his heavy flesh and his idleness.”
