I woke the next morning as if a heavy, damp shroud had been peeled from my bones. No more the fog of utter weariness that had dragged me under the night before – my mind was sharp, my limbs light, and courage hummed low and steady in my chest like a well-tended hearth fire. Just as I finished lacing my scuffed boots, there came a rough, sharp tap at the door. "Come in," I called, expecting the soft-spoken chambermaid. Instead, a burly, weathered man in a linen-smudged uniform trundled over my threshold, his accent thick as porridge. "Gif me your keys, Meess." "Why, sir?" I asked, confused. "Gif!" he snapped, half-snatching the key from my loose grip. "All right! Haf your tronc soon." Turned out he was from the custom house, come to clear my poor little portmanteau – no harm done, for all his gruffness.

I wandered down the staircase slow, noticing for the first time, when not half-dead with fatigue, that my tiny, closet-like chamber with its meager chipped basin was tucked away in the garret of a grand, sprawling hotel. The steps were uncarpeted, cool white marble streaked with grey veins, the walls hung with faded gilded murals, the ceiling so high the chandeliers dangled far overhead like distant stars. I paused on every step, musing half-amused, that the hotel staff had sussed out at a single glance the exact measure of my empty purse and my negligible social standing – no grand suites, no plush parlours for the unknown drifting English governess, of course. It was a strange little quirk of fate, but I refused to let it sink my new-found lightness of spirit.

When I finally reached the vast skylight-glowing great hall, I drifted to what I learned was the coffee room. My hands shook a little as I pushed the door open – every other patron there was a man, no woman in sight, and I felt small, solitary, almost ridiculous for daring to sit alone at a tiny table by the window. But none of them stared too long; the few that glanced my way murmured the word "Anglaise" to their companions, and turned back to their newspapers. I ate my roll and drank my bitter coffee in a daze, wondering where on earth I ought to go next.

A soft little voice nudged at the back of my head, pulling up a throwaway line Miss Fanshawe had tossed off in her hurry to run off with her friends the day I left: "Do go to Madame Beck’s in Villette! She’s got a passel of little marmots she needs someone to look after, she was advertising for an English gouvernante two months back." I knew nothing of Madame Beck, not her address, not even what she looked like, but it was the only thread I had to cling to, drifting as I was in that strange foreign city. Forty miles away, they said Villette was. But what did I have to lose? Nothing at all – the game I was playing could not make me worse off than I already was, and every step forward held the chance of winning something better. I booked a seat on the slow, lumbering diligence right then.

People might call me reckless, to chase a shadow of a job across forty miles of foreign countryside on no more than a half-remembered comment, but I drank in every minute of that ride, even when the rain lashed the windows and the grey sky hung low and heavy. The road was flat, lined with slimy green canals that coiled like half-asleep snakes, bordered by straight rows of pollard willows. My heart felt light, giddy almost, as if it was basking in secret sunshine, even while a quiet, niggling anxiety lurked at the edge of my thoughts, like a tiger crouched in the underbrush, waiting for dark.

We moved far too slow, delayed by long, unnecessary stops, thick fog, and fine, stinging drizzle. By the time we trundled through the garrisoned city gate, lamplight glowing through the mist, night had fallen thick and soft, you could almost touch it. The diligence rattled over rough flinty cobblestones and pulled up at the central bureau, all the passengers scrambling off to grab their luggage. I stood back, waiting for my little portmanteau, the one I'd tied a scrap of bright green ribbon to the handle of so I could spot it at once. One by one every bag, every tin case, every cloak and umbrella and hat box was hauled down from the roof. My portmanteau was not there. Not a wisp of green ribbon anywhere. All my clothes, my little pocket-book holding the last of my savings – gone. I could not speak a word of conversational French, I could not even ask where it had gone. I touched the conductor's arm, gestured wildly, but he misunderstood, and started hauling a stranger's trunk back towards the diligence roof.

A clear, pleasant voice cut in, first in English, then in sharp, rapid French. I nearly wept with joy to hear my own native tongue. "Sir," I turned to him at once, no care for how desperate I must look, "I cannot speak French, would you please ask that man what he did with my trunk?" He was young, tall, his face open and kind, noble without being arrogant, chivalric without being pretentious, the sort of man who could have been a prince for all his natural grace. He quizzed the conductor for me, then turned back to explain my trunk had been left behind at the last stop, Boue-Marine, it would be forwarded to the bureau the day after next. My heart sank, but then he asked if I had friends in Villette, if all my money was in the trunk. Thank goodness I'd tucked twenty francs in my pocket, safe and sound. He gave me a scrap of paper torn from his pocket book, scribbled the address of a quiet, cheap inn that spoke English, and told me the park shortcut was too dark and dangerous for a woman alone. He walked with me through the drizzle, down the deserted boulevard, through the black fog-shrouded park, his step steady ahead of me, and I never felt a flicker of fear – I would have followed that tread to the ends of the earth, I think. When we reached the far side of the park, he pointed me towards the street that led to the inn, wished me good night, and vanished back into the dark. That memory of his bright, kind eyes stayed with me like a warm cordial long after he was gone.

I hurried on, but two rough, sneering men with cigars hanging out of their mouths followed me, jeering, till I met a patrol of guards and they slunk off. By then I was lost, turned around completely, I could not find the steps the gentleman had described. I wandered breathless, my heart thudding, till I spotted a warm lamplight over the door of a tall, solid house. It was no inn. A brass plaque was nailed to the great door: *Pensionnat de Demoiselles*. Madame Beck.

I nearly staggered. That was the name Miss Fanshawe had mentioned. Before I could think, before I could hesitate, I rang the bell. The maid who answered thought I was the English teacher Madame had been expecting, so she let me right in, no questions, and sat me in the cold gilded salon where the porcelain stove was unlit, the pendule on the mantel striking nine. Fifteen minutes later, a plump, motherly little woman in a large shawl and neat nightcap crept up beside me – that was Madame Beck. She could barely speak two words of English, I could barely speak a scrap of useful French, so she fetched a teacher who'd been educated in an Irish convent, who butchered the English language but could make herself understood. I told her my plain tale: I was an Englishwoman looking for honest work, I could look after children, do lady's maid work, even light housework, I had no references, no luggage yet, but I would work hard to earn my keep.

Madame Beck studied me, no trace of pity or softness on her sharp, practical face. She was about to send me away and tell me to come back the next day, when a quick footstep sounded in the vestibule. That was Monsieur Paul, her cousin, the gruff little man in spectacles who came to give readings to the older students. She called him in, asked him to read my face, to tell her if I could be trusted. He stared at me, brow furrowed behind his glasses, squinting hard like he was trying to see right through my skin. "Engage her," he pronounced at last. "If she is good, it will be good for all. If not – well, it will still be a good work, to help a stranger in need."

And that was how, by the grace of some kind fate, I was hired that very night. I did not have to step back out into the cold, dark, hostile streets of Villette. I was where I was meant to be, at last.
