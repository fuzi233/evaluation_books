Madame Beck called for me on a drowsy Thursday afternoon, the pensionnat’s workroom humming with the soft snick of embroidery scissors and the girls’ quiet chatter, to ask if I was free to run small shopping errands for her across town.

I had no tasks to hold me, so I placed myself at her disposal at once. She pressed a list into my hand, scrawled in her neat, firm script: wools matched to the girls’ half-finished slippers, silks for new bell-pulls, skeins of glinting embroidery thread for the fancy purses they were stitching for fête day. I kitted myself out for the heavy, brooding clouds hanging low over the city, and had just flipped the spring latch on the front door to step out when Madame’s voice called me back to the dining room.

“Pardon, Miss Lucy!” she cried, feigning the bright surprise of a thought that had just popped unbidden into her head. “I have just remembered one more small errand for you, I hope you won’t think it too much of a favour to grant?”

Of course I assured her I was happy to help, and she darted into the little parlour to fetch a wicker basket lined with glossy dark green hothouse leaves, piled high with blushing, perfect summer fruit that looked far too fine to be carried through the muddy streets.

“There,” she said, pressing it into my hands. “It’s light, and won’t look like a servant’s chore that might rumple your neat dress. Do me the kindness of leaving this at the house of Madame Walravens, with my warm felicitations on her fête day. She lives in the old Basse-Ville, Number 3 Rue des Mages. The walk is a fair distance, but you have the whole afternoon ahead of you—no rush. If you miss dinner, I’ll have a plate saved, or Goton, who’s always so fond of you, will whip up a little something special just for you. You won’t be forgotten, my good girl. Oh, and one more thing!” she called again as I turned to go. “Make sure you insist on seeing Madame Walravens in person, hand the basket straight to her. She’s very particular about such things, I wouldn’t want any mishap. Goodbye, see you soon!”

I finally set off. The shopping errands ate up a good hour, picking through spools of silk and hanks of wool to match the exact hues Madame Beck wanted, selecting tassels and slides for the girls’ purse projects, until the whole tedious list was done, nothing left but the fruit basket and my fête day greeting.

I found myself looking forward to the long walk deep into the old, shadowed lower town. The evening sky over Villette was curdling into a thick, black-blue mass of storm cloud, its edges glowing with smouldering, heavy red. I never minded a storm’s rain, not the way I minded the sharp, effortful bluster of high wind: rain only asks for quiet resignation, to let your coat and your hair grow damp, and it sweeps the streets clean of all the noisy, crowded bustle, turns the grand boulevards empty and still as an enchanted dead city. Let the rain fall, I thought, so long as I deliver this basket first.

A distant tower clock tolled the quarter past five when I reached the address Madame Beck had given me. It was no proper street at all, but a quiet, forgotten square, grass pushing up between the wide grey flagstones, the tall old houses leaning back behind high walls where you could see the dark tops of old garden trees peeking over the roofline. Antiquity hung thick over the whole place, no shops, no carriages, no sign of the modern bustling Villette I knew. Rich families had lived here once, grandeur had made its home here, but wealth had long since flown, leaving these old hollow nests to fade. The dark, half-ruined turrets of the old Church of the Magi loomed over the square.

Large, fat raindrops, each the size of a five-franc piece, were beginning to spot the pavement when I crossed the empty square, and the only living soul I saw was a frail old priest, hobbling past with a staff, bent double with age. He had just stepped out of the very house I was looking for. I paused at the door he’d closed behind him and rang the bell, and he turned to stare at me, his gaze lingering—no doubt a young woman with a basket of fancy fruit looked very out of place in this quiet, decaying corner.

A very ancient woman in a tattered antique peasant dress answered the door, her cap draped with long ragged lace, her wooden sabots shaped like tiny little boats. Her face was sour, cantankerous, the grumpiest expression I had ever seen on a human being, and she nearly snatched the fruit basket straight out of my hand before the old priest stepped forward to stop her. He seemed half-deaf at first, so it took some effort to make him understand I had strict orders to hand the basket directly to Madame Walravens herself, no proxy. At last he convinced the grumpy old servant to let me cross the threshold, and escorted me up the creaking stairs to a dim, drafty old salon, where he left me to wait.

The room was huge, with a beautiful carved old ceiling and stained glass windows that looked more fit for a church than a private home, and in the gathering storm gloom it felt almost menacing. A second smaller room opened off it, its single casement blind pulled all the way down, so I could barely make out the shapes of the furniture. I amused myself squinting to pick out details, and my eye caught the outline of a large portrait on the far wall.

Then, to my utter shock, the portrait moved. It shook, sank, rolled back, vanishing completely to reveal an arched opening leading to a cold stone passage, a winding dungeon stair running down into the dark. I heard a tap, tap of a stick on the stone steps, a shadow crept up, and then the figure itself appeared.

She was barely three feet tall, no proper shape to her hunched little body, her skinny hands folded over the gilded knob of an ivory walking stick. Her great broad face sat almost on top of her chest, no neck to speak of, and her features looked a hundred years old, her eyes deep, malign, heavy under thick grey brows and livid swollen lids. She was dressed in a full bright blue brocade gown patterned with satin leaves, the colour as rich as gentian flowers, a massive costly shawl draped over her small frame, its fringed hem trailing on the floor. She dripped with jewels: great long drop earrings blazing with real, unmistakeable fire, rings on every skeletal finger set with thick gold hoops and stones of purple, emerald green, and deep blood red.

“Que me voulez-vous?” she croaked, her voice rough and deep as an old man’s, a thin silver beard bristling on her chin.

I held out the basket, recited Madame Beck’s greeting.

“Is that all?” she snapped.

“That is all,” I said.

“Truly, it was hardly worth the walk,” she sniffed. “Go back to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy my own fruit when I want it, and her little felicitations? I don’t care a fig for them.” She turned her back on me, sharp and rude.

The very second she turned, a great peal of thunder cracked overhead, a flash of lightning blazed so bright it lit the whole salon like midday. The old tales of enchantment felt suddenly very real: I was the wanderer lured into the evil fairy’s castle, the storm outside waking at the spell’s call. What strange sort of friend did Madame Beck keep, I wondered, offering gifts and greetings to this uncanny, ill-tempered little creature everyone called Malevola, the evil fairy?

The rain was pouring down in torrents now, the sky so dark the clouds looked pale with terror, streaked by forked lightning so bright it seared your eyes, thunder crashing so close the walls shook. For all my earlier bravado about not minding rain, I had no desire to step out into that downpour. I sat down on the cold stone seat on the landing to wait, and the old priest glided down the gallery above me.

“Mademoiselle, you cannot sit there,” he said gently. “Our benefactor would be most displeased to know a guest in his house was treated so poorly.” He insisted so earnestly that I come back inside out of the draft that I could not refuse to be rude. He led me into the smaller, more private room, pulled the blind up half way, and I saw it was less a parlour than a little oratory, solemn, full of relics and keepsakes.

The good father sat down beside me, pulled out a prayer book, and began whispering his litanies, his bald head gilded every few seconds by the yellow lightning flashes, his body deep in purple shadow, still as a statue. I realized then who he was: Père Silas, the same priest I had knelt to confess to in the Béguinage, the voice, the faint profile I remembered from that dim church service, all matching.

As I stared at him, I noticed the portrait that had earlier vanished to make the secret door: it was no Madonna, as I’d first thought, but a portrait of a young woman in a nun’s habit. Her face was soft, pale, sad, the shadow of deep grief hanging over her, and I found I could not look away.

Without lifting his eyes from his book, the old priest spoke in a slow, clear voice:
“She was much beloved.
“She gave herself to God.
“She died young.
“She is still remembered, still wept.”

“By Madame Walravens?” I asked, thinking that endless decades of grief might explain the old woman’s terrible temper.

He shook his head, a faint sad smile on his face. “No, no. A grandmother’s grief for her grandchild is great, but only a betrothed lover, whom fate, faith, and death have all three robbed of their happiness, can mourn as deeply as Justine Marie is mourned.”

He went on to tell me her story, as the storm died down outside. The man who loved her had once been a wealthy young banker’s son, engaged to the beautiful, gentle daughter of a rich jeweller. Then his father’s business had collapsed, he had been left penniless, and Justine Marie’s family, led by her vicious hunchback grandmother Madame Walravens, had forbidden the match. Too soft to run away with him, too good to marry another man for money, Justine Marie had entered a convent, and died during her noviciate.

Years later, Justine Marie’s own father had been ruined in a financial scandal, died of shame, leaving his wife and his terrible old mother Madame Walravens completely destitute. The man she had once cruelly forbidden her granddaughter to marry, the young man whose whole chance at domestic happiness she had destroyed, had come to their rescue. He housed them, cared for them, spent three quarters of his tiny income on supporting them, the old priest his former tutor, and a superannuated old family servant, keeping only enough for himself to buy bread and the most modest two-room lodging. He had never married, given his whole life over to piety and caring for the people who had ruined him.

When he named that man as Monsieur Paul Emanuel, my literature professor, my breath caught. All the little moments I had thought him petty, ungenerous, short-tempered, melted away at once, revealing the great, quiet, unshakable heart hidden under his gruff exterior. Père Silas, it turned out, had never lost track of me after that confession in the Béguinage, and this whole errand, Madame Beck’s sudden little request, my meeting Madame Walravens, this whole meeting, had been gently orchestrated by his careful Jesuit hand.

When the storm cleared, the sun breaking out pale and waterish in the west, I thanked the priest for his hospitality and stepped out to walk home, pondering all I had learned. The gruff, sharp little professor I had clashed with so many times was far greater, far kinder, than I had ever dreamed. And I knew now there was far more to this strange little errand than Madame Beck had ever told me.
