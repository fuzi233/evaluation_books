Neville Landless had risen with the first grey light, his boots kicking up dust as he pushed forward through the mist. By the time the ancient bells of Cloisterham tolled for morning service, marking the start of the day, he was already eight miles down the road. With nothing but a stale crust of bread to sustain him, he knew he required sustenance soon. He pulled his coat tight against the chill and sought refuge at the nearest roadside inn, "The Tilted Wagon."

The tavern was a sprawling, untidy affair perched atop a hill, a place where the earth outside seemed permanently mired in the churnings of horse hooves and stray straw. Inside, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of damp woodsmoke and unwashed linens. A scolding landlady, her face flushed with irritation, slapped a baby wrapped unevenly in socks—one red, one missing—in the corner of the bar. Elsewhere, cheese sat abandoned on a shelf beside a mold-stained cloth and a green-handled knife resting in a makeshift iron canoe, while pale loaves of bread appeared to weep crumbs into similar vessels nearby. It was a chaotic scene, lacking the polish promised by its sign, yet Neville, weary and hungry, accepted whatever comfort it offered. He sat in a sand-scattered parlor, gazing at the dying embers of the hearth, wondering how long it would take for the wet fuel to finally spark warmth for someone else.

Feeling restored, Neville stepped out, pausing at roughly a quarter-mile from the inn to decide his path. The main road stretched straight ahead, but parallel to it lay a cart track squeezed between two towering hedgerows, cutting across a windswept heath before presumably rejoining the highway. Choosing the latter, he set off, his steps heavy against the steep incline and the deep, rutted soil.

As he labored upward, he sensed movement behind him. Turning slightly, he saw four figures approaching at a brisk pace, while another four slowed to a loitering stop, clearly intending to follow. Then, abruptly, the remaining members of the party—a dozen souls in total—turned and fled back at a frantic gallop.

Neville froze, exchanging a sharp glance with the four advancing and the four lingering. They returned his gaze, unblinking. Resuming his trek, he watched the four ahead constantly checking over their shoulders, while the four behind steadily closed the distance. When the group emerged onto the open slope of the heath, maintaining this formation, Neville realized with a jolt that he was surrounded. He halted, testing them. They halted instantly.

"Why do you attend upon me in this way?" Neville called out, his voice ringing in the thin air. "Are you a pack of thieves?"

"Don't answer him!" a voice boomed from the crowd. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, his anger flaring. "Who said so?"

Silence answered.

"It's sound advice, whoever the coward was," Neville growled, pacing back and forth. "I refuse to be penned in between four men yonder and four men yonder. I intend to pass the four in front, and I shall!"

They stood immovable, including Neville, trapped in a circle of eight.

"If eight men, or four, or two set upon one," Neville shouted, his face flushing red, "the victim has no choice but to fight back! And by the Lord, I will, if I am harassed further!"

Clutching his heavy walking stick, he surged forward, aiming to dash past the leading pair. Instantly, the largest and strongest man among them pivoted, intercepting him with practiced speed. Before Neville could fully commit his blow, the stranger brought him crashing to the grass. A heavy strike landed, knocking Neville flat.

"Let him be!" the man commanded, his voice low and urgent as they rolled together in the dirt, their faces splattered with blood. "Fair play! Look at him—he looks like a girl compared to me, and he carries extra weight strapped to his back. Leave him alone. I'll handle him."

For a moment, they grappled fiercely, a tangle of limbs and curses, until the larger man finally gained the upper hand. He knelt on Neville's chest, then rose, panting. "Now, take him arm-in-arm, any two of you!"

Two of the men immediately complied, seizing Neville's arms.

"As to us being thieves, Mr. Landless," the leader spat, wiping blood from his lip, "you know better than that by noon. We wouldn't have touched you if you hadn't provoked us. We're taking you to the high road, where you can find real help against actual thieves. Wipe his face, someone! Look at the trickles running down him!"

When his face was cleaned, Neville recognized the speaker. It was Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus, whom he had glimpsed only once on his arrival day.

"And my recommendation for the moment," Joe added, "is to keep your mouth shut, Mr. Landless. A friend awaits you at the high road—we've split into two parties to cover the approach. You'd best remain silent until you reach him. Someone, bring that stick!"

Utterly bewildered, Neville said not a word. Flanked by his captors, who held his arms firm, he walked like a prisoner in a dream until they reached the main thoroughfare and a small gathering of locals. Among them were the men who had retreated earlier, and at the center stood Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle. The men released Neville with a nod of respect toward the Minor Canon.

"What is all this, sir? What is happening?" Neville cried, feeling as though his mind had fractured. "I feel as if I have lost my senses!"

"Where is my nephew?" Mr. Jasper demanded, his voice wild with panic.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville echoed, confused. "Why do you ask me?"

"I ask you," Jasper snapped, "because you were the last person seen with him, and Edwin Drood is nowhere to be found!"

"Not to be found!" Neville gasped, horror striking him.

"Hold on, hold on," Mr. Crisparkle interjected gently. "Mr. Neville, you are overwhelmed. Compose yourself; it is vital that you gather your thoughts. Attend to me."

"I will try, sir," Neville stammered, touching his temple. "But I feel mad."

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?" Crisparkle pressed.

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Was it twelve o'clock?" Neville asked, looking desperately at Jasper.

"Precisely," Jasper confirmed. "The hour I mentioned to Mr. Crisparkle. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly. To watch the wind."

"What happened next? How long did you stay?"

"About ten minutes, perhaps less. Then we walked to your house, and he took his leave at the door."

"Did he mention returning to the river?"

"No. He said he was heading straight back."

The bystanders exchanged uneasy glances, turning to Mr. Crisparkle. Jasper, whose eyes had been fixed intently on Neville, lowered his voice. "And what are those stains upon his dress?"

Every eye shifted to the blood soaking Neville's clothes.

"And here are the same stains upon this stick!" Jasper exclaimed, snatching the weapon from a bystander's grip. "I know this stick belongs to him, and he carried it last night. What does this mean?"

"In the name of God, explain it, Neville!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville pointed, indicating Joe, "had a struggle for the stick moments ago. You can see the identical marks on him. When I was surrounded by eight people, what else could I assume? Why would they offer no explanation?"

They admitted silently that discretion had dictated their silence, confirming the struggle. Yet, even as they acknowledged it, the men who had witnessed it stared darkly at the drying bloodstains on Neville's garments.

"We must return, Neville," Crisparkle said. "You will be glad to come back and clear your name, surely?"

"Naturally, sir."

"Mr. Landless will walk at my side," Crisparkle announced, surveying the crowd. "Come, Neville!"

They began the journey back, the others trailing at varying distances. Jasper walked on Neville's opposite side, never breaking stride or dropping his gaze. While Crisparkle repeatedly questioned Neville, who repeated his answers, Jasper remained a statue of silence. When they approached the city and Crisparkle suggested visiting the Mayor immediately, Neville nodded sternly but offered no words until they stood in Mr. Sapsea's parlor.

Informed by Crisparkle of their need to make a voluntary statement, Jasper broke his silence. He declared his absolute trust in Mr. Sapsea's discernment. If the Mayor could uncover a plausible reason for Edwin's sudden disappearance, Jasper would listen; otherwise, he would wash his hands of any sinister suspicions. He pleaded his own distraught state of mind, claiming it was too unstable to judge fairly, and deferred entirely to the Mayor's judgment.
