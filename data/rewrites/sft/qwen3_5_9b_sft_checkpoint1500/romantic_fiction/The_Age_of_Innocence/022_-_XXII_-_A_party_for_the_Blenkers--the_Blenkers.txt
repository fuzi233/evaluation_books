"The Party for the Blenkers—the Blenkers?"

Mr. Welland lowered his knife and fork, staring across the luncheon table with a mixture of anxiety and disbelief at his wife. Adjusting her gold-rimmed spectacles, Mrs. Welland read the card aloud in a tone of high, theatrical comedy.

"Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R.S.V.P."

"Good gracious—" Mr. Welland gasped, as if a second reading were necessary to grasp the sheer absurdity of the situation. "Poor Amy Sillerton—you never can tell what her husband will do next," Mrs. Welland sighed. "I suppose he's finally discovered the Blenkers."

Professor Emerson Sillerton remained a persistent thorn in the side of Newport society, a weed that could not be plucked because it grew from the roots of a venerable and venerated family tree. As the town whispered, he had enjoyed "every advantage." His lineage traced back to Sillerton Jackson and the Pennilows of Boston, weaving together a tapestry of immense wealth and position. Yet, despite these advantages, Emerson Sillerton had chosen to abandon tradition. He had married Amy Dagonet, a woman who, by all accounts, deserved better—a life of stability rather than a partnership with a man who filled their home with long-haired eccentrics and short-haired bohemians, or dragged her to explore tombs in Yucatán instead of the cultural capitals of Europe.

To the rigid minds of the Mingott circle, Amy's submission to her husband's whims was incomprehensible. They gave their annual garden parties with a smug indifference, unaware that the mere mention of the Sillerton-Pennilow-Dagonet connection forced nearly every family on the Cliffs to draw lots for an unwilling representative.

"It's a wonder," Mrs. Welland observed, "that they didn't pick the Cup Race day! Do you recall two years ago when they hosted a party for a black gentleman on the same afternoon as Julia Mingott's *thé dansant*? Fortunately, there appears to be no other event scheduled..."

"Some of us," Mr. Welland interjected nervously, "my dear—more than one? Three o'clock is terribly inconvenient. I must be here by half-past three to take my drops. If I break Bencomb's regimen, it won't serve any purpose, and if I arrive late, I shall miss my drive." At the thought, he resumed his cutlery, a flush of worry coloring his finely wrinkled cheek.

"There is no necessity for you to attend at all, my dear," his wife replied, her cheerfulness now a practiced reflex. "I have cards to deliver further down Bellevue Avenue, and I shall stop by around half-past three. I intend to linger long enough to assure poor Amy that she has not been slighted." She cast a hesitant glance toward her daughter. "And if Newland's afternoon is accommodated, perhaps May may drive you out with the ponies to inspect their new russet harness."

In the Welland household, a person's day was sacred ground, carefully "provided for" as Mrs. Welland termed it. The specter of unstructured time—the terrifying prospect of killing hours with nothing more engaging than whist or solitaire—haunted her imagination with the same dread that plagued the unemployed philanthropist. Furthermore, she adhered to the principle that parents must never visibly interfere with the domestic arrangements of their married children. Balancing this respect for May's autonomy with Mr. Welland's health needs required a delicate ingenuity that ensured not a single minute of Mrs. Welland's own schedule remained unplanned.

"I shall naturally drive with Papa," May said, her tone gently reminding her husband of his tendency toward silence. "I am certain Newland will manage something." This lack of foresight in her son-in-law caused Mrs. Welland constant distress. During the fortnight he had resided under her roof, whenever she inquired about his plans, he often offered the paradoxical answer, "Oh, I think for a change I'll just save it instead of spending it," or admitted to lying beneath a rock on the beach below the house when called upon to visit friends.

"Newland never seems to look ahead," Mrs. Welland once vented to her daughter, and May responded serenely, "No; but you see it matters not, for when there is nothing specific to do, he simply reads a book."

"Ah, yes—much like his father!" Mrs. Welland conceded, treating it as a harmless inherited quirk, and thereafter the subject of Newland's idleness was quietly dropped.

Yet, as the date for the Sillerton reception neared, May began to exhibit a genuine concern for her brother-in-law's welfare, suggesting tennis matches at the Chiverses' or sails on Julius Beaufort's cutter as penance for her brief absence. "I shall return by six, you know, dear: Papa never drives later than that." She remained unconvinced until Archer proposed hiring a runabout to drive up the island to a stud-farm to view a second horse for her brougham. Having sought this animal for some time, the suggestion pleased her so greatly that she looked at her mother as if to say, "You see, he understands how to plan one's time as well as anyone."

The notion of the stud-farm and the brougham horse had germinated in Archer's mind the very day the Emerson Sillerton invitation was first mentioned. However, he had kept the plan secret, fearing that discovery might thwart its execution. With caution, he engaged a runabout drawn by a pair of sturdy trotters capable of covering eighteen miles on level ground. At two o'clock, leaving the luncheon table abruptly, he sprang into the light carriage and drove away.

The day was flawless. A northern breeze scattered small puffs of white cloud across an ultramarine sky, while the sea ran bright beneath it. Bellevue Avenue lay empty at that hour. After depositing the stable lad at Mill Street, Archer turned down Old Beach Road, crossing Eastman's Beach.

He possessed that inexplicable thrill with which, on half-holidays at school, he would venture into the unknown. Driving at a leisurely pace, he calculated that he would reach the stud-farm, situated beyond Paradise Rocks, before three o'clock. Thus, after examining—and perhaps testing—the horse, he would possess four golden hours to dispose of as he wished.

Upon hearing rumors of the Sillerton party, Archer had reasoned that the Marchioness Manson would undoubtedly accompany the Blenkers to Newport, offering Madame Olenska another chance to spend the day with her grandmother. Consequently, the Blenker habitation would likely be deserted, allowing him to satisfy a vague curiosity without impropriety. Though he was uncertain whether he desired to see the Countess Olenska again, since gazing upon her from the path above the bay, he had developed an irrational, indefinable longing to see where she lived. He wished to trace the imaginary figure moving within those walls, just as he had tracked her real presence in the summer-house. This craving persisted day and night, an insistent, undefined desire akin to a sick man's sudden whim for food long forgotten. He could not envision what it might lead to, nor did he harbor a conscious wish to speak to her or hear her voice. Simply, he felt that if he could carry away the vision of the spot of earth she trod upon, and the way the sky and sea embraced it, the rest of the world might appear less hollow.

Arriving at the stud-farm, a quick glance revealed the horse was not what he sought. Nevertheless, he rode a circuit behind it to prove to himself that he was not in a hurry. At three o'clock, shaking out the reins over the trotters, he turned onto the by-roads leading to Portsmouth. The wind had died down, and a faint haze on the horizon signaled an approaching fog waiting to creep up the Saconnet with the turn of the tide; yet surrounding him, fields and woods were bathed in golden light.

He drove past gray-shingled farmhouses nestled in orchards, through hayfields and groves of oak, past villages with white steeples piercing the fading sky. Finally, after stopping to inquire of laborers in a field, he turned down a lane bordered by high banks of goldenrod and brambles. At the lane's end lay the blue glimmer of the river; to the left, standing before a cluster of oaks and maples, rose a long, dilapidated house, its white clapboard paint peeling away.

Beside the gateway stood one of the open sheds where New England farmers shelter their tools and hitch their teams. Archer dismounted, led his pair into the shed, and tied them to a post before turning toward the house. The patch of lawn before it had reverted to a hayfield, but to the left, an overgrown box-garden choked with dahlias and rusty roses encircled a ghostly summer-house of trellis-work. Once white, it now bore the marks of age, crowned by a wooden Cupid who had lost his bow and arrow yet continued to aim ineffectually at the sky.
