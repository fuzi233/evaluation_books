The three or four "To Let" boards had stood within the low paling as long as the inhabitants of the little triangular "Square" could remember, existing like skeletal sentinels guarding a tomb of forgotten commerce. Though they may once have been vertical, that era belonged to a mythological past; now they overhung the palings at chaotic, precarious angles, resembling a row of wooden choppers poised to cleave the heads of any passerby, yet strangely failing to sever a single tenant from the old house. There was never truly a "stream" of life flowing through the Square; the current bypassed the area entirely, rushing a furlong away beyond the labyrinthine sprawl of tenements and alleys that had choked the neighborhood since the old house's construction, effectively hemming it in as a sealed wound. Perhaps the house itself was merely tolerated standing, awaiting the expiration of a lease or two before a total clearance would finally purge the district. Built of blooming old red brick, its walls served as a museum of failed capitalism, embedded with the crowns and clasped hands of insurance companies long since dissolved into dust.

Children of this secluded square had once swung upon the low gate at the end of the entrance-alley until little more than the solid top bar remained, a rusted relic of a simpler time. The alley itself ran past boarded basement windows where tramps had scrawled their cryptic, transient marks, while the path above was washed and worn uneven by the relentless spilling of water from the eaves of the encroaching neighbor. Cats and dogs had claimed the approach as their sovereign territory. Given these odds, the chances of a tenant warranted neither the keeping nor the repair of the "To Let" boards, and indeed, they were left to rot. For six months, Oleron had passed the old place twice a day, on his way from his lodgings to the room, ten minutes' walk away, where he had secured work. For six months, no hatchet-like notice-board had fallen across his path. This absence might have been due to his habitual route, which skirted the other side of the square. But fate intervened one morning when he chose the path running past the broken gate and the rain-worn entrance alley, pausing before one of the inclined boards.

The board bore, alongside the agent's name, an announcement written in ink that had faded to the hue of Oleron's own early youth: *Key to be had at Number Six.* Now, Oleron was already paying, for his separate bedroom and workroom, more than an author who, devoid of private means and habitually disregarded by the public, could possibly afford. He paid additionally a small rent for the storage of the greater part of his grandmother's furniture. Furthermore, the logistical nightmare of a divided domicile plagued him: the book he wished to read in bed resided at his working-quarters, half a mile and more away, while the urgent note or letter needed during the day was as likely as not tucked into the pocket of another coat hanging behind his bedroom door. There were other inconveniences inherent in such a fractured existence. Therefore, brought suddenly to a halt by the hatchet-like notice-board, Oleron looked first down through scanty privet-bushes at the boarded basement windows, then up at the blank, grimy windows of the first floor, and so higher to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the leads. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, shaven jaw, before casting another glance at the board and walking slowly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked and waited two or three minutes. Although the door stood open, no answer came. He was knocking again when a long-nosed man in shirt-sleeves emerged. "I was asking a blessing on our food," he said in severe explanation. Oleron asked if he might have the key to the old house. The long-nosed man withdrew again. Oleron waited another five minutes on the step; then the man reappeared, masticating some of the food he had invoked, and announced that the key was lost. "But you won't want it," he sneered. "The entrance door isn't closed, and a push'll open any of the others. I'm an agent for it, if you're thinking of taking it..."

Oleron recrossed the square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, passed along the alley, and turned in at the old wide doorway. To the right, immediately within the door, steps descended to the roomy cellars, while the staircase before him featured a carved rail, broad and handsome, yet filthy with neglect. Oleron ascended it, avoiding contact with the rail and wall, and stopped at the first landing. A door facing him had been boarded up, but he pushed at the one on his right hand, and an insecure bolt or staple yielded easily. He entered the empty first floor. He spent a quarter of an hour exploring the space, absorbing its potential, before coming out again. Without mounting higher, he descended and recrossed the square to the house of the man who had lost the key.

"Can you tell me how much the rent is?" he asked. The man mentioned a figure, the comparative lowness of which seemed accounted for by the character of the neighborhood and the abominable state of unrepair of the place. "Would it be possible to rent a single floor?" Oleron pressed. The long-nosed man did not know; they might... "Who are they?" Oleron demanded. The man gave Oleron the name of a firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn. "You might mention my name—Barrett," he added.

Pressure of work prevented Oleron from descending to Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but he returned the following day and was instantly offered the whole house as a purchase for fifty pounds down, the remainder of the purchase-money to remain on mortgage. It took him half an hour to disabuse the lawyer's mind of the idea that he wished anything more of the place than to rent a single floor of it. This caused certain hums and haws of hesitation, and the lawyer was by no means certain that it lay within his power to accommodate Oleron's suggestion; but it was finally extracted from him that, provided the notice-boards were allowed to remain up, and provided it was agreed that in the event of the whole house letting, the arrangement should terminate automatically without further notice, something might be done. That the old place should suddenly let over his head seemed to Oleron the slightest of risks to take, and he promised a decision within a week.

On the morrow, he visited the house again, went through it from top to bottom, and then returned home to his lodgings to take a bath. He was immensely taken with that portion of the house he had already determined should be his own. Scraped clean and repainted, and furnished with that old furniture of Oleron's grandmother's, it ought to be entirely charming. He went to the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his half-forgotten belongings and to take measurements; thence he proceeded to a decorator's. He was very busy with his regular work, and could have wished that the notice-board had caught his attention either a few months earlier or else later in the year; but the quickest way forward would be to suspend work entirely until after his removal.

A fortnight later, his first floor was painted throughout in a tender, elder-flower white. The paint was dry, and Oleron was in the midst of his installation. He was animated, delighted, rubbing his hands as he polished and arranged his grandmother's effects—the tall lattice-paned china cupboard with its Derby and Mason and Spode ware, the large folding Sheraton table, the long, low bookshelves (of which he had had two "copied"), the chairs, the Sheffield candlesticks, the riveted rose-bowls. He set these treasures against his newly painted elder-white walls—walls of wood panelled in the happiest proportions, moulded and coffered to the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest that the builders of rooms no longer know. The ceilings were lofty, faintly painted with an old pattern of stars; even the tapering mouldings of his iron fireplace were as delicately designed as jewellery. Oleron walked about rubbing his hands, frequently stopping for the mere pleasure of the glimpses from white room to white room.

"Charming, charming!" he murmured to himself. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!" He bought a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, shutting off his quarters from the rest of the house. If he now wanted to read in bed, his book could be had simply by stepping into the next room. All the time, he thought how exceedingly lucky he was to have secured the place. He put up a hat-rack in the little square hall and hung up his hats, caps, and coats. Passers-through the small triangular square late at night, looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets, could see the light within Oleron's red blinds, or else the sudden darkening of one blind and the illumination of another as Oleron, candlestick in hand, moved from room to room, making final settlements of his furniture or preparing to resume the work that his removal had interrupted.

To understand the architecture of Paul Oleron's creative exile, one must first confront the absolute scarcity of his universe at the age of forty-four. He existed within a microscopic empire of five rooms, subjugated by a mortgage and haunted by the ghost of his own unmet potential. He was a writer who treated the world with a strange, brittle courtesy, yet he refused to calculate the crushing weight of his deficit—the staggering distance between his current output and the towering peaks of his ambition. To have balanced the ledger would have been to admit defeat; it would have revealed that at forty-four, he was already trailing his peers by decades, a fact that might have shattered the fragile equilibrium of his spirit. He had chosen this path, carved it into his soul, and was now locked within it, a prisoner of his own volition. Perhaps in the days when he was still susceptible to the seduction of idealism, he had selected this road because it demanded nobility, a generosity of spirit, and a disinterested pursuit of art that ignored the marketplace. Had he ever dared to question the foundation of his faith, he would likely have doubled down, convinced that a life devoid of such high-minded virtues was not worth living. Yet, lately, a shadow had crept in—a vague suspicion that his ideals were merely expensive delusions, that a life governed by less exigent, more pragmatic principles might have spared him this agonizing climb. But anticipation was useless; he could not wait for the inevitable decline of his powers to arrive and judge him then.

In the interim, his relocation to the old house, its bricks scarred with the insurance marks of a precarious tenure, served only to interrupt *Romilly Bishop* at the fifteenth chapter. As this tall man with the lean, ascetic face moved through his new domain, rearranging furniture and shifting objects with a precision bordering on the neurotic, he projected an air of spinster-like orderliness. For twenty years, drifting through a dozen garrets, flats, and lodgings, he had learned that method was the only currency that saved both time and temper. He had negotiated with Mrs. Barrett, a stout Welsh woman whose falsetto voice retained the distinct cadence of Merionethshire despite her long residency in London, to provide his breakfast and scrub the floors on Saturdays. For the rest, he welcomed domestic drudgery as a necessary counterweight to the strain of composition.

His kitchen, joined to a narrow apartment housing a modern bath, overlooked a grimy alley. At one end stood a large closet with a square sliding hatch near the top. Originally a powder-closet, designed for the elaborate concealment of a pistol, the space now held a mystery. Oleron puzzled over it, then smiled faintly, stirred by a sensation he could not name. He would repurpose this chamber of violence into a larder. Inside, on a shelf buried deep within the wall, he discovered two mushroom-shaped wooden wig-stands, relics of a vanished era. Their presence was inexplicable, likely unearthed by the painters during renovations. With cupboards scarce, Oleron exercised ingenuity to stash his linens, boxes, and accumulated papers, turning the house into a fortress of his own making.

It was early spring when he took possession, driven by the anxiety to launch *Romilly* by autumn. Yet, he resolved not to force the muse. If the book required more time, so be it; its importance to his artistic evolution was absolute, and it demanded its own rhythm. In his previous workspace, the novel had begun to breathe, to speak and act of itself. He trusted that once the distraction of moving settled, *Romilly* would resume her dominion. The transition was nearly complete; on a crisp March morning, he returned from the street carrying two great bunches of yellow daffodils, placing one on the mantelpiece beside Sheffield sticks and the other on his desk. He opened the half-finished manuscript, but before a single word could flow, he reached for his cheque-book and pass-book in a small rosewood cabinet.

The arithmetic was stark. His installation costs had exceeded his estimates, leaving him with a balance of less than fifty pounds and no immediate horizon for relief. "Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains mount up so quickly," he murmured, his monk-like face clouding with thought. "But it would have been a pity to spoil the sanctuary for the lack of ten pounds... Well, *Romilly* simply *must* see the autumn light. Here goes."

He drew his papers forward, but the ink failed to flow. The square outside imposed its own cacophony, a relentless barrage of new sounds that threatened to drown out his internal monologue. Hawkers shouted from their carts; at noon, a troop of children swung on his gate before departing for afternoon school; later, an itinerant musician plucked a mandolin beneath his window. Oleron pushed up the sash and tossed a penny, accepting the distraction as a temporary truce. But when he returned to his table, the silence was false. He drifted, waking periodically to wonder about the ghosts of his former rooms: had a buttercup satin settee stood by the window? Did a crystal chandelier hang from the lofty ceiling? These phantom furnishings haunted him, proving that he was not working, but merely staring into the void.

"It is better to do nothing than to tire fruitlessly," he conceded, deciding to take a walk. Instead, he dozed in his chair, awakening at half-past four with a yawn that betrayed his exhaustion. "This won't do," he sighed, contemplating the breach of an evening appointment. The next morning, however, the sun poured in with a violent, joyful clarity, casting shifting patterns of light and shadow across the polished table and worn floor. The noises resumed—the children, the hawkers—but Oleron fought them off. He dotted a pattern on the page, then stopped to align a jar of daffodils perfectly with a creamy panel. He wrote a sentence, then fractured it into notes and jottings. He persuaded himself that this was work, pacing the room until an idea struck him: the space needed color, a robust warmth to combat its pallid, devitalized atmosphere. More flowers, warmer cushions—he muttered that he couldn't afford it, yet measured the window recesses with a ruler.

As he stooped to measure, his posture shifted to one of intense curiosity. Rising, he rubbed his hands with gentle glee. "Oho, oho!" he exclaimed. "These look like window-boxes, nailed up. An adventure!" On the wall, beyond the open bedroom door, lay three windows. Their seats had been painted repeatedly, layers of history trapped beneath fresh coats. Beneath the ledge of one, an old keyhole had been puttied over. Oleron produced his penknife, working carefully for five minutes before fetching a hammer and chisel from the kitchen.

Driving the chisel under the seat, he pried the lid loose. The sound of the mallet striking the wedge echoed brutally in the sweet, pale apartment, causing the panelling to vibrate like a sounding board. The entire house seemed to awaken, a flock of echoes rising from the cellar to the garrets, irritating his nerves. Pausing to fetch a duster, he muffled the blows, lifting the lid slowly. Paint flaked; rusty nails squeaked; the lid rose to reveal emptiness save for a layer of scurf, mold, and cobwebs.

"No treasure there," Oleron noted, amused by his own fleeting hope. "*Romilly* must still emerge by autumn. Let us inspect the others."

He turned to the second window, spending the late afternoon prying open the remaining seats. The bedroom's box was empty, but the second box in the sitting-room yielded something yielding and folded, encrusted with an inch of dust. Carrying it to the kitchen, he brushed it over a bucket and unfolded it. It was a massive bag of ancient, frieze-like fabric, shaped like an irregular triangle with wide flaps and remnants of straps. The top patch was faded yellowish-brown, while the rest displayed varying shades of crimson, bleached by time and exposure.

"Now, whatever can that have been?" he mused, surveying the relic. "I give it up. Whatever it is, it has settled my work for today."

He folded the object carelessly into a corner, then armed himself with pans, brushes, and a knife, returning to the sitting-room to scrape, wash, and line the newly discovered receptacles with paper. Once finished, he stowed his spare boots, books, and papers inside, closing the lids with a sense of amusement mixed with a lingering anxiety for the day when he would truly settle down to write.

It irritated Oleron slightly that his friend, Miss Bengough, should dismiss with a mere glance the locale he himself had found so singularly captivating. Indeed, she scarcely lifted her eyes to it. Yet, such indifference was characteristic of her nature; she was a woman perpetually detached from the ornamental graces of existence, careless of appearances, and seemingly more vibrant when consuming biscuits from a paper bag than when dining with strict adherence to social convention. She was an unmarried journalist of thirty-four, possessing a large, showy frame, fair as butter and pink as a dog-rose, resembling a florist's most prized specimen bloom. Her movements were sudden and ample, her utterances moist and explosive. She "pulled a better living out of the pool"—as she phrased it—than Oleron did, and through cunningly disguised advertisements for drapers and haberdashers, she secured the greater portion of her remarkably varied wardrobe. When she moved, she left behind small whirlwinds of air in which her veils and scarves fluttered and spun. Oleron had heard the rustle of her skirts on his staircase and her single, loud knock at his door during the first month of his residence in this new abode. Her garments carried the raw outer air with them, and she would fling a bundle of women's journals down onto a chair.

"Don't knock off for me," she declared across a mouthful of large-headed hatpins as she removed her hat and veil. "I didn't know whether you were straight yet, so I've brought some sandwiches for lunch. You've got coffee, I suppose? No, don't get up—I'll find the kitchen—"

"Oh, that's perfectly all right; I'll clear these things away. To tell the truth, I'm rather glad to be interrupted," Oleron replied. He gathered his scattered work and stowed it away. She was already in the kitchen; he heard the rush of water filling the kettle. He joined her, and ten minutes later followed her back to the sitting-room carrying the coffee and sandwiches on a silver tray. They seated themselves, placing the tray on a small table between them.

"Well, what do you think of the new place?" Oleron asked as she poured the coffee.

"Hm!... Anybody'd think you were going to get married, Paul."

He laughed. "Oh no. But it's an improvement on some of them, isn't it?"

"Is it? I suppose it is; I don't know. I liked the last place, in spite of the black ceiling and no tap."

"How's *Romilly*?"

Oleron thumbed his chin meditatively. "Hm! I'm rather ashamed to tell you. The fact is, I've not got on very well with it. But it will be all right on the night, as you used to say."

"Stuck?"

"Rather stuck."

"Got any of it you care to read to me?"

Oleron had long been in the habit of reading portions of his work to Miss Bengough. Her comments were invariably quick and practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes indirectly suggestive. In return for his confidence, she kept all mention of her own work sedulously from him. His, she insisted, was "real work"; hers merely filled space, not always even grammatically.

"I'm afraid there isn't," Oleron replied, continuing to shave his chin with a dry, thoughtful expression. Then, with a sudden burst of candor, he added, "The fact is, Elsie, I've not written—not actually written—very much more of it. Not *any* more of it, in fact. But, of course, that doesn't mean I haven't progressed. I've progressed, in one sense, rather alarmingly. I'm now thinking of reconstructing the whole thing."

Miss Bengough gave a sharp gasp. "Reconstructing!"

"Making *Romilly* herself a different type of woman. Somehow, I've begun to feel that I'm not getting the most out of her. As she stands, I've certainly lost interest in her to some extent."

"But—but—" Miss Bengough protested, "you had her so real, so *living*, Paul!"

Oleron smiled faintly. He had been quite prepared for Miss Bengough's disapproval. He wasn't surprised that she liked *Romilly* as she currently existed; she would. Whether she realized it or not, there was much of herself in his fictitious creation. Naturally, *Romilly* would seem "real," "living," to her.

"But are you really serious, Paul?" Miss Bengough asked presently, with a round-eyed stare.

"Quite serious."

"You're really going to scrap those fifteen chapters?"

"I didn't exactly say that."

"That fine, rich love-scene?"

"I should only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something I thought better."

"And that beautiful, *beautiful* description of *Romilly* on the shore?"

"It wouldn't necessarily be wasted," he said a little uneasily.

But Miss Bengough made a large, windy gesture and then let him have it. "Really, you are *too* trying!" she broke out. "I do wish sometimes you'd remember you're human, and live in a world! You know I'd be the *last* to wish you to lower your standard one inch, but it wouldn't be lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you're sometimes altogether too godlike!... Why, it would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably, now. You've been working for nearly twenty years; you've now got what you've been working for almost within your grasp; your affairs are at a most critical stage (oh, don't tell me; I know you're about at the end of your money); and here you are, deliberately proposing to withdraw a thing that will probably make your name, and to substitute for it something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read—and small blame to them! Really, you try my patience!"

Oleron had shaken his head slowly as she spoke. It was an old story between them. The noisy, able, practical journalist was an admirable friend—up to a certain point; beyond that... well, each of us knows that point beyond which we stand alone. Elsie Bengough sometimes remarked that had she possessed one-tenth part of Oleron's genius, there were few things she could not have accomplished—thus rendering that genius a quantitatively divisible commodity, an ingredient to be added to or subtracted from in the admixture of his work. That it was a qualitative essence, essential and indivisible, informing the soul of the art, passed her comprehension. Their spirits parted company at that juncture. Oleron knew it. She did not appear to know it.

"Yes, yes, yes," he said a little wearily by and by, "practically you're quite right, entirely right, and I haven't a word to say. If I could only turn *Romilly* over to you, you'd make an enormous success of her. But that can't be, and I, for my part, am seriously doubting whether she's worth my while. You know what that means."

"What does it mean?" she demanded bluntly.

"Well," he said, smiling wanly, "what *does* it mean when you're convinced a thing isn't worth doing? You simply don't do it."

Miss Bengough's eyes swept the ceiling for assistance against this impossible man. "What utter rubbish!" she broke out at last. "Why, when I saw you last you were simply oozing *Romilly*; you were turning her off at the rate of four chapters a week; if you hadn't moved you'd have had her three-parts done by now. What on earth possessed you to move right in the middle of your most important work?"

Oleron attempted to dissuade her with a recital of inconveniences, but she would not have it. Perhaps in her heart she partly suspected the reason. He was simply mortally weary of the narrow circumstances of his life. He had endured twenty years of it—twenty years of garrets, roof-chambers, and dingy flats and shabby lodgings, and he was tired of dinginess and shabbiness. The reward was as far off as ever—or if it was not, he no longer cared as once he would have cared to stretch out his hand and seize it. It is all very well to tell a man who is at the point of exhaustion that only another effort is required of him; if he cannot make it, he is as far off as ever.

"Anyway," Oleron summed up, "I'm happier here than I've been for a long time. That's some sort of a justification."

"And doing no work," said Miss Bengough pointedly.

At that, a trifling petulance that had been gathering within Oleron came to a head. "And why should I do nothing but work?" he demanded. "How much happier am I for it? I don't say I don't love my work—when it's done; but I hate doing it. Sometimes it's an intolerable burden that I simply long to be rid of. Once in many weeks it has a moment, one moment, of glow and thrill for me; I remember the days when it was all glow and thrill; and now I'm forty-four, and it's becoming drudgery. Nobody wants it; I'm ceasing to want it myself; and if any ordinary sensible man were to ask me whether

As Oleron sat by his fire that evening, the crackle of the logs providing the only rhythm to the silence, he weighed Miss Bengough's prognostication that difficulties awaited him in his work. He arrived at a grim conclusion: it would have been infinitely preferable had she kept her beliefs locked within her own breast. No man executes a task with greater efficacy when his confidence is doused at the outset; to articulate difficulty is, in essence, to conjure it. Speech itself becomes a deterrent act, a seed to which other discouragements rapidly accrete, until the very event warned against becomes inevitable through the weight of its own anticipation. He heartily confounded her. An influence hostile to the completion of *Romilly* had been born in that conversation. And in some illogical, dogmatic fashion peculiar to women, she had attached this antagonistic force directly to his new abode. Was ever anything so absurd! "You'll never finish *Romilly* here." ... Why not? Was this her conception of the luxury that saps the springs of action and drags a man down into indolence and eventual obsolescence? The place was adequate—it was, in fact, entirely charming—but it was hardly so demoralizing as she implied. No; Elsie had missed the mark that time.

He shifted his chair, turning to survey the room that seemed to smile, positively to beam, in the firelight. He too smiled, as if pity were due for a maligned apartment. Even that slight lack of robust color he had noted earlier vanished under the soft, amber glow. The drawn chintz curtains—their fabric a trellis of flowers and oaten pipes—fell in long, quiet folds to the window-seats; the rows of bindings in the old bookcases drank the light richly; the last trace of sallowness had departed with the daylight. If the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed slightly out of the picture. That reflection struck him sharply, and presently he returned to it. Yes, the room had, quite accidentally, performed Miss Bengough a disservice that afternoon. It had, in some subtle but unmistakable manner, established a stark contrast of qualities. Assuming, for the sake of argument, the slightly ridiculous proposition that the chamber in which Oleron sat was characterized by a certain sparsity and lack of vigor, so much the worse for Miss Bengough; she certainly erred on the side of redundancy and general excess. And if one must contrast abstract qualities, Oleron inclined toward the austere in taste.

Yes, here Oleron had made a distinct discovery; he marveled he had not perceived it sooner. He pictured Miss Bengough again as she had appeared that afternoon—large, showy, moistly pink, radiating that quality of the prize bloom exuding from her pores—and instantly she suffered in his thought. He even recognized now that he had noticed something odd at the time, and that unconsciously his attitude, even while she stood there, had been one of critical scrutiny. The mechanism of her being was a little obvious; her melting humidity was the result of analyzable processes; and behind her there had seemed to lurk some dim shape emblematic of mortality. He had never, during the ten years of their intimacy, dreamed for a single moment of asking her to marry him; none the less, he now felt for the first time a profound thankfulness that he had not done so.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flamed with the heat of shame that he should be thinking thus of his friend. What! Elsie Bengough, with whom he had spent weeks and weeks of afternoons—she, the good chum, on whose help he would have counted had all the rest of the world failed him—she, whose loyalty to him would not, he knew, swerve so long as there was breath in her body—to be even in thought dissected thus! He was an ingrate and a cad. Had she been present in that moment, he would have abased himself before her. For ten minutes and more he sat, still gazing into the fire, watching that humiliating red fade slowly from his cheeks. All was still within and without, save for a tiny musical tinkling emanating from the kitchen—the dripping of water from an imperfectly turned-off tap into the vessel beneath it.

Mechanically, he began to beat with his finger against the armrest, syncing to the faintly heard falling of the drops. The tiny, regular movement seemed to hasten that shameful withdrawal from his face. He grew cool once more; and when he resumed his meditation, he was wholly unconscious that he had taken up the thread at precisely the same point. It was not only her florid superfluity of build that he had approached with an attitude of criticism; he was conscious also of the wide chasms between her mind and his own. He felt no thankfulness that up to a certain point their natures had ever run companionably side by side; he was now filled with questions extending far beyond that boundary. Their intellects diverged; there was no denying it. Looking back, he was inclined to doubt whether there had been any real coincidence. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had appeared to speak comprehensively and to the point; but what can a man do who, having assumed that another sees as he does, is suddenly brought up short by something that falsifies and discredits all that has gone before? He doubted everything now.

It did for a moment occur to him that the man who demands of a friend more than can be given is in danger of losing that friend, but he put the thought aside. Again he ceased to think, and again moved his finger to the distant dripping of the tap. And now (he resumed by-and-by), if these things were true of Elsie Bengough, they were also true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop. And since he could say of Romilly, with a shame he could not admit regarding Elsie, that she was flawed, he gave his thoughts rein. He did so in that smiling, fire-lit room, accompanied by the faintly heard tap. There was no longer any doubt about it; he hated the central character of his novel. Even as he had described her physically, she overpowered the senses; she was coarse-fibred, over-colored, rank. It became true the moment he formulated the thought: Gulliver had described the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honor thus. Mentally and spiritually, she corresponded—she was unsensitive, limited, common.

The model (he closed his eyes for a moment)—the model stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters to such a pitch that, without seeing the reason, he had been unable to begin the sixteenth. He marveled that it had only just dawned upon him. And *this* was to have been his Beatrice, his vision! As Elsie, she was to have gone into the furnace of his art, and she was to have emerged as the Woman all men desire! Her thoughts were to have been culled from his own finest, her form from his dearest dreams, and her setting wherever he could find one fit for her worth. He had brooded long before making the attempt; then one day he had felt her stir within him as a mother feels a quickening, and he had begun to write; and so he had added chapter to chapter.... And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced!

Again he sat, softly moving his finger. Then he bestirred himself. She must go—all fifteen chapters of her. That was settled. For what was to take her place, his mind was a blank; but one thing at a time; a man is not excused from taking the wrong course because the right one is not immediately revealed to him. Better would come if it was to come; in the meantime... He rose, fetched the fifteen chapters, and read them over before he should drop them into the fire. He became conscious of the dripping of the tap again. It had a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes, and it was foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like. No one listened. "Shut your heads," a heavy laborer growled gruffly. "She hasn't been found yet. Here come the inspectors; we shall learn more shortly."

Two inspectors approached, conferring with the constables guarding the breach. The little fat man surged forward eagerly. "She purchased the key from me! I recall the number because of its repetition—three ones and three threes—111333!" he cried, his excitement bordering on hysteria.

An inspector sidestepped him with practiced indifference. "Has anyone entered?" he asked a constable.

"No, sir."

"Then you, Brackley, accompany us. You, Smith, hold the gate. A squad is en route."

The two inspectors and the constable descended the alley and crossed the threshold into the house. They ascended the wide, carved staircase. "This does not suggest he has ventured out recently," one inspector muttered, kicking aside a litter of dead leaves and paper scattered before Oleron's door. "We need not knock—shatter a pane, Brackley."

The door featured two glazed panels. With a sharp crack of breaking glass, Brackley forced his elbow through the hole, pulling back the latch. "Faugh!" choked one inspector as they stepped inside. "Ventilate this place quickly. It reeks like a hearse—"

Outside, the assembly watched as the red blinds rose and the windows of the old house were thrown wide open to the sky.

"That improves matters," said one inspector, leaning out of a window to draw a deep, cleansing breath. "That appears to be the bedroom. Will you enter, Simms, while I survey the remainder?"

They had raised the bedroom blind as well, and the waxy-white, emaciated man lying on the bed had fashioned a blindfold from his own hand against the torturous flood of daylight. He could scarcely believe his senses, for there were two policemen in his chamber, bending over him, demanding the location of "her." He shook his head, confusion clouding his features.

"This woman Bengough… goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough… d'ye hear? Where is she?"

"No good, Brackley; rouse him; be gentle; I think I shall stick my head out of the window myself…"

Meanwhile, the other inspector had cleared Oleron's study of secrets and now stood in the kitchen, sweeping aside an ankle-deep mass of vegetable refuse that cluttered the floor. The kitchen window lacked a blind, overshadowed by the blank wall of the house across the alley. The room appeared vacant. Yet, as the inspector kicked aside dead flowers, he noted a shuffling track, alien to his own gait, leading to a cupboard tucked in the corner. On the upper portion of the cupboard door sat a square panel resembling a sliding hatch. The main door stood closed.

The inspector advanced, reaching for the small knob, and slid the hatch along its groove. Then, involuntarily, he recoiled. Framed within the aperture, falling slightly forward before jamming back into its frame, lay something resembling a large, lumpy pudding, wrapped in a pudding-bag of faded, brownish-red frieze.

"Ah!" breathed the inspector.

To reseal the hatch, he would have had to push that grotesque offering back with his hand; some primal revulsion prevented him from touching it. Instead, he turned the handle of the cupboard itself. There was immense weight behind it—so much weight that after opening the door merely three or four inches to peer inside, he had to brace his shoulder against it to force it shut again. In doing so, he left protruding, a few inches above the floor, a triangle of black-and-white checked skirt.

Retreating to the small hall, he shouted, "All clear!"

They had dressed Oleron. He still used his hands as shields against the light, his mind a chaotic storm. He could not comprehend the extraordinary profusion of dead flowers blooming everywhere; he could not fathom the presence of police officers in his sanctuary; he could not understand why one was summoned for a four-wheeler and a stretcher; nor could he grasp what heavy object they seemed to be maneuvering in his kitchen—*his* kitchen.

"What is the matter?" he murmured sleepily.

Then he heard a murmur rising in the square, punctuated by the halt of a four-wheeler outside. A police officer leaned at his elbow, and Oleron wondered why, when he whispered a question, the officer erupted into a string of words regarding something "used in evidence against you." They hoisted him to his feet, guiding him toward the exit.

No, Oleron could make sense of none of it. They carried him down the stairs and along the alley. Oleron registered confused, angry shouts; he gathered that a mob sought to lynch someone. His attention then locked onto the little fat, frightened-eyed man, who appeared to be making a statement that an officer was transcribing into a notebook.

"I'd seen her with him… they were often together… she came into my shop and said it was for him… I thought it was all right… 111333 was the number," the man stammered.

The crowd roared with anger; many police struggled to contain them. Yet, one inspector possessed a voice that Oleron perceived as kindly, even friendly. He directed someone to load another passenger into the cab before some unknown burden was extracted. Oleron noted that a four-wheeler was drawn up at the gate. It was clearly intended for him. As they lifted him aboard, he saw the inspector attempt to position himself between Oleron and something standing behind the cab, but failed to act swiftly enough to prevent Oleron's view. That object was a hooded stretcher.

The angry voices thundered like a sea; something hard, akin to a stone, struck the rear of the cab. The inspector followed Oleron inside and stood with his back to the window, facing the side where the mob pressed. The door they had placed Oleron in remained open, seemingly until the arrival of the second inspector. Through the gap, Oleron caught a glimpse of the hatchet-like "To Let" boards nestled among the privet trees. One bore the inscription that the key resided at Number Six.

Suddenly, the raging cacophony hushed. Shuffling footsteps echoed along the entrance-alley, and the second inspector appeared at the cab door.

"Right away," he ordered the driver.

He entered, fastening the door behind him, and blocking the second window with his broad back
