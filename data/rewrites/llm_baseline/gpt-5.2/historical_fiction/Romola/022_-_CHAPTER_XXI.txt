It was the seventeenth day of November in the year 1494—more than eighteen months since Tito and Romola had been bound to one another in that Easter brightness when, after the old Greek fashion, sugared comfits had rained upon them like a small, crackling hail of promised sweetness. Many a Florentine hearth since then had known the familiar rites of kneading and simmering—oil warmed, onions softened, bread revived with broth—yet the city’s larger life had altered as wholly as a loaf changes when the leaven takes it. For private fortunes in Florence were not unlike the smallest figs on a laden branch: each swelling fruit drew its sap from the same unseen circulation; and Tito’s and Romola’s married lot, with all its domestic duties and vanities, depended now on the grand public currents that were turning Italy.

Only a week earlier the old centuries seemed to have stepped back into Florentine blood. The great bell in the palace tower had sent out its hard, hammering alarm—sound not so much heard as felt, as when a cleaver strikes the butcher’s block—and at that vibration men had poured into the streets with rusty weapons, with axes from workshops, with cudgels snatched up as readily as a baker snatches the peel when he smells scorching. The Medici had been driven out. San Gallo had shut its gate upon Piero—arrogant, exasperating Piero—fleeing toward Bologna with hired horsemen stumbling after him in their panic; and it had shut upon his younger brother too, the cardinal slipping away in a Franciscan’s disguise. A price had been set on both their heads. There had been sackings after the old precedent, as if the city, having long fasted from its own will, made a rough feast of vengeance; the ignominious painted images on public walls—those reminders of past conspiracies—had been effaced; exiled enemies were called home again, names and families coming back like spices long kept in a chest, pungent the moment the lid is lifted. The half-fledged tyrants were out of their splendid nest in the Via Larga, and the Republic could taste its own tongue again.

And now, scarcely a week later, that same palace in the Via Larga had been swept and dressed for a new tenant. If bright drapery roofing the streets with unwonted colour, if banners and hangings poured from windows like streamed ribbons of saffron and crimson, if carpets and tapestry were laid over every step and stone where exceptional feet might tread—if these things were proof of joy, then Florence was joyful in expectation of its guest. Colour ran like a rich sauce from the palace in the Via Larga round by the Cathedral, on to the great Piazza della Signoria, and across the Ponte Vecchio to Porta San Frediano, the gate that looks toward Pisa. There, close to the gate, a platform and canopy had been erected for the Signoria; Messer Luca Corsini, doctor of law, could feel his heart fluttering at the thought of the Latin oration he must read, as a man’s stomach flutters when he is pledged to carve before a crowded table and fears his hand may shake. Chief elders prepared themselves—smooth chin, well-lined silk lucco—ready to walk in procession; well-born youths examined their rich tunics cut after the French mode, trusting that on Florentine bodies the new fashion would seem not foreign but graceful. And the clergy, from the archbishop in his effulgence down to the long file of monks—black, white, and grey—had been early at counsel on how to marshal themselves, how to bear relics and sacred banners and jeweled vessels so that every pace would agree with the expected arrival of the illustrious visitor at three o’clock.

An unexampled visitor. He had crossed the Alps with an army Italy had not seen: Swiss in thousands, grim as winter, well used to fight for love and hatred as well as for hire; cavaliers proud of lineage; an infantry such as astonished the eye, where one man in a hundred carried an arquebus; and cannon of bronze that did not throw stones but iron balls, drawn not by bullocks slow as oxen at a press but by horses, and able to fire again before a city could patch the breach made by the first shot. Men compared him to Charlemagne, welcome conqueror and rebuilder, regulator and benefactor of the Church; others spoke rather of Cyrus, liberator of a chosen people, restorer of the Temple. For he came with projects that sounded like a sung legend: he would march through Italy amid jubilees; he would satisfy all complaints at Rome; he would take Naples by hereditary right and a little fighting; and from Naples, that convenient threshold, he would go on to the Turks, cutting some down and converting the rest to Christ.

It befitted, they said, the Most Christian King, head of a nation strengthened by the devices of subtle Louis the Eleventh—dead these ten years in much fear for his soul. This antitype of Cyrus and Charlemagne was Louis’s son: young Charles the Eighth of France. And because the tale had such breadth, because the tread of so great a host seemed to shake the ordinary pattern of days, there spread through Florence a conviction that the French king’s advent belonged to those events at which marble statues might sweat, fiery warriors might fight in the air, and beasts might bring forth monstrous births—that this was not mere human policy, but in a peculiar sense the work of God. So myth made itself at home beside the baker’s hearth and the dyer’s vat: a woman might set her beans to soak overnight and speak, in the same breath, of omens in the sky; a man might sharpen his knife for cutting meat and whisper of statues perspiring.

That conviction rested not only on the sheer weight of a foreign invasion, but on moral emotions that the times had shaped into presentiments—emotions that had found a remarkable utterance in one voice. Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of San Marco, had spoken in such a way that men felt his words as they felt the city’s bells, entering not only the ear but the marrow.

On a September morning, when news rang through Italy that the French army had entered, Savonarola preached in the Cathedral from the text, “Behold I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth.” He believed—by a guidance beyond his own—he had reached that place in Genesis at just the hour when the world’s fact would fit the ancient figure; and he declared the “flood,” at once avenging wrath and purifying mercy, to be the French host. Many in his audience—men counted among the choicest spirits of the age, the cultivated minds of the most cultivated city—believed him too, and listened with a shuddering awe. For in Savonarola there was a power rarely paralleled: he pressed his belief into other men’s minds as a strong hand presses the stamp into warm wax.

As long as four years earlier he had proclaimed from Florence’s chief pulpit that a scourge was coming upon Italy and that by it the Church would be purified. He spoke as if he bore a mission like the Hebrew prophets, and as if the Florentines, to whom his warning was sent, were in some way a second chosen people. Prophetic talk was no rarity in that age—visions and heraldings were common enough in street and cloister. Yet precisely because so many candles flickered with petty gossip about fate, Savonarola stood out like a beacon whose flame shows the sea-banks and the rocks. His prophecy did not peer into corners; it cast light far out for warning and guidance.

To some sober minds, even, the supernatural character of his insight seemed attested by the very state of the world. At the close of 1492—the year Lorenzo de’ Medici died and Tito Melema came to Florence a wanderer—Italy was enjoying peace and prosperity with no near and definite danger. There was no fear of famine: corn, wine, and oil had been plenteous; new palaces rose in fair cities, new villas on pleasant slopes and summits; and those who had more were not much troubled by those who had less. Citizens’ armour was rusting; populations had grown tame, licking the hands of masters who bought ready-made armies as they bought goods from Smyrna. Even the fear of the Turk had dulled, and the Pope found it more immediately profitable to take bribes from him for a little prospective poisoning than to plan crusade or conversion.

It was a handsome establishment for the few—partitioned empire, roomy universal Church—in which lust and obscenity, lying and treachery, oppression and murder could be pleasant and useful and, if managed with dexterity, not dangerous. And as a fringe to the solid delights of tyranny and avarice, there was patronage of learning and art: flattery in the choicest Latin, painters who could set saint and harlot on the same wall with impartial skill. The Church, it was said, had never been more disgraced at its head, never shown fewer signs of renewing vital belief below; and yet it prospered. The heavens smiled; the earth did not tremble. And still there was a man in Florence who for two years and more had been declaring that a scourge was at hand—that the world was not framed for the lasting convenience of hypocrites, libertines, and oppressors. He saw, he said, a sword hanging from those smiling heavens: the sword of God’s justice, soon to fall in purifying punishment on Church and world.

In Ferrara, seventeen years before, the contradiction between men’s professed beliefs and their lives had struck him with force enough to destroy his appetite for the world, and at twenty-three he fled into the cloister. He believed God had entrusted the Church with the sacred lamp of truth for the guidance and salvation of men; and he saw the Church’s corruption turning it into a sepulchre that hid the lamp. As years passed, scandal multiplied; hypocrisy gave way to impudence. Had the world then ceased to have a righteous Ruler? Was the Church forsaken? No: in the Sacred Book, he held, there was a record of the past that showed the future in a glass; and when the wickedness of the chosen people—type of the Christian Church—had grown crying, God’s judgments had descended. Reason itself, he argued, declared vengeance imminent: what else could turn men from obstinate evil? And unless the Church were reclaimed, how could the promises be fulfilled that the heathen should be converted and the whole world subject to one true law?

He had seen his belief reflected in visions, as he had since youth. Yet the force of his demonstration lay less in those inward pictures than in his burning indignation at wrong; in his fervent faith in an unseen Justice that would end wrong, and an unseen Purity to which lies and uncleanness were abomination. To his ardent, power-loving soul—believing in great ends and longing to achieve them by a strong will—faith in a supreme righteous Ruler became one with faith in speedy divine interposition that would punish and reclaim.

Meanwhile, under the splendid masquerade of sacred and secular dignities which made the lives of fortunate churchmen and princely families so luxurious, conditions were at work that slowly disturbed the general festivity. Ludovico Sforza of Milan—gallant, splendid patron of Leonardo da Vinci—held the ducal crown in his grasp and wanted to set it on his own head rather than let it rest on the brow of a feeble nephew, who might, with little trouble, be poisoned out of the way. But Ludovico feared the Spanish-born old King Ferdinand and Crown Prince Alfonso of Naples, who, not disliking cruelty and treachery when they served themselves, objected to poisoning a near relative for the advantage of a Lombard usurper. Naples, in turn, feared its suzerain Pope Alexander Borgia. All three watched Florence anxiously, for its midway territory might, by underhand backing, determine the game; and all four, with every small state, feared Venice—cautious, stable, strong—stretching arms along the Adriatic and dreaming of ports on the western coast.

Lorenzo de’ Medici, they said, had long kept such jealousies from outbreak, maintaining Florence’s alliance with Naples and the Pope while persuading Milan it was for general advantage. But young Piero’s rash vanity soon nullified his father’s wary policy, and Ludovico, roused to suspicion of a league against him, sought a move that would checkmate his adversaries: he would invite the French king into Italy to take Naples as heir of Anjou. Ambassadors—“orators,” in that haranguing time—went and came; a recusant cardinal, refusing to acknowledge a Pope elected by bribery and being Alexander’s enemy, went and came too and seconded the invitation with hot rhetoric; the young king listened willingly. Through 1493 the rumour grew louder: Charles the Eighth would cross the Alps with a mighty army. Italian populations, long accustomed since Italy ceased to be the heart of empire to look for an arbitrator from afar, began to regard his coming as a means of redressing wrongs and avenging grievances.

In that rumour Savonarola heard the proof of his prophecy. What filled the ears of the prophets of old, if not the distant tread of foreign armies coming to do justice? He no longer stared vaguely at the horizon: he pointed to the rising cloud. The French host was the new deluge to cleanse the earth of iniquity; Charles was God’s chosen instrument, as Cyrus had been. All who desired good rather than evil should rejoice. The scourge, he promised, would fall destructively on the impenitent alone. Let any city—Florence above all, Florence beloved of God, since to its ear the warning had been sent—repent and turn as Nineveh had done, and the storm-cloud would roll past, leaving only refreshing drops.

Fra Girolamo’s word had been mighty; yet now, with the new Cyrus three months already in Italy and not far from Florence’s gates, expectation was mixed, and fear and distrust predominated. It was not clear he had redressed anything. The Florentines had little to thank him for. He held their strong frontier fortresses, surrendered to him by Piero without honourable terms; he had not quelled the alarming revolt of Pisa, encouraged by his presence to throw off the Florentine yoke; and the orators—even with a prophet among them—could win no assurance beyond this: he would settle everything when he was within Florence’s walls.

Still, there was one satisfaction: Piero had been fairly pelted out for that ignominious surrender, and in the act the Republic’s spirit had tasted its old heat. Therefore the preparations for this guest were not merely those of a city resigned to submission. Behind the bright hangings and banners that proclaimed joy, another kind of readiness lay hidden, agreed upon by government and people as naturally as a household keeps flour and salt against hard weather. Within walls, well out of sight, hired soldiers had been hastily called in from the surrounding districts. Old arms were furbished; sharp tools and heavy cudgels were laid carefully at hand to be snatched up on a moment’s notice. Excellent boards and stakes waited to become barricades; stones were stored for an unexpected hail from upper windows. Above all, there was a temper in Florence—newly relishing the pleasure of resistance—that was eager to fight any personage who might try to hector over them.

That humour was not softened by the sight of occasional parties of Frenchmen arriving beforehand to choose their quarters—perhaps with a hawk on the left wrist, and, in the figure that ran through men’s talk, a piece of chalk in the right to mark Italian doors; and creditable historians imply that many sons of France carried, at that time, something approaching swagger, a seasoning sure to whet Florentine appetite for stone-throwing. Such was Florence on the morning of the seventeenth of November, while the city’s ears lived between two sounds—the festive murmur under drapery and the remembered iron bell—and while even in kitchens, where oil hissed quietly and bread was cut, men spoke in their own dialect of fate and foreigners, and found that no dominant, polished Latin could translate the taste of fear that lay, sharp as raw garlic, on the tongue.
