Two weeks had slipped by since Newland Archer’s visit to Madame Olenska, and he found himself once again adrift in the familiar currents of his life—until the summons came.

Seated in his office at Letterblair, Lamson & Low, the soft hum of routine around him, he was called into the presence of the firm’s patriarch, Mr. Letterblair. The old gentleman, a monument to New York’s legal aristocracy, sat behind his massive mahogany desk, his white whiskers neatly trimmed, his brow furrowed with uncharacteristic vexation. Archer observed him with detached amusement—the man resembled nothing so much as a physician baffled by a stubborn ailment.

"My dear sir," Letterblair began—he always addressed Archer with formal deference—"I’ve asked you here to discuss a matter of some delicacy. One I’d prefer not to bring before Skipworth or Redwood just yet."

Archer lifted an eyebrow. The other senior partners were, of course, as much relics of the past as the names on the firm’s letterhead; Letterblair himself was, in effect, his own successor.

"For family reasons," Letterblair continued, then paused meaningfully.

Archer’s pulse quickened despite himself.

"The Mingotts," the old man clarified, with a slight bow, as if the name explained everything. "Mrs. Manson Mingott sought my counsel yesterday. Her granddaughter, the Countess Olenska, wishes to pursue a divorce." He tapped the desk with blunt fingers. "Given your… impending connection to the family, I thought it prudent to consult you before proceeding."

The words struck Archer like a physical blow. He had not thought of Ellen Olenska with any real urgency since that evening by the fire, though her presence lingered at the edges of his mind, a ghostly contrast to May Welland’s radiant solidity. The idea of divorce was distasteful—nearly unthinkable—in their world. Yet here it was, thrust upon him.

He resisted. "Surely one of her uncles—"

"They’ve been consulted. The family opposes the action, but the Countess insists on legal recourse."

Archer exhaled sharply. The packet of documents Letterblair slid toward him felt unnaturally heavy.

"Does she intend to remarry?"

"It’s been suggested, though she denies it."

Archer hesitated, then took the papers. He had no wish to involve himself, yet some stubborn instinct refused to let another man—any other man—sift through the intimate details of her life.

---

The letters revealed little and too much. Dry legal exchanges between solicitors, a terse note from Count Olenski—enough to curdle Archer’s stomach. By the time he returned to Letterblair’s office, his jaw was set.

"I’ll see her."

The old lawyer nodded, satisfied. "Dine with me tonight. We’ll discuss it further."

---

The evening air was crisp, the sky a clear, cold blue. Archer walked home slowly, as if the weight of the day pressed upon him. The thought of Ellen—exposed, vulnerable, reckless—gnawed at him. He had resented the Mingotts’ presumption in dragging him into this, yet now he could not turn away.

That night, over an opulent dinner in Letterblair’s dim, old-fashioned dining room, the conversation circled the inevitable.

"The family opposes the divorce," Letterblair declared, savoring his port. "Rightly so."

Archer’s grip tightened on his glass. "And if she chooses otherwise?"

Letterblair’s gaze sharpened. "Consider the consequences. Scandal. Unpleasantness."

The word struck Archer like a slap. *Unpleasantness.* The great sin of their world. He thought of May, of her serene certainty in the order of things. And then of Ellen, who dared to challenge it.

"I must hear her side before deciding," he said at last.

Letterblair studied him, wary but conceding. "Prudence, sir, is always advisable."

Archer left soon after, his mind already racing ahead—to the meeting tomorrow, to the woman who, despite everything, he could not bring himself to abandon.
