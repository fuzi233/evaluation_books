I made a full circuit of the ancient castle’s outer battlements before I circled back to its wide, unguarded gates, crossed the ragged, ravine-deep moat, and stepped into a flagstone court lined at regular intervals with towering, poplar-like trees whose trunks rose straight as spear shafts toward the hazy sky. At its center stood a specimen far taller than the rest, its upper boughs fanning out in a broad fronded crown that gave it the stark, otherworldly look of a wild palm. Peering between their massive shadowed stems, I caught glimpses of the palace itself: a long, low structure of a style entirely foreign to my world, its architecture carrying the faint, distinct echo of far-off Indian design. Lofty square towers jutted from each corner, and a single massive central dome rose from its roof to climb half the height of those towers. The main entrance, set dead center in the palace front, was a low, half-elliptical arch, and no guards, no servants, no living soul stood between me and its yawning, unlatched oak doors. I walked unchallenged straight inside.

The hall beyond was an elongated ellipse, dim and cool, and against one far wall stood a heavy barred cage. Curled inside it was a huge leopardess, head resting on her front paws, secured by a thick steel collar locked about her neck, her jaws muzzled with stitched leather and her pads swaddled in thick soft cloth. Her coat was pure white splotched with dark oval rosettes, her eyes wide and round with canoe-shaped slitted pupils and vivid glowing green irises. She watched me pass, but not a whisker twitched, not a claw shifted, not the tip of her tail (stretched rigid behind her like a bar of forged iron) moved a fraction of an inch. I could not have said for certain if she was a living creature or a masterfully crafted statue.

Two low, narrow passages branched off the far end of the vestibule. I picked the left one, and it quickly split into a maze of even tighter, irregular corridors, narrow enough that two people could barely squeeze past each other. At the tightest turn, a boy in the tattered livery of a palace page sprinted straight into my chest. He staggered back in naked terror, then scanned my unfamiliar face, puffed out his chest in a surge of insolent bravado, and demanded to know my business.
“To see the princess,” I told him.
“A likely tale!” he huffed. “Her highness hasn’t granted audiences to anyone all morning, not even me!”
I grabbed him by the scruff of his neck and shook him hard. “Take me to her this instant, or I will drag you through every corridor of this palace until I find her. She will hear exactly how her servants turn away uninvited guests.”
The boy gaped at me, then turned and pulled so hard at my wrist he moved like a blind man’s harnessed dog, leading me straight to a large steamy kitchen. A dozen listless servants loitered over cold hearths, their movements slow, their eyes half-lidded as if they had not properly woken in days. I braced for them to shout and fall on me to drive me out, but instead they froze, eyes blowing wide—not on me, but on something over my shoulder. Their faces bleached ghastly white the longer they stared.

I turned my head. The white leopardess stood at my heels, no cage, no muzzle, no padded paws, her green gaze fixed on the servants with a quiet menace that would have unsteadied the steeliest of hearts. One of the cooks, gathering his wits when he realized she made no move to lunge, started to stagger toward me. I released the page.
“Take me to the princess,” I said to him. “Tell her I wait audience.”
“Her highness will beat me within an inch of my life if I disturb her now, my lord,” he stammered.
“Tell her one who knows the white leech has come to see her.”
He paled further. “I dare not. She will kill me for bringing such a message.”
“You refuse?”
He darted one panicked glance at the great white leopardess standing warm and glowing against the dim kitchen stone, then turned and ran. The rest of the servants could not tear their eyes off her, rooted to the spot by unshakable fear. I turned to the graceful creature, who had dropped her muzzle to rest against my boot, white as fresh milk, a patch of warm light in the gloom of the room, and stooped to pat her head. She lifted her chin to look at me, and that small movement was enough to send every last servant scrambling for cover through the back doors. She rose onto her hind legs, rested her heavy soft paws on my shoulders, pricked her ears at a distant sound, and vanished around the corridor bend before I could even call out to her.

The cook I had sent to fetch the princess appeared in the doorway a moment later, bowing so low his forehead nearly brushed the floor. “Please follow me, my lord.”
My heart thudded hard against my ribs, bracing itself for the encounter I had chased across miles of this strange half-dead world. I followed him down winding, lightless passages until he pushed open a door and gestured me inside. The room beyond was so vast and dark I could not see its walls at all. A single small circle of faint light pooled on the floor, and everything beyond that circle was swallowed in absolute black. I tilted my head up, and far above, I spotted an oval aperture cut into the domed roof, the seams between blocks of polished black marble glinting faintly around its edge. The pool of light fell on the same black marble floor, perfectly fitted, and the entire elliptical wall of the space was hewn from the same stone, absorbing every scrap of light that touched it. I thought for a split second I caught a flicker of thin red lines crawling across the shadowed stone, but when I blinked to look closer, they were gone.

Without warning, a radiant form bloomed in the center of the darkness, casting soft gold light across every shadowed corner. She wore a robe of soft flowing white, and her hair spilled down her back in a cataract of jet black, as dark as the marble floor under her feet. Her eyes were a luminous, endless black, her arms and bare feet the color of warm polished ivory. She greeted me with the guileless, sweet smile of a girl barely just stepped over the threshold of womanhood.
A cold twist of dread knotted in my chest. This was nothing like the sharp, cruel woman who had struck me, scorned me, and left me bleeding by the banks of the hot stream. I stood frozen in the dark, staring at her, and she stared back into the shadows as if searching for my face. For a heartbeat she vanished entirely, and I thought she would refuse to acknowledge me at all—then her eyes blazed out of the dark directly into mine. She had spotted me, and crossed the room to meet me.
“You have found me at last,” she said, laying a light hand on my shoulder. “I knew you would.”
My whole frame quivered with warring feelings I had no name for: I was drawn to her utterly, and repelled in equal measure, neither sensation able to win out.
“You are shivering,” she said softly. “This cold stone room is no place for you. Come.”
I could not find words to speak; her beauty had struck me dumb, and her gentle sweetness held me frozen. She took my hand, pulled me into the pool of light, and I saw her face clearly for the first time.
“You have grown brown and sunburnt since last I saw you,” she said.
“I have barely been under a proper roof at all since you left me,” I replied.
“Whose was the roof you did stay under?” she asked, her tone light.
“I do not know the woman’s name. The one who tended me by the hot stream.”
She laughed, soft and low. “I would very much like to meet her. The instinct of hospitality runs very thin in my people.”
She kept hold of my hand, led me dozens of steps back through the dark to a thick curtain of black velvet, and drew it aside to reveal a broad flight of white marble stairs. She led me up them to a bright, sunlit chamber hung with silken tapestries.
“You must miss that hot flowing river terribly,” she said. “But there is a bath sunk in the marble floor in the corner, and there are no white leeches in its water. You will find a clean new wool garment waiting at the foot of your couch. When you are done, come down, and I will be waiting for you in the room to your left at the stair foot.”

I stood still long after she left, my chest tight with confused doubt. How could I possibly treat this sweet, sisterly woman as a thing of evil, when she had welcomed me so gently, fed me, offered me rest? When last we met she had struck me hard enough to make me bleed, and now she greeted me almost like a long-lost lover. She had reviled me, spat cruel words at me, and now she said she had known all along I would track her down. Did she know the doubts that gnawed at me? Could she possibly explain everything? Could I even bring myself to disbelieve her, if she did? The water in the great white bath was perfectly clear, sparkling like cut crystal, glowing so bright it looked almost celestial. I had not seen fresh clean water since I left my home, save for the hot stream, a few cups of water at the veiled woman’s cottage, and the muddy pools left behind the limping wounded leopardess. I plunged in.
The second the water closed over my skin, a strange, faint, delicate scent flooded my nostrils, something that made my skin prickle in a way I did not like. My old doubt flooded back, sharp and unyielding. Had she laced the bath water with some strange drug? Had she enchanted it? Why was there such abundant clean water here in the palace, when not a single drop could be found in the dead city stretching for miles outside its walls? I thought of the leopardess’ crushed, bleeding paw, and scrambled out of the bath before I could immerse myself further. I dried off quickly, pulled on the soft white wool robe embroidered along the hem and neckline, and headed down the stairs.

The room she had guided me to was perfectly round, hewn entirely from solid alabaster, with no windows at all. Light seeped through every inch of the walls, a soft pearly shimmer instead of harsh bright shine, and vague shadowy forms drifted across the dome and walls like loose rain clouds skittering over a pale grey-blue sky. The princess waited for me, wearing a stiffer robe stitched tight with argent rings, discs, rectangles and lozenges that made it look like a light suit of silver mail. It fell straight from her neck to hide her feet, its long open sleeves leaving her pale arms bare. On the ivory table at its center sat cakes, ripe golden fruit, a jug of frothy milk, a crystal carafe of pale rose wine, and a crusty white loaf.
“We do not kill to feed ourselves here,” she said. “But I think you will enjoy what I have to offer.”
I told her I could not have asked for anything finer. She settled on a velvet couch beside the table, and gestured for me to sit next to her. She poured me a bowl of milk, handed me the loaf, and bade me break off as large a piece as I liked, then filled two exquisitely carved silver goblets with the pale rose wine. “You have never tasted wine like this,” she promised.
I drank, and marveled: every wild flower from the slopes of Hybla and Hymettus must have lent their ghost to the honeyed, sun-warmed taste in my mouth.
“Now that you are rested and fed,” she went on, her voice soft and serious, “I will do my best to make myself clear to you. Our natures are so very different, it will not be easy. Your kind live to die: you ripen for a short while, then fade, and old age is a terror you all dread. My kind—there are very few of us—we live to keep living. Old age is our greatest desire, for the longer we exist, the closer we draw to perfect, endless ripening. I am not even fully ripe yet, and I have lived thousands of your mortal years. I never bothered to count exactly. Eternity is not something you can measure in numbers.
“So many suitors have sought me over the ages, and I have never loved a single one of them. They only wanted to own me, to enslave me, to hoard me like the rare gems my people hoard in their vaults. But when you found me by the stream, I found a real man. I tested you, and you withstood it. Your love was genuine—but it was not ideal, not the kind of love I crave. Pity is not love. What woman in any world would return true love for pity? Your love for me then was full of pity, and it was hateful to me. I knew if I showed you my gentle self, you would fall for me like all the rest, just to possess me, and I would not have that. I wanted a love that outlasts hopelessness, that outmeasures indifference, scorn, even hate. That is why I wore cruelty and ingratitude like a mask, why I left you there in the woods. I wanted you to follow me not out of pity, not out of any debt you thought you owed me, but for the sake of finding me, content with nothing but my presence, even my coldness. I failed the contest first. I yield to you. You found me.”
She looked at me, eyes soft with tears, and hid her face in her hands. But I had caught a sharp, glinting flash of calculation behind that tenderness, and I did not believe a single word of it. She had laid every trap to fascinate and enslave me, and she had nearly succeeded.
“Beautiful princess,” I said, “tell me how you came to be injured and helpless by that hot stream.”
“There are things I cannot explain to you yet,” she replied, “not until your love grows perfect enough to understand them. But I will answer any question you can ask. I set out to visit a far edge of my dominions, home to a savage dwarf people, strong and fierce, enemies to all law, all order, all progress. I did not fear them, I went alone, and I never thought to take any precautions. I did not know that the veiled woman who tended you by the stream, a mortal far weaker than I, had cast what you call a spell over its waters—no magic, just a natural force that operates in a realm mortals cannot perceive. I bounded across the stream, and the second my feet left the far bank, an indescribable cold flooded through every bone in my body. I dragged myself through the trees, unaware of anything else until I found you asleep, that horrible white leech fastened to your neck. I pulled it off you, and sucked the poison out of your wound. When you started to wake, I hid myself in the leaves.”
She stood, her eyes blazing brighter than any human eyes could ever blaze, and lifted her arms high over her head. “Everything you saved from death that day is yours,” she cried. “I will repay you as no woman in any world ever has. My power, my immortality, my beauty, all of it is yours.” She dropped to her knees beside me, rested her arms across my knees, and looked up at my face. For the first time I noticed a thick, clumsy leather glove covering her entire left hand. Behind it, I could see in my mind’s eye sharp claws and coarse dark fur, not soft human skin. Her other hand was perfect, as lovely as any hand could be, and I felt that if I let even a fraction of my guard drop, I would love her completely. I turned my eyes away before the temptation could take root.
A deafening, bone-shaking roar split the air. The alabaster walls trembled so hard I thought they would shatter into a thousand shards. The princess shuddered visibly.
“The wine was too strong for you,” she said, her voice trembling. “I should not have let you drink a full goblet. Go rest now. When you wake, you may ask me anything you like. I will walk you back to your room.”
She led me up the stairs ahead of her. “I do not wonder that roar startled you,” she said, with a forced little laugh. “It startled me, too, for a second. I thought for an instant the white leopardess had gotten loose. But that is impossible.”
I knew the roar had not come from her caged leopardess. It had been a warning, directed straight at me. As she left me at my chamber door with a bright, false smile, I caught the tight, anxious line around her mouth. She was lying.
