My crossing of Atlantis, though it dragged itself over leagues of thorn and hunger and fear, does not deserve the dignity of a full telling. The continent had learned to taste of ash. Even the wind seemed to have forgotten clean scents.

Only once did I stop long enough for a place to put its mark upon me.

I had run a stag down on open savannah, the kind of clean, wide grassland where a man can hear his own breath and measure danger by sound: the far-off cough of a cat, the grind of hooves, the low insect-drone that thickens when something large moves through it. When at last the animal went down, I worked quickly, because meat is a clock that starts the moment life stops. I rolled it, split the hide, and began to portion what I could carry—dense haunch, sweet backstrap, organs still warm and metallic. I salted nothing; I had no salt. I laid the strips on broad leaves to cool, and the air filled with that feral perfume of blood and crushed grass.

I took a moment to listen—an old habit from the Mountain days, where the world’s smallest noises could mean the difference between ritual and ruin. The savannah gave me nothing but wind and the thin clatter of seed heads.

Underfoot, though, the grass hid a thing which did not sing.

A great-legged worm—green as unripe fruit, glossy, obscene—lay coiled where my heel brushed it. It struck like a flash, a needle-thin head snapping forward. Pain bit into my lower thigh, sharp and clean as a knife’s point, then spread with a burning that felt as though the flesh itself had been salted with fire. I saw its tiny fangs withdraw, and with the same motion I drove my knife through its neck. It twisted, a hundred legs scrabbling like frantic fingers on a drumskin, writhing in loops that made a dry, sickening rustle in the grass.

I did what a man must do with venom: I made the wound bleed. I scraped the blade clean in earth, then stabbed around the punctures—hard, deep—so the blood would run and carry what poison it could away. The red streamed warm down toward my heel. The worm’s body slackened at last.

I shouldered my bundle of meat and went on, thankful, for I believed I had paid the price and bought my escape.

By afternoon the price demanded more.

Tightness filled the leg, then a throbbing which matched my pulse, as if something inside it beat against the skin. Each hour swelled me further, until the flesh looked stretched like parchment drawn over a drum. Nausea rose in waves that emptied me, again and again, until there was nothing left but bile and the bitter taste of fear. The forest around me—darkening as the sun sank—had become a place of noises too slow and too large: the distant crack of a branch under weight, the hush of something sliding through leaves. Had any of the great hunters found me then, I would have been no fight at all, only a stagger and a fall and meat to be torn.

When darkness came, I forced myself into a tree, hauling my swollen leg with both hands, teeth clenched until my jaw ached. I wedged myself into the crutch of a limb and stayed there, awake and shivering, listening to night’s acoustic tyranny: the moan of wind through canopy, the wet cough of unseen beasts, the drip of sap like slow footsteps. Pain kept time without mercy.

At dawn, when the night-feeders withdrew to their dens and the smaller birds began to stitch the air with thin calls, I climbed down. I leaned on my spear and limped onward through sombre forest. In better days, moss on the north side of trunks was a steady compass. Here, in my fever, I saw moss everywhere, as though the world had turned and left me behind. My head swam; the forest spun its own language around me—leaf-whisper, insect-hum, the sudden silence that comes when something watches.

Still I drove myself on, because the last instinct left to me was obedience: the High Council’s command, and the promise they had hung before my eyes like a lamp—Nais enlarged, Nais restored.

My final clear memory was of a figure moving in the black trees toward me—someone human, quick, intent. A hand took my arm. A voice spoke, close and urgent. Then waking tore loose and flew.

When sense returned, it came in layers: first the smell of smoke and crushed herbs; then the taste of bitterness on my tongue; then the sound of fire licking sticks and the soft crackle of a cave’s breath. I lay on a bed of leaves in a cleft between two rocks. The shelter was crude but cunning: stakes and rough-built barricades bristled at the entrance against the larger marauders. My thigh was bound and smeared with a poultice dark as mud, sharp with plant oils. Across the cavern squatted a woman over a small fire, turning a mess of wood-grubs glazed in honey. The grubs popped as they heated; fat hissed; the honey caramelized at the edges, sending up a scent that was both sweet and animal, like a poor man’s feast.

“How came I here?” I asked. My voice sounded strange in that confined stone, too loud, as if the cave amplified every weakness.

“I brought you,” she said, without looking up.

“And who are you?”

“A nymph, they call me,” she replied, and there was pride in it, though her clothes were patched and her hair tangled. “I practise as such. I gather herbs. I cure the diseases of those who come. I tell fortunes. I make predictions. For that I take what they can afford. And if they do not pay according to their means”—she stirred the grubs with a stick, and her eyes flashed in the firelight—“I clap on a curse to make them wither. It’s lean enough living when wars and pestilence leave so few poor folk in the land.”

“Do you visit Atlantis?”

“Not I. Phorenice would have me boiled in brine alive if she could lay hands on me. Our dainty Empress tolerates no magic but her own. They say she is for pulling down the Priests off their Mountain now.”

“So you do get news of the city?”

“Assuredly. It is my trade to get news—good or bad—or else how could I sell fortunes to the vulgar? And I marked your quality by your speech, my lord. You are not one of those who come to me for love-philtres and dung-charms. I have no fear of speaking plain.”

“Tell me then. Phorenice still reigns?”

“Most vilely.”

“As a maiden?”

“As the mother of twin sons. Tatho’s her husband now, and has been these three years.”

“Tatho,” I said, and the name tasted like old iron. “Who followed him as viceroy of Yucatan?”

“There is no Yucatan.” She clicked her tongue, as if scolding a child for asking after a dead goat. “A vast nation of little hairy men—so the tale goes—came from the West and overran it. Clubs of wood tipped with stone, that was their only arm, but numbers were their chief weapon. They wanted no plunder, no slaves, no cities. To eat the flesh of Atlanteans was their only lust, and they followed it prodigiously. Their numbers were like bees in a swarm.

“They came to each city in turn. The colonists slew them in thousands, but the weight of them always prevailed. They ate clean each city they took—clean, mind you—and left the ruins for the beasts. Then on to the next. At last they reached the coast towns. Tatho and the few that survived took ship and sailed home. They even ate Tatho’s wife for him.” She shrugged as if the Gods had merely changed the weather. “Curious persevering things, these little hairy men. The Gods send they do not cross the seas to Atlantis, or they would be worse plague to this poor country than Phorenice.”

I had heard of those creatures before—neither fully human nor wholly beast, a link held in check by divine restraint in deep forests, loosed at times as punishment. I did not trouble to share such knowledge with this cave-nymph. Her craft lived by what men could be made to fear. But grief struck me, sharp and private, at the thought of Yucatan—of all the labour, the planning, the building, the hopes fed like bread to a future that was now chewed to nothing.

I kept my name quiet. The time was not yet full for it to be spoken aloud.

“What about Egypt?” I asked.

“In its accustomed darkness,” she said, and spat into the fire. “Who cares for Egypt these latter years? Who cares for anyone but himself and his own estate? Time was, the country folk and the hunters brought me offerings here for sheer piety. Now they come only if they can bargain. So instead of living fat and hearty, I make lean meals off honey and grubs.” She lifted the stick, letting a strand of honey stretch and snap. “A poor life, a nymph’s, in these latter years. It’s the fashion for all classes to believe in no kind of mystery now.”

“What manner of pestilence did you speak of?”

“I have not seen it.” For a moment, genuine relief softened her face. “Thank the Gods it has not come this way. But they say it has grown from the folk Phorenice has slain and left unburied. She is always slaying. The bodies lie thicker than birds and beasts can eat them. For which of our sins did the Gods let her reign? I wish she and her twins were boiled alive in brine before they came between an honest nymph and her living.

“They say she has put an image of herself in all the temples now, and ordered prayers and sacrifices night and morning. She has decreed all other Gods inferior to herself and forbidden their worship. Those that are not sufficiently devout have their hamstrings slit by her tormentors, to aid them constantly into a devotional attitude.” She looked at me then, as if remembering I was made of flesh. “Will you eat of my grubs and honey? There is nothing else. Your back was bloody with carrying meat when I found you, but you had lost your load. You must either taste this mess of mine now, or go without.”

Hunger is not proud. I ate.

The grubs were soft inside their browned skins, like marrow, and the honey lent them a sweetness that almost made them respectable. Smoke from the sticks gave a faint resinous bitterness. I chewed slowly, forcing my mouth to remember pleasure even in such fare, because a man who forgets taste forgets the world he fights for. Outside the cave, the forest’s noises thickened and thinned with the hours, and each change in its chorus seemed to tug at memory—voices of villages, lullabies in dialects already thinning like old cloth, the prayers of women spoken in half-forgotten words.

I stayed six days. She worked on my leg with herbs and with that half-theatrical certainty of the self-styled wise. Some of it was trick, no doubt, but not all. The poultices stung and cooled; the swelling drew back; the fever broke. When at last I could bear weight again, I paid her as best a man can who carries no coin: I hunted and brought her down a fat cloven-hoofed horse from the plains, and left it dressed, the meat portioned cleanly so it would not spoil. She watched my cuts with keen eyes, as if judging not just my skill but my respect for the animal’s gift.

Then I went on.

From there onward the land showed scars where once it had been sturdy. Here had been homesteads—some built in trees to escape cave-bear and cave-tiger, some built on the ground of ponderous logs clamped and dovetailed so no beast could pull them down. A man within those log walls could thrust a spear through the interstices and wound, sometimes kill, anything that came for him.

Now silence owned them. Fire-hearths before doors were choked with weeds. Vegetable patches had been taken back into scrub and jungle by the greedy fingers of the forest. Farther on, villages appeared with their strong walls—but the gateway logs lay strewn, half-swallowed by sprouting undergrowth. Only kitchen middens remained: heaps of broken bones and cracked potsherds, mute proof that once there were mouths here, and words spoken over meals.

Phorenice’s influence seemed to have spread like blight over what had once been smiling, easy-living land. Even the soundscape had changed. Where villages should have held the dawn-clatter of pestles, the chatter of children, the lowing of penned beasts, there was only wind and the harsh calls of opportunist birds.

So far, men had interfered little with me. Many fled with their women at the bare sight of me; some stood with threatening faces but did not attack, seeing I offered no insult. A few gave shelter and provision—thin soups, bitter roots, what little their fear allowed them to spare. But as I neared the city and began to cross muddy beaten paths, the density of people increased, and with it the chance of delay.

Each village had its watcher in the tower. If he spied me, he would cry, “Here is a masterless man,” and the people would rush out to spoil me of weapons and appoint me labourer, as though a free body were merely a tool to be seized.

I had no desire to slaughter these wretches. Pity weighed heavier than anger; they had been ground down until they could imagine no law but hunger. Often words served. Sometimes I let my voice go hard as iron, and they would stare at my fierceness and step aside. When words failed, I struck as lightly as I could, my object always to move forward and leave no unnecessary dead behind. Soon it became my custom to turn into the forest and make circuits whenever I came within smell of their garbage—sour refuse, stale smoke, the rank reek of crowded misery.

Among greater towns I kept beyond their walls, refusing to be snagged by any governor’s whim, any new edict that might turn a traveller into property. My progress became furtive, but pride did not bark at me too loudly. The fever of haste burned hot, and I had little space within me for other emotions.

At last, within half a day’s journey of Atlantis itself, I saw the Sacred Mountain rise with its ring of fires looming high beside the city, a constant blaze like a crown half-set into earth. Caution trebled. Everywhere the country showed it had been drained of fighting men. Everywhere women prayed—not for victory’s glory, but for an ending: the rout of the Priests or the killing of Phorenice, so the land might at last have peace enough to lick its wounds.

An army invested the Sacred Mountain, and its single approach was guarded narrow as a needle’s eye. Even after such a journey, it seemed I would have to sit down helpless, unable to fulfil the High Council’s command and earn the reward promised—Nais.

Force would be useless. I could have one good fight—a gorgeous fight—one man against an army, and then my usefulness would end in a heap of meat for crows. No. This was the occasion for guile.

I found covert on the outskirts of a wood and lay there a day and a night, brain aching with plans that broke like thin pottery. Across the plain stood the grim walls of Atlantis, and beyond them the heads of temples and palaces and pyramids. The step-sides of the royal pyramid caught the light strangely. Phorenice had overlaid their former whiteness with sheets of shining yellow metal. Gold—soft, hungry metal—turned architecture into proclamation.

But it was not the gaudy change that moved me. I remembered the square before that pyramid, and the granite throne carved with the snake and the outstretched hand. In the hollow beneath that throne lay Nais, my love, asleep these eight years because of the drug given to her—alive still, waiting, if only I could reach Zaemon where he defied the Empress and announce my coming.

In that wood’s edge I raged, not aloud—there were too many ears in the world now—but inwardly, furious at my own failure to find a way in. Then, on the morning that followed, a man came running toward me.

“You need not threaten me with your weapons,” he called. “I mean no harm. It seems you are Deucalion—though I should not have known you in those rags and skins, behind that tangle of hair and beard. You will give me your good word. Believe me, I have not loitered unduly.”

He was a lower priest I knew and held in little esteem: Ro, greedy and slick, a man who worshipped comfort as faithfully as any god. His breath rasped. Even his haste had a cautious sound, as if he feared the noise of his own footsteps.

“From whom do you come?” I asked.

“Zaemon laid a command on me. He came to my house—how he got there I cannot tell, seeing Phorenice’s army blocks all passage to and from the Mountain.” Ro licked his lips, and the gesture made him look more anxious than respectful. “I told him I wished to be mixed with none of his schemings. I am a peaceful man, Deucalion, and I have taken a wife who requires nourishment. I still serve in the same temple, though we have swept out the old Gods by order of the Empress and put her image in their place. The people are tidily pious nowadays, those that are left, and the living is consequently easy. Far more offerings now than in the old days. I had no wish to be mixed with matters that might deprive me of a snug post—and my head to boot.”

“I can believe it all of you, Ro.”

“But there was no denying Zaemon.” He thrust out his right hand. The fingers hung lifeless, the wrist slack. “You see that? Dead and paralysed from the wrist. A gift of Zaemon. He bestowed it, he said, to make me collect my attention.” His voice wavered, part fear, part indignation. “He called me apostate, would not let me put up a word in defence of how the change was forced upon me. Finally he said I might either do his bidding on a certain matter to the letter, or take the punishment my falling away had earned. ‘I shall not kill you,’ said he, ‘but I will cover all your limbs with paralysis, such as you have tasted already, and when at length death reaches you in some gutter, you will welcome it.’”

“If Zaemon said those words,” I told him, “he meant them. So you accepted the alternative.”

“Had I—having a wife depending on me—any other choice? I asked his pleasure. It was to find you when you came in from some distant part of the land, and deliver to you his message.

“‘Then tell me where is the meeting place,’ said I, ‘and when.’

“‘There is none appointed, nor is the day fixed,’ said he. ‘You must watch and search always for him. But when he comes, you will be guided to his place.’” Ro’s eyes darted toward the trees, as if the very leaves might be informers. “Well, Deucalion, I think I was guided, but how, I do not know. Now I have found you, and if there is such a thing as gratitude, I ask you to put in your word with Zaemon that this deadness be taken away. It is an awful thing for a man to go through life like this, for no real fault of his own. Zaemon could cure it from where he sat, if he was so minded.”

“You seem still to have very full faith in some of the old priests,” I said.

“I serve the one that gives me employment,” he muttered, then hurried on, eager to finish. “The message is simple. You are to make a great blaze. Fire the grasses of the plain in front of this wood if you choose. And on the night that follows, you are to go round to that flank of the Sacred Mountain away from the city where the rocks run down sheer. There they will lower a rope and haul you up to their hands above.”

“It seems easy. I thank you for your pains. I will ask Zaemon that your hand may be restored.”

“You shall have my prayers if it is.” His gaze crawled over my rags, my crude skins, my emptiness. “And look—Deucalion—it is a small matter. It would be less likely to slip your memory if you saw to it at once on your landing. Later you may be disturbed. Phorenice is bound to pull you down off your perch up there now she has made her mind to it. She never fails once she sets her hand to a thing. Indeed, if she was no Goddess at birth, she is making herself into one very rapidly. She has got all the ancient learning of our Priests, and more besides. She has discovered the Secret of Life these recent months—”

“She has found that?” The words tore out of me. For a moment even the wood seemed to hush. “How? Only the Three know that. It is beyond our knowledge even who are members of the Seven.”

“I know nothing of her means. But she has the secret, and now she is as good an immortal—so she says—as any of them. Dangerous for me to be missing from my temple overlong, so I will go. You will carry that matter in your mind? It means much to me.” He hesitated, shame and greed battling in his throat. “And if you think my service is of value to you—some small coin, or even a link of bronze? I am at great expense buying nourishment for my wife.”

“You see me poor,” I said. “Practically destitute.”

“If you have nothing, you cannot give.” His voice turned brisk with self-justification. “So I’ll bid you farewell.”

He went, and I was not sorry. Ro had always smelled to me of stale incense and fresh appetite.

I wasted no time. Almost before his timorous heels were clear, I put fire to the grasses.

The plain took flame with a roar like a great beast waking. Dry stalks caught in a bright rush; seed-heads exploded into sparks; smoke rolled thick and bitter, a dark tongue licking the sky. The crackle became a wall of sound, drowning the smaller life-noises—no birds, no insects, only the huge consuming hiss and pop. It was a signal, yes, but it was also a declaration: that the land could still be made to speak in sudden, ruthless language.

That night, under cover of darkness and smoke’s lingering stink, I went to the appointed place. The cliff on the Mountain’s far flank fell sheer and smooth, gaunt as bone. No rope hung there at first, and I waited, listening for any human sound above—boots on stone, murmured prayer—but heard only the distant, steady roar of the Mountain’s ring-fires, and far below, the faint dull undertone of the city’s unrest.

Then a rope slid down the face like a slender snake.

I made a loop, slipped it over one leg, and pulled hard as signal. Those above hauled. The rope bit into my flesh; stone scraped my palms; my muscles shook with the effort, but the ascent was clean. Phorenice’s troops had not thought to guard that seamless precipice.

So I went back to the Sacred Mountain after so many toilsome, warring years.

The men who hauled me up were old. Their breath came in harsh, wet pants, and I wondered, at first, why younger priests had not been set to such work. But with the Clan it is always taken for granted that an order is given for the best. Besides, these old ones did not offer themselves for questioning. They took me at once to Zaemon, and that was what I wished.

Zaemon greeted me with the royal sign. His voice cut through the constant fire-noise of the crest, steady as a bell.

“All hail to Deucalion,” he cried, “King of Atlantis, duly called thereto by the High Council of the priests.”

“Is Phorenice dead?” I asked.

“It remains for you to slay her and take your kingdom,” Zaemon said, “if, indeed, when all is done, there remains a man or a rood of land to govern. The sentence has gone out that she is to die, and it shall be carried into effect—even though we have to set loose the most dreadful powers that are stored in the Ark of the Mysteries and wreck this continent in our effort. We have borne with her infamies all these years by command sent down by the most High Gods; but now she has gone beyond endurance. They it is who have given the word for her cutting off.”

“You are one of the highest Three,” I said. “I am only one of the Seven. You best know the cost.”

“There can be no counting the cost now, my brother—and my king. It is an order.”

“It is an order,” I repeated, and the formality tasted like vow and chain. “So I obey.”

Zaemon’s face was hard, but beneath it lived a fury older than any one man. “If it were not impious to justify the Gods,” he said, “it would be easy. The woman usurped the throne—yet she was forgiven and bidden rule wisely. She tampered with our holy religion—yet she was forgiven. She killed the peoples of Atlantis in greedy useless wars and destroyed the country’s trade—yet she was forgiven. She desecrated the old temples and has set up images of herself to be worshipped as deity—yet she was forgiven. But at last her evil cleverness discovered the tremendous Secret of Life and Death, and there she overstepped the boundary of the High Gods’ forbearance.

“I myself went to carry a final warning and faced her in the great banqueting-hall. Solemnly I recited the edict. She chose to take it as a challenge. She would live eternally herself, and share her knowledge with those that pleased her. Tatho, her husband, should also be immortal. Indeed, if she thought fit, she would cry the secret aloud so even the common people might know it, and death from mere age would become a legend.

“She cared no wit how she might upset the laws of Nature. She was Phorenice, and the highest law of all. And finally she defied me there, and defied also the High Gods that stood behind my mouth. ‘My magic is as strong as yours, you pompous fool,’ she cried, ‘and presently you shall see the two stand side by side upon their trial.’

“She began to collect an army from that moment. We made our preparations. It was discovered by our arts that you still lived, and King of Atlantis you were made by solemn election. How you were summoned, you know as nearly as it is lawful one of your degree should know. How you came, you understand best. But here you are, my brother, and being King now, you must order all things as you see best for the preservation of your high estate—and we others live only to give you obedience.”

“Then being King,” I said, “I can speak without seeming to threaten. I must have my Queen first, or I am not strong enough to give my whole mind to this ruling.”

“She shall be brought here,” Zaemon said, and for the first time in that harsh place his voice softened a grain.

“So. Then I will be a general now,” I answered, “and see to the defences of this place, and view the men who are here to stand behind them.”

I went out, and Zaemon gave place and followed.

Night still lay over the world, but there is no darkness on the upper Sacred Mountain. The ring of fires—fed eternally from the earth-breath that wells from below—burns round one half of the crest, lighting it always as bright as day, and forming no small part of its fortification. The flames made their own weather of sound: a continuous roaring, broken by sudden gusting flares like shouted syllables. It was said that, in the early dawn of history, men first came to the Mountain as stronghold because of this natural defence—because fire is a wall that never sleeps, and its voice drowns the approach of enemies until they are already burning.

Under that ceaseless flame-song, with the city and its golden pyramid crouched beyond, I stood returned at last to the Mountain, and the siege tightened like a noose around all our fates.
