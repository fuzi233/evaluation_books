"Mist clogs the sunshine, / Smoky dwarf houses / Hem me round everywhere." — Matthew Arnold.

The following afternoon, approximately twenty miles north of Milton, the carriage turned onto a narrow branch line leading toward Heston. Heston itself stretched along the coast as one long, straggling street, possessing a distinct character that set it apart from the southern English bathing resorts and those on the European continent. Using a Scottish term, everything possessed a "purpose-like" quality. The country carts were built with more iron and less wood or leather; the pedestrians, though ostensibly on holiday, moved with a busy, industrious mindset. The colors were grayer, more enduring, lacking the fleeting gaiety found elsewhere. Notably absent were the smock-frocks common among rural folk in the south; deemed impediments to movement and prone to snagging on machinery, they had fallen out of fashion. In southern towns, Margaret observed, shopkeepers would lounge idly at their doors when not working, enjoying the breeze. Here, however, even moments of leisure were spent engaging in commerce within the shops—unnecessarily unrolling and re-rolling ribbons.

These contrasts struck Margaret deeply as she and her mother sought lodgings the next morning. Having already incurred costs exceeding Mr. Hale's expectations at local hotels, they gratefully accepted the first clean, cheerful rooms available. For the first time in days, Margaret felt a profound sense of rest, accompanied by a dreamy luxuriance. The distant sea lapped the sands with a rhythmic sound; nearby, donkey boys cried out; unusual scenes drifted before her like paintings she cared not to analyze before they vanished. A stroll to the beach revealed a soft, warm sea-air despite the late November date, with a vast, misty horizon touching a tender-colored sky and the white sails of distant boats glinting silver in pale sunlight. It seemed she could dream her life away in such a state of pensiveness, making the present all-encompassing, refusing to dwell on the past or fear the future. Yet, the future awaited, however stern.

One evening, plans were set for Margaret and her father to visit Milton-Northern the next day to secure housing. Mr. Hale had received correspondence from both Mr. Bell and Mr. Thornton and was eager to clarify his prospects through an interview with the latter. Though Margaret harbored a repugnance toward manufacturing towns and hoped her mother's health benefited from the Heston air, duty prevailed.

Approaching Milton, a deep leaden cloud hung over the horizon, darkened against the pale winter sky—a sign of frost that had already touched Heston. Closer to the town, the air carried a faint taste of smoke, a loss of herbal fragrance rather than a pungent odor. They were whisked rapidly through long, straight, seemingly endless rows of uniformly built brick houses. Occasionally, a massive, multi-windowed factory loomed like a hen among chicks, belching black, "unparliamentary" smoke that accounted for the ominous cloud. Heavy-laden lorries frequently blocked the thoroughfares between the station and the hotel. While Margaret had driven into cities before, the vehicles here differed; every wagon bore cotton, either in raw burlap sacks or woven bales of calico. The populace thronged the sidewalks, dressed in materials that suggested wealth but worn with a slovenly looseness that struck Margaret as distinct from the shabby smartness of London's lower classes.

"New Street," Mr. Hale noted. "I believe this is the principal artery. Bell mentioned that the opening of this street from a lane into a major thoroughfare thirty years ago significantly increased his property's value. Mr. Thornton's mill must be nearby, as he rents from Bell, though I suspect his operations center around his warehouse."

"Where is our hotel, Papa?" Margaret asked.

"Near the end of this street. Shall we eat lunch before or after viewing the houses listed in the *Milton Times*?"

"Oh, let us finish our business first."

"Agreed. I will check for any notes from Mr. Thornton, who promised to inform me regarding suitable properties, and then we shall depart. We will keep the cab; it is safer than wandering and risking missing the afternoon train."

Finding no letters, they proceeded to house-hunting. With a budget of thirty pounds per year, they could have secured a spacious home with a garden in Hampshire. In Milton, however, finding even basic accommodations—two sitting rooms and four bedrooms—proved elusive. After visiting numerous options and rejecting each, they exchanged looks of dismay.

"We must settle for the second option," Mr. Hale decided. "That one in Crampton, isn't it? It had three sitting rooms. Do you recall our laughter at the disparity with only three bedrooms? Nevertheless, I have devised a plan. The downstairs front room shall serve as your study and our dining room. Mamma deserves the cheeriest sitting room possible upstairs. That room with the atrocious blue-and-pink paper and heavy cornice actually offers a fine view over the plain, bending down toward the river or canal. You may take the small bedroom behind, in the projection above the kitchen. You and Mamma can share the room behind the drawing room, and that closet in the roof will make a splendid dressing room for you."

"But what of Dixon and the maid we intend to hire?"

"Wait, Father. I am overwhelmed by my own genius for management. Dixon gets the rear sitting room; she complains so bitterly of the stairs at Heston. The maid takes the sloping attic above yours and Mamma's. Does that suffice?"

"I suppose it will. However, the wallpaper... What taste! And the excessive color and heavy cornices!"

"Never mind, Papa! Surely you can persuade the landlord to re-paper the drawing room and your bedroom, since Mamma will frequent them most. Your bookshelves will conceal much of the gaudy pattern in the dining room."

"Do you truly consider it the best choice? If so, I shall immediately visit Mr. Donkin, referenced in the advertisement. I will escort you back to the hotel for lunch and rest; by the time the meal is served, I shall return. I trust I can procure new papers."

Margaret shared his hope silently. She had never encountered a taste for ornate decoration that rivaled the plain simplicity she considered the true framework of elegance. Mr. Hale guided her through the hotel entrance, leaving her at the foot of the stairs to await his return. Just as she placed her hand on the door of their sitting room, a brisk waiter approached.

"I beg your pardon, madam. The gentleman departed so swiftly I failed to notify him. Mr. Thornton arrived shortly after you left. Believing you would return within the hour based on his instructions, I informed him. He returned about five minutes ago and requested to wait for Mr. Hale. He is currently in your room, madam."

"Thank you. My father returns momentarily; you may inform him then."

Opening the door, Margaret entered with her characteristic straight, fearless, and dignified demeanor. She felt no awkwardness; accustomed to social proprieties, she intended to extend full courtesy to a business associate of her father. Mr. Thornton, conversely, was considerably surprised and disconcerted. Expecting a quiet, middle-aged clergyman, he found instead a young woman of striking, frank dignity, unlike anyone he typically encountered. Her attire was austere: a high-quality straw bonnet trimmed with white ribbon, draped in a large Indian shawl worn with imperial grandeur. Confused by her appearance, he noted her simple, unabashed gaze, which conveyed that his presence held no significance to her beautiful, pale countenance, eliciting no flush of surprise. He recalled hearing of Mr. Hale's daughter but had assumed she was a child.

"Mr. Thornton, I presume!" Margaret said after a brief pause, allowing his hesitation to pass. "Please, sit down. My father brought me to the door only a moment ago but was unaware of your arrival and has stepped out briefly on business. He will return instantly. I apologize for the inconvenience of your double visit."

Though accustomed to authority, Mr. Thornton found himself immediately under her subtle command. Previously impatient due to lost time on a market day, he now calmly seated himself at her bidding.

"Do you happen to know where Mr. Hale has gone? Perhaps I might locate him."

"He has gone to Mr. Donkin in Canute Street. He is the landlord of the house my father intends to rent in Crampton."

Mr. Thornton recognized the house. He had viewed it at Mr. Bell's request to assist Mr. Hale, partly motivated by sympathy for a clergyman displaced under difficult circumstances. Initially, he had deemed the Crampton residence ideal. Now, observing Margaret's superior bearing and movements, he felt a sudden shame for having previously considered it adequate for the Hales, despite noting a certain vulgarity that had struck him earlier. Margaret could not suppress her innate poise, yet features like her short, curled upper lip, round massive chin, and the way she carried her head imparted an impression of haughtiness to strangers. Exhausted, she preferred silence, yet felt compelled to maintain gentleness toward this stranger, who appeared somewhat rough after his exposure to Milton's streets and crowds. She wished he would leave, as he had once threatened, rather than sit there responding to her remarks with curt brevity. Removing her shawl and draping it over the chair back, she faced him squarely, illuminated by the light.
