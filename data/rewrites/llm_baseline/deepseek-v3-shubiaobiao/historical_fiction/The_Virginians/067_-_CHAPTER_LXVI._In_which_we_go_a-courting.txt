The Regent’s Park, with its menagerie of finned and feathered captives, often draws visitors who, in observing nature’s marvels, find their own thoughts mirrored in the creatures before them. So it was that I, while gazing at the flounder’s graceful dance through its watery sphere, found my mind wandering not to fish, but to our Virginian friends.

Here was a creature plain upon a plate, yet in its element, a vision of elegance—its dark back arching, its pale belly flashing, every motion a harmony. Out of water? A pitiful flapping, a gasping struggle. So too with men: place them where they belong, and they thrive; exile them, and they wither. Harry Warrington, long adrift, had at last found his sea.

The epaulet upon his shoulder was more than ornament—it was a summons to life. The camp, the march, the camaraderie of the mess, the crisp orders of the parade ground—all invigorated him. Had I the pen of a campaign chronicler, I would paint Harry in his scarlet and gold, recount his midnight vigils, his dawn drills, his laughter among comrades. But that task is for abler hands. Let it suffice that Harry had become a soldier, and a good one.

His brother George, ever generous, rejoiced in Harry’s renewal. With pride, he wrote to their mother, extolling Harry’s courage—modest as ever, Harry would never boast for himself. George proposed to share the cost of Harry’s advancement, feeling duty-bound to restore what fortune had taken. For had not Harry, believing himself heir, lived as one? Now George, restored to life, would make amends.

But George’s letter held more than fraternal duty. He spoke of love.

The Lamberts—kind, steadfast friends—had been a second family to the Warringtons. In their home, George had found more than shelter; he had found a heart that answered his. Miss Theodosia Lambert was no Helen, nor a Sappho—yet in her presence, George’s own spirit brightened. Her voice was the sweetest music; her company, his keenest joy. To her, he was not the heir of Virginia, but the man she cherished.

Their courtship was a quiet idyll—stolen glances, shared books, walks through St. James’s where the sermon went unheard. He sent her verses, flowers, notes that begged no reply (yet received them). He charmed her siblings, sparred with her sister Hetty, and paid such court to Mrs. Lambert that the General jested his wife was half in love herself.

But love, however pure, must reckon with the world.

At Castlewood, Madam Esmond received George’s tidings with a measured nod. A prudent match, she allowed, if the girl was bred as befitted an Esmond. Yet Mountain, her companion, sniffed at the notion. "They’ll all be scheming for his fortune," she muttered.

"Take care your own schemes don’t show too plain," Madam retorted. The barb struck home—Mountain had nursed hopes for her Fanny. A bitter exchange followed, revealing more than either wished.

Meanwhile, George, dutiful son, obeyed his mother’s commands to live as his station demanded. His table was lavish, his liveries fine, his credit unquestioned. The Lamberts, though no mercenaries, could not ignore his prospects. A virtuous, well-born suitor, devoted to their Theo—what more could they wish?

Yet one obstacle remained: Madam Esmond’s consent. Until her word arrived, hearts hung suspended. The French privateers, ever a threat, made each post-day an agony. Would the letter come? And when it did—what then?

Love, like the flounder, thrives in its rightful sphere. Out of it, even the noblest heart may gasp. But place it where it belongs, and behold—it shines.
