At sight of the room, perfectly undisturbed except for the harsh, percussive shattering of oak that had deeply wounded the delicate acoustic ecology of the ancestral schloss, we began to cool a little. The violent splintering of the door had been a jarring dissonance, a violent intrusion upon the centuries-old quiet of our home. Once the heavy, disruptive footfalls of the majordomo and his myrmidons had faded down the corridor, restoring a fragile semblance of domestic peace, we recovered our senses sufficiently to dismiss the men entirely. 

It had struck Mademoiselle that possibly Carmilla had been wakened by the cacophonous uproar at her door, and in her first panic had jumped from her bed to hide in a press. We imagined her cowering among the heavy linens that breathed the olfactory history of the house—deep, resonant notes of dried woodruff, crushed juniper berries, and aged lavender—unable to emerge until the chaotic auditory disruption had ceased. We recommenced our search, calling her name into the resonant stone archways. It was all to no purpose. Our perplexity and agitation increased. We examined the windows, but they were perfectly secured. I implored Carmilla to end this cruel trick, craving the return of our quiet, rhythmic domesticity. It was all useless. I was convinced she was not in the room, nor in the dressing room, the door of which was still locked on this side. 

Had Carmilla discovered one of those secret passages which the agrarian elders of the valley—speaking in a dying local dialect that the modern world was rapidly forgetting—claimed were woven into the very masonry of the schloss? The tradition of their exact situation had been lost, swallowed by the erosion of cultural memory. We waited in Madame’s room through the remaining hours of darkness, the circular pacing of our anxieties mirroring the interwoven timelines of all the women who had kept vigil in these ancient halls.

Daylight brought no solution. The rhythmic, sensory routines of our household were paralyzed. The morning kitchen—usually a symphony of rhythmic chopping, the scraping of cast-iron skillets, and the comforting, yeasty exhalation of the multi-generational sourdough starter—was abandoned in the panic. Every part of the chateau was searched; the grounds were explored. The stream, known to the old villagers by an untranslatable term meaning 'the deep weeping of the earth', was about to be dragged. My father was in distraction, horrified by the tale he would have to translate to the poor girl’s mother. I, too, was almost beside myself, my grief manifesting as a hollow, gnawing physical hunger, a draining of my very essence.

By one o'clock, with still no tidings, I sought the grounding solace of sustenance. I meticulously steeped a restorative broth of roasted marrow bone, foraged wild garlic, and cellar-aged parsnips, hoping the rich, complex steam and the quiet, bubbling rhythm of the copper pot would anchor my fraying mind. Carrying the porcelain bowl, I ran up to Carmilla’s room. 

I found her standing at her dressing table. I was astounded. She beckoned me to her with her pretty finger, in absolute, pristine silence. Her face expressed extreme fear. I ran to her in an ecstasy of joy; I kissed and embraced her again and again, catching the faint, unplaceable scent of cold night air and crushed minerals on her skin. I ran to the bell and rang it vehemently, a sharp brassy intrusion into the silence, to bring others to the spot and relieve my father’s anxiety.

“Dear Carmilla, what has become of you all this time?” I exclaimed. “Where have you been? How did you come back?”

“Last night has been a night of wonders,” she said softly, her subjective experience already resisting my desperate need for explanation.

“For mercy’s sake, explain all you can.”

“It was past two last night,” she murmured, “when I went to sleep as usual in my bed, with my doors locked... My sleep was uninterrupted, and, so far as I know, dreamless; but I woke just now on the sofa in the dressing room there, and I found the door between the rooms open, and the other door forced. How could all this have happened without my being wakened? It must have been accompanied with a great deal of noise, and I am particularly easily wakened; and how could I have been carried out of my bed without my sleep having been interrupted, I whom the slightest stir startles?”

By this time, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a babble of servants had crowded into the room, their overlapping voices creating an auditory clutter that threatened to overwhelm the senses. Carmilla was overwhelmed with inquiries. She had but one story to tell. Her subjective experience of the night remained deeply untranslatable, a lost dialect of shadows that our waking vocabulary could not articulate. 

My father took a turn up and down the room, thinking. I saw Carmilla’s eye follow him for a moment with a sly, dark glance. When my father had cleared the room of the servants' noise, Mademoiselle having gone in search of a little bottle of valerian and salvolatile, he came to her thoughtfully. He took her hand, led her to the sofa, and sat down beside her, preparing to analyze the mystery with the meticulous rigor of a food historian dissecting a corrupted, archaic recipe manuscript.

“Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture, and ask a question?”

“Who can have a better right?” she said. “Ask what you please, and I will tell you everything. But my story is simply one of bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Put any question you please, but you know, of course, the limitations mamma has placed me under.”

“Perfectly, my dear child. I need not approach the topics on which she desires our silence. Now, the marvel of last night consists in your having been removed from your bed and your room, without being wakened... I will tell you my theory and ask you a question.”

Carmilla was leaning on her hand dejectedly; Madame and I listened breathlessly, attuned to the quiet frequency of his voice. 

“Now, my question is this. Have you ever been suspected of walking in your sleep?”

“Never, since I was very young indeed.”

“But you did walk in your sleep when you were young?”

“Yes; I know I did. I have been told so often by my old nurse.”

My father smiled and nodded. He was ready to impose the dominant lexicon of rationality over the mythic ambiguity of her absence. “Well, what has happened is this. You got up in your sleep, unlocked the door... you again took the key out, and carried it away with you to some one of the five-and-twenty rooms on this floor... Do you see, now, what I mean?”

“I do, but not all,” she answered. “And how, papa, do you account for her finding herself on the sofa in the dressing room, which we had searched so carefully?”

“She came there after you had searched it, still in her sleep, and at last awoke spontaneously... I wish all mysteries were as easily and innocently explained as yours, Carmilla,” he said, laughing. “And so we may congratulate ourselves on the certainty that the most natural explanation of the occurrence is one that involves no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, or poisoners, or witches...”

The mythic occurrence had been safely assimilated into our everyday domestic routine. Carmilla was looking charmingly. Nothing could be more beautiful than her tints—her complexion was flushed with the vibrant, saturated hues of poached plums and rich, slow-simmered fruit reduction, a deep gustatory vitality enhanced by her peculiar graceful languor. I think my father was silently contrasting her physical vibrancy with my own; I felt like an over-kneaded, exhausted dough, entirely stripped of nourishment. 

“I wish my poor Laura was looking more like herself,” he said, and sighed. So our alarms were happily ended, and Carmilla restored to her friends.
