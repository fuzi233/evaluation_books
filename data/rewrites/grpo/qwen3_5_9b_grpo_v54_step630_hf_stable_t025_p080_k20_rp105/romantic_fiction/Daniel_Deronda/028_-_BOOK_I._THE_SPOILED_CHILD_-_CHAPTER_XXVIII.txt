"Knowing a man in general is easier than knowing a particular man." — La Rochefoucauld.

An hour after Grandcourt departed, the news of Gwendolen's engagement rippled through the rectory, transforming the evening at Offendene into a celebration of social ascent. Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, accompanied by Anna, gathered to witness the triumph. The rector, beaming with paternal approval, congratulated Mrs. Davilow on securing such a formidable attachment. "You look serious," he observed, patting Gwendolen's shoulder with a smile that treated her gravity as innocent naughtiness. "But consider the good arising from our adversity; it has revealed your future husband's delicate liberality." He alluded to Grandcourt's calculated implication that he would provide for Mrs. Davilow, a detail Gwendolen had memorized with precise accuracy.

Mrs. Gascoigne, feeling the weight of her duty to validate the match, assured Gwendolen that Grandcourt would have behaved equally handsomely had she remained in England and been engaged months prior. "Now, there is no room for caprice," she declared, urging her daughter to feel the proper debt of gratitude for a man who persevered. Gwendolen offered a saucy, grave reply, admitting she was unsure what was "proper" to feel, a sentiment that drew a knowing nod from the rector and a suppressed sigh from Anna.

Anna, usually the quiet observer, found herself struggling to maintain her composure. In recent days, she had nurtured a fragile romance in her mind, imagining a future where Rex's longing might soften Gwendolen's heart, allowing them to marry once Rex secured wealth. Now, with Gwendolen bound to Grandcourt, Anna's hope had curdled into a prayer for grace, wishing only to serve as a useful sister while Rex remained poor. The evening was dominated by the rector's decisive remarks on settlements, which he deemed unnecessary, leaving everything to Grandcourt's discretion. Mrs. Davilow inquired about the properties, Ryelands and Gadsmere. Mr. Gascoigne described Ryelands as a magnificent seat built by Inigo Jones, worth twelve thousand a year, complete with livings in the Grandcourt gift. Mrs. Gascoigne counted off the Grandcourt assets on her fingers—the estates, the baronetcy, and the peerage—lamenting only that the peerage carried no additional land. The rector dismissed the peerage as a remote chance, cautioning against the concentration of such immense power in one man, yet acknowledging the value of Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger.

As the evening wound down, Mrs. Gascoigne urged Henry to write to Mrs. Mompert, citing the engagement as a shield against offense. Gwendolen, internally dismissing the name Mompert as mere mumbo-jumbo, remained silent throughout the festivities. That night, however, she could not sleep. In her rare wakefulness, she lay staring into the darkness, appalled by the realization that she was committing to a path she had once fled with repugnance. For the first time, the question of right and wrong terrified her; the old comforts of atonement through gifts and caresses held no sway. The lawlessness she had once embraced—the belief that she need only amuse herself—now frightened her with the shadow of possible calamity. Religious teachings and a deeper, inexorable sense of awe converged in her mind, framing her choice as sacrilege. The brilliant position she craved tasted of poison. Images of the unhappy woman and Grandcourt's past relations with her haunted her, clinging like a disgrace until she finally cried out for her mother.

Mrs. Davilow responded instantly, and Gwendolen fell asleep on her mother's shoulder, only to dream of a lit ballroom before waking to find her mother holding a small packet. "The groom has brought Criterion," Mrs. Davilow explained, handing over a delicate enameled casket containing a splendid diamond ring and a letter.

*Pray wear this ring when I come at twelve in sign of our betrothal. I enclose a check drawn in the name of Mr. Gascoigne, for immediate expenses. Of course Mrs. Davilow will remain at Offendene, at least for some time. I hope, when I come, you will have granted me an early day, when you may begin to command me at a shorter distance.—Yours devotedly, H. M. GRANDCOURT.*

The check was for five hundred pounds. Gwendolen showed it to her mother with feigned delight. "How very kind and delicate!" Mrs. Davilow exclaimed, though she expressed a desire not to be dependent on a son-in-law. "Mamma, if you say that again, I will not marry him," Gwendolen snapped, her anger flaring at the attempt to diminish her agency. Mrs. Davilow pleaded that she did not wish Gwendolen to marry solely for her sake, but Gwendolen tossed her head away, irritated by the motive. Deep down, she knew she was drawn to the marriage by forces stronger than her mother's renunciation could counteract. She resolved to meet the night's alarms with daylight logic, convincing herself that the doubts would prove weak.

"I thought the lover always put on the betrothal ring himself," Gwendolen said laughingly, slipping the ring onto her finger with a charming tilt of her head. "He would rather make me put it on than ask me to let him do it. Aha! He is very proud. But so am I. We shall match each other." She dismissed the idea of a suitor kneeling, declaring she would hate a man who fawned. "He really is not disgusting," she added, a moderate praise that satisfied her mother.

Dressed in her riding-habit to avoid delay, Gwendolen descended the stairs, eager to stir her blood with the intoxication of youth and reclaim her daring. "Go back and make yourself look like a duchess, mamma," she commanded, turning sharply. "Put your point-lace over your head. You must not take things humbly."

When Grandcourt raised her hand to inspect the ring, he spoke gravely of his foresight. "You will tell me if there is anything I forget?" he asked, keeping her hand softly within his own. "I will do anything you wish." Gwendolen smiled, admitting she was unreasonable in her wishes. "Yes, I expect that. Women always are."

"Then I will not be unreasonable," Gwendolen retorted, withdrawing her hand and tossing her head saucily. "I will not be told that I am what women always are."

"I did not say that," Grandcourt replied with his usual gravity. "You are what no other woman is."

"And what is that, pray?" Gwendolen teased, moving away with a hint of menace.

Grandcourt paused before answering. "You are the woman I love."

"Oh, what nice speeches!" Gwendolen laughed, the familiarity of his love for another woman under strange circumstances settling over her. She demanded a return speech regarding their wedding date. "Not yet," Grandcourt said, his eyes alight with anticipation. "Not till we have had a gallop over the downs. I am so thirsty for that... It will begin in ten days!"

"Let us be married in ten days, then," Grandcourt agreed. "And we shall not be bored about the stables."

"What do women always say in answer to that?" Gwendolen asked mischievously.

"They agree to it," the lover replied, caught slightly off guard.

"Then I will not!" Gwendolen declared, putting on her gauntlets while her eyes gathered fun.

The scene was a delightful drama, played out with mutual understanding. Grandcourt preferred the theater of courtship, and Gwendolen, left at ease, found her spirits rising as she played at reigning. After a glorious gallop, she was in a state of exhilaration that made her eager to hasten the marriage, consenting to a date three weeks hence despite the difficulties of the trousseau.

Meanwhile, Lush, aware of the engagement through abundant signs, grew impatient under Grandcourt's silence. He sensed the change would affect his prospects and, lacking a motive for opposition, sought to secure his own future. He wrote to Sir Hugo Mallinger, suggesting that Daniel Deronda be invited to Diplow to discuss a potential financial arrangement. Lush argued that Deronda, possessing Sir Hugo's confidence, could approach Grandcourt with a definite offer without arousing suspicion, potentially trading the chance of the Diplow estate for a substantial sum.

Sir Hugo, reading the letter with Deronda, found the suggestion plausible. Though Deronda expressed a reluctance to visit on such transparent terms, he eventually agreed, driven by a complex mix of obligation and a latent curiosity about Gwendolen's story. He recalled hearing of her flight from a previous suit and her subsequent poverty, seeing in her a nature prone to struggle and difficulty—a quality that attracted his sympathy. Deronda felt a strange pull toward her, a fascination with her womanhood that mingled with his Utopian visions, yet he remained reticent, guarding his feelings as one might protect a delicate bird.

Grandcourt, meanwhile, spent the evening in solitary meditation, his thoughts circling like ripples in a dark pool. He derived no satisfaction from the belief that Gwendolen loved him; rather, he found pleasure in the fact that she had accepted him despite her initial resistance. He viewed her as a spirited woman destined to command everyone but himself, a master to be ruled by a woman who would have liked to master another. He had no taste for tenderness or petitioning solicitude; he desired a partner who would submit to his mastery. As he sat amidst new publications, he felt a strange, premature energy, a chemical reaction within him that hinted at biting or explosive potential, preparing him for the uncertain future that awaited him with Gwendolen.
