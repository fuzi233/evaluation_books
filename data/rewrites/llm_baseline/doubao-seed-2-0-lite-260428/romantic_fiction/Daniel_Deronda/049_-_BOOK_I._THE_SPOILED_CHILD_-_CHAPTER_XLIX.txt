Deep in Daniel Deronda’s soul lived that rare, generous brand of larger justice that always let gratitude rise higher than any petty resentment. It was the mark of a spirit made for more, a heart big enough to hold joy far fuller than the cynical little wits who spend their days picking goodness apart like bug specimens, sneering that nothing is worth a single “thank you.” Those men are the devil’s own acolytes, poor as he is when it comes to love and trust, forever begging scraps of human warmth from a world they insist has none left to give.

When Daniel parted from Gwendolen that day, he bit back the words “I will not see you again for a long while — I am going away.” He dared not say them, for fear the watching Grandcourt would twist that statement into proof that his absence mattered to her, and land her in more of the quiet, cruel trouble he already knew she lived under. He was heading off to keep a promise to call on her already weighed down by a solemn, thrumming emotion, a sense that something life-altering loomed, that woke the oldest, deepest buried feeling of restlessness he had carried his whole life.

Sir Hugo had summoned him to his chambers with a sharp, urgent note scrawled in his familiar hand: “Come immediately. Something has happened.” Daniel had braced himself for the worst, his chest tight with dread all the way to the baronet’s study, so that when he stepped through the door and was met with grave, warm affection instead of the white-faced grief he’d feared, the rush of relief made his knees feel soft. “It is nothing to grieve me, sir?” he said, taking the outstretched hand, his tone far more steady with recovered hope than it was a question.

There was a weight to Sir Hugo’s gaze he had never seen before, his voice soft with subdued emotion as he gestured to a chair. “No, Dan, no. Sit down. I have something to say.” Daniel obeyed, a faint, heavy presentiment curling low in his stomach. It was almost unheard of for the usually jovial, lighthearted Sir Hugo to show such unguarded seriousness.

“Nothing to grieve you, my boy, no. At least, I hope there is nothing in this that will cut too deep. I never thought this moment would come, truly. There have been reasons for years I never prepared you for it, reasons I never told you a single thing about your parentage. But every day I have tried to make sure that secret never became a harm to you.”

Sir Hugo paused, and Daniel found he could not speak at all. He could not even say he had never felt that lack of knowledge as a wound — not when his throat was so thick with unshed feeling. More than anyone else could ever guess hung on this very second, the moment the decades of secrecy finally broke. Sir Hugo had never seen his favorite face so deathly pale, his lips pressed tight together with an ache so raw it was almost visible. He spoke again, his tone softer, laced with anxious tenderness, as if he was suddenly terrified he would wound the boy he had raised.

“I acted all those years at your mother’s bidding. The secrecy was her wish. But now, she has asked to lift it. She wants to see you.” He held out a thin letter, its page made of soft, foreign paper. “This is from her. You can read it later, at your leisure. It only tells you what she asks of you, and where you will find her.”

Daniel slipped the letter straight into the inner pocket of his coat, pressed right over his heart, relieved he did not have to force his shaking hands to read the words right now. The raw, overwhelming emotion on Daniel’s face had shaken Sir Hugo’s usual composure too, and for a long moment neither man could find the words to speak. Daniel’s whole soul was seized by the one question he had never dared to voice all his life, the question that felt too sacred to waste on a casual moment. He could not let this hush, this perfect, reverent tension pass — if he did, he knew he would never get the chance to ask it honestly, and hear the answer unvarnished. His eyes stayed cast down for long, quiet seconds, the air between them thick with all the unspoken thoughts neither had ever said out loud. Finally, he lifted his gaze to Sir Hugo, his voice soft and tremulous, no hint of blame for all the years of hidden truth: “Is my father also living?”

The answer came immediately, low and firm. “No.”

In the tangled rush of feeling that flooded him after those words, Daniel could not tell joy apart from grief, relief apart from the old soft sorrow he had carried for his unknown family. After a long silence, where Daniel felt as if the old, unformed beliefs he had held about his own past were melting away before he could embrace whatever new truth waited for him, Sir Hugo spoke, his voice thick with quiet confession. “Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to take on that secret. Perhaps I was even a little selfish about it — I liked having you all to myself, like the son I never got. If I ever caused you any pain I could have spared you, I ask you to forgive me.”

“The forgiveness was yours long ago,” Daniel said, his throat loosening at last. “The only real pain I ever carried was for that unknown person, the mother I never got to meet, the one I am going to see now. It never once dimmed the affection I have for you — that love is the biggest, brightest part of every memory I have of my life.”

As if moved by a single shared impulse, the two men reached for each other, their hands clasped tight for a long, wordless beat.
