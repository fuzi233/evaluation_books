Gerard stirred back into awareness, only to be swallowed by despair. By the second day, fever had seized his mind, and he raged in delirium, his thoughts torn apart by pain and confusion. Near his bed lay his long auburn hair—rich as autumn flame, cut from his head like a sacrifice. The sickness advanced swiftly, one symptom following another with cruel haste. On the fifth day, the physician left him, declaring there was no hope. That same evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, Gerard sank into a deep, unbroken sleep.

Some whispered he would wake only to die. But an old woman, once a nurse of many years’ experience, held firm in her belief. “If he sleeps twelve hours,” she said, “his youth may yet save him.” So she cleared the room, drove away all but the faithful, and stayed alone by his side. She vowed a candle to the Virgin for each hour he rested. At twelve hours, he still breathed evenly. She knelt in joy, thanking the Mother of God. Then twenty-four hours passed. Still he slept. Her certainty wavered. By the thirtieth hour, she sent for the midwife, the wise woman of the town.

“Thirty hours!” the midwife said, peering closely at him. “Shall we rouse him?”

She studied him long, then shook her head. “His breath is calm, his hand soft. I’ve seen learned doctors wake patients to examine their tongues, only to find nothing new. But we women know better—we trust nature. Sleep has never harmed a wounded mind or a broken heart.”

Forty-eight hours passed before word spread. Soon, people gathered outside, eager to glimpse the dying poet. But only two were allowed in: Fra Colonna, his friend and mentor, and the towering Fra Jerome, a man of quiet strength and unwavering faith. They took turns watching over him, silent and solemn. Colonna sat near the bed, eyes brimming with sorrow, while Jerome prayed softly, beads in hand, his face as steady as stone.

It was near the sixtieth hour when the landlady nudged Colonna gently. He turned—and saw it: Gerard’s eyes had opened, not with alarm, but with the softness of a dreamer awakening. He blinked, stared, then lifted himself slightly on his elbows. His hand went to his head—and found it bare. A shock passed through him. Then he saw Colonna, smiled faintly—then froze.

His smile shattered. His face contorted in agony so deep it seemed to tear his soul. A cry tore from his throat, raw and wild, and he turned his face to the wall, burying it in the sheets.

The landlady wept. She knew that grief—the sudden, terrible loneliness of waking to loss.

Fra Jerome recited prayers from his breviary, his voice low and soothing. Gerard curled tighter beneath the covers, trembling.

Colonna approached, voice trembling too. “My dear boy… all is not lost. Remember the Muses—they are eternal. You must give your love to them now. They will never betray you, never abandon you, as flesh and blood do.”

He spoke with tenderness, but then stepped back, overwhelmed. He could bear no more.

Fra Jerome remained. After a pause, he said, “Young man, the Muses live only in the minds of pagans and dreamers. Only the Church offers true peace—here and beyond. If the world has deceived you, if passion has torn your heart, the Church stands open. Offer your talents to her. In her arms, you will find rest. The Church is peace.”

He spoke these words gravely, then turned and left.

“Peace?” Gerard shouted, rising abruptly, his fist clenched toward the door. “Malediction upon the Church! If it weren’t for the Church, I wouldn’t lie here broken—while she lies cold, cold, cold in Holland! Oh, Margaret! My darling! My love! And I had to flee from you—just when you needed me most! Cruel! Cruel! These monsters let her die! Death comes with signs—signs they ignored, or didn’t care to see. But I saw them—I who loved her! If I had been there, I would have saved her. I would have saved her! Fool! Fool! To leave her even for a moment!”

He wept long and hard, his body wracked with sobs. Then, suddenly, rage flared again. “The Church!” he screamed. “For whose sake I was cast from her! Cursed be the Church! And cursed be the hypocrites who speak its name to my shattered heart! Accursed be this world! Ghysbrecht lives—Margaret dies! Thieves, murderers, harlots live forever. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And curse whoever made them so!”

The friar heard only the roar of fury behind him. It was better so. Had he understood, he might have summoned the Inquisition without hesitation—just as he would have roasted a kid for supper.

The landlady, who had followed Colonna down the stairs, heard the shouting and returned, alarmed. She found Gerard dressing, his movements frantic.

“What good does it do to lie here?” he muttered. “Can I find what I seek?”

“Saints preserve us!” she gasped. “Are you mad again? What do you seek?”

“Oblivion.”

“Oblivion, my child? You’re too young to say such things.”

“Young or old, what else is there to live for?”

He pulled on his finest clothes, ignoring her protests.

“My dear Gerard,” she pleaded, “remember—it’s Tuesday. Not Sunday.”

“Oh,” he said dully, “Tuesday? I thought it was Saturday.”

“No, you’ve slept long. You never wear these clothes on weekdays. Think, boy.”

“I did what I did when she lived,” he replied. “Now I’ll do what I never dared. The past is gone. There lies my hair—gone with my old life. I served one Master faithfully. See my reward. Now I’ll serve another—and give him a fair trial.”

“Alas,” she whispered, turning pale, “what do you mean? What new master?”

And with those dreadful words on his lips, he stepped out into the dim light—cap tilted jauntily, feather crooked, limbs weak, face drawn and ash-white, eyes hollow, mouth twisted in defiance. A man undone, walking toward ruin with the grim resolve of one who has nothing left to lose.
