What perverse quirk of Fate is it that always lands me in the minority? If a bill were tabled to hand this realm over to the Pretender of Rome, or the Grand Turk, and submit all of us to his new faith, it would no doubt pass—for I would be the only soul voting nay.

Back home in Virginia, I found myself out of step with everyone as usual. The Patriots branded me a Tory, as I had never troubled to hide my loyalties; the Tories before long declared me a dangerous Republican. The times were utterly out of joint, cursed spite to match the bard’s old grumble. Ere I had been twelve months on Virginian soil, I ached to return to the banks of the Waveney, but the air hummed with such trouble that I could not leave my mother, a lone widow lady, to face looming war and chaos alone. Nor would she quit the colony in such a hour, and no man of spirit could abandon it either. At Governor Dunmore’s table, over brimming flagons of his plentiful claret, that vow was sworn by dozens of the loyalist gentry, repeated across countless bumpers. *Statue signum, signifer!*—Raise the standard, standard-bearer! We Cavaliers would rally round it, and in those days, the Governor talked like the boldest of brave men.

Of all my Virginian acquaintance, Madam Esmond alone was unshakably consistent. Our local gentry had flocked to Williamsburg for the season, and a great number of them planned to host a grand ball for the Governor’s lady, Lady Dunmore—until news of the Boston Port Bill landed on our shores. Straightway the House of Burgesses passed an indignant protest against Parliament’s measure, and decreed a solemn public fast and humiliation across the colony, with prayers to Heaven to fend off the calamity of civil war. But the invitation to Lady Dunmore had already been extended and accepted, so the stewards agreed the ball would go forward on its appointed night, and they would don sackcloth and ashes three days thereafter for the fast.

“A ball?” scoffed Madam Esmond, when she heard the plan. “I will not attend a feast thrown by rebels who mean to publicly insult his Majesty a week later! I would die first!” She wrote the ball stewards to say that, given the dangerous state of the country, she for one could not in conscience join their entertainment.

What was her surprise, then, when next she ventured out in her sedan chair, to be greeted by cheers from a hundred souls, white and black alike, shouting “Huzzah, Madam!” “Heaven bless your ladyship!” The crowd had mistaken her refusal to attend the ball for a patriotic stand against the Crown. To leave no room for misinterpretation, Madam stuck her head out of the sedan window and cried “God save the King” as loud as her sharp old voice could carry. The crowd took up the cry, shouting it back. *Everybody* cried “God save the King” in those days, no matter what plots they were hatching behind closed doors.

On the night of the ball, my poor Harry, a Burgess and one of the feast’s hosts, donned his old scarlet uniform from General Wolfe’s campaign—the coat he would soon trade for a very different colour—and set off with his wife Fanny. My Lady Warrington and I, being counted as strangers and near-English in the colony, were permitted by Madam to attend the assembly, which she of course absented herself from. I had the honour of dancing a country-dance with the lady of Mount Vernon, whom I found a lively, pretty, and most amiable partner; but when Theo returned home and sang Mrs. Washington’s praises, my mother received her account with the grimmest of scowls. Could not Sir George Warrington have danced with Lady Dunmore or her daughters, or anyone else, she huffed, rather than Mrs. Washington? To be sure, the Colonel thought so well of himself that he no doubt deemed his wife the grandest lady in the room—she remembered him when he was nothing but a road-surveyor earning a guinea a day! There was no measuring the pride of these provincial upstarts, she declared, and no doubt Lord Dunmore’s favour had turned the man’s head entirely. I do not pretend to judge Mr. Washington’s pride; I only know my good mother never could abide him, nor anything that belonged to him.

She was no more pleased with him three days later, when the appointed fast and humiliation came, and he attended the solemn service our new clergyman conducted. She had invited the Reverend Mr. Belman to dinner that day, along with several colonial officials, but the parson excused himself to join the public fast. Madam Esmond tossed her head and said he might do as he liked. She put on a full parade of a feast, lit every window in her house that night when all the rest of Williamsburg was wrapped in darkness and gloom, and begged Mr. Hardy, one of the Governor’s young aides fresh from England, to sing “God Save the King.” The crowd gathered outside in the street listened quietly, thinking it some devotional rite Madam was holding, until she called for “Britons, Strike Home!” The simple young gentleman had barely struck up the tune when a great roar burst from the street, and a large stone, flung from some rebellious hand, plumped straight into the punch-bowl set before me, scattering rum, citrus, and spiced sugar all over our dining room carpets.

My mother did not flinch an inch. I can see her rigid little figure now, standing framed in the open window, head tossed high, frilled arms outstretched against the twinkling starlight, singing in full chorus at the top of her lungs: “Britons, strike home! strike home!” The crowd at the palings hooted and roared, but other voices began to call out: “Silence! For shame! Go back, you rascals!” “Fling more stones, if you dare!” the brave little lady shouted down at them. More missiles might have come, but a party of gentlemen issued from the Raleigh Tavern and pushed through the crowd. “You will not insult a lady,” said a voice I knew well. “Huzza, Colonel! Hurrah, Captain! God bless your honour!” the crowd shouted back, and the disturbance died away.

My mother, protesting the whole ruckus was over, would have made Mr. Hardy sing another song, but he gave a sickly grin and said he really did not care to perform to such accompaniments, and the impromptu concert ended for the night. I am bound to add, though, that some scoundrels returned long after dark, frightened my poor wife nearly out of her wits, and broke every single pane of glass in the front of our house. “Britons, Strike Home!” was a step too far; Madam ought to have contented herself with “God Save the King.”

All across the colony, militia drilled, bullets were cast, ammunition stores were stashed, and clever schemes to thwart royal ordinances were devised and carried out—but still every patriot swore loyalty to the Crown. When I protested, “You are scheming for separation! You are calling down the inevitable wrath of the greatest power on earth!” they always made the same retort: “We mean no separation at all! No man surpasses us in loyalty! We glory in the name of Britons!” The gunpowder was stacked in cellars, the fuse was laid, but Mr. Fawkes still dutifully petitioned King and Parliament, and swore he meant no harm at all.

It is easy now, after we have lost, to look over the map of that great empire we tore ourselves from, and point out all the ways we ought never to have lost it. Long Island ought to have wiped Washington’s ragged army out of existence; he ought never to have emerged from Valley Forge except as a prisoner. The South was ours after the battle of Camden, but for the unthinkable meddling of the Commander-in-Chief in New York, who paralysed the only capable British general to appear all war, and sent him stumbling into that miserable cul-de-sac at Yorktown, from which he could only emerge defeated and a prisoner. Oh, for a week more! a day more, an hour more! When I read over our American campaigns, from their unhappy start to their inglorious end, now that we can see the enemy’s movements and straits as clearly as our own, I fancy a single well-timed advance, a single swift march, would have put men who had no means to withstand us in our power, and turned the whole tide of the struggle. But Heaven willed it so, for the good of both empires, that the great Western Republic should split away from us. And the gallant soldiers who fought on its side, their indomitable chief above all, earned the glory of facing and overcoming not only well-supplied veteran troops hardened to war, but cold, hunger, wretchedness, mutiny, and treason in their own ranks—where all would have fallen to ruin, but for that pure unquenchable flame of patriotism burning in their leader’s breast. What constancy, what magnanimity, what stubborn persistence against the worst fortune! Washington in battle was no better nor braver than hundreds who fought beside or against him—who has not heard the sneers at “Fabius” his factious captains were forever spouting? But Washington as the chief of a nation in arms, navigating clashing parties, calm in the middle of conspiracy, serene against the open foe before him and the darker enemies at his back; Washington breathing order and spirit into troops hungry and in rags, stung by ingratitude but showing no anger, ever ready to forgive; invincible in defeat, magnanimous in victory, and never more sublime than on the day he laid down his victorious sword and retired quietly to his Mount Vernon estate—there is a character to admire and revere, a life without a stain, a fame without a flaw. *Quando invenies parem?* In that larger work on the great war I have planned and partly written, I hope I have done full justice to the character of its greatest leader. [And I trust my recorded opinions of him prove I too can be just and magnanimous to those who bear me no personal favour. For my brother Hal, ever eager to make peace between me and his beloved chief, showed his Excellency some early sheets of my history. General Washington, who read few books and made no pretensions to literary taste, remarked: “If you want my honest opinion, my dear General, Sir George’s projected work, from the sample I have seen, is certain to offend every single party on both sides.” — G. E. W.]

That respect was earned purely by the force of his eminent virtues. In my young days I had little sympathy for the young Mr. Washington, though my brother, far more frank and affectionate than I, was his fast friend from the days they were equals, to later years when the General, as I freely own, stood above most of mankind.

I have spoken of that contrariness in our dispositions which somehow landed Hal and I on the wrong sides of the quarrel that raged for five years, until the mother country was forced to acknowledge her defeat. Hal should have been the Tory, and I the Whig. My theoretical opinions were far more liberal than his; after his marriage especially, he grew what our Indian nabobs call a *Bahadoor*—stately, ceremonious, and quick to demand respect. When Lord Dunmore talked of freeing all enslaved people to draw them to the King’s standard, Hal flew into a rage, swearing he would hang the Governor and all the “Black Guards” he had crimped. “If you gentlemen are fighting for freedom,” I pointed out, “sure the negroes may fight for it too.” Hal roared, shaking his fist: “Infernal villains, if I meet any of them, they shall die by this hand!” My mother agreed the idea of a negro insurrection was the most abominable, parricidal notion ever to spring up in her unhappy country. She was at least more consistent than Hal: she would have all souls, black and white, obedient to the ruling powers, whereas Hal insisted freedom was a right reserved only for white men.

As proof of her argument, Madam Esmond and Hal both pointed to Gumbo, our old family servant. I had freed him years before, as a reward for his unfaltering fidelity to me in our darkest hours, and when he returned to Virginia he was far from a welcome guest in our household. He was free, and the rest of the servants were not: he was a walking centre of unrest. He gave himself no end of airs, bragging of his friends and adventures in “home” (as he called London), and for a while drew all the household staff around him to listen to his tales, like the monkey who had seen the world. Hal’s personal servant Sady, who had visited England of his own will, was not free, and soon grew deeply jealous of Gumbo. The two fought repeated brawls, practicing the boxing and butting tricks they had learned at Marybone Gardens and Hockley-in-the-Hole. Nor was Sady the only jealous one: almost all of my mother’s servants hated Gumbo for his strutting ways, and I am sorry to say his own wife Molly was as resentful as the rest. The other black women would not pardon her for marrying a man who was free; she got no respect, could wield no authority, and came to her mistress with endless complaints of the idleness, knavery, lies, and thefts of the other servants, before finally working herself into a jealous fury over a girl named Dinah, who I heartily hope was as innocent as her namesake, the moonlit visitant of Endymion.

On the matter of morality, Madam Esmond was a very Draconess: an accused person was a guilty person, as far as she was concerned. She laid charges against Gumbo, and he replied to them with asperity, forgetting he was a free gentleman. My mother ordered him to be whipped, and at that the fiery Molly flew at her ladyship, all her wrath over her husband’s supposed infidelity vanishing at the thought of the indignity done him. A full rebellion broke out at Castlewood. I took Gumbo’s side against my mother, and Hal and Fanny sided with her, a rift that did some small good by drawing Madam closer to her younger son and his family. Our little disagreement was soon healed, but it was clear the “Standard of Insurrection” had to be removed from our house, so we determined Gumbo and his lady would sail back to Europe.

God knows my wife and I would gladly have gone with them. Our little boy had sickened and wasted away, laid low by the deadly swamp fever of the low country, and Theo was now great with child, soon to lie in of our son Henry. She would not hear of leaving me, though: her father and Hetty back in England would care for our elder children, and she would not leave my side. I see her still, sitting silent at her worktable, stitching piles of little linen shirts for the voyage, stealing soft, stealthy visits to the children’s bedrooms while they slept, the terrible morning of the parting when the barge carrying the servants and little ones rowed out to the waiting ship, and she stood on the shore with silent tears running down her face. If the Prince of Wales himself had sailed on that voyage, he could not have been better provided for. Where, my lad, is the Tompion watch your grandmother gave you? And how did you survive all the boxes of sweet cakes she stowed away in your cabin?

The same ship that carried our children away returned bearing the Reverend Mr. Hagan and my Lady Maria, who had meekly resigned her rank, and was known in the colony (which would not be a colony very long) only as plain Mrs. Hagan. When I was in favour with Lord Dunmore, a parish living fell vacant in Westmoreland County, and he gave it to our kinsman. They arrived in Virginia just in time to christen our baby boy Henry, and to preach sermons on the gloomy state of the times that Madam Esmond pronounced to be prodigiously fine. My Lady Maria won Madam’s heart entirely by refusing to walk ahead of her at any doorway. “My father, your brother, was an earl, ‘tis true,” she said, “but you know your ladyship is a marquis’s daughter, and I never could dream of taking precedence of you!” Madam grew so fond of her niece that she even allowed Hagan to read plays aloud—including my own humble compositions—and was fairly forced to own there was merit in my tragedy of *Pocahontas*, which our parson delivered with uncommon energy and fire.

Hal and Fanny rarely came to Castlewood or Richmond while the chaplain and his wife were staying with us. Fanny was curt and rude to Maria, giggling strangely in her company, and repeatedly reminding her of her advanced age, to my mother’s vast astonishment. She often asked if there was some hidden quarrel between her niece and her daughter-in-law, but I held my tongue. My heart ached for the meek way the elder lady bore Fanny’s petty persecutions. Fanny loved to torment her in her husband’s presence, and the poor elder woman winced as little as she could under the lash. Once I remonstrated with Madam Harry, and asked her if she had been raised by Red Indians, that she tortured her victims so. “Have I not had enough torture in my own time?” the young lady snapped back, her eyes sharp. “The blows that wound worst are the ones that never are aimed. The people who hate us are rarely the people we have injured.” I thought of little quiet Fanny in our early days, ever smiling, ever eager to run and do all our biddings, and my heart grieved for my poor brother Hal, who had taken this sly, bitter creature into his bosom.
