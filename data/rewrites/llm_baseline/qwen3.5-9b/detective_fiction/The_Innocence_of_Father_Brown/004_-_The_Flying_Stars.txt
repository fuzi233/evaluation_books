"The most beautiful crime I ever committed," Flambeau would later declare in his pious old age, "was also, by a singular coincidence, my final transgression. It occurred at Christmas. As an artist, I had always endeavored to craft crimes suited to the specific season or landscape in which I found myself, selecting terraces or gardens for catastrophes as one might choose a setting for a sculptural group. Thus, squires should be swindled within long rooms paneled with oak; whereas Jews, conversely, should discover themselves unexpectedly destitute amidst the lights and screens of the Café Riche. Similarly, in England, if I wished to deprive a dean of his riches—a task not as simple as it appears—I sought to frame him against the green lawns and grey towers of a cathedral town. Likewise, in France, when I managed to extract funds from a wealthy and wicked peasant (an endeavor nearly impossible), it gratified me to see his indignant head relieved against a grey line of clipped poplars, beneath the solemn plains of Gaul where the mighty spirit of Millet broods.

"Well, my last crime was a Christmas offense: a cheerful, cozy, English middle-class crime; a crime straight out of Charles Dickens. I committed it in a fine old middle-class house near Putney, featuring a crescent of carriage drive, a stable adjacent to it, a nameplate on the outer gates, and a monkey tree. You know the type. I truly believe my imitation of Dickens's style was dexterous and literary. It seems almost a pity I repented the very same evening."

Flambeau would then recount the tale from the inside; viewed from within, it remains peculiar. Seen from without, however, it is perfectly incomprehensible, and it is from this external vantage point that the stranger must study it. From this perspective, the drama commenced when the front doors of the house with the stable opened onto the garden containing the monkey tree, and a young girl emerged with bread to feed the birds on the afternoon of Boxing Day. She possessed a pretty face with brave brown eyes, yet her figure was beyond conjecture, for she was so enveloped in brown furs that it was difficult to distinguish hair from fur. Without her attractive visage, she might have been mistaken for a small, toddling bear.

The winter afternoon was reddening toward evening, and a ruby light was already rolling over the bloomless beds, filling them, as it were, with the ghosts of dead roses. To one side of the house stood the stable; to the other, an alley or cloister of laurels led to the larger garden behind. After scattering bread for the birds—for the fourth or fifth time that day, as the dog consumed it—the young lady passed unobtrusively down the lane of laurels and into a glimmering plantation of evergreens situated behind. Here, she uttered an exclamation of wonder, real or ritualistic, and gazing upward at the high garden wall above her, beheld it fantastically straddled by a somewhat fantastical figure.

"Oh, don't jump, Mr. Crook," she called out in alarm; "it's much too high."

The individual riding the party wall like an aerial horse was a tall, angular young man with dark hair sticking up like a hairbrush, possessing intelligent and even distinguished features, though his complexion was sallow and almost alien. This pallor was accentuated by an aggressive red tie, the sole element of his costume he seemed to take any care with. Perhaps it was a symbol. Ignoring the girl's alarmed admonition, he leaped like a grasshopper to the ground beside her, landing where he might very easily have broken his legs.

"I think I was meant to be a burglar," he stated placidly, "and I have no doubt I should have been if I hadn't happened to be born in that nice house next door. I can't see any harm in it, anyhow."

"How can you say such things!" she remonstrated.

"Well," said the young man, "if you're born on the wrong side of the wall, I can't see that it's wrong to climb over it."

"I never know what you will say or do next," she replied.

"I don't often know myself," responded Mr. Crook; "but then I am currently on the right side of the wall."

"And which is the right side of the wall?" asked the young lady, smiling.

"Whichever side you are on," said the young man named Crook.

As they walked together through the laurels toward the front garden, a motor horn sounded thrice, growing louder and closer. A car of splendid speed, great elegance, and a pale green hue swept up to the front doors like a bird and stood throbbing.

"Hullo, hullo!" exclaimed the young man with the red tie. "Here's somebody born on the right side, anyhow. I didn't know, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus was so modern as this."

"Oh, that's my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer. He always arrives on Boxing Day."

Following an innocent pause, which unconsciously betrayed a certain lack of enthusiasm, Ruby Adams added:

"He is very kind."

John Crook, a journalist, had heard of that eminent City magnate; nor was it his fault if the magnate had not heard of him, for in certain articles in *The Clarion* or *The New Age*, Sir Leopold had been treated with austerity. Yet he said nothing and grimly watched the unloading of the motor-car, a rather lengthy process. A large, neat chauffeur in green emerged from the front, and a small, neat manservant in grey from the rear, and between them they deposited Sir Leopold on the doorstep, beginning to unwrap him like some very carefully protected parcel. Rugs sufficient to stock a bazaar, furs of all the beasts of the forest, and scarves of every color of the rainbow were unwrapped one by one until they revealed something resembling a human form: that of a friendly, yet foreign-looking elderly gentleman with a grey, goat-like beard and a beaming smile, who rubbed his large fur gloves together.

Long before this revelation was complete, the two large doors of the porch had opened in the middle, and Colonel Adams—the father of the furry young lady—had emerged himself to welcome his eminent guest. He was a tall, sunburnt, and remarkably silent man, wearing a red smoking-cap like a fez, giving him the appearance of one of the English Sirdars or Pashas in Egypt. Accompanying him was his brother-in-law, recently arrived from Canada, a large and rather boisterous young gentleman-farmer with a yellow beard, named James Blount. Also present was the more insignificant figure of the priest from the neighboring Roman Church; for the colonel's late wife had been Catholic, and the children, as is customary in such cases, had been raised to follow her faith. Everything appeared undistinguished about the priest, even down to his name, which was Brown; yet the colonel had always found something companionable about him and frequently invited him to family gatherings.

Inside the spacious entrance hall, there was ample room even for Sir Leopold and the removal of his wraps. The porch and vestibule were unduly large in proportion to the house, forming, as it were, a vast room with the front door at one end and the base of the staircase at the other. Before the large hall fire, over which hung the colonel's sword, the process concluded, and the company—including the saturnine Crook—was presented to Sir Leopold Fischer. However, the venerable financier still seemed to struggle with portions of his well-lined attire. Eventually, he produced from a very interior tailcoat pocket a black oval case, which he radiantly explained was his Christmas present for his goddaughter. With an unaffected vanity that possessed something disarming about it, he held out the case before them all. It flew open at a touch and half-blinded them. It was as if a crystal fountain had spurting forth into their eyes. Nestled in orange velvet lay, like three eggs, three white and vivid diamonds that seemed to set the very air ablaze around them. Fischer stood beaming benevolently, drinking deeply of the astonishment and ecstasy of the girl, the grim admiration and gruff thanks of the colonel, and the wonder of the entire group.

"I'll put 'em back now, my dear," said Fischer, returning the case to the tails of his coat. "I had to be careful of them coming down. They're the three great African diamonds called 'The Flying Stars,' because they've been stolen so often. All the major criminals are on the trail; but even the rough men about in the streets and hotels could hardly keep their hands off them. I might have lost them on the road here. It was quite possible."

"Quite natural, I should say," growled the man in the red tie. "I wouldn't blame them if they had taken them. When they ask for bread, and you don't even give them a stone, I think they might take the stone for themselves."

"I won't have you talking like that," cried the girl, glowing curiously. "You've only talked like that since you became a horrid what's-his-name. You know what I mean. What do you call a man who wants to embrace the chimney-sweep?"

"A saint," said Father Brown.

"I think," said Sir Leopold, with a supercilious smile, "that Ruby means a Socialist."
