Romola sat on the stone beneath the cypress tree, and for the first time in her life, she felt truly alone—free from walls, from streets, from the weight of expectation. The morning air was still; the road stretched before her like a ribbon unspooling into distance. Behind her, Florence receded. Ahead, the path wound toward Siena, where Tito Melema now rode, unaware that his wife had slipped away—not in flight, but in search of something else: silence, perhaps, or escape from a life that no longer held meaning. She had left behind not just a husband, but a world of duties, names, and roles she could no longer bear.

The mountains loomed dark and distant, separated from her by a deep hollow in the land—a gulf between her and the cold quiet of nature. It was as if the earth itself had opened to let her breathe. No one watched. No one claimed her. For an instant, she believed she was free.

Then, a voice—low, firm, yet unmistakable—broke the stillness.

“You are Romola de’ Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema.”

She did not turn. She knew that voice. It had pierced through her thoughts more than once before, when she stood at the edge of decisions too great for her own reason. But this time, it came not from memory, but from the present—close, undeniable.

It was one of those monks in black robes who moved through Florence like shadows, their presence always suggesting a deeper order than the world’s surface allowed. And yet, though she resented the intrusion, Romola felt a tremor pass through her—not fear, not yet, but a kind of awe, as if some unseen force had reached out and touched her soul.

“I have a command from God,” the voice said, “to stop you. You are not permitted to flee.”

Her anger flared. She refused to look at him. Her body remained rigid, seated, but her mind burned with defiance.

“What right do you have to speak to me? To hinder me?”

“The right of a messenger,” he replied. “You wear the garb of a nun, but your purpose is not holy. You have used it as a disguise. Yet you were not meant to pass me unseen. I know who you are. I know what you seek—to hide your name, your place, your duty. You wish to choose a new life, without law, without rule, without obligation. But I am sent to call you back. My daughter, you must return to your place.”

Each word struck her like a blow. She wanted to reject them, to rise and walk away, but something held her—something deeper than pride. She was shaken. Not by the threat, but by the certainty in his tone. This man did not speak from power, but from conviction. He spoke as if he carried a truth that could not be denied.

At last, she rose. Her eyes met his.

It was Fra Girolamo—Savonarola. She had known it all along, even before seeing his face. And now, standing before him, nearly his height, she felt small.

She opened her mouth to speak, but no words came. His gaze held hers—not with judgment, but with a quiet intensity that seemed to see into her very being. There was nothing divine in his features—no halo, no radiant beauty. Only strength, discipline, and a calm that humbled her.

He saw her, not as a woman fleeing, but as a soul in conflict. And in that gaze, Romola sensed something she had never known before: human fellowship—not born of blood or love, but of shared purpose, of a bond forged in truth.

And so she fell silent.

“Your freedom,” Savonarola said gently, “is proud. But tell me—what is more base than a debtor who thinks himself free?”

A chill ran through her. The words cut to the core of her belief.

“You are fleeing,” he continued, “from your debts—the debt of a Florentine woman, the debt of a wife. You turn your back on the lot appointed for you, and seek another. But can any man or woman choose their duty? No more than they can choose their birth or their parents. You are running from God’s presence into the wilderness.”

She tried to argue, but the fire of her resistance cooled. In its place came a strange sense—of possibility. Could submission bring strength? Could obedience mean freedom?

She looked at him again, and this time, she spoke with reverence, though still with hesitation.

“My father,” she said, “you cannot know the reasons that drive me. None can. Only I know them. I have been driven by sorrow. I must go.”

“I know enough,” he replied. “My mind has been illuminated concerning you. You are not happy in marriage—but I am not your confessor. I need not know what lies within your heart. I only know this: you were warned before your marriage, by a message from heaven. You chose the bond. Now you seek to break it. That is a betrayal—not only of your husband, but of the sacred trust between man and man, between citizen and city.”

She shrank as if struck. The thought of Tito—his deceit, his cowardice—came rushing back, and then, suddenly, she saw herself reflected in his sin. Was she not also breaking faith? Was not her flight a form of betrayal?

“And why flee?” Savonarola pressed. “Florence is the home of your soul, not just your birthplace. Your people suffer. Hunger walks our streets. Misery cries out. And you say, ‘I care not—I will go.’ You think yourself above them? But you are not. You have lived apart, high on your hill, blind to the needs of others. You scorned the Church, yet you have no faith of your own. You have become a wanderer, seeking freedom, but finding only emptiness.”

She raised her head. “I was not going for ease. I was going to hardship. I expect no joy. It is gone from my life.”

“Then you seek only your own will,” he said. “But good is not chosen—it flows from obedience. It is a river from the foot of the Invisible Throne. If you forsake your duty, you will find only sorrow without purpose—bitter herbs, and no bread.”

Tears gathered in her eyes. She clasped her hands, pleading. “If you knew—how impossible it seemed to endure!”

He pointed to the cord around her neck. “Draw forth what you carry.”

She hesitated, then pulled out the crucifix.

He nodded. “There, my daughter, is the image of a Supreme Offering—made by Supreme Love, because the need of man was great.”

She stared at the cross, trembling. How far she had come since she first took it from his hands! Once, she had seen it as a symbol of piety, a relic of tradition. Now, it seemed to glow with meaning she had never understood.

“Conform your life to that image,” he said. “Make your sorrow an offering. When the fire of Divine charity burns within you, you will see the needs of others, and you will not call your sacrifice great.”

She looked up, her eyes filled with a dawning recognition.

“You have carried yourself proudly,” he continued, “as one apart, untouched by common blood or thought. But you have been unborn to true life. Your love for your father is gone? Then you are without law, without religion—no better than a beast robbed of her young. But if you had faith, you would not flee. You would stay, and work, and suffer—for Florence, for your people.”

She turned away, unable to meet his gaze. The path ahead was clear, yet terrible.

“If I return,” she whispered, “I must face him. My husband… he is not… my love is gone.”

“He is not a malefactor?” Savonarola asked.

“No,” she said quickly. “I accuse him of nothing.”

“Then you have no excuse,” he said softly. “For if he were guilty, your place would be beside him in prison. But he is not. And so your duty remains. Marriage is not merely flesh and desire. It is a sacrament—a vow made before God. You may not break it.”

She shook her head. “Yet how can I bear—”

“Bear it,” he said. “Make your sorrows an offering. For the work of redemption begins here, in Florence. Sin and sorrow are being made to cease. And you, my daughter, are called to partake in it. You are a child of Florence. Fulfil your inheritance.”

His voice grew stronger, his face alight with passion. He stood with arms folded, yet radiating a power that drew her closer, not by force, but by truth.

“Come,” he said. “Come back to your place.”

And in that moment, Romola felt the old doubts dissolve. The cold fears melted under the warmth of his faith. She was not being commanded—she was being called.

With a low, prayerful cry, she said, “Father, I will be guided. Teach me. I will go back.”

She sank to her knees.

Savonarola reached out his hands over her—but said nothing. For words were no longer needed. The silence between them spoke louder than any sermon ever could.
