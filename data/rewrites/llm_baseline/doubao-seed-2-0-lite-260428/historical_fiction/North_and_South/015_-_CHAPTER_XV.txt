“Margaret,” her father said the next day, “we must return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Your mother is far too unwell to walk the distance, and thinks she cannot manage it; so you and I will go that afternoon, the pair of us.”

As they set out, Mr. Hale began to speak of his wife’s health with a quiet, veiled anxiety Margaret had long hoped he would finally acknowledge.
“Did you send for a doctor, Margaret? Did you consult anyone?”
“No, papa. You talked of having a doctor come to see me, and I was well enough again soon after. But if I could only find a good, reliable physician, I would go and beg him to call today—for I am certain mamma is seriously unwell.”
She spoke plainly, laying the truth before him with firm gentleness, for he had firmly shut his mind against all her previous worries about his wife’s condition. This time, the news landed heavy. He answered in a despondent, trembling tone.
“Do you think she has some hidden complaint? That she is really very ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh Margaret! I am haunted by the fear our move to Milton is killing her. My poor Maria.”
“Papa, don’t torment yourself with such fancies,” Margaret said, shocked. “She is unwell, that is all. Many people are laid low for a time, and with careful advice, they recover stronger than they were before.”
“But Dixon hasn’t hinted anything of the severity?”
“No. You know Dixon loves to make a grand mystery out of trifles; she has been a little secretive about mamma’s health, which alarmed me at first, but I daresay there is no real cause. You told me only the other day I was growing fanciful, after all.”
“I pray to God you are,” he said. “But do not think for a moment I meant what I said. I will not have you hiding your fears about your mother from me, no matter how fanciful they seem. We will ask Mrs. Thornton if she can recommend a first-rate doctor—we will not waste our money on some quack of no reputation. Now, we turn down this street.”

Marlborough Street stretched before them in long, uniform rows of tiny terraced cottages, broken only by stretches of blank, windowless wall. It seemed impossible a woman as tall, stately, and finely dressed as Mrs. Thornton could live in such a neighbourhood. Margaret had unconsciously imagined her home would match her own grand, imposing bearing.
“He assuredly told me he lived on Marlborough Street,” Mr. Hale said, frowning in confusion.
“Perhaps it is still one of his old thrifty habits, to live in a small house,” Margaret said. “But there are people about; let me ask.”
She stopped a passerby, who pointed them towards the end of the long blank wall, where the factory lodge door stood half-hidden beside great heavy iron gates, built for the passage of wagons and lorries carrying cotton in and finished cloth out. The lodge keeper let them through into a vast oblong yard: one side lined with business offices, the other dominated by the looming, many-windowed mill, where the endless clank of looms and the deep groaning roar of the steam engine thundered so loud it threatened to deafen anyone standing within the enclosure. Facing the street, along the short end of the yard, stood a handsome stone-faced house, blackened with decades of factory smoke, but every window, step, and coat of paint kept scrupulously, almost fiercely clean. It had been built some fifty or sixty years prior, its long narrow windows, twin flights of railed steps leading up to the front door, and heavy stone trim all marking its age. Margaret could not fathom why people who could afford such a fine, well-kept home would choose to live surrounded by the mill’s constant din, rather than a smaller, quieter house out in the country, or even a leafy suburb. Her ears, unaccustomed to industrial noise, could barely catch her father’s voice as they waited on the steps for the door to open.

When they stepped inside, the drawing room struck Margaret as eerily untouched, as if the furniture had been carefully wrapped and bagged in preparation for a volcanic eruption, waiting to be unearthed perfectly preserved a thousand years later. The walls were papered pale pink and gilded, the carpet printed with delicate bunches of flowers on an ivory ground, but its entire centre was covered by a stiff glazed linen drugget to shield it from smoky dust. Lace curtains hung at every window, every armchair and sofa draped with its own custom net or knitted cover. Great heavy alabaster figurines sat on every flat surface, sealed safely from dust under glass domes. A large circular table sat beneath the wrapped, unused chandelier, its polished surface dotted with smartly bound books arranged at perfect equal intervals like the bright spokes of a wheel. Every surface reflected light, none absorbed it. The entire room glinted and glowed with a painful, icy perfection, so spotless and sterile Margaret could not help but feel uneasy, aware only subconsciously of the endless hours of labour required to keep everything so snowy white in the smoky, grimy Milton air. There was no hint of warmth, no sign of daily use: all the endless care lavished on the room went only to preserve its ornaments, never to make it a comfortable space for quiet rest or gentle home work.
They had plenty of time to observe it, speaking to each other in quiet whispers, as if afraid to wake the long-dormant echoes of the unused room, before Mrs. Thornton finally swept in, rustling in her customary fine black silk, her crisp muslin collars and lace cuffs matching the blinding whiteness of the net draperies covering the furniture. Margaret stumbled over her explanation that Mrs. Hale could not join them on their call, too anxious not to rile her father’s fresh fears to give a clear account of her mother’s illness, leaving Mrs. Thornton with the distinct impression that Mrs. Hale’s ailment was nothing more than the fussy, fanciful complaint of a fine lady, who could easily have made the trip if she had cared enough. Remembering that she had sent her own carriage out to visit the Hales, ordering Fanny to come with her to show them all possible respect, Mrs. Thornton drew back slightly, offended, and gave Margaret no sympathy for her mother’s supposed indisposition, barely crediting her story at all.
“How is Mr. Thornton?” Mr. Hale asked. “I feared he was unwell, from his hurried note yesterday.”
“My son is rarely sick, and when he is, he never speaks of it, nor uses it as an excuse to neglect his work. He told me he could not make time to read with you last night, sir. He regrets it deeply; he values the hours spent in your company.”
“They are every bit as precious to me,” Mr. Hale said warmly. “Seeing how much he enjoys and appreciates the finest passages of classical literature makes me feel young again.”
“I do not doubt classics are very pleasant for people with nothing else to fill their days,” Mrs. Thornton said, cool as ice. “But I will confess I was against my son taking them up again. The world he lives in, the work he does, demands every last scrap of his energy and attention. Classics are all very well for men who loiter their lives away in country parishes or college quads—but a Milton manufacturer ought to have every thought and power of his mind fixed entirely on the work of the present day. That is my opinion, at least.”
“But surely,” Margaret put in, “if the mind is fixed too long on only one single object, it grows stiff and rigid, unable to take in any other interest.”
“I do not understand what you mean by a mind growing ‘stiff and rigid,’” Mrs. Thornton replied. “Nor do I admire those flighty, whirligig people who are utterly consumed by one new passion today, and completely forget it exists when the next fancy strikes them. A life full of scattered trivial interests does not suit a Milton manufacturer. It is, or ought to be, enough for him to hold one great, single desire, and bend every purpose of his life to fulfil it.”
“And that desire is?” Mr. Hale asked.
Her sallow cheek flushed bright, her eyes lighting up as she answered.
“To hold and maintain a high, honourable place among the merchants of this country—among the businessmen of this very town. My son has earned that reputation for himself. Go anywhere— I do not say just England, but all across Europe—every man of business knows the name of John Thornton of Milton, and respects it. Of course, no one in idle fashionable circles has ever heard of it,” she added, with a sneer of disdain. “Idle lords and ladies do not trouble themselves about a Milton manufacturer, unless he is elected to Parliament, or marries the daughter of an earl.”
Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt a strange, awkward amusement as they realised they had never heard of this great renowned name before Mr. Bell wrote to tell them Mr. Thornton would be a good friend to have in Milton. The proud mother’s world of hard industrial renown was entirely separate from their own old world of London gentility, Hampshire squires, and quiet country clergymen. Margaret’s face, for all her efforts to keep it calm and neutral, betrayed that thought to the sharp, sensitive Mrs. Thornton.
“You think you never heard of this wonderful son of mine, Miss Hale. You think I am just an old woman whose whole world is bounded by Milton’s factory walls, who thinks her own crow is the whitest bird alive.”
“No,” Margaret said, with a little spark of spirit. “It is true I had barely heard Mr. Thornton’s name before I came to Milton. But since arriving here, I have heard more than enough to respect and admire him, and to see how completely justified you are in being so proud of him.”
“Who spoke to you of him?” Mrs. Thornton asked, a little mollified, yet jealous that no one else’s account of her son could possibly do him full justice.
Margaret hesitated, not caring for this authoritative, inquisitorial line of questioning. Mr. Hale stepped in to rescue her, as he thought.
“It was what Mr. Thornton himself showed of his character, that let us see what kind of man he is, was it not Margaret?”
Mrs. Thornton drew herself up, stiffening again.
“My son is not the kind of man to boast of his own deeds. May I ask you again, Miss Hale, whose account you formed that good opinion from? A mother is always greedy to hear her children praised, you know.”
Margaret answered. “It was far more what Mr. Thornton did not say about the hard early life Mr. Bell told us of, than anything he did say, that made us realise just how much you have to be proud of.”
“Mr. Bell! What can a man who lazes his days away in a drowsy old college know of John? But I thank you, Miss Hale. Many a flighty young lady would have refused to give an old woman that small pleasure, of hearing her son well spoken of.”
“Why would they refuse?” Margaret asked, looking at Mrs. Thornton in complete bewilderment.
“Why! Because they would know they were making the old mother their advocate, just in case they had designs on the son’s heart,” Mrs. Thornton said, giving a grim little smile. She was pleased by Margaret’s frankness, and aware she had been prying a little too deeply. Margaret laughed outright at the absurd idea, her merriment so bright it grated on Mrs. Thornton’s ear, as if no words could possibly be that funny.
Margaret stopped laughing the moment she saw the older woman’s annoyed look.
“I beg your pardon, madam. But I am very grateful to you for exonerating me from having any kind of designs on Mr. Thornton’s heart.”
“Young ladies have done exactly that, before now,” Mrs. Thornton said, stiffly.
“I hope Miss Thornton is well?” Mr. Hale put in, hastily changing the subject.
“She is as well as she ever is. She is not strong,” Mrs. Thornton answered shortly.
“And Mr. Thornton? I may hope to see him for our readings on Thursday, I suppose?”
“I cannot answer for my son’s engagements. There is trouble brewing in the town, talk of a strike. If that comes, every one of his friends will be coming to him for his experience and judgement. But I expect he can make it on Thursday. At the very least, he will send word if he cannot come.”
“A strike!” Margaret exclaimed. “What are they striking for?”
“To seize control of other people’s property, that is what they always strike for,” Mrs. Thornton said, snorting fiercely. “If my son’s workers walk out, I will say only that they are a pack of ungrateful hounds. And I have no doubt they will do it.”
“I suppose they are asking for higher wages?” Mr. Hale said.
“That is the pretty face they put on it. But the truth is, they want to be masters, and turn the masters into slaves on their own ground. They are always plotting it, always thinking of it, and every five or six years there is a great fight between masters and men. They will be caught out this time, I think—they will find they miscalculated. If they walk out, they may not find it so easy to get their jobs back again. The masters have made plans, and they will teach the men not to be so hasty to strike in future, if they dare try it.”
“Will the town become very dangerous?” Margaret asked.
“Of course it will. But surely you are no coward, are you? Milton is no place for cowards. I remember once I had to walk straight through a crowd of white, furious men, all swearing they would tear Makinson to pieces the moment he stepped foot outside his factory. He had no idea the mob was waiting for him, and someone had to go warn him, or he was a dead man. It had to be a woman, so I went. Once I got inside the factory gates, I could not get back out. It was as much as my life was worth to try. So I climbed up to the roof, where we had piles of heavy stones stacked to drop on any man who tried to break the doors down, and I would have lifted those stones and dropped them as straight and true as any man there, if I had not fainted dead away from the heat and exertion. If you are going to live in Milton, Miss Hale, you must learn to have a brave heart.”
“I will do my very best,” Margaret said, a little pale. “I do not know if I am brave or not, until I am tested. But I fear I might be a coward.”
“Soft south country folks are easily frightened by what we Darkshire folk just call living and struggling. But when you have spent ten years living among people who always hold a grudge against their betters, waiting for their chance to pay it back, you will find out for yourself if you are a coward or not, you may take my word for it.”

That evening, John Thornton turned up at the Hales’ cottage. He was shown straight into their drawing room, where Mr. Hale had been reading aloud to his wife and Margaret.
“I come partly to bring you a note from my mother, and partly to apologise for missing our reading last night. The note has the address of the doctor you asked for, Dr. Donaldson.”
“Thank you,” Margaret said hastily, holding out her hand to take the note, not wanting her mother to overhear that they had been seeking out a physician for her. She was pleased to see Mr. Thornton immediately understood her unspoken wish: he handed her the note without a single word of explanation that might alarm Mrs. Hale.
Mr. Hale soon steered the conversation around to the coming strike. Mr. Thornton’s face hardened, taking on the same fierce, unyielding expression his mother had worn earlier, and Margaret was immediately repelled by it.
“Yes, the fools are determined to strike. Let them. It will suit us well enough. We gave them a fair chance. They think trade is as flourishing as it was last year. But we see the storm gathering on the horizon, and we are taking in our sails to ride it out. Because we will not explain every last detail of our books to them, they refuse to believe we are acting reasonably. They demand we give them a line by line account of every penny we choose to spend or save. Henderson tried a trick with his men out in Ashley, and it backfired. He actually wanted a strike, it would have balanced his books perfectly, so when the men came to ask for their five percent raise, he told them he would think it over and give them his answer on payday—already knowing full well he would refuse. He thought he would string them along and let them get cocky, but they were smarter than he expected. They got wind of the bad trade prospects, and came to him on Friday and withdrew their claim, so now he is trapped into keeping them all employed. But all the Milton masters met today and gave our answer. We will not raise wages a single penny. We told them we may soon have to cut wages, not raise them. So here we stand, waiting for their next move.”
“And what will that move be?” Mr. Hale asked.
“I expect a simultaneous strike. You will see Milton completely smokeless in a few days, Miss Hale, I imagine.”
“But why,” Margaret asked, “could you not just explain the good, honest reasons you have for expecting trade to turn bad? I do not know if I am putting this rightly, but you must understand what I mean.”
“Do you give your full servants a line by line explanation for every penny you spend or save in your own house? We who own the capital have a perfect right to choose what we do with it.”
“A human right,” Margaret breathed, so softly he barely heard it.
“I beg your pardon, I missed what you said.”
“I would rather not repeat it,” she said. “It touches on a feeling I do not think you would share.”
“Won’t you let me try to understand?” he pleaded, his thoughts suddenly fixed on learning what she had said. She was annoyed by his persistence, and did not want to make too much of her own words.
“I said you had a human right. I meant that there is no reason outside of religious principle to stop you doing exactly as you please with your own property.”
“I do not agree with all your religious views,” he said, speaking in a low voice, as if the two of them were alone. “But do you not credit me with having any of my own, even if they are different from yours?”
