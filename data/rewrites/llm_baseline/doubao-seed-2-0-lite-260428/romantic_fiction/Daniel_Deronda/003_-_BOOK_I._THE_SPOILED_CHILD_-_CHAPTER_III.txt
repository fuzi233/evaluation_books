*“Let no flower of the spring pass by us; let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they be withered.”* — *Book of Wisdom*

It is a small, soft tragedy that Offendene was never stitched into the fabric of Gwendolen Harleth’s childhood, never made dear to her by the giddy rush of summer barefoot runs across its lawns, the familiar cluck of the hens in the backyard, the face of a long-dead neighbor who once slipped her a honey cake on the way home from school. A human life, after all, deserves to root itself deep in one small patch of native earth: a place where the stars above your bedroom window feel like your own private constellation, where the sound of the local blacksmith’s hammer drifting over the fields is as much a part of you as your own heartbeat. At five years old, no one is born a citizen of the world, hungry for abstract ideas and impartiality; that blind, fierce love of your own home milk, your own home hearth, is the very stuff that nourishes body and soul.

But that sweet, unthinking habit of affectionate belonging had never grown in Gwendolen’s life. Offendene had only been chosen as her mother’s home a year before they returned from Leubronn, picked for no more romantic reason than its proximity to Pennicote Rectory. On a late October afternoon, the whole party — Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen, her four little half-sisters, and the governess and maid trailing behind in a second carriage — had first driven up its long avenue, rooks cawing loud overhead, golden elm leaves whirling and sticking to the hems of their black traveling dresses.

The season suited the old oblong red-brick house perfectly, its stone carvings tracing every line of its facade, down to the rows of narrow mullioned windows and the heavy square portico out front. The stone was crusted over with soft green lichen, the brick dusted with pale gray, so that for all its rigid, straight lines, it felt warm, softened by decades of weather. Three avenues of old plantation trees stretched east, west and south from the grounds, and though the house sat tucked behind their screen of foliage, it still offered far-off glimpses of the tall, graceful curves of the chalk downs, glowing honey-gold under the low sun. It was just large enough to be called a proper mansion, moderately rented, no grand manor lands attached, its dark faded upholstery and sombre old paintings making it hard to let to newer, showier tenants. But no one glancing up at its walls could ever mistake it for the home of a retired tradesman: that quiet air of old, gentle birth was worth more to Mrs. Davilow than a dozen modern conveniences, for she and her daughters occupied that delicate border territory of rank where social respectability was a prize worth chasing. To live in a house that had once belonged to a dowager countess gave her a quiet, unspoken thrill of satisfaction, finally having a proper establishment of her own.

The sudden death of her last husband, Captain Davilow, who had only drifted in and out of their lives for nine long years, had made this new life possible, but Gwendolen cared far more for the change itself than the unspoken, awkward explanations behind it. She had always hated their old wandering life, drifting from one foreign watering place to the next, from Parisian apartment to German boarding house, always surrounded by strangers’ scratchy hired furniture, meeting strangers who never treated her like the extraordinary person she knew she was. Even two years spent at a fashionable showy school, where she was always pushed to the front at every recital and prize day, had only deepened her certainty that someone as exceptional as her was never meant to muddle through ordinary, unremarkable circumstances. All that anxiety vanished the moment they settled on Offendene. She never thought to question how her West Indian grandfather had made the fortune inherited by her and her mother, nor did she wonder why her father’s lofty, aristocratic family had never spoken to her mother again — the last time she had tried to ask about her mysterious, long-dead father, at twelve years old, her mother had shut the old memorial box of his miniatures with a sharp, uncharacteristic snap and snapped that she had no feeling at all, and Gwendolen, who loved her soft mother dearly, had never dared bring the subject up again.

She was not a cruel girl, not really: even as a child she had fished drowning insects out of puddles to nurse them back to life, and when she had once strangled her little sister’s canary in a fit of overwrought fury, its shrill singing interrupting her piano practice for the tenth time, she had bought the child a soft white mouse as penance, though she still winced a little to think of that moment even now, at twenty. Her whole life she had been the household’s little princess, waited on hand and foot by her mother, her sisters, the governess, and the maids, and it had never occurred to her that her own pleasure was not the most important thing in the room. Now, as a young woman, that old fiery will had hardened into easy, practiced charm, sharpened with a quiet calculation no one else quite saw beneath it.

On that first day of arrival, none of them had seen the house before — Mrs. Davilow’s brother-in-law Mr. Gascoigne had arranged the rental for them. They all stood clustered under the portico as the front door swung open, the glow of a bright wood fire spilling out into the stone hall, walls hung with old dark paintings, and no one spoke: the four half-sisters, ranging from sixteen-year-old Alice down to ten-year-old Isabel in their shabby black dresses, the quiet, neutral Miss Merry the governess, all of them turned their eyes to Gwendolen, as if their entire future happiness rested on what she thought of the place. Her mother’s worn, gentle face looked up at her with a total, soft appeal, and Gwendolen gazed around her, head held high, the picture of a sleek young racehorse prancing in the paddock among plodding, unremarkable hacks.

“Well, dear, what do you think of the place?” Mrs. Davilow asked at last, her tone soft and tentative.

“It’s charming,” Gwendolen said at once, bright and delighted. “Romantic. Any wonderful, unexpected thing could happen here. It’s the perfect background for anything grand — no one would ever need to be ashamed to live here.”

“There’s certainly nothing common about it,” her mother agreed.

“Oh, it would suit fallen royalty perfectly, grand genteel poverty exactly. It would be far more romantic if we had once lived in a palace and come down to this, wouldn’t it?” Gwendolen laughed, then her tone shifted, sharp with surprise. “But where are uncle and aunt Gascoigne? And cousin Anna? I thought they’d be here to meet us.”

“We’re early,” Mrs. Davilow said, stepping into the hall to greet the waiting housekeeper, who assured her the Gascoignes had been by the day before to check the fires, every room had been aired for a full week, all the brasses polished until they shone, and they would arrive sharp at five o’clock.

Satisfied, Gwendolen tripped up the matted stone staircase and back down again, leading her sisters on a quick tour of the ground floor: the dark oak-paneled dining room hung with a painting of snarling fighting dogs over the sideboard, the library thick with the old, comforting smell of worn brown leather, and last of all the drawing room, reached through a small antechamber crammed full of dented old knickknacks.

“Mamma, come see!” Gwendolen called, throwing off her hat and gloves, spinning to point at the organ tucked against the far wall. “There’s an organ! I shall be Saint Cecilia — someone must paint me as Saint Cecilia. Jocosa, unpin my hair, quickly!” The meek governess fished the comb holding her coils of light brown hair free, and shook the mass out until it flowed smooth and thick far below her slim waist. Gwendolen seated herself on the organ bench, head tilted up in the perfect saintly pose, and her mother laughed, charmed to see her favorite daughter looking so radiant, even in front of the housekeeper.

Then little Isabel gave a small, gasping cry from the other end of the room. She had pried open a hinged wainscot panel, and behind it was a hidden painting: the upturned face of a dead man, an obscure shadowy figure fleeing away from it, arms outstretched in horror. Mrs. Davilow made a face of disgust, but Gwendolen shuddered, cold down to her bones.

“You’ll never dare stay in this room alone now, Gwendolen,” Isabel said, her small voice ringing out.

“How dare you pry open things that are meant to stay shut, you naughty little thing?” Gwendolen snapped, her face flushing hot with anger as she snatched the panel from the little girl’s hand and slammed it hard shut. “Find the key for this lock at once. If there isn’t one, make a new one, and no one — no one — is to open this panel ever again. Bring the key straight to me.”

They hurried up to their black and gold bedroom soon after, the tall mirror between the windows reflecting Gwendolen in all her bright glory, the mass of her loose hair falling over her shoulders. She preened a little, brushing the strands back from her face, laughing that her nose turned up just a little too much to be a proper saint’s nose, but that a crown of white roses would fix it perfectly. When her mother sighed, heavy and weary, she turned to her, sharp with gentle reproach.

“Now mamma, don’t start being dull already,” she said, wrapping her arms around the older woman’s waist. “We can be happy here. Everything is going to be wonderful now. You must stop moping, you hear? I’m never going to waste my life muddling through like everyone else does. I’m going to be happy, no matter what. And I promise I’ll be nice to uncle and aunt, and cousin Anna, I swear it.”

No sooner had they finished dressing than the Gascoignes arrived. Gwendolen greeted them with all her practiced, graceful charm, determined that the relatives who had not seen her since she was sixteen would admire every inch of the woman she had grown into. Mrs. Gascoigne, the rector’s wife, was sharp and capable, used to running a parish and a household of eight children with quiet beneficent authority. Mr. Gascoigne, once a army captain who had only taken orders a few years before his marriage, was a strikingly handsome man of fifty-seven, iron gray hair, a fine straight nose, the kind of man everyone at the local sessions trusted implicitly, who could preach a better sermon than any clergyman in the diocese, and who knew the history of every European campaign like the back of his hand. He looked at Gwendolen with unmistakeable admiration, then drew his shy tiny daughter Anna forward, the girl’s face a soft miniature copy of his own.

“You’ve quite outgrown Anna, my dear,” he said, smiling. “She’s a year younger than you, and she’s done growing long ago. I do hope the two of you will be the very best of companions.”

Gwendolen leaned in to kiss her quiet cousin, warm and genuine, already certain that she would be the brightest, most beloved star of this quiet little corner of Wessex, and that some wonderful, glittering fate was waiting for her at Offendene.
