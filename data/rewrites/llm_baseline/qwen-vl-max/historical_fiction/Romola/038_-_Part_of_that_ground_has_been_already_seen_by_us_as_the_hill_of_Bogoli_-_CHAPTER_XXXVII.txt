Romola awoke to the soft tap of a knuckle against her door. The pale, cold light of dawn seeped through the shutters, casting long shadows across the room. Maso stood there, waiting for the travelling-wallet. He started slightly when she opened the door—no longer the woman he knew, with hair like sunlight and grace in every line, but a figure wrapped in thick grey folds, her face hidden beneath the dark cowl, pale and still as winter stone.

“It is well, Maso,” she said, forcing calm into her voice. “The wallet is ready. You may go on quietly—I shall not be far behind. Once beyond the gates, you may walk more slowly; I may join you before Trespiano.”

She closed the door gently, then reached for the key she had taken from the casket the night before. It was the original key to the small painted tabernacle—the one Tito had forgotten to cast into the Arno. It had slipped unnoticed into the corner of his embroidered scarsella, worn with the purple tunic, and years later, after their marriage, Romola had found it there. She had kept it, not used it, but held it close—knowing it was within reach, a quiet certainty in a life that had grown uncertain.

Now, she turned toward the cabinet where the tabernacle stood. It had been moved near the window, so the morning light fell upon its painted surface just enough to reveal the familiar scenes: Bacchus triumphant, crowned with vines and clusters, embracing Ariadne; the Loves scattering roses, dolphins gliding through rippled seas, all framed by a border of flowers like a paradise bower.

But now, these images struck her with a new bitterness. They were no longer beautiful—they were mocking. Foolish Ariadne, her eyes wide with love, her curls like tendrils entwined in the vines—how she must have believed in joy! How different she would look now, Romola thought, if she could see what lay beneath the surface of this world.

Romola lifted the mirror and looked at herself. The reflection startled her—the pale face, the shadowed eyes, the cowl drawn tight. She set the mirror down quickly, as though repelled. This disguise, this act of concealment, made her feel unclean. Was she becoming one of those fanatics, those weeping nuns she had once despised? The thought filled her with dread.

She thrust the key into the lock of the tabernacle, opened it swiftly, and took out the crucifix without looking at it. With trembling fingers, she threaded a cord through the ring, fastened it around her neck, and hid it beneath the folds of her mantle.

“For Dino’s sake,” she whispered.

There were letters still to write—letters Maso would carry back to Bologna. The first was to Tito:

“Tito, my love for you is dead. And so, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not seek to enforce laws to bring me back—it would only bring you sorrow. The Romola you married will never return. I need explain nothing. The words I spoke to you last—those were not anger, but the truth of an irreversible change.

I ask only that my bridal chest be sent to my godfather, who gave it to me. It holds my wedding garments, the portraits, and relics of my parents.”

She folded the ring inside the letter and wrote Tito’s name on the outside.

The second letter was to Bernardo del Nero:

“Dearest Godfather—If I could have brought comfort to your life by staying, I would not have left. But I have gone. Do not ask why. If you love my father, prevent anyone from seeking me. I could not endure Florence any longer. I cannot speak of why. Let my life remain silent. I have asked that my bridal chest be sent to you. When you open it, you will understand.

Give all my mother’s things to my cousin Brigida, and ask her to forgive me for not saying farewell.

Farewell, my second father. The best thing I have in life is still the memory of your kindness—and gratitude.

Romola.”

She placed the letters beside the crucifix in the depths of her mantle. Everything was done. She was ready.

No sound stirred in the house. She descended the stairs like a ghost, her steps soft on the stone. Outside, the street was empty, the city still asleep. Her heart pounded, yet she felt a strange strength in each step—like a chain broken at last. The urgency of her choice, the fear of delay, pushed sorrow aside. As she reached the Ponte Rubaconte, she noticed less the sight of Santa Croce than the widening streak of yellow light cutting through the grey sky. She quickened her pace—fearful of faces, of recognition.

Her path led straight to Borgo Pinti, then along the city walls to Porta San Gallo. It passed through Piazza di Santa Croce, but she walked through it without pause, without looking toward the church. The idea of being watched—of strangers speculating on her grief—filled her with a physical shiver. She felt degraded even by the act of her husband that had driven her here. Yet no one noticed her—not a glance from a window, not a word from a passerby.

She was alone.

Beyond the gate, the city dwindled. The sky opened wide above her. She stepped into freedom—a life of solitude and hardship, yes, but free. She had broken the chains she had once accepted blindly. From now on, no breath of false affection would touch her cheek, no oppressive mind suffocate her spirit.

The air was sharp, the trees bare, the hills bleak. These were not lands haunted by gods of beauty and pleasure—gods she had abandoned forever.

Then, suddenly, the sun broke through. Light flooded the road, casting long shadows. In that instant, the world seemed alive with divine presence—so strong, so clear, that Romola felt a deeper awe settle within her. This was no mere sunrise. It was a moment of revelation, of transformation. She stood in the golden glow, her own shadow stretching before her, a silent companion she could not escape.

Until now, she had seen only peasants with mules, and the winding road had hidden Maso’s distance. But as she passed Pietra and climbed higher ground, she lifted the edge of her cowl to look ahead.

And froze.

It was not Maso.

Two monks approached, only yards away.

The cowl had shielded her vision—she had been seeing only the path, her shadow, the brightness beneath her feet. Now, the encounter startled her. Monks—her disguise made them dangerous. They might demand prayers, passwords, signs of faith she could not give. She walked on, pretending indifference, her hands clenched, her breath shallow.

They passed, moving downhill. Relief came quickly. She saw a flat stone beside a cypress tree rising from a green bank. She sat down, turning her back to Florence, refusing to look until they were gone.

She raised the cowl again—and saw Maso and the mules ahead, not far off. She could catch up. She might linger.

She sat there, breathing deeply, feeling the cold wind on her face, the weight of the world lifted from her shoulders.

She was free. Alone. And finally, hers.
