Nothing stirred in the house through the night. And—thankfully—no secret exchange passed between Miss Rachel and Rosanna, despite the watchful eyes of Sergeant Cuff. I had expected the detective to leave for Frizinghall at first light, but he lingered as if something else demanded his attention. I let him be, and soon after dawn, I stepped into the gardens, where I found Mr. Franklin walking along his usual path beside the shrubbery.

We had scarcely exchanged two words when Sergeant Cuff appeared, stepping forward with a quiet purpose. He approached Mr. Franklin, who greeted him with an air of cold formality. “Have you anything to say to me?” Mr. Franklin snapped, offering no more than a curt reply to the sergeant’s polite greeting.

“I have something to say to you, sir,” the sergeant replied, “regarding the inquiry I’m conducting here.” He paused, then continued: “You saw yesterday how the investigation was really turning. Naturally, given your position, you’re shocked—distressed. And naturally, you project your anger over family disgrace onto me.”

“What do you want?” Mr. Franklin cut in sharply.

“I want to remind you,” said the sergeant, “that so far, I’ve not been proven wrong. Bear that in mind. At the same time, remember I am an officer of the law acting under the authority of the mistress of this house. Under those circumstances—is it not your duty, as a good citizen, to assist me with any information you may possess?”

“I possess no special information,” Mr. Franklin declared.

Sergeant Cuff dismissed the answer as if it had never been spoken. “Then you may save my time,” he went on, “by choosing to understand me—and speaking plainly.”

“I don’t understand you,” Mr. Franklin retorted. “And I have nothing to say.”

“Last night,” the sergeant pressed, “one of the female servants—without naming her—spoke to you privately.”

Again, Mr. Franklin interrupted. “I have nothing to say.”

I stood by in silence, recalling the sudden movement I’d seen in the swing door the previous evening—the coat-tails vanishing down the hall. It struck me that the sergeant had likely just heard enough to suspect Rosanna had confessed something to Mr. Franklin. The thought barely formed—when, from the end of the walk, came Rosanna Spearman herself, followed closely by Penelope, who seemed determined to turn her back toward the house.

Seeing Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna stopped abruptly, clearly torn. Penelope waited behind her. Mr. Franklin noticed them instantly. But Sergeant Cuff, with his sharp cunning, pretended not to see them at all.

All this unfolded in seconds. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could speak, the sergeant smoothly resumed his line of questioning, raising his voice so Rosanna might hear:

“You needn’t fear harming the girl, sir,” he said. “On the contrary, I urge you to trust me—if you feel any interest in Rosanna Spearman.”

Mr. Franklin, without glancing at the girls, answered loudly: “I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman.”

I turned to look down the path. At once, I saw Rosanna whirl around the moment the words left his lips. Instead of resisting Penelope, she allowed her daughter to take her arm and lead her back to the house.

The breakfast bell rang just as they disappeared. Even Sergeant Cuff had to concede defeat. He turned to me quietly. “I shall go to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge. I’ll return before two.”

And with that, he walked off—silent and gone—for several hours, leaving us free from his presence.

When we were alone, Mr. Franklin spoke to me. “You must make it right with Rosanna,” he said. “It seems I’m destined to offend her, however unintentionally. You saw what happened—Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. If he could confuse me—or provoke her into speaking—either of us might have revealed something useful to him. In the moment, I saw no better way out than to shut her down and show him I saw through his game. He was listening, Betteredge, when I spoke to you last night.”

He had done worse than listen, I thought to myself. He had remembered my telling him that Rosanna was in love with Mr. Franklin—and he had used that knowledge deliberately, appealing to Mr. Franklin’s supposed interest in her—while she stood nearby.

“As to listening,” I remarked, keeping my thoughts to myself, “we’re all in danger of being caught in the same net if this goes on. Peering, prying, eavesdropping—these are the natural habits of people in our situation. In another day or two, we’ll all be silenced—not because we lack courage, but because we’ll all be too busy spying on one another to speak. Excuse my outburst, sir—but this cursed mystery has taken hold of me like liquor, making me reckless. I won’t forget what you told me. I’ll find the chance to speak to Rosanna.”

“You haven’t said anything to her about last night, have you?” Mr. Franklin asked.

“No, sir.”

“Then say nothing now. I won’t invite her confidence while the sergeant is watching. My behavior isn up consistent, Betteredge—is it? I see no way out of this mess that isn’t terrifying—unless the Diamond leads us straight to Rosanna. Yet I can’t, and won’t, help the sergeant pin her down.”

Unreasonable, perhaps. But I understood him perfectly. If you can, just once, remember that you are mortal—then you’ll understand him too.

While Sergeant Cuff made his way to Frizinghall, things inside the house settled into uneasy stillness. Miss Rachel remained locked in her room, waiting for the carriage to take her to her aunt’s. My lady and Mr. Franklin shared breakfast. Afterward, Mr. Franklin took one of his sudden decisions and rushed out for a long walk to clear his mind. Only I saw him leave, and he promised he’d return before the sergeant did.

The weather, which had hinted at change the night before, had arrived. Heavy rain fell early, followed by a strong wind that grew fiercer as the day wore on. Though clouds gathered again, the rain held off. It wasn’t a bad day for a walk—if you were young, strong, and could face the gusts sweeping in from the sea.

After breakfast, I attended my lady and helped settle the household accounts. She mentioned the Moonstone only once—warning us not to discuss it yet. “Wait till that man comes back,” she said, meaning the sergeant. “We *must* talk then. But we don’t have to talk now.”

Later, I found Penelope waiting in my room. “Father,” she said, “I wish you’d come and speak to Rosanna. I’m terribly worried about her.”

I guessed the cause at once. But I follow a rule: men, being superior creatures, should guide women—if possible. When a woman asks me to do something—daughter or not—I always insist on knowing why. The more often you make them search their own minds, the more manageable they become. It’s not their fault they act first and think later—it’s the fault of those who indulge them.

Penelope’s reason, in her own words: “I’m afraid, father, Mr. Franklin hurt Rosanna deeply—though he didn’t mean to.”

“What brought Rosanna to the shrubbery?” I asked.

“Her own madness,” Penelope replied. “She was determined to speak to Mr. Franklin today, no matter what. I tried to stop her—you saw that. If only I’d gotten her away before she heard those awful words…”

“There! there!” I said. “Don’t panic. I don’t recall anything alarming happening to her.”

“Nothing alarmed her, father. But Mr. Franklin said he took no interest in her at all—and oh, he said it in such a cruel tone!”

“He said it to shut the sergeant up,” I explained.

“I told her that,” Penelope said. “But you see, father—even though Mr. Franklin isn’t to blame—he’s been rejecting and humiliating her for weeks. And now this adds to it all! She has no right to expect him to care. It’s monstrous of her to forget her place. But she seems to have lost all pride, all feeling. When he said those words, she froze. A strange silence came over her—and since then, she’s moved like a woman in a dream.”

I began to feel unease. There was something in Penelope’s voice that silenced my usual certainty. I recalled the scene between Mr. Franklin and Rosanna the night before—she had looked shattered. Now, unluckily, she’d been wounded again, poor soul, on the same tender spot. Sad—so sad—especially since she had no justification for her pain and no right to feel it.

I had promised Mr. Franklin to speak to Rosanna. This seemed the moment to keep my word.

We found her sweeping the corridor outside the bedrooms—pale, composed, dressed neatly in her plain print gown. Her eyes held a curious dullness—not from tears, but as if she’d stared too long at something unseen. Possibly, it was a fog of her own thoughts. Nothing in the corridor offered a new sight—everything she saw had been familiar hundreds of times.

“Cheer up, Rosanna,” I said. “Don’t torment yourself with fancy fears. I’ve got a message from Mr. Franklin.”

I delivered it kindly, gently, trying to comfort her. My principles toward women are strict—but practice, I confess, often fails me when faced with them directly.

“Mr. Franklin is very kind and thoughtful,” I said. “Please thank him.”

That was all she said.

My daughter had already observed that Rosanna moved like a woman in a dream. Now I added: she also listened and spoke like one.

I doubted she grasped what I’d said.

“Are you sure you understand me?” I asked.

“Quite sure,” she echoed—not like a living person, but like a machine.

She kept sweeping.

I took the broom gently from her hands.

“Come, come, my girl,” I said. “This isn’t like you. You’re burdened. I’m your friend—and I’ll stand by you, even if you’ve done wrong. Confess it, Rosanna—confess it!”

Once, my words would have drawn tears. Now, no change came to her eyes.

“Yes,” she said. “I’ll confess it.”

“To my lady?” I asked.

“No.”

“To Mr. Franklin?”

“Yes. To Mr. Franklin.”

I didn’t know what to say. She was too far gone to understand the warning Mr. Franklin had given me: not to speak to him alone.

I tread carefully. “Mr. Franklin has gone out for a walk.”

“It doesn’t matter,” she replied. “I won’t trouble Mr. Franklin today.”

“Why not speak to my lady?” I urged. “To relieve your mind, you should go to your merciful, Christian mistress—who has always been kind to you.”

She studied me a moment, her gaze steady, as if committing my words to memory. Then she took the broom back from me and walked slowly down the corridor.

“No,” she said, continuing to sweep. “I know a better way to relieve my mind than that.”

“What is it?”

“Please let me finish my work.”

Penelope stepped forward to help. Rosanna shook her head. “No. I want to do my work. Thank you, Penelope.”

She turned to me. “Thank you, Mr. Betteredge.”

There was no moving her. No further words could be spoken.

I signaled to Penelope to leave with me. We walked away, leaving Rosanna exactly as we’d found her—sweeping the corridor, like a woman lost in a dream.

“This is beyond me,” I said. “This is a matter for the doctor.”

Penelope reminded me of Mr. Candy’s illness—caused by a chill he caught on the night of the dinner party. His assistant, Mr. Ezra Jennings, was available—but few knew much about him. He’d been hired under odd circumstances, and none of us trusted him. There were other doctors at Frizinghall—but they were strangers. Penelope feared they might do more harm than good in Rosanna’s state.

I considered telling my lady—but hesitated. The weight of worry she already carried was heavy enough. Still, something had to be done. Rosanna’s condition terrified me—and my mistress deserved to know.

Reluctantly, I went to her sitting-room. It was empty. My lady was with Miss Rachel. I couldn’t see her until she emerged. I waited—uselessly—until the clock on the front staircase struck quarter to two.

Five minutes later, I heard my name called from the drive.

I recognized the voice at once.

Sergeant Cuff had returned from Frizinghall.
