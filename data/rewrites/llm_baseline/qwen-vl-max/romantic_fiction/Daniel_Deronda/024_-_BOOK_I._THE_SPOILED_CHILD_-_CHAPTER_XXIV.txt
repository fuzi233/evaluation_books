“I question things but do not find
One that will answer to my mind:
And all the world appears unkind.” —WORDSWORTH.

Gwendolen was relieved to have finished her meeting with Klesmer before facing her uncle and aunt. She had made up her mind—there would be no comfort in what lay ahead, only discomforts. Yet she resolved to stand firm, to keep her composure no matter how humbling the situation might become.

The meeting didn’t take place until Monday, when Gwendolen went to the rectory with her mother. On the way, they stopped at Sawyer’s Cottage, where they walked through every corner of the cramped rooms, lit by harsh midday sun unsoftened by curtains or blinds. The furnishings were still incomplete—meant to be filled from the rectory’s own meager surplus.

“How can you possibly bear it, mamma?” Gwendolen asked as they left, unable to speak while standing inside the bare, dusty space—the tiny garden with its cabbage stalks, the yew arbor covered in dust and cobwebs. “You and the four girls crammed into that room, with those green-and-yellow walls pressing in on you? And without me?”

“It will be some comfort that you won’t have to endure it too, dear,” her mother replied gently.

“If it weren’t for needing money, I’d rather live there than become a governess,” Gwendolen said, her voice sharp with resentment.

“Don’t decide against it yet,” her mother urged. “If you go to the bishop’s house, you’ll have every luxury. You’ve always cared about such things. It won’t be nearly as hard as climbing those narrow stairs, hearing dishes clatter, and listening to the girls talk.”

“It feels like a nightmare,” Gwendolen burst out. “I can’t believe Uncle Henry would let you go to such a place. He should have found another way.”

“Don’t be unreasonable, child. What could he have done?”

“That was his responsibility,” Gwendolen insisted. “It seems absurd that people in our position must fall so suddenly. The world feels unjust.” Her usual sense of order—the idea that life should unfold with fairness and grace—had been shattered.

She spoke with a temper sharpened by sudden hardship, though she might have handled others’ misfortunes more thoughtfully. Still, she wasn’t seeking virtue in her words—only cleverness. That mattered now, even if it made her sound worse than she felt.

Yet despite her inner turmoil, she couldn’t help being moved when her uncle and aunt greeted her with kindness far greater than ever before. Their quiet dignity, their calm acceptance of necessity, struck her deeply. Mr. Gascoigne, whose character had once been dimmed by worldly comforts—like a woman’s beauty obscured by fashion—now shone clearly under this new burden. He had already sold his carriage, rethought his wardrobe, given up meat at breakfast, canceled subscriptions, pulled Edwy from school, and arranged study hours for the boys himself. He managed the household with frugal precision, and his spirit had infected everyone around him.

Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who always modeled themselves after their father, scarcely missed anything for themselves. They truly believed the hardest blow was for Mrs. Davilow and her children. Anna, for the first time, set aside her anger over Rex to feel compassion for Gwendolen. Mrs. Gascoigne hoped trouble might shape her niece for good—without feeling the need to add further pain for that purpose. Together, they had quietly planned ways to furnish the cottage using household items, but they kept those plans quiet, focusing instead on Gwendolen’s return and the comfort it brought her mother.

There was no reason for Gwendolen to resent them. Instead, she found herself alert, almost startled, when her uncle began speaking in a steady, kind tone about the efforts he’d made to secure her a suitable position.

“I knew we couldn’t wait, Gwendolen,” he said. “A good post isn’t found overnight. And even if we waited longer, I doubt we’d find one better than Bishop Mompert’s. I know both him and his wife well—that helps. Our correspondence has gone well, but Mrs. Mompert wants to meet you before making a final decision. She may arrange for you to see her in Wanchester on her way to London. I know it might be difficult, but you’ll have time to prepare.”

“Do you know why she wants to see me, Uncle?” Gwendolen asked, already imagining the reasons—judgmental glances, hidden expectations, disapproval.

Her uncle smiled. “Don’t worry. She wants a clearer picture than I can give. A mother is careful about choosing a companion for her daughters. I told her you’re young, but she supervises her girls closely, so age isn’t much of an issue. She’s refined and principled—she doesn’t want a French governess. I’m confident she’ll find your manners and talents excellent. And both she and the bishop will oversee the moral and religious aspects of your work.”

Gwendolen said nothing, but the flush rising on her cheeks betrayed her deep unease. Anna, worried, slipped her hand into Gwendolen’s. Mr. Gascoigne, kind-hearted as he was, sensed the weight of this change upon her. Still determined to present things cheerfully, he added, “I value this position so highly I’d have tried to get it for Anna—if she fit the role. It’s not just a job—it’s a home, with real education continuing. ‘Governess’ is really a misnomer. The bishop’s views are more Low Church than mine—he’s close to Lord Grampian—but he’s not narrow-minded. In fact, he’s one of the most respected bishops in the diocese. We once debated the Bible Society, but he’s always remained respectful toward me.”

To him, these words carried reassurance. To Gwendolen, they were like snakes slithering across her path—each phrase a threat. “Continuation of education,” “bishop’s views,” “privately strict,” “Bible Society”—they echoed like warnings. She saw not opportunity, but confinement. Already repelled by the prospect, these words intensified her dread. She began to search desperately for another way.

“There was another position, wasn’t there, mamma?” she asked, forcing control.

“Yes,” the rector replied, slightly hesitant. “But that’s in a school. I wouldn’t feel as satisfied with that choice. It’s harder work, less rewarding, and you’d have less chance of getting it.”

“Oh, dear no,” Mrs. Gascoigne said quickly. “It would be much harder for you, my dear—less suitable. You might not even have your own room.” Memories of school flooded back—crowded dormitories, harsh rules—and Gwendolen realized this alternative offered no relief.

She turned back to her uncle. “When might Mrs. Mompert send for me?”

“It’s uncertain,” he said, “but she promised not to consider anyone else until she meets you. She understands your situation deeply. Likely within two weeks. But I must go—I’m letting part of my glebe land.”

He left cheerfully, convinced he’d helped Gwendolen accept her fate. Having spoken wisely, he assumed wisdom would produce results. As a man used to guiding others, he expected obedience to follow speech.

“What a support Henry is to us all,” Mrs. Gascoigne said after he left.

“He truly is,” Mrs. Davilow agreed. “I wish I had that kind of cheerfulness. It’s like having a fortune.”

“And Rex is just like him,” Mrs. Gascoigne added. “I must tell you about a letter he sent. Let me read you a bit,” she said, pulling it from her pocket. Anna looked nervous—she hadn’t been allowed to mention Rex in front of Gwendolen.

Mrs. Gascoigne scanned the letter, searching for passages to share. But she paused, folded it, and said, “Still, he says our troubles have made him a man. He sees meaning in hard work. He wants a fellowship, to teach pupils, to help his brothers—everything remarkable. The letter is full of humor—just like him. He writes, ‘Tell mother she’s advertised for a jolly good hard-working son, just in time to stop me from shipping off; I offer myself for the role.’ It came Friday. I’ve never seen Henry so moved since Rex was born. It felt like a gain to balance our loss.”

That letter had softened both Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna toward Gwendolen. Gwendolen, in turn, smiled at Anna, pinching her chin playfully—“Everything’s fine now, isn’t it?” She wasn’t cruel or petty. She simply couldn’t bear being made miserable by others.

But when conversation turned to furnishing the cottage, Gwendolen withdrew entirely. She felt she’d done enough for the day. Inside, a fierce struggle raged. The thought of presenting herself to Mrs. Mompert—being judged, approved or rejected—felt like adding insult to injury. Even as a governess, she’d be tested. Once accepted, she’d still be watched, expected to perform tasks she loathed. The bishop might quiz her on serious matters. This wasn’t a career—it was imprisonment.

Wild thoughts flared: running away to become an actress, free from all restraint. But Klesmer’s words lingered—his warning of vulgar men, crude familiarity, coarse laughter behind polite masks. She wasn’t reckless. She demanded respect. She dreamed of being a heroine—not at a gaming table, but in a world where she’d still be treated with dignity. To be cast alone among strangers, relying on their uncertain kindness, felt unbearable. Even the episcopal household, though rigid, seemed preferable. At least there, she wouldn’t be mocked.

Yet here, too, she’d be unseen, unpetted, her feelings ignored. The injustice of it grew heavier each hour. Why her? She’d been shaped for something else—something grander. Now, everything felt ruined. Hope felt foolish. Her talents meant nothing. No direction promised fulfillment. Some girls might imagine romantic futures—governesses becoming beloved, sought-after figures. But Gwendolen couldn’t dream such dreams. Her heart was crushed by what was real, not what might be. She felt sick of life. Nothing seemed worth living for. Religion offered no solace—her suffering felt caused by others’ cruelty, not divine design. The world, in her eyes, had nothing pleasant to offer. All talk of courage, duty, or joy in labor sounded hollow. For her, becoming a governess meant falling—descending, never rising. She’d never separated happiness from distinction, from being admired.

No wonder she felt life unworthy of her breath. When personal glory fades, what remains? Perhaps we all feel this way, in moments of despair—when our pride is wounded, when the world seems indifferent. A young girl facing life’s maze with no map, suddenly stripped of confidence, deserves pity, not judgment.

Despite her strength, her despair affected her body. She felt numb, unable to act. Meals became burdens. Others’ conversations seemed irrelevant, ignorant of her pain. She didn’t entertain thoughts of suicide—those were for weak minds. Her torment was simpler: the certainty that she must live a life she hated.

She avoided the rectory. Pretending compliance felt like a betrayal. She refused to care about the cottage’s furniture. Miss Merry stayed to help—people like Jocosa loved such tasks. Her mother had to make excuses when Anna visited. The calm Gwendolen had vowed to keep had turned into helpless emptiness. “I suppose I’ll pretend soon,” she thought. “But why start now?”

Her mother watched her, silent with sorrow. She fell back into old habits of tenderness, trying to guess what Gwendolen was thinking, wishing she could ease her daughter’s pain.

One afternoon, in the black-and-yellow bedroom, as her mother lingered over her clothes, Gwendolen suddenly sat up.

“Mamma,” she said, lifting the lid of the casket. “I forgot these. Why didn’t you remind me? Please sell them. You won’t miss them. You gave them all to me long ago.”

She lifted the tray, looking beneath.

“If we don’t need them, darling, I’d rather keep them for you,” Mrs. Davilow said, sitting beside her. “Why did you put that handkerchief in here?”

It was the torn one—stuffed with the turquoise necklace.

“It was with the necklace. I was in a hurry,” Gwendolen said, pulling it out and tucking it in her pocket. “Don’t sell the necklace, mamma,” she added, a new emotion stirring within her.

“No, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said softly. “It was made from your father’s chain. I’d rather not sell the rest—they’re not valuable. My best jewelry was taken years ago.”

She blushed, avoiding the painful truth of her husband’s theft.

“These haven’t been counted for expenses. Take them with you.”

“That’s useless, mamma,” Gwendolen said coldly. “Governesses don’t wear jewels. Get me a gray frieze dress and a straw bonnet—like Aunt’s charity children.”

“No, dear. I’m sure the Momperts will appreciate elegance.”

“I’m not sure what they’ll expect,” Gwendolen snapped. “All I know is that I must be what they want.”

“If there’s anything you’d prefer—anything else we could do instead—tell me, Gwendolen. Say what’s in your heart. I’ll try anything for you.”

“Oh, mamma, there’s nothing. I can’t do better. I’ll be lucky if they’ll have me. I’ll bring you eighty pounds. That’s all I care about. I won’t spend anything this year—you’ll have it all. I don’t know how far it’ll go, but you won’t have to sew yourself blind or strain your eyes anymore.”

She didn’t touch her mother. Her eyes were fixed on the necklace, turning it slowly in her fingers.

“Bless you, my dear,” Mrs. Davilow whispered, tears forming. “Don’t despair. You’re young. There may still be great happiness.”

“I don’t see any reason to hope, mamma,” Gwendolen said, her voice hard.

Mrs. Davilow said nothing. She wondered again—what happened between her daughter and Grandcourt?

“I’ll keep this necklace, mamma,” Gwendolen said, placing it aside and closing the casket. “But sell the rest—even if they fetch little. Ask Uncle Henry what to do. I’ll never wear them again. I’m going to take the veil. I wonder if all the poor souls who’ve done it felt this way.”

“Don’t exaggerate, dear.”

“How can anyone know I’m exaggerating when I’m speaking of my own pain? I didn’t say what others feel.”

She took the handkerchief from her pocket, wrapped it carefully around the necklace, and placed it inside the casket.

Mrs. Davilow watched, puzzled. But Gwendolen’s tone discouraged questions.
