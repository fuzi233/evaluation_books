Following the departure of her discredited spiritual advisor, Madam Esmond and her son finally reached a truce. Yet, though George never voiced the grievance, the shadow of the quarrel haunted him deeply. Soon after these domestic upheavals, a fever seized the boy, driving him into a delirium wherein he screamed, "Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!" His mother sat vigil beside his tossing form, terrified by his ravings. Her remedies proved futile; the malady grew stronger despite the salves and potions she hoarded. Desperate, she sought out Mr. Dempster, riding through the snow on her pony to plead for his aid. With characteristic humility, Dempster promised to bury his resentment as she had her pride. He gathered his supplies and his sole enslaved attendant, returning to Castlewood with a renewed hope. For several days, it seemed likely that poor Harry would inherit the estate, but Dempster's skill prevailed. The fever broke, the spasms subsided, and George was restored to near-health.

A physician advised a voyage to England for a change of air, but the widow, contrite for her past haste in quarreling with her kin, decided against it. Instead, they resolved on a journey north and east. Accompanied by Mr. Dempster as tutor and two servants, the two youths sailed to New York and ascended the majestic Hudson River to Albany. There, they were welcomed by the province's elite before traveling deeper into French territory, where they received warm hospitality from the local gentry. While Harry spent his time camping with the Indigenous tribes, trading furs, and hunting bears, George, whose constitution remained fragile, became a darling of the French ladies. Fluent in the language, he mastered the *bel air*, dancing the minuet with grace and playing the violin with such proficiency that he could easily pass as a native Frenchman. He even cultivated a romantic entanglement with a young ensign of the Auvergne regiment, the Chevalier de la Jabotiere, dueling honorably with him and swearing an eternal friendship—a feat Harry, busy on a hunt, could only envy. Madame de Mouchy, the Superintendent's lady, wrote a glowing letter to Madam Esmond praising her son's conduct, a triumph that likely would have pained Mr. Whitfield.

Upon their return after ten delightful months, the transformation was striking. George had grown to match his younger brother in stature, the twins nearly indistinguishable save for their hair: George's raven tresses and Harry's light locks bound with ribbons. However, the traveler who had followed the lad's early history would note that George Esmond retained a jealous and suspicious nature, generous yet incapable of forgiveness, and unable to harbor goodwill toward an honorable gentleman whose name would later echo through the ages. Though Mr. Washington, then barely of age, possessed an aura of maturity and gravity beyond his years—trusted by the gentry as a manager of affairs since childhood—George returned from his travels unchanged in his low opinion of his mother's favorite friend.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Mountain, Madam Esmond's droll and jealous companion, delighted in speculating on matrimonial designs. She believed every unmarried man harbored intentions for the mistress of the mansion. She convinced herself that the odious Mr. Ward courted the widow, who reciprocated, and that the ambitious Mr. Washington, though fifteen years her junior, sought a wealthy bride. "Great tall men always marry little chits," she reasoned, dismissing the height disparity between the six-foot-two Washington and the five-foot-three widow. When she shared these "sage" theories with George, begging him to listen, he sternly interjected, "For Heaven's sake, hold your tongue." Mrs. Mountain protested that she could not keep her eyes shut, citing the Colonel's constant presence and the dedicated "room" at Castlewood reserved for him. "One day will come when he won't go away," she sighed.

Consequently, George adopted a frigid courtesy toward the Colonel, which chafed the honest gentleman, while Mrs. Mountain resorted to stinging sarcasm that George would have torn through like brambles. George retreated to his study, sulking amidst books of mathematics, French, and Latin, growing increasingly isolated.

While Harry was away with sporting companions, the Major visited Castlewood. Tender and kind to the housekeeper, he received a special cordiality that nearly ignited George's jealousy. Yet, this visit marked an adieu. Major Washington was departing for a perilous journey to the western Virginia frontier and beyond. News had reached them of French incursions into British territory; the Lords of Canada and Louisiana were aggressively expanding, driving settlers from their homes. Under the convenient legal fiction that coastal possession entitled one to all inland lands to the Pacific, the British charters failed to define eastern boundaries, leaving the colonies vulnerable. The French, meanwhile, aimed to connect their northern and southern colonies via the Mississippi and the Great Lakes. By 1753, the diplomatic pretense collapsed on the Ohio River, where British and French settlers clashed. Native Americans, the true proprietors of the soil, were ignored by the European logicians of St. James's and Versailles, their tomahawks merely tools in a dispute they deemed strictly European.

The Ohio Company, holding grants from Virginia, found their settlements invaded by French military detachments. Seeking protection, they appealed to Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie, who dispatched an embassy to demand the cessation of hostilities. Young Mr. Washington eagerly volunteered, leaving his rural pursuits to lead a guide, an interpreter, and a small retinue across the wilderness in the fall of 1753. Reaching Fort Le Boeuf, he delivered Governor Dinwiddie's message, only to receive a blunt refusal: the French held the place and intended to possess the entire Ohio region.

Returning through danger, across frozen rivers and lonely forests, Washington faced a different reality upon his arrival home. Harry, returning from a cockfight, lamented missing such noble sport, while the Colonel, now a hero of sorts, was lavishly praised by Madam Esmond. "Think of you," she exclaimed, contrasting Harry's frivolities with the Major's watchfulness over the frozen wilds. "I wish my eldest son were doing something in the service of his country!" George, however, offered a pragmatic plea: "I desire no better than to go home and seek for employment... You surely will not have me serve under Mr. Washington?" The widow refused, insisting an Esmond could only serve with a King's commission.

Washington, meanwhile, raised a regiment of Virginians, hoping to impose a peremptory veto on the French invaders. A smaller force under Colonel Trent had been sent ahead to fortify a site on the Pennsylvania border—the future location of Pittsburgh. There, Trent's forty men, vastly outnumbered by Canadians, were forced to withdraw. The French seized the fort, strengthened it, and named it Fort Duquesne in honor of their governor. War had not yet been declared; the guns were loaded, but no order had been given. It was a strange irony that a young Virginian officer, firing a single shot, would awaken a conflict destined to last sixty years, reshape continents, and ultimately birth a great republic, leaving the prize of fame to the man who struck the first blow.

Little did he know his fate. A simple gentleman eager to serve his King, he commanded a small body of provincial troops the following year. He encountered the French vanguard and, in a moment of impulse, fired upon them, killing their leader. But the odds were insurmountable; he was compelled to retreat and eventually capitulate. On July 4, 1754, Colonel Washington marched out from his hastily built Fort Necessity, surrendered to the conqueror, and departed.

Saddened and humbled, the young officer returned to Castlewood. Before his departure, he had boasted of victory, angering George with his perceived scorn. Now, standing before his old friend, he confessed his shame. "I was angry when I parted from you," he admitted, extending a hand that George grasped eagerly. "I thought you laughed at us. My ridicule made me angry, and I boasted too much."

"Nay, you have done your best, George," Harry replied, his jealousy forgotten in sympathy for his fallen comrade. "A hundred and fifty starving men with scarce ammunition could not face five times their number. Everybody knows Mr. Washington would do his duty."

"Oh, but that some of ours were here!" cried Madam Esmond, tossing her head. "I promise you a few good English regiments would make the white-coats run."

"You think nothing of the provincials," Washington said gloomily, hanging his head. "All those fine dreams are over. 'Tis kind of your ladyship to receive a poor beaten fellow."

George, moved by the Colonel's raw emotion, prepared to offer words of comfort, but his mother interrupted. "Kind of us to receive you, Colonel Washington!" she declared, performing a deep curtsy. "I never heard that when men were unhappy, our sex were less their friends."

The gesture, meant as kindness, instantly reignited George's jealousy, casting a new, darker shadow over the reunion.
