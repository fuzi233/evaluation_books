“This, too is probable, according to that saying of Agathon: ‘It is a part of probability that many improbable things will happen.’”
—ARISTOTLE: *Poetics*

The fire in Deronda’s blood had not cooled hours after he’d parted from Mordecai, the gaunt, fervent Jewish visionary who locked eyes with him on the bridge as if he had been waiting for Daniel his whole life. For a man far less sensitive, far less prone to turning every small encounter over in his mind until he uncovered its hidden threads, that meeting would have been little more than a curious oddity of a London afternoon. But for Deronda, who felt every current of the world around him far deeper than most, who had spent years questioning the quiet, unmoored state of his own life, that chance meeting had torn a hole in the fabric of the predictable days he had been drifting through, and now he found himself fighting not to be swept away entirely.

He was sharp enough to feel quiet alarm at how thoroughly Mordecai’s burning certainty, his absolute, unshakable trust that Deronda was the long-awaited companion to carry his life’s work forward, had wrapped around his heart. It was never his nature to brush off a soul that had laid itself bare before him, to dismiss as trivial a moment that felt as sacred as the turning points he had read about in old histories—the moments that changed lives and shaped centuries. He found himself thinking: if he had read this story in an old scroll, of a young man just as unmoored as himself, living two thousand years ago in Palestine or Rome, who stumbled on a dying dreamer who claimed he was fated to be the keeper of a great, burning purpose—would he not have understood completely why that young man’s whole world shifted? Why, simply because he wore a white tie to dinner, lived in a world of polite society that laughed too easily at any earnest passion as pretension, should he feel ashamed of being moved? He would not cower before the mocking wit of people too dull to feel any great emotion, who sneered at anyone who dared to take their own life’s purpose seriously.

But nor would he let himself be swept along by mere feeling, by the contagion of Mordecai’s fervor, without giving his reason its due say. He would not let his pity for a man wasting away before his eyes drag him down a path he could not yet see clearly.

What had actually happened, after all? He could hear Sir Hugo’s practical, worldly voice in his head, clear as day: A consumptive Jew, driven half out of his mind by the double weight of his failing body and the consuming passion of his faith, has fixed on you as the hero of his own private dream, a figure he conjured out of despair for his own short life and desperate hope that his work would not die with him. It’s a strange case, but far from rare. Fanatics are not quite as common as bankrupts, but you find them everywhere: the man who swears he has rewritten Newton’s laws of physics and all the learned professors are conspiring to hide his discovery, the man who has invented a perfect metaphysical system no one else can see, all of them begging for someone, anyone, to listen to them. This Mordecai is more poetic, more touching in his speech than most, but he falls right in with the rest. You can be kind to him, help him where you can, but there is no reason to take his wild notions seriously. Any man of the world would tell you that. And when he realizes you are not the savior he imagined, that will be the cruelest, final disappointment of his life.

Deronda let all those cold, reasonable thoughts wash over him, repeated them to himself slowly, testing their weight. He had faced this question before, but never so urgently: how do you tell the difference between a true prophet, the man who will change the course of human history, and a harmless monomaniac chasing a fantasy of perpetual motion? Both hold their convictions with the same unshakable ferocity, both defy every skeptical voice that tells them they are wrong. You cannot sort them out with a simple test, a quick label to dismiss one or exalt the other. If we wait for the ages to judge who was right and who was mad, then nothing will ever get done. The steam engine would have never existed if James Watt had not been taken seriously in his own time, by the people who could see what he was reaching for, before it was proven real.

That line of thinking was old, familiar ground to Deronda, and it kept him from writing Mordecai off out of hand, even before he considered the strange, personal claim the dying man had laid at his feet: the quiet, almost feverish certainty that Deronda was of Jewish blood. That claim, on the surface, looked absurd, impossible to prove. But it did something far deeper than appeal to his ready sympathy: it laid hold of his unquiet, searching conscience.

Deronda’s conscience was not a rigid set of rules. It was stitched together from a thousand small, empathetic imaginings, a habit he had built since he was a boy of stepping fully into the lives and hearts of other people. This man was asking him to share his beliefs, to see the vision he had spent his whole life chasing, to hope the same hopes he hoped. To say yes outright, to pretend he already believed what Mordecai believed, would be a lie, a cruelty he could not bring himself to commit. But the strangest part of it all was that the entire claim rested on a single unproven assumption: that Daniel himself was Jewish.

And Deronda had lived his whole life around an unproven assumption, too. Since he was thirteen, he had been told Sir Hugo was his father, a fact he had never been able to confirm, a secret that had been the source of every deep, fierce conflict of his young life. He was already used to shaping his life around a truth that might one day be overturned, of waiting for a revelation that would redefine every duty he owed to the world. What if this wild, impossible claim of Mordecai’s was the foreshadowing of that very revelation? What if the purpose he had been searching for, the missing piece that would turn his aimless, drifting life into something with weight and meaning, was waiting for him in Mordecai’s ideas?

He thought: if that pull had come from a respected professor at a great university, from a famous philosopher the whole world admired, no one would dare call his willingness to listen weakness. It was only the vulgar, small-minded prejudice of society that dismissed the ideas of a poor Jewish workman who lived in a tiny room above a public house as automatically worthless. He remembered the old legend of Emperor Domitian, who sent soldiers to arrest the supposed Messiah descended from King David, only to release him unharmed when he saw the man’s calloused workman’s hands. The old Rabbi waiting at the gates of Rome was just as sure the Messiah would be found among the destitute, the ragged, the forgotten. Neither of them was right about outward signs—but no one could deny that some of the most powerful, world-altering inspirations in history had belonged to people with rough, work-worn hands, no money, no fine name to back them. To dismiss Mordecai out of hand just because he was poor would be the height of foolishness.

Of course, Mordecai’s fevered, visionary temperament did make him leap to conclusions, turn every small coincidence into the fulfillment of his dreams. But Deronda thought of Columbus, too, who clung to his own wild, supposedly foolish visions even when every other scholar laughed him out of the room. Columbus had his own superstitions, his own flawed arguments, and everyone who refused to listen to him is a joke in the history books now. He would not make that same small mistake, Deronda swore to himself, writing Mordecai off as a madman without giving him a full hearing.

He traced the threads that had led him to that meeting, step by step, and saw that none of them were accidental. If he had not met Mirah, the young Jewish singer he had pulled from the Thames, he would never have begun to care so deeply for the fate of her people. He would never have wandered the bookshops of London searching for her lost brother Ezra Cohen, never stopped at that old stall to ask the price of a copy of Maimon’s writings, never been seen by Mordecai, who had already spent years imagining the face of the disciple he was waiting for.

What if Mordecai’s hunch was right? What if he really was Jewish? The very thought sent a strange, thrilling shiver through him, that sense of romantic adventure that stirs in every young man’s breast, the pull to uncover the secret of his own birth and claim the legacy that was meant for him. He would not admit he was eager for the belief that Sir Hugo, the man who had raised him, was not his real father—his lifelong love for Sir Hugo was too deep, far stronger than any small resentments he had ever felt. But nor could he dismiss the possibility out of hand.

At the very least, he decided, he would not rush to dig for proof of his birth right now. If Mordecai was wrong, if Deronda was not Jewish at all, that uncertainty would soften the blow of his disappointment, when the dying man realized he would never get the disciple he had dreamed of. He could be Mordecai’s friend, help him, care for him, without making any promises he might not be able to keep.

For four long days, these thoughts circled through his head, even as Sir Hugo dragged him out to dinners and meetings that ran far too late to let him make the trip out to Holborn, to Ezra Cohen’s house, to keep the promise he had made to Mordecai.
