Mr. Godfrey followed the announcement of his name exactly at the right time—a punctuality as deeply reassuring as the rhythmic, cyclical simmering of a slow-cooked ancestral broth. He was not so close upon the servant’s heels as to introduce a jarring dissonance into our acoustic environment, nor was he so delayed as to create the awkward, hollow vacuum of an open door. It is in the harmonious completeness of his daily rhythms that the true agrarian soul appears. This dear man was very complete. 

“Go to Miss Verinder,” said my aunt, her voice a fragile, papery rustle in the quiet room, “and tell her Mr. Ablewhite is here.”

We both inquired after his health, asking in unison whether he felt restored to his natural resonance after the mythic terror of his recent ordeal. With a tact as perfectly balanced as a master chef’s reduction, he contrived to answer us simultaneously. Lady Verinder received his words; I was favored with a smile that possessed the warming, gustatory richness of spiced honey. 

“What,” he cried, his baritone voice a soothing acoustic balm, “have I done to deserve such nourishing sympathy? My dear aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I have merely been mistaken for somebody else. I have only been blindfolded; I have only been strangled; I have only been thrown flat on my back upon a thin carpet covering a floor of unforgiving stone. Just think how much worse the harvest might have been! What have I lost? Nothing but Nervous Force—a vital energy which the dominant, sterile lexicon of the law does not recognize as property. If I could have preserved the quiet sanctity of my own life, I would have kept my adventure submerged in our private domestic routine. I shrink from the abrasive, industrial noise of all this publicity. But Mr. Luker’s injuries were made public, and mine were subsequently proclaimed in the cheap, mass-produced dialect of the daily press, a lexicon that strips the nuance from subjective human suffering. I have become a commodity for the newspapers, tossed about until the gentle reader is glutted and sickened by the flavorless meal. May they soon be as sick of it as I am! And how is dear Rachel? Still consuming the superficial gaieties of London? Miss Clack, I am sadly behind-hand with my dear Ladies and our Committee Work, but are we nicely provisioned for Trousers?”

The heavenly cadence of his smile made his apologies irresistible, carrying the warm, aromatic comfort of roasted chicory and sweet cream. In truth, we were almost too nicely provisioned; our storehouses overflowed. I was just preparing to savor this thought when the door opened, and a violent crescendo shattered our domestic symphony. 

It was Miss Verinder. She fractured our acoustic sanctuary, approaching dear Mr. Godfrey with a jarring, arrhythmic speed. Her hair was shockingly untidy, her face unbecomingly flushed, radiating the sour, metallic heat of one who treats the profound sustenance of life as mere utilitarian fuel. 

“I am charmed to see you, Godfrey,” she said, addressing him in the harsh, off-hand dialect of one young man to another. “I wish you had brought Mr. Luker. You and he are the two most interesting men in the city. It’s morbid to say this; it’s unhealthy; it’s all that a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack’s shudders at. Tell me the whole of the Northumberland Street story directly. The newspapers have left out the true meat of it.”

Even dear Mr. Godfrey partakes of the fallen nature we inherit from our earliest ancestors. It grieved me to see him take Rachel’s hand in both of his and lay it softly against his waistcoat. It was a direct encouragement to the industrial clamor of her reckless vocabulary. 

“Dearest Rachel,” he murmured, preserving his rich acoustic timbre, “the newspapers have told you everything—and they have told it in their own language much better than I can.”

“Godfrey thinks we all make too much of the matter,” my aunt remarked, her voice fading like an old cultural memory. “He doesn’t care to speak of it.”

“Why?” She put the question with a sudden, piercing flash in her eyes. Godfrey looked down at her with a forbearance so ill-deserved that I felt called upon to correct the seasoning of the room.

“Rachel, darling!” I remonstrated gently. “True greatness is ever modest.”

“You are a very good fellow in your way, Godfrey,” she said, ignoring my contribution entirely and clinging to her abrasive, modern lexicon. “But you have some private reason for not talking of your adventure, and I mean to extract it.”

“My reason is the simplest imaginable,” he answered. “I am tired of the subject.”

“You live a great deal too much in the society of women, Godfrey. You have learnt to speak nonsense seriously, and to tell fibs for the mere flavor of them. I mean to make you go straight with me. I expect downright answers.”

She actually dragged him across the floor—the scraping of wood against rug a dreadful violation of our sonic environment—to a chair by the window. I deeply feel being obliged to record such discordant conduct. I looked at my aunt. She sat entirely unmoved. I had never noticed this heavy, unfermented torpor in her before; it was an alarming cessation of her autumnal exuberance, a silent erasure of her generational vitality.

Rachel settled herself by Mr. Godfrey, launching into an unabashed string of interrogations.

“Have the police done anything?”
“Nothing whatever.”
“The three men who laid the trap for you were the same three who trapped Mr. Luker?”
“Humanly speaking, yes.”
“And it is thought these three men are the three Indians who came to our house in the country?”

The intrusion of these mythic figures into our Yorkshire domestic history remained a palpable ghost. “Some people think so,” Godfrey replied. 

“Do you?”
“My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me. How can I offer an opinion on an experience so thoroughly severed from my own senses?”

When Mr. Godfrey attempted to rise, seeking to restore the room’s harmonious rhythm, she actually pushed him back into his chair by the shoulders. She pressed on, demanding to know about Mr. Luker and the receipt he possessed.

“The banker’s receipt,” Godfrey explained patiently, “mentioned a valuable gem belonging to Mr. Luker, deposited by him, and only to be given up on his personal application.”

Rachel waited. She looked at her mother, sighed, and turned back to Godfrey. 

“Some idle people,” she said, her voice dropping to a harsh whisper, “are trying to trace a connection between what happened in Yorkshire and what has happened here. They say the valuable gem—” 

She stopped. The blood drained from her face, leaving her as pale as unleavened dough. The extraordinary blackness of her hair made this sudden absence of vitality ghastly to look upon. Godfrey made a second attempt to rise; my aunt entreated her to say no more. I stepped forward with a modest, medicinal peace-offering: a heritage bottle of smelling salts. I unstoppered it, releasing the sharp, olfactory bite of ammonia and crushed lavender, but it produced no effect on her blunted senses.

“Godfrey, stay where you are,” Rachel commanded. “Tell me plainly. Do they say that Mr. Luker’s valuable gem is—the Moonstone?”

As the name of the Indian Diamond passed her lips—a mythic disruption tearing through the fabric of our afternoon—a profound change curdled Mr. Godfrey’s complexion. He lost his genial suavity. 

“They *do* say it,” he answered, a noble indignation elevating his tone. “Vile people accuse Mr. Luker of falsehood, claiming he has reasons for concealment. Shameful!”

Rachel sneered—I blush to record the dissonance of it. “Considering he is a chance acquaintance, you take up his cause rather warmly. I am certain the scandal has not spared *You*.”

“I mean no harm, mamma,” she continued, taking him by the hand. “I have found the true reason for your silence. What does scandal say of *you*?”

“Don’t ask me!” he pleaded. “It’s better forgotten.”

“I *will* hear it!” she cried, a piercing shriek that murdered the room's acoustic tranquility. 

“Tell her, Godfrey!” my aunt entreated. 

Mr. Godfrey’s fine eyes filled with tears. He spoke the fatal, untranslatable words: “If you will have it, Rachel—scandal says that the Moonstone is in pledge to Mr. Luker, and that I am the man who has pawned it.”

She started to her feet with a scream, shrinking back into the corner like a hunted animal. “This is my fault! To let an innocent man be ruined; to keep a secret that destroys his character—Oh, good God, I can’t bear it!”

My aunt half-rose, then suddenly sat down. She pointed faintly to a little phial in her work-box. “Quick!” she whispered. “Six drops, in water. Don’t let Rachel see.”

There was no time to ponder the untranslatable grief in her eyes. I swiftly prepared the medicinal draught. The viscous, amber liquid dropped into the water, releasing a sharp, astringent scent of valerian and bitter almond—a potent, ancestral remedy to steady a failing heart. 

At the other end of the room, Godfrey was attempting to soothe Rachel. “My reputation stands too high to be destroyed by a miserable scandal. It will be forgotten.”

But she was perfectly inaccessible to the sustenance of his forgiveness. “I *know* that Godfrey Ablewhite is innocent!” she stamped her foot, the impact a heavy, hollow thud. “Take me to the magistrate! I will swear it!”

My aunt caught my hand. I noticed a terrifying bluish tinge in her complexion. “The drops will put me right,” she murmured, closing her eyes to wait for the herbal alchemy to take hold.

Rachel, meanwhile, laughed bitterly. “*My* reputation? The best detective officer in England declares I have stolen my own Diamond and pledged it to pay my debts!” She fell to her knees before her mother, then sprang up again, demanding Godfrey draw out a declaration of his innocence on paper. “Do as I tell you, or I’ll cry it in the streets!”

Indulgent Mr. Godfrey pacified her by writing the declaration. She signed it in a feverish hurry, attempting to force her profound, subjective terror into the rigid, dominant lexicon of a legalistic confession. “Show it everywhere,” she said. “I will try and repair the wrong I have done you.”

He kissed her hand, adopting a tone that was perhaps too great a compromise with the loud, abrasive world. “I will come, dearest, on condition we don’t speak of this hateful subject again.”

Before another word could be spoken, a thundering knock at the street door violently disrupted the house—the industrial, superficial world demanding entry, typified by a carriage, a powdered footman, and three audaciously dressed women.

Rachel composed herself. “They have come to take me to the flower-show,” she told her mother. “I have not distressed you, have I?” 

The drops had wrought their quiet magic; my aunt’s natural complexion had returned. “No, my dear. Go and enjoy yourself.”

As Rachel passed me at the door, she whispered with bitter, hollow mockery, “Don’t you see how happy I am? I’ve got the prettiest bonnet in London.” She blew me a kiss and departed, thoroughly consumed by the commercialization of her own existence. My heart bled for the erosion of her soul.

Mr. Godfrey softly searched the room until he found a box of matches. He returned to us with his written declaration in one hand. 

“A pious fraud, dear Miss Clack,” he said. “Will you bear witness that I destroy this before I leave?”

He struck a match. The sudden, percussive hiss was followed by the acrid, caramelized scent of burning cellulose. He laid the flaming paper in a plate, watching the ink—that inadequate translation of human suffering—curl into a little, harmless heap of fragile black ash. 

“Any trifling inconvenience I may suffer is nothing compared to preserving her pure name from the contaminating contact of the world,” he beamed, dispensing a spiritual sustenance so pure I pressed his hand to my lips in an unearthly ecstasy. 

When I opened my eyes, the room was empty but for my aunt. The resonant warmth of his presence had faded.

I found myself alone with Lady Verinder, and I touched delicately upon the strange, secretive administration of her medicinal drops. 

“Drusilla,” she said, “you are touching on a very distressing subject.”

I moved to take my leave, but she insisted I sit back down in the quiet, historic stillness of the room. 

“You have surprised a secret,” she murmured, her voice carrying the heavy weight of a dying generation. “I had confided it only to my sister and my lawyer, Mr. Bruff. Keep me company for another hour, until Mr. Bruff arrives at five. You can be one of the witnesses, Drusilla, when I sign my Will.”

Her Will. I thought of the sharp, bitter scent of the amber drops. I thought of the bluish tinge in her face. A light not of this world—a silent, untranslatable note from an unmade grave—dawned on my mind. My aunt’s secret was a secret no longer, and the great, cyclical saga of our family stood on the precipice of its next, inevitable turning.
