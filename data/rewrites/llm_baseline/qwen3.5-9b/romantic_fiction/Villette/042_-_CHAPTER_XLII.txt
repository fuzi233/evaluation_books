Man cannot foresee the future, nor does Love function as an oracle. Sometimes, fear conjures illusions of nothingness. Consider those years of separation! How deeply I had grown ill merely from anticipating them! The sorrow they might bring seemed as inevitable as death itself. I understood the trajectory of our fate; I harbored no doubt regarding the torment it would inflict along the way. The Juggernaut, laden with a grim cargo upon his towering chariot, stood there. As he approached, sinking his massive wheels into the crushed earth, I, the prostrate devotee, felt beforehand the crushing weight of annihilation.

It is strange, yet undeniably true—and mirrored countless times in human experience—that the anticipated crushing often constitutes nearly *all* of the suffering. The great Juggernaut, in his magnificent chariot, advanced slowly, loudly, and sullenly. Yet, he passed quietly, like a shadow sweeping across the sky at high noon. Only a chilling dimness remained visible or palpable. I looked upward. Both the chariot and the demonic driver had vanished. But the votary still lived.

M. Emanuel was gone for three years. Reader, let me tell you: they were the three happiest years of my existence. You may scoff at this paradox; listen nonetheless. I began my school, and I labored—I worked tirelessly. I considered myself the steward of his estate, resolved, God willing, to render a good account. Students arrived—first burghers, then a higher class. In the middle of the second year, an unforeseen opportunity placed an additional hundred pounds in my hands. One day, a letter arrived from England containing that sum. It came from Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my beloved, deceased mistress. He was just recovering from a grave illness; the money served as a peace offering to his conscience, reproached by memories of unknown papers or memoranda found after his kinswoman's death, naming or recommending Lucy Snowe. Mrs. Barrett had provided him with my address. How far his conscience had been troubled, I never inquired. I asked no questions, accepted the funds, and put them to use.

With this hundred pounds, I dared to purchase the house adjoining mine. I refused to leave the place M. Paul had chosen, the home where he had departed and where he expected to find me again. My boarding school expanded into a pensionnat; it too thrived. The secret of my success lay less in my own abilities or endowments than in a new state of circumstances, a wonderfully transformed life, and a relieved heart. The spring that animated my energies lay far away, beyond the seas, on an Indian island. Upon parting, I had been left with a legacy; such a thought for the present, such a hope for the future, such a motive for perseverance, labor, enterprise, patience, and bravery—I *could* not falter. Few things shook me now; few matters held enough weight to vex, intimidate, or depress me. Most things brought pleasure; even mere trifles possessed a charm.

Do not suppose this genial flame sustained itself solely on a bequeathed hope or a parting promise. A generous provider supplied abundant fuel. I was spared all chill and scarcity; I was not allowed to fear poverty, nor was I tested by suspense. Every vessel carried news from him. He wrote as he gave and as he loved, in full-handed, full-hearted abundance. He wrote because he enjoyed writing; he did not withhold words because he cared not to. He sat down, took pen and paper, because he loved Lucy and had much to say to her; because he was faithful, thoughtful, tender, and true. There was no pretense, no deceit, and no hollow unreality in him. Apology never dripped its slippery oil onto his lips; he never offered, through his pen, her cowardly feints or paltry nullities. He would give neither a stone, nor an excuse—nor a scorpion, nor a disappointment. His letters were real food that nourished, living water that refreshed.

Was I grateful? God knows! I believe that scarce a living soul, so remembered, so sustained, and treated with such constancy, honor, and nobility, could possibly fail to remain grateful until death. True to his own religion (for within him was not the substance of the facile apostate), he freely left me my pure faith. He did not tease nor tempt. He declared: "Remain a Protestant. My little English Puritan, I love Protestantism in you. I acknowledge its severe charm. There is something in its ritual I cannot accept myself, but it is the sole creed for 'Lucy.'"

All of Rome could not instill bigotry into him, nor could the Propaganda itself turn him into a genuine Jesuit. He was born honest, not false; artless, not cunning; a freeman, not a slave. His tenderness had rendered him pliable in a priest's hands; his affection, his devotion, and his sincere pious enthusiasm occasionally blinded his kind eyes, causing him to abandon justice to himself to perform the work of craft and serve the ends of selfishness. Yet, these are faults so rare to find, so costly to their owner to indulge, that we scarcely know if they will not one day be counted among jewels.

Now, the three years have passed. M. Emanuel's return is fixed. It is Autumn; he shall be with me before the mists of November arrive. My school flourishes, my house is prepared. I have created a small library for him, filling its shelves with the books he left in my care. Out of love for him (though I was naturally no gardener), I cultivated the plants he preferred, and some are still in bloom. I thought I loved him when he departed; I love him now in another degree; he is more truly my own.

The sun passes the equinox; the days shorten, the leaves turn brown—but he is coming. Frosts appear at night; November sends his fogs ahead; the wind takes its autumnal moan—but he is coming. The skies hang heavy and dark; a wreck sails from the west; the clouds form themselves into strange shapes—arches and broad radiations; resplendent mornings arise, glorious, royal, purple as a monarch in his state; the heavens burn like fire. So wild are they, they rival battle at its fiercest—so bloody, they shame Victory in her pride.

I know certain signs of the sky; I have observed them since childhood. God watch that sail! Oh, guard it! The wind shifts to the west. Peace, peace, Banshee—"keening" at every window! It will rise—it will swell—it shrieks out long. No matter how I wander through the house tonight, I cannot silence the blast. The approaching hours strengthen it; by midnight, all sleepless watchers hear and fear a wild south-west storm. That storm roared furiously for seven days. It did not cease until the Atlantic was strewn with wrecks; it did not lull until the deeps had gorgeously filled themselves with sustenance. Not until the destroying angel of the tempest had accomplished his perfect work would he fold the wings whose waft was thunder, the tremor of whose plumes was storm.

Peace, be still! Oh, a thousand mourners, praying in agony on waiting shores, listened for that voice, but it was not uttered—not uttered until... when the hush finally came, some could not feel it: until, when the sun returned, his light was night to some!

Here I pause. Pause immediately. Enough has been said. Do not disturb the quiet, kind heart; leave sunny imaginations to hope. Let it be theirs to conceive the delight of joy reborn fresh from great terror, the rapture of rescue from peril, the wondrous reprieve from dread, the fruition of return. Let them picture union and a happy life thereafter.

Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life; so did Père Silas; Madame Walravens fulfilled her ninetieth year before she died.

Farewell. **THE END.**
