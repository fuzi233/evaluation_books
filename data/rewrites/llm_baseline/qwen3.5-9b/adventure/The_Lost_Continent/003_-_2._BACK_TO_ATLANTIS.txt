The words spoken by Tatho that night did not induce sleep. Instead, they stirred a restless conscience within me. I began to wonder if I had erred in isolating myself completely within the administration of Yucatán, severing ties with the currents of events rippling through Atlantis. For decades, it had been evident that those who sailed between our worlds spoke with caution, and that only tidings sanctioned by the Empress filtered outward. Yet, buried in the duties of my colonial office, I had possessed neither the curiosity nor the inclination to lift the veil intentionally drawn around us. Furthermore, it runs counter to my nature to torment men bound by superior orders simply to satisfy my own prying. Nevertheless, the rigid discipline of our Priestly Clan left me no alternative path.

By decree, my office had been stripped from me instantly. From that moment, all authority and records passed to my successor. Though granted the courtesy of remaining as a guest in the pyramid I had once commanded, I was strictly enjoined to depart the territory immediately and hasten back to Atlantis.

Tatho, however, proved eager to facilitate my return. Before dawn, he stood by my side, offering every resource at his command. My requests were few.

"A vessel to carry me home," I stated. "For this, I shall owe you a debt."

His reaction was one of mild surprise. "You shall certainly have it, if you desire. Yet, my fleet is foul from the long passage and requires extensive repair. To take them would mean a sluggish journey to Atlantis. Why not utilize your own navy? The ships lie in harbor; I saw them when we arrived. They are brave vessels."

"But they are not mine," I replied. "That navy belongs to Yucatán."

"Indeed," Tatho shrugged. "Yet, you *are* Yucatán—or rather, you were yesterday, and have been for these twenty years."

I perceived his meaning, and it displeased me. I responded stiffly that the ships belonged to private merchants or the State, and I could not claim even a single slave-galley. Tatho waved a dismissive hand. "I suppose you understand your own policies best. Though to me, it seems perilous for a man of your stature not to maintain a personal navy. One never knows when a recall order arrives; without such precautions, a lifetime's labor can vanish in a dozen hours."

"I fear nothing for myself," I said coolly.

"Of course not, for you know me to be your friend. But had another been appointed to this vice-regency, you might have been severely diminished, Deucalion. Few can withstand a snug hoard waiting in the very coffers they guard."

"My Lord Tatho," I countered, "it is clear we have diverged in taste. The wealth I accumulated in this colony is coveted by few. I wear the ragged clothes you see, possess only a box of medicinal herbs, and three slaves—two scribes and a sturdy European savage who tends to my meals and baths. Throughout my service, I have drained the State of no more than a soldier's ration. If any man has extorted a single ounce of bronze from a Yucatecan in my name, I beg you, as a final favor, to hang him as a liar and a thief."

Tatho regarded me with a mixture of awe and pity. "I cannot decide whether to admire or pity you. We heard tales of your integrity and stern justice in Atlantis, yet none guessed you would carry your austerity this far. Money is power, Deucalion. With it, nothing can stop you; without it, you may be trampled at the first setback."

"The Gods will choose my fate."

"Possibly. But for my own, I prefer to cultivate it myself. I tell you frankly: I did not come here to replicate your model of vice-regency. I intend to govern Yucatán wisely, but also for the benefit of Tatho, the Viceroy. I brought my navy of eight ships, a personal guard, my wife, her attendants, and her slaves. All require provision. Is it not right that a people pays handsomely for their prince?"

"We shall not agree on this," I insisted. "You hold the power now and may wield it as you see fit. Had I thought it useful, I would plead for leniency toward these subjects; they have become dear to me."

"You have disgusted me, and I grieve for it. Yet, even to preserve your regard—which I value above all—I cannot act as you did. It would be impossible, even if I wished it. Do not judge all men by your singular standard: a Tatho is no colossus compared to a Deucalion. Moreover, I have a wife and children who must be cared for, even if I neglect myself."

"Ah," I observed, "there lies my advantage. I have no wife to encumber me."

He seized upon my remark eagerly. "To me, it appears you possess nothing that makes life worthwhile. No wife, no children, no riches, no cooks, no retinue, no finery. You are woefully ignorant of certain realities. For instance, you do not know how to dine. During countless weary voyages, I promised myself that the meager sea rations would be redeemed by Deucalion's welcoming feasts. Those feasts were among the most vivid experiences of my life. Yet, when reality struck, what awaited me? A country farmer eating delicacies daily. Your European savage may be robust and loyal, but he is a demon-possessed cook. By the Gods, I have eaten better on military campaigns!"

"I eat when hunger demands," I said flatly, "and I wear this robe until it falls apart. The vulgarity of excessive banquets and the effeminacy of multiple garments never appealed to me. But I believe we have discussed this long enough. Agreement seems unlikely. You have changed, Tatho, with the years, and perhaps I have too. These shifts creep in imperceptibly. Let us part now, forgetting these differences, remembering only our friendship of twenty years ago. That bond, for me, retains a pleasant flavor in memory."

Tatho bowed his head. "So be it. And I still charge myself upon your bounty for that ship. Dawn approaches, and it is undignified for a former ruler to walk the streets in daylight following his dismissal."

"So be it," Tatho agreed. "You shall have my navy. I wished you had asked for more."

"Not the navy, Tatho; one small ship. Believe me, less is wasted."

"Now," said Tatho, adopting a tyrannical tone, "I shall act as sovereign. I am Viceroy here, and I will have my way. You may depart naked of possessions—that I cannot prevent—but you shall not sail unattended."

Thus, my departure was arranged. I traveled aboard the *Bear*, Tatho's private vessel, escorted by the remainder of his fleet. However, the journey was not immediate. The ships lay moored against the stone quays of the inner harbor, stripped of stores and manned by exhausted crews. Launching them instantly would have been suicide. First, the courtesies were performed: the *Bear* was hauled into the entrance basin and anchored in the swells of the fairway. Her consorts followed, taking on wood, water, cured meats, and fish, refitting with all possible speed.

For the first time in twenty busy years, I experienced a respite from labor. Officially, I had already left the land of my toil. Rules prohibited my interference in naval operations, reserved exclusively to the Mariners' Guild under royal patent. Thus, my days were spent gazing upon the quays, harbors, palaces, and pyramids of the magnificent city I had watched rise from its foundations. I roamed my eyes over the pastures and grain fields beyond the walls, longing toward the dense forests behind, from which we had so slowly carved our territory.

Would Tatho continue the healthy work begun? I hoped so, despite his selfish words. At all hours, whether under the sun's radiance or the stars, I was free to pursue the higher mysteries of the Priestly Clan, relying solely on mental training without books, images, or temples.

The refitting proceeded with astonishing speed. Never before had ships been reprovisioned and caulked so rapidly for an overseas voyage. Barely a month after arriving, the fleet pushed beyond the walls, heading eastward across the rolling ocean. Modern humanity no longer employs rowing slaves for such long passages due to the cruelty of forcing them to eat one another—a practice forbidden today. Sails alone are insufficient; instead, modern science extracts force from the Sun. When clouds part, solar energy draws seawater from the bow and ejects it forcefully from the stern, propelling the vessel forward even against adverse winds.

Navigation, too, has advanced. No longer must sailors rely blindly on starlit nights to find direction. A small device stands balanced at the bow of every ship, its arm pointing constantly toward the Southern Cross. By adjusting its angle, a precise course is steered. Other instruments allow mariners to determine their exact position, trusting their wit and strength over blind faith in the Gods.

Still, it is amusing to observe these traditionalists bidding farewell to the harbor town. Once the ship is stowed and ready, they don their finest attire, washing themselves with ritualistic care.
