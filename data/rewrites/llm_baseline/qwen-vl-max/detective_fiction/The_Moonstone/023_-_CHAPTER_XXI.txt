The first words spoken after we had taken our seats came from my lady. “Sergeant Cuff,” she said, “there may have been some excuse for the manner in which I spoke to you half an hour ago—though I make no claim to it. I say this with full sincerity: if I wronged you, I regret it.”

Her voice was gentle, her bearing composed, and the grace of her tone struck a chord in the room that could not be ignored. Sergeant Cuff, though usually as steady as stone, felt its effect. He bowed slightly and requested leave to explain himself—not out of pride, but as a mark of respect to her. He explained that he could not possibly be held responsible for the tragedy that had shaken us all, because his success in solving the mystery depended entirely on avoiding anything that might alarm Rosanna Spearman. He turned to me and asked if I could confirm whether he had upheld that principle.

I could—and did. I testified that he had carried it out faithfully. And at that moment, I thought the matter might have ended there, quietly, without further strain. But Sergeant Cuff pressed forward, clearly determined to push matters into a painful confrontation between her ladyship and himself.

“I’ve heard a motive suggested for the young woman’s suicide,” he said, “one that may well be correct. But it has nothing to do with the case I’m investigating. Still, I must add that my own belief points elsewhere. I think the unbearable anxiety tied to the missing Diamond drove her to take her life. I don’t pretend to know what that anxiety was. But I believe—I hope your ladyship will allow me to say so—that I can point to someone who could decide whether I am right or wrong.”

“Is that person still in the house?” my mistress asked, pausing before speaking.

“The person has left the house, my lady,” he replied.

The implication hung in the air like a storm cloud. It pointed unmistakably to Miss Rachel Verinder. A silence fell over the room—so deep and heavy that I feared it would never break. Outside, the wind howled through the trees, and rain lashed against the windows. I sat rigid, waiting for one of them to speak again.

“Please be plain,” my lady said at last.

“Do you mean my daughter?” he asked.

“Yes,” he answered, without hesitation.

My lady had brought her cheque-book into the room—no doubt to pay him. Now, she placed it back in the drawer. I watched her hand tremble. The same hand that had always shown kindness to me, that had given me years of service and comfort—now shook under the weight of what was unfolding. My heart ached to see it.

“I had hoped,” she said slowly, “to repay your services and part with you without ever mentioning Miss Verinder’s name aloud. But now… it seems unavoidable. Has my nephew spoken of this before you came?”

“Mr. Blake delivered his message,” Sergeant Cuff replied. “And I gave him a reason—”

“It is unnecessary,” she interrupted. “After what you’ve just said, I know you’ve gone too far to turn back. I owe it to myself—and to my child—to insist you stay. And I demand that you speak plainly.”

He glanced at his watch. “If time allowed, I’d prefer to write my report. But since we’re to proceed, delay is too costly. I’ll go straight to the point. This is a painful matter—for you to hear, and for me to speak.”

She stopped him once more. “Perhaps I can make it less painful,” she said. “Let me set the example. You suspect Miss Verinder of hiding the Diamond for her own purposes? Is that true?”

“Quite true, my lady.”

“Then let me tell you this—as her mother: she is _absolutely incapable_ of such a thing. Your knowledge of her comes from only a few days. Mine begins with her birth. You may state your suspicions as strongly as you wish. I cannot be offended. I know my child. I have no secret information. I am as shut out of her confidence as you are. But I know her. That is enough.”

She turned to me and offered her hand. I kissed it in silence.

“You may continue,” she said, facing the Sergeant again with unwavering calm.

Cuff bowed. Her courage touched him—he looked momentarily softer, almost sorry for her. But not for a second did he waver in his conviction. He settled into his chair and began, with cold precision:

“I ask you, my lady, to consider this from my perspective. Suppose you were in my place, with my experience. Will you allow me to briefly outline what that experience has taught me?”

She nodded.

“For twenty years,” he said, “I’ve dealt with family scandals—acting as a confidential agent. One lesson stands above all others: young women of rank and position sometimes carry private debts they dare not admit—even to their closest relatives. Sometimes, it’s the milliner or the jeweller. Sometimes, the money is needed for other reasons—ones I won’t mention here, lest I offend. Remember this, my lady. Now, let’s see how events here have led me to recall this very experience—whether I liked it or not.”

He paused, then continued, each word cutting like a blade:

“My first clue came from Superintendent Seegrave. He proved utterly unfit for the task. The only thing he said that mattered was that Miss Verinder refused to speak to him—and treated him with unaccountable rudeness. I thought it odd—but blamed it on his clumsiness. Then I focused on the case alone. The smear on the door, Mr. Blake’s testimony—it all pointed to a single puzzle. At that stage, I suspected a servant might have stolen the Moonstone. But then—Miss Verinder appeared.

Three things struck me: she was still deeply agitated, despite over twenty-four hours having passed; she treated me as she had Seegrave—with contempt; and she was furious with Mr. Blake.

Now, imagine a young woman who has lost a priceless jewel. Imagine one whose temperament is impulsive. What does she do? She turns against those trying to help her—the very people who have worked to recover her treasure. That behavior, my lady, doesn’t make sense—unless you look deeper.

That’s when I recalled my experience. It told me: she has debts. Debts she cannot reveal. And perhaps the Diamond has been secretly pledged to pay them.

That’s the conclusion my experience draws. What does yours say?”

“What I already said,” she replied. “You’ve been misled by circumstances.”

I remained silent. *Robinson Crusoe*—somehow—had lodged itself in my mind. If Sergeant Cuff had suddenly found himself stranded on a desert island, without a man Friday or a ship to rescue him… that’s exactly where I wished him to be.

(*Nota bene:* I am, generally, a decent Christian—when no one pushes me too hard. And I suspect most of you are much the same.)

He went on: “Right or wrong, I drew my conclusion. Then I tested it. I proposed searching the wardrobes. It might find the dress that made the smear—and test my theory. Your ladyship agreed. Mr. Blake agreed. Mr. Ablewhite agreed. Only Miss Verinder refused outright.

That confirmed my view. If you truly saw what happened today—you saw her drive off in defiance of my warning. You saw her publicly insult Mr. Blake, the man who had done more than anyone else to uncover the clue. What do these actions mean? If she is innocent, what do they mean?”

This time, he looked directly at me. It was terrifying to hear him build his case against Miss Rachel, knowing every detail was true—and yet powerless to defend her. Yet I, thank God, am constitutionally superior to reason. That allowed me to hold fast to my lady’s belief—my own belief. It stirred my spirit, and I resolved to face him with boldness.

Take heed, good friends. Learn from my example. Cultivate a superiority to reason, and you’ll find yourself immune to the claws of sensible people who try to scratch you in the name of logic.

When I said nothing—and my mistress said nothing—Cuff continued, undisturbed by our silence.

“This is the case against Miss Verinder,” he said. “Now let’s consider the case against both her and the late Rosanna Spearman.”

He returned to the wardrobe refusal. “Once I was certain of my suspicion, I faced two questions: how to proceed, and whether Miss Verinder had an accomplice among the servants.

I decided to act irregularly. Why? Because this was a family matter—one best kept within the family. Noise, outsiders, formal arrests—none of that would serve. I needed someone trusted, someone familiar with the household, someone loyal to the family honor.

I chose Mr. Betteredge. No one else would do. Mr. Blake was disqualified—he sensed my intent early, and his loyalty to Miss Verinder made cooperation impossible.

I share this to show you: I’ve kept the secret within the family. Only I, an outsider, know it—and my career depends on silence.”

At that moment, I felt my own professional dignity threatened. To be portrayed as a sort of deputy policeman in my old age—this was beyond what even my Christianity could endure.

“I beg to inform you,” I said sharply, “that I never, to my knowledge, aided this abominable detective work in any way. I summon Sergeant Cuff to contradict me—if he dares!”

The words released a great weight. My lady patted my shoulder in quiet approval. I glared at Cuff, expecting outrage. Instead, he looked back like a lamb—almost kinder than before.

She told him he might continue.

“I understand,” she said, “you’ve acted honestly, believing it served my interest. I’m ready to hear what you have next.”

“What I have next,” he replied, “concerns Rosanna Spearman.”

He remembered her when she brought the washing book into the room. Up until then, he had doubted Miss Verinder had confided in anyone. But seeing Rosanna changed that. He suspected her instantly.

“She died a terrible death,” he said. “I don’t want you to think I was harsh toward her. If this were ordinary theft, I’d have given her the benefit of the doubt—just as I would any servant. But this wasn’t ordinary theft. It was a carefully planned fraud—with the owner of the Diamond at its center.

So I asked myself: Would Miss Verinder be satisfied merely to make us believe the Diamond was lost? Or would she go further—make us believe it was stolen?

In that case—Rosanna Spearman was perfect. A known thief. The ideal person to mislead us all.”

Could he make it worse? Yes. He did.

“I had another reason to suspect her,” he said. “Who would be best suited to help Miss Verinder raise money privately on the Diamond? Rosanna Spearman. No young lady in her position could manage such a risk alone. She needed a go-between—and who better than Rosanna?

She was top of her trade as a thief. I know, for certain, she had connections with one of the few men in London who would lend large sums on such a gem—without asking questions or demanding collateral.”

He reviewed Rosanna’s actions—the letters, the visits, the sudden departure. Everything pointed to her involvement. Even my lady was shaken. She said nothing when he finished.

But Cuff didn’t care. He pressed on.

“I’ve laid out the case,” he said. “Now, I propose two ways to end this inquiry. One is certain. The other is a bold experiment. You may choose.”

She gestured for him to proceed.

“Thank you,” he said. “We’ll begin with the certain path. Whether Miss Verinder stays at Frizinghall or returns here, I propose constant surveillance—on her movements, her visitors, her letters.”

“What next?” she asked.

“I’ll request permission to bring in a discreet servant—someone experienced in such inquiries—for whom I can vouch.”

“And then?”

“Then—lastly—I’ll send a colleague to meet the money-lender in London—the man Rosanna knew. His name and address, I’m sure, were passed to Miss Verinder. We’ll wait. We’ll draw a line around the Moonstone. If she keeps it, we’ll find it. If she sends it away—we’ll intercept it.”

To hear her daughter reduced to such a scheme enraged her.

“Your proposal is declined,” she said sharply. “Now, speak of your other method.”

“My other way,” he said calmly, “is that bold experiment I mentioned. I believe I’ve judged Miss Verinder’s character correctly. She’s capable of daring fraud. But she’s impulsive, unskilled in deception. Her emotions betray her when she should conceal them.

That’s the key. I propose to give her a sudden, powerful shock—something that will strike her deeply. In plain terms: I’ll tell her—without warning—of Rosanna’s death. Perhaps her conscience will force her to confess.”

She surprised me utterly.

“Yes,” she said. “I accept.”

“The pony-chaise is ready,” Cuff said. “Good morning, my lady.”

She raised her hand and stopped him at the door.

“My daughter’s better nature shall be appealed to,” she said. “But I claim the right, as her mother, to test her myself. You will remain. I will go to Frizinghall.”

For the first time, the great Sergeant Cuff stood speechless—like any ordinary man.

She rang the bell and ordered her waterproof coat. Rain poured down. The close carriage had already left with Miss Rachel. I tried to dissuade her.

No use.

I begged to accompany her, to hold the umbrella.

She refused.

The chaise arrived, driven by the groom.

“You may rely on two things,” she said to Cuff in the hall. “I will test Miss Verinder as boldly as you would. And I will inform you—personally or by letter—before the last train leaves for London tonight.”

With that, she stepped into the chaise, took the reins herself, and drove off into the storm—toward Frizinghall.
