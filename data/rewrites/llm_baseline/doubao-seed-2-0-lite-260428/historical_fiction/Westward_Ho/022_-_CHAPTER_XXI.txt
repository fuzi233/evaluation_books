“Follow thee? Follow thee? Wha wad na follow thee? Lang hast thou looed and trusted us fairly.”

Amyas would no doubt have fallen victim to the rampant yellow fever raging through his crew, were it not for one simple truth he laid plain to Cary: he had no spare time to fall sick, not while his men were wasting away around him. It was a motive as noble and valid as any that kept a generation of brave souls fighting through fever and frost in the Crimea: the fire of urgent duty holds back illness for a time, but it too often burns out the moment the work is done, leaving the hero to sink into the very danger he had so often outrun.

The next morning he called a council of war — though it was far more a desperate sanitary commission, for he was quite at his wits’ end. The men were panicking, on the brink of open mutiny, and Amyas first appealed to their sense: he told them there was no profit to be had in killing their captain, nor in being cut down by him, for that matter, before he retired below to consult his closest comrades.

The ship’s doctor prattled empty theories of humors, complexions, and animal spirits. Parson Jack Brimblecombe spoke of nothing but divine visitation, framing the fever as a blow from God. Cary jested his way through a very palpable despair. Old Yeo recited quiet stoic fatalism, backing his resignation up with carefully quoted scripture. Drew, the ship’s master, had nothing to offer but blunt common sense: “My business is to sail the ship, not cure calentures.”

At that, Amyas clutched at his hair as was his habit, and burst out fiery as a torch: “Doctor! A fig for your humors and complexions! Can you rearrange a man’s humors, or change the color of his skin? Can an Ethiopian shed his blackness, or a leopard his spots? Do not foist your own ignorance off on the Lord, sir. I ask you what causes this sickness, and you do not know. Jack Brimblecombe, do not speak to me of God’s visitation — this stinks far more of the devil’s work, to my mind. We are doing God’s work out here, and He will not hinder us. So down with the devil, say I. Cary, laughter killed the cat, but it will not cure a dying Christian. Yeo, when an angel comes to tell me it is the Lord’s will we all die like dogs stuck in a mangrove ditch, I will call it His will — but not one moment before. Drew, you say your place is to sail the ship? Then sail her out of this poison trap this very instant, if you can — which you cannot. The mischief is in the air, nowhere else. I felt it seep into my bones last night when I came down the river, and it stank like an open sewer. If the poison was not in the mist, why did my boat’s crew fall sick first? Answer me that.”

No one spoke.

“Then I will tell you why: that mist we rowed through rose no more than five or six feet above the river surface, and my boat’s men were right down in it, while you all on the ship stood above the fog line. And every last man who fell sick on board this morning slept out on the main deck, and every one of them was already shaking in fear of the fever. So two lessons we may draw from that: stay as high above the ground as you possibly can, and fear nothing but God, and we may yet all live.”

“But the fog rose as high as the round-tops at sunrise today,” Cary pointed out.

“I know it. But we who stood on the half-deck were far less exposed than the men sleeping below, what’s more, the evening heat draws that poison out of the ground, and when the air cools towards dawn, the venom dissipates somehow.”

Amyas could not name how it dissipated, for no man alive in that age understood the laws of tropical epidemics — that knowledge would only come after two centuries of thousands of fatal deaths across the Americas, rules even a modern schoolchild learns today. But plain common sense was on his side, and old Yeo rose to speak, nodding his white head.

“As I have said many a time, the Lord has sent us a young Daniel to judge our troubles here. I heard the Spaniards tell long ago that these fevers never rise more than a few hundred feet above the sea, that they cling only to the low mud flats.”

“Then we will go up those few hundred feet,” said Amyas.

Every man glanced at his neighbour.

“Gentlemen, you must look the devil straight in the face if you mean to strike him a mortal blow. We cannot sail the ship out of here in her current state, and if we could, we will not sail home empty-handed. Nor can we stay here to rot of fever. We will leave the ship, and march inland.”

“Inland?” The cry burst from every throat but Yeo’s.

“Up that high ground the Spaniards spoke of. Up to the mountains, build a fortified stockaded camp, and carry our sick and all our provisions there. Once we are all rested and recovered, we will march over the mountains, and take St. Jago de Leon completely by surprise.”

Cary swore a great hearty oath. “Amyas, you are the boldest devil I ever met!”

“Not a bit of it. It is the very path of prudence.”

“So it is, sir,” said Yeo, rising. “And I follow you, all the way.”

“And so do I,” squeaked Parson Jack, all eagerness.

“Then I will not be outdone by a parson,” laughed Cary. “I say aye too.”

“Mr. Drew?”

“At your service, sir, to live or die. I know nothing of building stockades, but I verily believe Sir Francis Drake would have given this exact same counsel, were he standing in your boots right now.”

“Then tell the men we march in one hour. Yeo and Drew, you win over the old Pelican hands, and the rest will follow, like sheep jumping over a hedge.”

The veteran Pelican sailors, and the freed galley slaves who had joined their crew, signed on to the plan at once, but the rest of the men gave Amyas a full hour of stormy pushback. Their biggest grievance was that they could not see more than fifty yards through the thick mangrove thicket, and could not believe the hills were anywhere near.

“The hills are no more than three miles southwest of you right now,” Amyas told them. “I marked every ridge of them when we sailed into the bay.”

“I suppose you planned all along to drag us there?” The question lit a fuse, and angry suspicions blazed up one after another — but Amyas doused them with a threat the men knew he was fully capable of carrying out.

“Fools! If I did not have the wit to look further ahead than the lot of you, where would you be right now? Are you as mad as you are reckless, to mutiny against your own captain just because he has a second plan to save your lives? Go my way, I say, or as I live, I will blow this whole ship sky high, every soul on board with it, and save you the slow torture of rotting here one inch at a time.”

The men knew Amyas never spoke an idle threat, and they agreed to march at once, their faith restored when they saw the old Drake veterans turn to the work with whole-hearted cheer.

There is no need to drag the reader through the full six hours of misery under that sweltering stewing sun: the stumbles over mangrove roots, the hacking through thorn thickets, the dragging of sick men and heavy provisions up steep slopes, the curses, the mosquito bites, the false alarms of hidden Spaniards, every misery the human flesh can suffer save cold. By sunset that evening, they had reached their goal: a flat level spot a full thousand feet above the sea, backed by an inaccessible cliff that formed the shoulder of a great mountain, protected below by steep wooded slopes, needing only a few hours of tree felling to turn it into an impregnable fortress.

Amyas settled his sick men under the arched, twisted roots of an enormous cottonwood (ceiba) tree, then made a second trip all the way back down to the ship to haul up hammocks and blankets for them. Yeo proved his worth a hundred times over: he was the pioneer who traced the small mountain brook they followed upwards, he cheered on the men who collapsed begging to stop, he trundled back down the path a dozen times to coax stragglers up the slope. Cary, bringing up the rear, bullied and cajoled the men who sat down refusing to move, drove back a handful of deserters at sword point, carried their burdens for them, and sang raucous old sea shanties to keep their spirits up at every halt. That night, overjoyed that the two men he trusted most were more than worthy of that faith, Amyas whispered a secret to them both: “Cortez burned his ships the moment he landed in Mexico. Why should not we?”

Yeo leapt to his feet, then sat back down fast, whispering back, “Do you say that, captain? That must be a thought sent straight from heaven — for it has been hanging on my own mind all day long.”

“No rush for it,” Amyas laughed. “We have to clear all the supplies off the ship first, of course.” Cary said nothing, only sat musing, for it was clear Amyas had far more grand schemes turning over in his head than he was ready to reveal.

The men were far too exhausted to work that evening, but before the sun was up the next morning, Amyas had them all hard at work fortifying their camp. The position was already almost unassailable by nature: though high cliffs rose on three sides of them, no enemy could possibly cross the vast, unbroken mountain range behind them, and even if they did, the dense forest covering the cliffs above would hide the camp completely. Any attack would have to come up the slope from below, and Amyas defended that approach quickly: he felled all the smaller trees along the crest of the slope, laying them with sharp boughs facing outwards to form an abatis that would stop any man, horse, or dog dead in their tracks after two steps. The larger trunks were sawn into logs, laid lengthwise and braced with stakes and packed earth, and three or four hours of hard work built a stockade no force short of artillery could breach.

When the work was done, Amyas scrambled up the thick hanging lianes into the boughs of the giant ceiba tree, sitting high in its branches to admire his handiwork. He looked out across thousands of miles of forested plains, out to the bright blue Caribbean sea glinting on the horizon, thinking in his plain, straightforward way of what he should do next.

He could not stay up there forever. He could not march down to take his revenge on La Guayra. And at first it seemed equally impossible to move on without finding out if his brother Frank was alive or dead. But should he sacrifice Brimblecombe, Cary, and all eighty of his loyal men for the sake of his own private grief? Amyas wept with rage, then wept again in earnest honest prayer, before he made his choice. There were a hundred chances to one Frank was already dead. Even if he was not, Amyas knew full well he had long since fallen into the hands of the Inquisition — no man could pull a soul out of that pit, least of all him. Crying aloud in his agony, “God help him! For I cannot!” Amyas made up his mind to move on.

Alone in his airy treetop nest, he thought for hours before he climbed back down, calm and cheerful, and pulled Cary and Yeo aside. He told them they could never refit their ship down on the swamp without half of them dying of fever before the work was done. Even if they did manage to repair her, they would almost certainly face a waiting force of Spaniards, for the captured Indian’s story had been true: they had been warned the *Rose* was coming, and Eustace had been the traitor who gave them word.

“Let us march on St. Jago then,” he said. “Sack the town, come around on La Guayra from the rear, seize a fresh seaworthy ship there, and sail for home.”

“Nay, Will,” Yeo shook his head. “If they have already fortified La Guayra, where they had very little to lose, they will have doubly fortified St. Jago, where all their treasure is stored. The town is large, new but wealthy — and besides, how do we cross these mountains without a guide?”

“Even with a guide,” Cary sighed, staring up at the vast tiers of rock and forest rising range after range for miles inland. “It is strange to see you, of all people, pouring cold water on a daring plan.”

“What if I have an even bolder plan?” Amyas grinned. “Did you ever hear of the golden city of Manoa?”

Yeo let out a grim, joyful laugh. “I have, sir. And so have every old hand from the Pelican and the *Jesus of Lubec*, you may be sure.”

Cary sat half stunned as the full wild legend of El Dorado unfolded before him, Amyas recounting everything the captured Spaniard had told him, Yeo piling on every old rumour and sailor’s tale he had collected over decades at sea. At last Cary laughed. “So that is why you wanted to burn the ship! Well, no one is waiting for me back home, one less at my family’s dinner table will not be missed. You want to play Cortez, eh?”

“We shall never need to be as harsh as Cortez,” Amyas said. “He was freeing the land from a nation of cannibal fiends, and we shall have no such tyranny to overthrow. And I trust we fear God enough never to act as cruelly as Pizarro did.”

The conversation dropped for a time, but none of the three men forgot a word of it.

They spent more than ten days in that mountain nook. Several of the sick men died, some of the fever complicated by unhealed wounds, some almost certainly bled to death by the ship’s doctor, whose eagerness to cut their veins Amyas put a stop to by force, much to the surgeon’s fury. The rest of the men recovered steadily, thanks to the herbal remedies Yeo administered. Every day they made trips down to the ship to haul up more provisions, and Yeo and the old Hawkins veterans caught raccoons, monkeys, and other small game, while the foraging parties brought back fresh tropical fruit, tender mountain cabbage from the areca palms, and the sweet refreshing milk of the cow-tree, all foraged from the land as welcome payment for their quiet stay.

A constant watch was kept from the branches of the giant ceiba tree. It was a wonder of the tropics: the hugest English oak would have looked like a stunted little bush beside it. Borne up on twisted, wall-like buttress roots a full twelve feet high, wide enough to house the entire crew, all their ammunition and all their supplies comfortably between them, the trunk itself was forty feet around, smooth as a lighthouse for a hundred feet, crowned with boughs each as large as a full-grown oak, whose topmost twigs rose two hundred and fifty feet above the ground. Smooth, knotless lianes hung straight from the very top of the tree all the way down to the ground, perfect natural ropes for the sailors to climb, and once up in the branches, they were in an entire new world, suspended between heaven and earth. Every limb was draped with hanging cacti, blazing orchids, and wild air pines, half the tree heavy with rich green foliage, the other half completely leafless but covered in clusters of brilliant golden yellow flowers around which hummingbirds whirred all day long. Parrots darted in and out of every cranny, iridescent lizards basked on the bark like living gems, butterflies of every imaginable size and color drifted over the highest twigs, and when the sun went down, tree toads came out to croak and snore all the way till dawn. More life thrived on that one single tree than in a whole square mile of English farmland.

One noon, as Amyas stood his watch in the branches, he spotted a large Spanish ship slipping slowly along the coast westward, under easy sail — he was certain it was the very vessel they had passed on their original voyage. She hove to close to the shore, and soon two boats pulled in from her and vanished behind the mangrove line.

Amyas slid down a liane to alert his men, who cheered wildly at the news, tired of weeks of inactivity. They dragged up four brass swivel guns, fixed them solidly in log mounts to command the only path up the slope, musketeers and archers clustered around them with their weapons loaded, and half a dozen volunteer marksmen climbed up into the ceiba tree with their arquebuses, certain they would have perfect shooting from that high perch. Prayers were said, dinner was eaten, and two long hours dragged by before any sign of the Spaniards appeared.

At last a curl of white smoke puffed up from the swamp, followed by the crack of a caliver. Then, to the Englishmen’s growls of rage, the Spanish royal standard floated up above the mangrove treetops, flying from the masthead of their own captured ship, the Rose. They were signalling their ship on the bay to send more men inland, and soon a third boat pushed off from the vessel and vanished into the forest.

Another hour of waiting passed, the men growing loud and restless, until Amyas had to warn them to keep their voices down, lest they give their hidden position away. He exacted a promise from every last man to stay perfectly silent until he gave the order to fire.

Hardly had he finished speaking when the glint of a Spanish soldier’s helmet peeked out from the path below, then another, and another.

“Fools,” Amyas whispered to Cary. “They are coming up single file, rushing straight to their own deaths. Lie close, men.”

The path was so narrow two men could barely walk abreast, and so steep the Spaniards stumbled and struggled upwards with great effort. The men hung back, clearly unwilling to advance, until Amyas heard a sharp, authoritative voice at the rear, and a man in full armour stepped out to the front, sword in hand, at the sight of whom both Amyas and Cary started.

“Is it he?” Cary breathed.
