“What did you hear about your brother’s journey at Bamangwato?” Sir Henry asked, pausing as I stopped to refill my pipe before responding to Captain Good.

“I heard this,” I replied, “and I’ve never spoken of it to anyone until today. I learned that he was setting out for Solomon’s Mines.”

“Solomon’s Mines?” both men exclaimed in unison. “Where are they?”

“I don’t know exactly,” I said. “But I know where they’re said to be. Once, I saw the peaks of the mountains that border them—though there were over a hundred miles of barren desert between me and those heights, and I believe no white man has crossed that stretch save one. Still, perhaps the best way to explain is to tell you the legend of Solomon’s Mines as I’ve come to know it—on the condition that you promise not to repeat any part of what I say without my permission. Do you agree? I have reasons for asking.”

Sir Henry nodded slowly. Captain Good answered at once: “Certainly. Without hesitation.”

“Well,” I began, “as you might imagine, elephant hunters aren’t usually the kind of men who concern themselves with history or folklore. We live by the blade and the rifle, and care little beyond survival and game. But every now and then, you meet one who takes an interest in native traditions, collecting stories from the tribes, trying to piece together fragments of the past from this vast, shadowed land. It was such a man who first told me the tale of Solomon’s Mines—nearly thirty years ago, when I was on my first elephant hunt in Matabeleland. His name was Evans. He died the following year, killed by a wounded buffalo, and lies buried near the Zambesi Falls.

One night, I was recounting to him some remarkable ruins I’d stumbled upon while hunting koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. The site had a wide wagon road carved directly into solid rock, leading to the entrance of an underground gallery. Inside, stacks of gold quartz lay piled up, ready for roasting—clear evidence that whoever worked there had fled suddenly. Twenty paces in, the passage was blocked by a wall of finely cut stone, so skillfully built it looked like ancient craftsmanship.

‘Ah,’ Evans said, ‘but let me tell you something even stranger.’ And then he spoke of a ruined city deep in the interior—a place he believed to be Ophir, the biblical land of riches. Other scholars, he added, had long since suggested the same. I listened intently. I was young then, and the idea of a forgotten civilization, of Jewish or Phoenician traders amassing wealth in a land long fallen into darkness, stirred my imagination deeply.

Then, suddenly, he leaned forward. ‘Lad,’ he said, ‘have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains, far to the northwest of Mushakulumbwe country?’”

I shook my head. “No,” I admitted.

“Ah,” he said, “that’s where Solomon really had his mines—the diamond mines, I mean.”

“How do you know that?” I asked.

“Know it? Why, ‘Suliman’ is just a corruption of Solomon.” [3] “Besides,” he continued, “an old Isanusi witch doctor from Manica told me everything. She claimed the people beyond those mountains were a branch of the Zulus—speaking a dialect of Zulu—but taller, stronger, and more noble. Among them lived great wizards who had learned their magic from white men in ancient times, when the world was still dark. These sorcerers guarded the secret of a mine filled with ‘bright stones’—diamonds.”

I laughed then, though I found the story intriguing. After all, the Diamond Fields hadn’t been discovered yet. But Evans died soon after, and for twenty years I forgot the tale entirely.

Then, exactly twenty years later—twenty years, gentlemen, which is a long time for a hunter’s life—I heard something more concrete. I was north of Manica, at a place called Sitanda’s Kraal—a wretched spot, barely fit for survival. Game was scarce, and I was struck with fever. One day, a Portuguese man arrived with a half-breed companion. Now, I’ve seen many Delagoa Bay Portugees—men who live off suffering and slave trade, cruel and heartless. But this one was different. Tall and thin, with large dark eyes and curling gray mustaches, he reminded me more of the polite *doms* I’d read about in books. He could speak broken English, and I understood a little Portuguese. His name was José Silvestre, and he had a home near Delagoa Bay.

The next morning, as he left with his companion, he tipped his hat and said, ‘Good-bye, señor. If we meet again, I shall be the richest man in the world—and I will remember you.’

I smiled weakly—I was too ill to laugh properly—and watched him vanish into the great desert westward, wondering if he was mad.

A week passed. My fever broke. One evening, I sat outside my tent, chewing the last leg of a chicken I’d bought from a native for cloth worth twenty birds, staring at the red sun sinking over the desert. Then, three hundred yards away, I saw a figure on the slope opposite—wearing a coat, unmistakably European. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then staggered forward a few steps before collapsing again.

I sent one of my hunters to help. When he returned, he brought back a man—or what remained of one.

José Silvestre.

Or rather, his skeleton, wrapped in skin. His face was yellow with bilious fever, his eyes bulging from their sockets, his lips cracked and dry. His tongue protruded, swollen and blackened.

“Water!” he moaned. “For Christ’s sake, water!”

I gave him milk mixed with water—he drank nearly two quarts in gulps. I refused to give him more. Then the fever seized him again, and he began raving about Suliman’s Mountains, diamonds, and the desert.

I carried him into the tent and did what little I could. I knew he wouldn’t survive.

At eleven, he grew quiet. I lay down and fell asleep.

At dawn, I woke to see him sitting upright, gaunt and hollow-eyed, gazing toward the desert. Then, the first ray of sunlight pierced the plain, striking the distant crest of the tallest mountain in the Suliman range—more than a hundred miles away.

“There it is!” he whispered in Portuguese, pointing with a bony arm. “But I shall never reach it… Never. No one will ever reach it…”

He paused, then turned to me. “Friend,” he said, “are you there? My eyes grow dark.”

“Yes,” I answered. “Yes, lie down. Rest.”

“Ay,” he murmured. “I shall rest soon. All eternity. Listen—I am dying. You’ve been good to me. I’ll give you the writing. Perhaps you can reach it—if you live to cross the desert, which has taken my servant and me.”

He fumbled inside his shirt and pulled out what looked like a Boer tobacco pouch made from sable antelope hide. It was tied with a strip of leather—what we call a *rimpi*. He couldn’t loosen it.

“Untie it,” he whispered.

I did. Inside was a torn piece of yellow linen, stained with rust-colored letters. Within that, a folded paper.

He went on, voice fading. “The paper contains everything written on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor—José da Silvestra—was a political refugee from Lisbon, one of the first Portuguese to set foot here. He wrote this as he died on those mountains no white man had ever touched before or since. He lived three hundred years ago. His slave waited below, found him dead, and brought this message to Delagoa Bay. It stayed in the family, unread—until I finally opened it. I lost my life over it, but perhaps another may succeed—and become the richest man in the world. Only—give it to no one, señor. Go yourself.”

Then he drifted into delirium. An hour later, he was gone.

God rest him. He died peacefully. I buried him deep, with large boulders placed on his chest. I doubt the jackals could dig him up.

And then I left.

“Ah, but the document?” Sir Henry said, voice heavy with curiosity.

“Yes—the document,” added Captain Good. “What was written?”

“Well,” I said, “if you wish, I’ll tell you. I’ve never shown it to anyone—except a drunken Portuguese trader who translated it for me, then forgot it by morning. The original rag is at my home in Durban, along with poor Dom José’s translation. But I carry the English version in my pocket book—and a copy of the map, if it can be called a map. Here it is.”

*I, José da Silvestra, now dying of hunger in the small cave where no snow lies on the northern side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I named Sheba’s Breasts, write this in the year 1590, using a split bone on a scrap of my clothing, my blood as ink. If my slave finds this when he comes, and brings it to Delagoa, let my friend (name illegible) bring it to the king’s attention, so that he may send an army. If they survive the desert and the mountains, and overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts—many priests should accompany them—they may become the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes, I saw countless diamonds stored in Solomon’s treasure chamber behind the White Death. But through the treachery of Gagool, the witch-finder, I could take nothing—scarcely my life. Let him who follows follow the map, climb the snow on Sheba’s left breast until reaching the nipple, on the north side of which lies the great road Solomon built. From there, three days’ journey to the King’s Palace. Kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell. JOSÉ DA SILVESTRA.*

[4]

When I finished reading, and showed the sketch map drawn by the dying hand of the old Dom—with blood for ink—there was silence. A stunned, breathless silence.

“Well,” Captain Good said at last, “I’ve sailed around the world twice, docked in every port, but I swear I’ve never heard anything like this—neither in a storybook nor out of one.”

“It’s a strange tale, Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said. “Are you sure you’re not making it up? Some men think it’s sport to fool newcomers.”

“If you think that, Sir Henry,” I said, offended, slipping the paper into my pocket—because I detest being mistaken for a liar, one of those fools who boast of adventures that never happened—“then there’s no point continuing.” I stood to leave.

Sir Henry laid his hand on my shoulder. “Sit down, Mr. Quatermain,” he said. “I beg your pardon. I see clearly you’re not deceiving us. It’s just that the story sounds so impossible I could scarcely believe it.”

“You’ll see the original map and writing when we reach Durban,” I replied, somewhat calmed. “But I haven’t told you about your brother. I knew the man Jim who traveled with him. A Bechuana by birth, a skilled hunter, clever for a native. On the morning Neville departed, I saw Jim standing by my wagon, cutting tobacco on the disselboom.

‘Jim,’ I said, ‘where are you going this trip? Elephants?’

‘No, Baas,’ he answered. ‘We’re after something worth more than ivory.’

‘And what might that be?’ I asked. ‘Gold?’

‘No, Baas. Something worth more than gold.’ He grinned.

I didn’t press further. But I was curious. Soon, Jim finished his work.

‘Baas,’ he said.

I ignored him.

‘Baas,’ he repeated.

‘Eh, boy? What is it?’

‘Baas—we’re going after diamonds.’

‘Diamonds? Then you’re heading in the wrong direction. You should go to the Fields.’

‘Baas, have you ever heard of Suliman’s Berg?’—that is, Solomon’s Mountains, Sir Henry.

‘Ay.’

‘Have you ever heard of the diamonds there?’

‘I’ve heard a foolish tale, Jim.’

‘It’s no tale, Baas. Once I knew a woman who came from there. She reached Natal with her child. She told me—she’s dead now.’

‘Your master will feed the vultures, Jim,’ I said, ‘if he tries to reach Suliman’s land. And you’ll join him, if they find anything to feast on.’

He grinned. ‘Mayhap, Baas. Man must die. I’d rather try a new country myself. The elephants are getting scarce.’

‘Ah! my boy,’ I said, ‘wait till the pale old man grips your yellow throat, and then we’ll hear what tune you sing.’

Half an hour later, I saw Neville’s wagon move off. Then Jim returned, running.

‘Good-bye, Baas,’ he said. ‘I didn’t want to leave without saying farewell. You’re probably right—we may never trek south again.’

‘Is your master really going to Suliman’s Berg, Jim? Or are you lying?’

‘No, Baas. He is. He said he had to make his fortune somehow—or try to. So why not aim for diamonds?’

‘Oh!’ I said. ‘Wait—Jim, will you take a note to your master? Promise not to give it to him until you reach Inyati?’ That was about a hundred miles away.

‘Yes, Baas.’

So I tore off a scrap of paper and wrote: *Let him who comes… climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast, till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is Solomon’s great road.*

‘Now, Jim,’ I said, ‘when you give this to your master, tell him to follow it exactly. Don’t give it to him now—because I don’t want him coming back with questions I won’t answer. Now go—you idle fellow, the wagon’s nearly out of sight.’

Jim took the note and left. That’s all I know, Sir Henry. But I’m afraid—”

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry interrupted, “I’m going to find my brother. I’ll trace him to Suliman’s Mountains, and beyond if necessary, until I find him—or know he’s dead. Will you come with me?”

I am, as I’ve said, a cautious man—perhaps even timid. This idea frightened me. To attempt such a journey seemed to invite certain death. And aside from that, I had a son to support. I couldn’t afford to die.

“No, thank you, Sir Henry,” I said. “I think I’d rather not. I’m too old for such wild-goose chases. We’d only end like poor Silvestre. I have responsibilities.”

Both Sir Henry and Captain Good looked disappointed.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “I’m well-off. I’m determined to do this. Name your price—any reasonable amount—and I’ll pay you before we start. Also, I’ll ensure your son is provided for if anything happens to us. You’ll see how essential I consider your presence. And if we find diamonds—well, they’ll belong to you and Good equally. I don’t want them. Of course, that promise means nothing—but the same applies to any ivory we might get. Make your terms with me, Mr. Quatermain. I’ll cover all expenses.”

“Sir Henry,” I said, “this is the most generous offer I’ve ever received—no hunter or trader could sneeze at it. But this is the biggest venture I’ve ever heard of. I need time to think. I’ll give you my answer before we reach Durban.”

“Very good,” he replied.

I said goodnight and turned in. That night, I dreamed of poor Silvestre—and of diamonds gleaming in the dark.
