Just within the precincts of the White Horse Tavern, leading up to the windows of the dining room, lay a bowling green dotted with a couple of tables where guests might sit to sip punch or tea. Having finished their dinner roughly simultaneously, Mr. Morris suggested they move to the green for a cool bottle. "Jack Morris would use the excuse of moving to the Dust Hole to get another drink," observed my Lord. To this, Jack retorted that surely every gentleman had his own preferred method of heading toward the deuce, admitting frankly that his own weakness was a bottle.

"My Lord Chesterfield's deuce is deuce-ace," noted my Lord March. "His lordship cannot keep away from cards or dice."

"My Lord March possesses not one devil, but several," countered Jack. "He loves gambling, horse-racing, betting, drinking, eating, money, and women. You have certainly fallen into bad company, Mr. Warrington, by associating with his lordship. He will play you for every acre you hold in Virginia."

"With the greatest pleasure in life, Mr. Warrington!" interjected my Lord.

"And for all your tobacco, spices, slaves, oxen, and asses, and everything else you possess."

"Shall we begin now?" asked my Lord. "Jack, you are never without a dice box or a bottle screw. I propose setting Mr. Warrington against whatever he desires."

"Unfortunately, my lord," replied Harry, "the tobacco, slaves, asses, and oxen are not yet mine. I am just of age, and my mother, who is scarcely twenty years older than myself, has as good a chance of longevity as I do."

"I will bet you that you survive her," my Lord offered. "I will pay you a sum now against four times that amount payable at her death. I will set a fair stake over this table against the reversion of your estate in Virginia upon the old lady's departure. What is your place called?"

"Castlewood."

"A principality, I hear it is," my Lord mused. "I wager its value has been exaggerated tenfold among the gossip-mongers here. How did you acquire the name Castlewood? Are you related to my lord? Ah, wait—I know. Your mother descends from the true head of the house. He took the losing side in '15. I have heard this story a dozen times from my old Duchess. She knew your grandfather; he was a friend of Addison, Steele, Pope, and Milton, I dare say, and the bigwigs. It is a pity he did not stay home and transplant the other branch of the family to the plantations."

"I have just been staying at Castlewood with my cousin there," remarked Mr. Warrington.

"Hm! Did you play with him? He is fond of pasteboard and bones."

"Never, save for sixpences and a pool of commerce with the ladies."

"So much the better for both of you. But did you play with Will Esmond if he was at home? I will lay ten to one you did."

Harry blushed and admitted that, in the evenings, he and his cousin had played a few games of cards.

"And Tom Sampson, the chaplain?" cried Jack Morris. "Was he of the party? I wager Tom made a third, and may the Lord deliver you from Tom and Will Esmond combined!"

"Nay; the truth is, I won of both of them," said Mr. Warrington.

"And they paid you? Well, miracles will never cease!"

"I did not speak of miracles," remarked Harry, smiling over his wine.

"And you do not tell tales out of school—the *volto sciolto*—hey, Mr. Warrington?" asked my Lord.

"I beg your pardon," said straightforward Harry. "French is the only language besides my own of which I know a little."

"My Lord March has learned Italian at the Opera, and a pretty penny his lessons have cost him," noted Jack Morris. "We must show him the Opera—mustn't we, March?"

"Must we, Morris?" my Lord asked, as if he barely tolerated the other's familiarity.

Both gentlemen were dressed identically in small, unpowdered wigs, blue frocks with plate buttons, buckskin breeches, riding boots, and small hats adorned with a narrow lace cord, showing no outward marks of high fashion.

"I care little for the Opera, my lord," said Harry, warming to his Burgundy. "But I should like to go to Newmarket and long to see a good English hunting field."

"We will show you Newmarket and the hunt, sir. Can you ride well?"

"I think I can," Harry replied. "And I can shoot fairly well, and jump some."

"What is your weight? I bet we weigh even, or I weigh more. I bet Jack Morris beats you at birds or a mark at fifty paces. I bet I jump farther than you on flat ground right here on this green."

"I do not know Mr. Morris's shooting—I have never seen either gentleman before—but I accept your bets, my lord, at whatever you please," cried Harry, now thoroughly warmed by the wine.

"Ponies on each!" shouted my Lord.

"Done and done!" cried my Lord and Harry together. The young man felt it was for the honor of his country not to be ashamed of any bet made to him.

"We can try the last bet now, if your feet are steady," said my Lord, springing up, stretching his arms and limbs, and examining the crisp, dry grass. He removed his boots, then his coat and waistcoat, buckled his belt around his waist, and threw his clothes to the ground.

Harry treated his garments with far more respect. It was his finest suit. He removed the velvet coat and waistcoat, folded them carefully, and, seeing the nearby tables slopped with drink, placed them on a table in the dining room, where the windows stood open.

At that moment, a new guest entered: none other than Mr. Wolfe, soberly eating a chicken and salad with a modest pint of wine. Harry, in high spirits, told the Colonel he had a bet with my Lord March and asked if the Colonel would stand him halves. The Colonel claimed he was too poor to bet, but would he come out to witness fair play? With all his heart, he agreed. Colonel Wolfe set down his glass and stalked through the open window after his young friend.

"Who is that tallow-faced Put with the carroty hair?" asked Jack Morris, whose Burgundy had taken full effect.

Mr. Warrington explained that this was Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe of the 20th Regiment.

"Your humble servant, gentlemen!" the Colonel said, offering a rigid military bow.

"Never saw such a figure in my life!" cried Jack Morris. "Did you—March?"

"I beg your pardon, I think you said March?" the Colonel asked, looking very surprised.

"I am the Earl of March, sir, at Colonel Wolfe's service," the nobleman bowed. "My friend, Mr. Morris, is so intimate with me that, after dinner, we are quite like brothers."

"Why is not all of Tunbridge Wells here to hear this?" thought Morris. Delighted, he shouted, "Two to one on my lord!"

"Done!" called Mr. Warrington; and the enthusiastic Jack was obliged to cry "Done!" as well.

"Take him, Colonel," Harry whispered to his friend.

But the Colonel said he could not afford to lose, and therefore could not hope to win.

"I see you have won one of our bets already, Mr. Warrington," my Lord March remarked. "I am taller than you by an inch or two, but you are broader round the shoulders."

"Pooh, my dear Will! I bet you you weigh twice as much as he does!" cried Jack Morris.

"Done, Jack!" said my Lord, laughing. "The bets are all ponies. Will you take him, Mr. Warrington?"

"No, my dear fellow—one's enough," said Jack.

"Very good, my dear fellow," said my Lord. "Now we will settle the other wager."

Having already arrayed himself in his best silk stockings, black satin-net breeches, and neatest pumps, Harry did not wish to remove his shoes, unlike his antagonist, whose heavy riding-boots and spurs were ill-suited for leaping. Before them lay a fine, even stretch of green turf about thirty yards long, sufficient for a run and a jump. A gravel walk bordered this green, beyond which stood a wall and gate sign featuring a field azure with the Hanoverian White Horse rampant between two skittles, bearing the motto of the landlord and the animal depicted.

My Lord's friend laid a handkerchief on the ground as the mark from which the leapers were to start, while Mr. Wolfe stood at the far end of the grass plat to note where each landed.
