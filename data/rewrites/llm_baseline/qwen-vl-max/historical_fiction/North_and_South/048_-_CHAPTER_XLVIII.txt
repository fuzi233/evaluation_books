“My own, my father’s friend! I cannot part with thee! I ne’er have shown, thou ne’er hast known, How dear thou art to me.” —ANON.

The dinner parties hosted by Mrs. Lennox were a well-ordered symphony of society: her guests brought grace and charm; Captain Lennox offered an easy mastery of current affairs; and Mr. Henry Lennox, along with the select circle of rising men welcomed into their fold, contributed wit, intellect, and a refined fluency that never descended into pedantry. Conversation flowed like wine—lively, polished, sparkling. Yet for Margaret Hale, even in this glittering world, there was no peace. Every gift, every sentiment, every learned pursuit was reduced to mere ornament. The soul beneath—the quiet fire of truth, reverence, or moral depth—was consumed in fleeting brilliance. Art was discussed not for its meaning, but for its surface beauty; lofty ideas were celebrated in company, then forgotten in solitude. Their minds, sharp and capable, were spent on elegance rather than insight, on words instead of feeling.

One evening, after the gentlemen had joined the ladies in the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret. It was the first time since her return to Harley Street that he had spoken to her without formality or hesitation.

“You did not look pleased at what Shirley said during dinner,” he observed.

“Didn’t I? My face must be very expressive,” she replied, smiling faintly.

“It always was. It has not lost its eloquence.”

“I didn’t like,” she said quickly, “his manner of defending something he knew to be wrong—so clearly wrong—even in jest.”

“But it was clever. Each word landed perfectly. Did you catch the felicity of his phrases?”

“Yes.”

“And yet you despise them, I suppose?” he added, with a quiet smile. “Please don’t hesitate, even though he is my friend.”

There—she paused. He waited, sensing she might continue. But she only flushed, turned away, and walked toward the window. As she did, she heard him speak again, low and clear:

“If your disapproval lies in my tone, or in how I think, will you do me the justice to tell me plainly? That way, I may learn to please you.”

For weeks, there had been no word from Mr. Bell about his journey to Milton. He had mentioned it briefly at Helstone, as something likely to happen soon. But perhaps he had settled matters through letters. Margaret believed he would avoid Milton if he could—he disliked the place, and worse, he would not grasp the deep significance she attached to speaking with him in person. She knew he would feel the necessity of doing so, but whether in summer, autumn, or winter made little difference to her. Now it was August. Still, no mention had been made of the Spanish trip he’d alluded to Edith. Margaret began to accept that the hope of seeing him again might fade into nothingness.

Then one morning, a letter arrived. He wrote that he intended to come to London the following week. He wanted to see her about a plan he had formed—and, in a lighter tone, confessed he meant to consult a doctor. He had begun to agree with her: when irritability overtook him, it was easier to blame ill health than temper.

There was a forced cheerfulness in the letter, which Margaret noticed only later. At the time, her thoughts were caught up in Edith’s exclamations.

“Coming to town!” Edith gasped. “Oh dear! And I’m so exhausted by the heat—I can’t possibly host another dinner. Besides, everyone’s gone except us two poor fools who can’t decide where to go. There’ll be nobody to greet him!”

“I’m certain he’d prefer dining with us alone,” Margaret said firmly, “to meeting any strangers, however pleasant. If he’s unwell, he won’t want invitations. I’m glad he finally admitted it. I’ve known he was ill ever since reading his letters—but he wouldn’t answer me when I asked. I had no one else to turn to.”

“Oh, he’s not seriously ill,” Edith insisted. “If he were, he wouldn’t be thinking of Spain.”

“He never mentions Spain,” Margaret said.

“No—but his plan must concern it. Still, would you really travel in such weather?”

“Oh, it will cool down soon enough. Yes—just think. I’m afraid I’ve wished and thought too much—too passionately, too wilfully. That kind of longing either brings disappointment or fulfillment, down to the letter, but never true joy.”

“That’s superstition, Margaret,” Edith said gently.

“No,” Margaret replied, “I don’t believe it is. But it should warn me. It should stop me from giving in to such intense desires. It’s like saying, ‘Give me children, or else I die.’ Mine is, ‘Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.’”

“My dear Margaret!” Edith exclaimed. “You’ll be persuaded to stay there forever—and then what shall I do? Oh, I wish I could find someone here for you to marry—someone I could count on!”

“I shall never marry,” Margaret declared.

“Nonsense! Double nonsense! Sholto says you’re such a draw, he knows half a dozen men who’d visit just to see you next year.”

Margaret stiffened. “Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your life in Corfu has taught you—”

“Well?”

“Just a shade or two of coarseness.”

Edith burst into tears, sobbing violently, declaring that Margaret no longer loved her, that she was no longer her friend. Margaret, struck by the sudden intensity of her sister’s distress, realized she had spoken too harshly in her own wounded pride. She ended up spending the rest of the day tending to Edith, who lay on the sofa like a broken thing, sighing deeply, until she finally fell asleep.

Mr. Bell did not arrive on the day he had rescheduled. The next morning came a letter from Wallis, his servant. His master had been unwell for some time—that was why he had delayed his journey. But just as he was preparing to leave for London, he suffered a sudden apoplectic seizure. The doctors believed he would not survive the night. By the time Miss Hale received the letter, they feared, he would already be dead.

Margaret read the letter at breakfast and turned deathly pale. Without a word, she handed it to Edith and left the room.

Edith was devastated, weeping in a frightened, childlike way that upset her husband deeply. Mrs. Shaw was eating alone in her room, and thus Sholto found himself tasked with calming his wife, who was overwhelmed by the sudden proximity of death—a concept she had never truly faced before. Here was a man they expected to dine with them today now lying dying—or dead.

It took some time before she could think of Margaret. Then she rose, hurried upstairs, and found her in her bedroom. Dixon was packing a few personal items. Margaret was frantically putting on her bonnet, tears streaming down her face, her hands trembling so badly she could barely tie the strings.

“Oh, dear Margaret! This is terrible! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto could telegraph—do anything you want!”

“I’m going to Oxford,” she said, voice firm despite her distress. “There’s a train in half an hour. Dixon offered to go with me, but I could have gone alone. I must see him again. He may still be alive, needing care. He’s been like a father to me. Don’t stop me, Edith.”

“But I must,” Edith insisted. “Mamma won’t approve at all. Come talk to her, Margaret. You don’t know where you’re going. I wouldn’t mind if he had a house of his own—but in his Fellow’s rooms? Please come to mamma and ask her first. It’ll take only a minute.”

Margaret hesitated, then gave in. The moment passed. Mrs. Shaw, thrown into confusion and hysteria by the news, could not act decisively. Time slipped away.

But another train was due in a couple of hours. After heated debate about propriety and decorum, it was decided that Captain Lennox would accompany Margaret—because she was resolute: she would go, by herself or with someone, on the next train, regardless of social conventions. Her father’s friend, her own beloved mentor, lay near death. The thought struck her with such force that she surprised herself with the strength of her resolve.

Five minutes before departure, she sat across from Captain Lennox in the railway carriage.

She found comfort in knowing she had gone—even if only to hear that he had died that night. She saw the rooms he had occupied, and from that moment forward, they became sacred in her memory, linked forever to her father and to the man who had stood beside him in loyalty and affection.

They had promised Edith they would return for dinner if the worst had happened. So, after a long, silent farewell to the familiar, gentle face that had once smiled and joked with her father, they left.

Captain Lennox slept during the journey home. Margaret sat quietly, allowing herself to grieve. She reflected on the year that had brought so many sorrows—loss upon loss, each one reopening wounds not yet healed. But as she stepped into the warm, welcoming light of her aunt’s home, hearing Edith’s tender voice, Sholto’s joyful greeting, and seeing the cheerful rooms filled with love and sorrowful kindness, she stirred from her numb despair.

She took Edith’s usual seat on the sofa. Sholto was carefully instructed to bring Aunt Margaret’s tea. By the time she went upstairs to dress, she could whisper a prayer of gratitude—for having spared her dear friend a prolonged or painful end.
