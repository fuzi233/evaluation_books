“I will spare you a description,” my wife began, smoothing a crease in the sun-warmed canvas as if she could iron the whole day flat by will alone, “of our first day’s occupations. Truth to tell, I spent most of it with my thoughts in a knot—watching your progress and straining my eyes for your signals.”

She had risen before the boys, she said, while the night’s salt still clung to everything like a second skin. When she saw my answering sign that all was well, she felt her lungs loosen for the first time since the wreck; yet relief, in that hard bright place, never stayed relief for long.

“For it is perfectly impossible,” she told herself as she sat with the sleeping children and listened to the bay’s steady breathing—water folding on stone, gulls calling, our own little world held inside a ring of cliffs—“to live much longer where we are now. The sun beats upon this bare rock the livelong day; our only shelter is a poor tent, and even beneath its canvas the heat thickens, as though the air were being cooked. Why should I and the little boys do nothing but endure? Why should not we, too, accomplish something useful?”

She had tasted the thought as one tastes a sharp berry—sour at first, then bracing.

“If we could exchange this melancholy, unwholesome spot for a shady dwelling-place,” she went on, “we should all improve in health and spirits. Among those woods you and Fritz described—cool, green, full of living sound—there must be some retreat where we could establish ourselves. There must be, and I will find it.”

By the time the boys woke, her resolve had already become routine—like kneading dough before breakfast because bread must be had. Jack, meanwhile, had found occupation of his own. She noticed him crouched with his knife beside the place where Fritz’s jackal lay, his tongue between his teeth in concentration. He had cut two long narrow strips of skin, scraping and cleaning them with a patience that surprised even her. Then he produced, with the proud stealth of a boy revealing treasure, a handful of large nails from his pocket and drove them through the skin so their points stood outward. After that he cut sailcloth strips twice as broad as the thongs, doubled them, and laid them over the raw side to cover the nail heads.

At this stage Master Jack presented his work with the air of a chef offering a dish that must be finished properly: would she kindly stitch canvas and damp hide together?

She gave him needle and thread and refused to steal the satisfaction from him. Yet his fingers, willing but clumsy, fought the stubbornness of skin that wanted to curl and shrink. When she saw how good-humouredly he persevered—working by feel, grimacing at the smell, blinking against the heat—she took pity, conquered her own disgust, and finished the linings for the dogs’ spike-collars. Afterward she was called upon to complete, in the same manner, a fine belt he had fashioned for himself.

“Think how you’ll keep it from shrinking,” she advised, because she knew—whether it was a hem or a hide—materials always tried to return to their old language, their old shape.

Ernest, who ridiculed Jack’s manufacture with his usual airy superiority, offered a sensible plan nonetheless. Jack nailed the skin stretched flat upon a board and set it in the sun to dry, like fruit laid out on a roof in a Swiss village.

Then she announced her journey.

The scheme was embraced with instant joy. Weapons were checked, provisions portioned with a care that made the biscuits feel precious—food no longer an abundance but a history being rationed. The two elder boys carried guns; she took the water flask and a small hatchet. The dogs came too. Turk, who had accompanied me on my first expedition, understood at once. His ears pricked; his paws chose the path as confidently as if the route were a remembered song, and he led with the pride of one who knows his usefulness.

Looking at her two young sons, each with a gun, and weighing in her mind how much their safety depended on those small hands, she felt gratitude toward me for teaching them early—gratitude sharpened by fear, as salt sharpens broth.

They filled the water-jar, crossed the stream, and climbed to the height from which, as I had told her, the prospect opens. The view struck her like cool water on a fevered brow. Something buoyant—hope, a sensation she had not felt in days—rose in her chest. Even the air sounded different there: less of surf’s unchanging hammer, more of wind combing leaves, birds threading notes through branches.

A pretty little wood in the distance caught her eye. Toward it they set their course. But the tall grass grew in dense luxuriance, higher than the children’s heads, and it clutched at them with a rasping whisper, as if the island itself objected to being crossed. Unable to force their way, they turned toward the open beach on their left, followed it until they were nearer the little wood, then left the strand and made toward it again.

They had not entirely escaped the tall grass. They were pushing through reeds—fatiguing, sweat-slick work—when suddenly a great rushing noise burst up at their feet. A very large powerful bird sprang upward on the wing. Both boys tried to take aim; both were too slow. The bird was already a dark stroke against the bright sky.

“Oh dear, what a pity!” Ernest exclaimed. “If I had only had my light gun, and if the bird had not flown quite so fast, I should have brought him down directly!”

“Oh yes,” she answered, “no doubt you would be a capital sportsman if only your game would always give you time to make ready comfortably.”

“But I had no notion that anything was going to fly up just at our feet,” he protested.

“A good shot must be prepared for surprises,” she said. “Neither wild birds nor wild beasts will send you notice.”

“What sort of bird can it have been?” Jack asked.

“Oh, it certainly must have been an eagle,” little Franz declared, round-eyed. “It was so very big!”

“Just as if every big bird must be an eagle,” Ernest replied with derision.

“Let’s see where he was sitting,” said their mother.

Jack sprang forward—and instantly a second bird, larger than the first, exploded into the air with a startle of wings and a harsh tearing sound, like a sail ripping free. The boys stood staring upward, stupefied. She laughed—an honest laugh that relieved the tightness in her throat.

“Well, you are first-rate sportsmen, to be sure! You will keep my larder famously supplied!”

At that Ernest flushed and looked as though tears threatened; Jack pulled off his cap and made a low bow toward the fleeing bird.

“Adieu for the present, sir! I live in hopes of another meeting!”

On searching the ground they found a rude nest, untidy with dry grass. It was empty. Broken egg-shells lay nearby. They concluded the young had escaped into the grass, and indeed, at a little distance, the reeds waved oddly as the chicks ran through them.

Now Ernest’s eyes, though his gun-hand had been slow, had been sharp.

“Just consider, Franz,” he said presently, warmed by his own certainty, “how this could possibly have been an eagle. Eagles never build on the ground; neither can their young leave the nest and run as soon as they are out of the egg. That is a peculiarity of the gallinaceous tribe alone, to which these must belong. The species is indicated by the white belly and dull red colour of the wing-coverts which I observed; I believe them to be bustards—especially as in the largest I noticed the moustache-like feathers over the beak, peculiar to the Great Bustard.”

“My dear boy,” she said, amused and a little proud, “your eyes were actively employed, I must confess, though your fingers were unready. And after all, it is just as well perhaps that we have not thrown the bustard’s family into mourning.”

So chatting, they approached her “pretty wood”—and found it was hardly a wood at all: only a dozen trees, but each one so enormous it made the mind falter. Their roots rose up and outward, propping the trunks on arching buttresses, like a cathedral held on living stilts. The space beneath was open, shaded, and astonishingly clean—soft green herbage underfoot, no thorns, no briar, no tangled undergrowth to snag cloth or skin. The air there was cooler, thick with leaf-scent and the faint sweetness of sap. Birds sang above—clear, layered calls that seemed to measure the place, as if sound itself were a kind of shelter.

She gave Jack some twine. Scrambling up one of the open-air roots, he managed to measure the trunk: eighteen yards round. She saw no fruit, but the foliage was dense and generous, pouring shade like a blessing onto the ground. They ate their midday meal there with an enjoyment that made even hard biscuit seem softened, especially when followed by water that tasted, by contrast with seawater and sweat, like something almost civilized.

The dogs joined them after a while. They had lingered along the shore, and she was surprised to see them lie down and sleep without begging, as they usually did when we ate.

The longer she sat in that green, hushed palace, the more it charmed her. She imagined a dwelling among those grand branches—an airy nest made human. Up there, above jackals and creeping night-things, she felt she could breathe without bracing herself against fear.

It seemed absurd to her to suppose they would find another place half so lovely; she decided to search no further. Instead, they would return to the beach and see whether anything from the wreck had been cast up—anything they could carry away with them.

Before starting, Jack persuaded her to sit a little longer and finish the belt and the spike-collars. He had, in fact, been carrying the board on which the skins were stretched all the while, so they would take the full benefit of the sun, like strips of meat being dried for a winter that might yet come.

Now the leather was dry. She completed the stitching easily. Jack buckled on his belt with immense pride, slid his pistols into it, and marched about with a self-important swagger while Ernest fitted the collars onto Turk and Juno.

When they reached the shore, it was strewn with objects—casks, chests, heavy pieces of wreckage—valuable but too weighty for them to lift. They rolled a few casks beyond the reach of high water and dragged a chest or two higher. While doing so, they noticed the dogs among the rocks, intent as foragers. They nosed crevices and pools, pounced suddenly, and swallowed what they found with obvious relish.

“They are eating crabs,” Jack said. “No wonder they have not seemed hungry.”

Indeed, the tide-pools teemed with little green crabs. The dogs snapped them up like crisp morsels. Yet it did not seem to satisfy them entirely.

A little later, as they were preparing to turn inland toward the ford, Juno began scraping furiously in the sand, uncovering round pale objects which she devoured with greedy haste.

Ernest inspected the spot and reported, with his calm air of announcing a fact from a book, that she had found turtles’ eggs.

“Oh,” cried my wife, “then let us by all means share in the booty!”

Mrs. Juno did not approve of sharing. It took effort to drive her aside while they gathered two dozen eggs and stowed them carefully into provision bags, as if each smooth shell contained not only food but a future breakfast—warmth, comfort, continuation.

While they worked, they caught sight of a sail approaching beyond the cliffs. Ernest declared it must be our raft. Little Franz, forever haunted by tales of savages, turned pale. For a moment even my wife’s certainty wavered. But they hurried to the stream, crossed by the stepping-stones, and came in sight of the landing-place—where, to their joy, they met us.

She finished her account with the brisk satisfaction of someone presenting not adventure for its own sake, but useful gain.

“Now I hope you approve of the proceedings of your exploring party,” she said, “and that tomorrow you will do me the favour of packing everything and taking us away to live amongst my splendid trees.”

“Aye, little wife,” I answered, half laughing, half honest in my doubt, “so that is your idea of comfort and security? A tree—on which we are to perch and roost like birds? If we had wings or a balloon, it would be a capital plan.”

“Laugh as much as you like,” she returned, “my idea is not so absurd as you pretend. We should be safe up there from jackals’ visits. And I know I have seen, at home in Switzerland, a pretty arbour with a strong floor up among the branches of a lime-tree, reached by a staircase. Why could we not contrive such a place to sleep safely at night?”

“I will consider it seriously,” I said. “Perhaps something may come of it after all. Meantime, as we have finished supper and night is coming on, let us commend ourselves to Almighty protection and retire to rest.”

Under our tent we slept soundly as marmots until break of day. Then my wife and I woke and took counsel again, our voices kept low so as not to disturb the boys—though the morning itself was already loud with the island’s living sounds: surf, wind, birds, the occasional crack of a shell under a dog’s tooth.

Concerning the tree-dwelling she had proposed, I admitted that any removal involved difficulties that must be examined closely.

“In the first place,” I said, “I am unwilling hastily to quit a spot to which, I am convinced, we were providentially led as a landing-place. See how secure it is—guarded by high cliffs, accessible only by the narrow passage to the ford. From here the ship is within reach; its valuable cargo is at our disposal. Suppose we stay patiently here for the present—until at least we have brought ashore everything we possibly can?”

“I agree to a certain extent,” she replied, “but you do not know how dreadfully the heat among these rocks tries me. It is almost intolerable for those who remain here all day while you and Fritz are away—out on the sea, or wandering shaded woods where cool fruits refresh and fair scenes delight you.”

She hesitated, then confessed what sat beneath her impatience like a stone in a pot.

“As for the ship’s contents, much has already been cast ashore. I would rather give up all the remainder than endure the anxiety that gnaws at me whenever you speak of venturing again on the faithless deep.”

“There is much right on your side,” I said. “Suppose we remove to your chosen abode, and make this rocky fastness our magazine—a place of retreat in case of danger. I could render it still more secure by blasting portions of rock with gunpowder. But first a bridge must be constructed to enable us to cross with baggage.”

“Oh, I shall be parched to death before we can leave if a bridge must be made,” she cried, impatience breaking through her careful self-command. “Why not take our things on our backs and wade across as we have done already? The cow and the donkey could carry a great deal.”

“They will have to do so in whatever fashion we move,” I said. “But bags and baskets we must have, to put things in. If you will give your attention to providing those, I will set about the bridge at once. It will be needed not once, but continually—the stream may swell and become impassable, and even as it is, an accident might happen.”

“Well, well,” she yielded, “I submit to your opinion—only pray set about it without delay. I long to be off. And it is an excellent idea to store the gunpowder here when we go. It is frightfully dangerous to keep so much close to our habitation.”

“Gunpowder is indeed the most dangerous and at the same time the most useful thing we have,” I said, “and for both reasons we must be careful of it. In time I will hollow a place in the rock where it can be kept safe from fire and damp.”

Thus, by morning counsel, we settled the great matter: we would move—yet not abandon our landing-place entirely. It would become our storehouse, our refuge, our remembered beginning.

When the children heard of the change, their joy was boundless. They began at once to call it our “journey to the Promised Land,” and regretted only that time must be “wasted,” as they put it, on bridge-building before the journey could truly begin.

Breakfast was hurried, not from lack of appetite but from eagerness. The cow and goats were milked, and we ate biscuit boiled in milk—plain fare, yet the warm sweetness of it steadied us like a hymn in the mouth.

Then I prepared to go toward the wreck, intending to bring planks for the bridge. Ernest and Fritz accompanied me. We soon fell under the influence of the current and were carried swiftly out. Fritz steered.

No sooner had we passed beyond the islet at the mouth of the bay and come in sight of its seaward beach than we were astonished to see a countless multitude of sea-birds—gulls and others—rising in a cloud, disturbed by our approach. Their cries battered the air, a wild screaming so thick it felt like noise you could chew. It was the opposite of the grove’s layered music—a harsh, competitive din, like a market where everyone shouts and no one listens.

Fritz caught up his gun and would have fired, but I forbade it. I was curious what drew so many.

A fresh breeze rose. I set the sail and directed our course toward the island. The swelling canvas and flying pennant delighted Ernest; Fritz narrowed his keen eyes toward the sand where the flocks settled again.

“Aha!” he shouted at last. “Now I see what they are after! They’ve got a huge monster of a fish there—and a proper feast they are making! Let’s have a nearer look, father!”

We could not bring the boat close, but landed at a short distance, secured our raft by looping a rope around a large stone, and drew near.

A monstrous fish lay stranded, and the birds were feeding on it with ravenous fury. It was extraordinary—almost instructive—to watch envy and gluttony flare among them as they fought for bites, shrieking, striking, tearing, as if the island had set out a banquet and the guests had forgotten all order.

“There was nothing on this beach when we passed yesterday,” Fritz said. “It seems strange to see this creature here.”

“Why, Fritz!” Ernest cried. “It must be the shark! Your shark! I believe I can see where you hit him in the head.”

“You are right, I do believe, Ernest,” I said, though I suspected imagination did much of his seeing, given the birds’ destruction. “Look, boys, at those terrific jaws—beneath that projecting snout. See the rows upon rows of teeth, and thank God we were delivered from them. Let us try if we can persuade these greedy birds to spare us a little of the shark’s skin. It is extremely rough; when dry it may be used like a file.”

Ernest drew the ramrod from his gun and charged into the crowd, striking right and left with such hearty zeal that he soon killed several birds and scattered most of the rest. Fritz detached broad strips of skin with his knife, and we returned toward the boat.

To my satisfaction the shore was strewn with boards and planks—just what I needed. I lost no time collecting them. We formed a small raft to tow behind us, and within a short time we were able to head homeward without visiting the wreck at all.

As we sailed, pleased with our good fortune, Fritz—at my direction—nailed part of the shark-skin flat on boards to dry, and stretched the rest over the rounded mast.

“Will that be a good idea, father?” he asked. “It will be bent and crooked when it hardens.”

“That is just what I want,” I said. “We may find it useful both flat and curved. It would make beautiful shagreen to smooth and polish wood.”

“I thought shagreen was made from asses’ hides,” Ernest remarked.

“And you thought rightly,” I answered. “The best is prepared in Turkey, Persia, and Tartary from the skins of horses and asses. The roughness is produced artificially: while the skin is newly flayed and still soft, hard grains of corn are spread on the under surface and pressed into it as it dries. The grains are removed afterward, and the roughness remains indelibly. Shagreen is useful in polishing joiners’ work. It is made in France also from the rough skin of a hideous creature called the angel-fish.”

“Angel-fish!” Fritz exclaimed. “What a name to give to anything hideous!”

“There are bad angels as well as good ones,” Ernest observed dryly. “It is better to leave people to see for themselves which is meant.”

By then we were close in shore. We lowered the sail and moored our craft—raft in tow—safely to the bank.

No one was in sight. Not a sound answered but the water and the birds. We hallooed loudly together. After a moment a shrill reply came, and my wife appeared with Jack and little Franz running from behind the high rocks near the stream, each carrying a small bundle knotted in a handkerchief, while Franz held aloft a landing-net like a flag.

Our return so soon surprised them. They asked anxiously the reason, which we explained. Then their bundles were opened, and a great heap of crawfish tumbled out—fine, glossy creatures snapping their claws and scuttling away in every direction the instant they touched ground. The boys shrieked with laughter as they chased them, only to find others escaping in a dozen new ways.

“Now, father, have we not done well today!” Jack cried, holding up one as though presenting a prize lobster from a grand kitchen. “Did you ever see such a splendid crawfish? Oh, there were thousands—two hundred at least. Just look at their claws!”

“No doubt you were the discoverer,” I said.

“No! Fancy young Franz being the lucky man,” Jack answered. “He and I went toward the stream while mother was busy, to look for a good place for the bridge. Franz was picking up pebbles and alabasters—some because they’re pretty, some to strike sparks with in the dark, and some he insisted were ‘gold.’ Then he cried, ‘Jack! Jack! Come and see the crabs on Fritz’s jackal!’ You know we threw it away there—and to be sure it was swarming with these creatures.”

He looked up, eager for approval, and asked the question that mattered most in our new life: would it feed us?

“Are you glad we found them, father? Will they be good to eat?”

“Very excellent,” I told him, “and we may be thankful that food for our wants is thus provided day by day.”

When each party had told its adventures, my wife set about cooking the crawfish. Even in our rough circumstances she treated the task with care—rinsing them in fresh water, letting them purge, then boiling them until their shells blazed red and the air filled with a rich briny sweetness that made the camp feel, for a moment, like a home with a pot on the hearth. The scent cut through the tent’s stale heat, and even the gulls’ distant racket seemed less insulting with such honest nourishment at hand.

While she worked, we went to bring our planks ashore. Even that simple labour required contrivance. I improvised rope-harness for the cow and donkey, so they could drag each board separately from the water’s edge to the margin of the stream, the ropes creaking, hooves grinding pebbles, the animals’ breath steady and patient.

Jack showed me where he thought the bridge should be. The banks were there tolerably close, steep, and of equal height.

“How shall we find out if our planks are long enough to reach across?” I asked. “A surveyor’s table would be useful now.”

“What do you say to a ball of string, father?” Ernest suggested. “Tie one end to a stone, throw it across, then draw it back and measure the line.”

We adopted his idea. The distance proved eighteen feet. Allowing three feet more on each side, I calculated we needed boards of twenty-four feet.

How to lay the planks across—that was the difficulty. We decided to discuss it over dinner, which now called us. My wife brought her needlework to show me: with hard labour she had made two large canvas bags for the ass to carry. Having no suitable needle, she had bored each stitch-hole with a nail. The work was rough, but strong—like everything we were learning to make.

Dinner was quickly dispatched; no one lingered when there was building to be done and daylight to spend. A scheme had occurred to me for conveying one end of a plank across the water, and I rose at once to set about it.
