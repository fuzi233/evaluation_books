Two hours later—somewhere about four o’clock—I started up out of sleep as though a hand had seized me by the throat. The first brutal weight of weariness had been paid off; the debt of thirst, compounded all day, came due at once and with interest. In my dream I had been kneeling in a running stream, scooping and sipping, the water quick and cold, the banks green as young mealies after rain. I could hear it too, that private music of a brook that never clangs or rings, only talks to itself over stones. Then my eyes opened on the real world: sand, scrub, and a heat that had turned the air into a thick, breathless paste.

My hands were so dry and horny that when I rubbed my face it felt like scraping leather. My lips and eyelids had glued themselves; I had to work them loose with effort, as though my own skin had begun to betray me. The others slept on—Sir Henry, Good, Umbopa—each drawn into himself, saving what little moisture the body can hide away. There was no bright edge of dawn, no freshening. Even the silence had weight; it was the muffled hush of a land that had forgotten the sound of water.

When it grew light enough to make out print, I took out the little pocket “Ingoldsby Legends” I had carried more from habit than wisdom, and read “The Jackdaw of Rheims.” I came to the line about the “golden ewer… filled with water as pure as any that flows between Rheims and Namur,” and the words might as well have been a knife. My mouth twitched, trying to smack lips that had split and stiffened; the thought of clean, running water made me half mad. I remember thinking—thirst loosens the mind’s bolts—that if the Cardinal himself had stood there with bell, book, and candle, I would have pushed in my dirty head and drunk his consecrated basin dry, soap-suds and all, curse or no curse. The picture of it—me, a burnt-up, grizzled little elephant hunter, bursting into that polite legend like a drought into a garden—struck me as so absurd that I laughed, or rather cracked out a cackle.

That woke the camp. They all sat up and went through the same grim business of rubbing grit from their faces, tearing eyelids apart, testing stiff tongues against dry teeth. Good’s first instinct was for the brandy flask—he looked at it with the longing of a man staring at a roast he is forbidden to touch—but Sir Henry took it from him at once.

“To drink that neat would only hasten it,” he said quietly.

“If we do not find water we shall die,” he added; and there was nothing theatrical in it. It was like stating that a man without air must suffocate.

“If we can trust the old Dom’s map, there should be some,” I said; but I might as well have quoted scripture to a dying bullock. We had all begun to suspect that the map was a tale told by a dead man to amuse himself.

We tipped the water-bottles upside down. Not a bead came. We licked the mouths like dogs. They were dry as bone.

Then I noticed Ventvögel rise. The Hottentot had the look of a creature born under open sky—mean little body, snub nose, eyes trained to the ground as if it were a book. He began to walk, slow and intent, and presently stopped dead and pointed.

“What?” we asked, and went to him.

“Fresh springbok spoor,” I said, disappointed; to my thirst it was merely another pattern in sand.

“Springbucks do not go far from water,” he answered in Dutch.

I had forgotten that law of the veld, and it struck through me like sudden shade. It is astonishing how a man in extremity clutches at the smallest promise and feels a foolish, blessed lift of hope. On a black night even one star seems a sort of salvation.

Ventvögel lifted his nose and sniffed, the way an old impala ram tests the wind for danger. He sniffed again, and his face—ugly as it was—changed.

“I smell water,” he said.

At that we were near jubilant; these wild-bred men have senses civilisation blunts, as if our iron pots and our engine-smoke and our clatter of cities drown out the finer messages of the world.

Just then the sun came up in splendour, and with it came a sight so sudden and so grand that for a moment even thirst stood back, as if awed. There, forty or fifty miles away, glittering like silver laid on brown cloth, rose Sheba’s Breasts; and running away from them, east and west as far as the eye could follow, the great wall of the Suliman Berg.

They stood like the pillars of a gate—two enormous mountains, each a white solemnity, each swelling round and smooth from the plain, and each crowned with a hillock of snow, like the nipple on a woman’s breast. Between them a cliff linked them, sheer as a cut made by some ancient sword, and along their flanks the same cliff-ranges marched away, broken here and there by flat-topped masses like the table mountain at the Cape. Sometimes, in mist and shadow, the hollows beneath take the shape of a recumbent woman veiled in sleep; and whether that is only the mind’s trick or some older memory of the land, I cannot say. But the sight had a weight in it that pressed on the heart. You could almost hear it—a great, mute hush, like a cathedral that has forgotten hymns.

For a little while the morning light played upon snow and brown slopes, then vapours gathered as though the mountains drew a curtain over their own privacy. The pure outlines went ghostlike behind gauze; and when the last clear whiteness blurred, thirst returned like a fire that had only been banked, not put out.

We looked and looked. Sand, scrub, heat—nothing that shone, nothing that promised. We went round the hillock and stared from the other side; still nothing. No pan, no pool, no spring.

“You are a fool,” I said to Ventvögel, anger rising from misery; “there is no water.”

But he kept sniffing, stubborn as a dog.

“I smell it, Baas,” he insisted. “It is somewhere in the air.”

“Yes,” I snapped, “in the clouds—and in two months it will fall and wash our bones.”

Sir Henry, who had the calm of a man whose courage is not dependent on comfort, stroked his yellow beard and looked up at the little sand koppie behind us.

“Perhaps it is on the top of the hill,” he said.

“Rot,” said Good. “Whoever heard of water at the top of a hill?”

“Let us go and look,” I answered; hope is a tyrant, and makes you climb even when you know better.

We scrambled up, Umbopa leading—Umbopa, who carried himself with a dignity that thirst could bend but not break. Halfway, he stopped so suddenly it was as though he had turned to stone.

“Nanzia manzie!” he cried—“Here is water!”

We rushed to him, and there, on the very crown of that sand mound, was a deep cut in the surface, and in it a pool.

It was blackish, brackish-looking, and gave off a faint sour smell, as though old leaves had stewed there; but it was water—or close enough to it for men who had been dying of it. We did not ask how it came there. We did not examine, or weigh risks. We threw ourselves down and drank like animals—no cups, no ceremony—our mouths in the pool, sucking and gulping, the tepid mud tasting like salvation.

Heavens, how we drank.

Then, when our bellies could hold no more, we tore off our clothes and sat down in the pool like pigs wallowing, letting the damp soak through our skins. There are luxuries that men who turn taps in town will never understand: that first wetness on a body that has become a dry husk; the way the ears seem to clear, as if the world’s quiet returns; the way even the heart’s beat sounds less frantic. The pool had its own little sound too—not the clean talk of a stream, but the thick, lazy suck of mud when you moved, and the faint lap against the bank. It was an ugly music, but it was life.

After a while we rose, dripping and revived, and fell upon the biltong we had not been able to stomach for a day. Now it tasted almost rich—salt and dried meat, the chew working the jaw like a machine that had rusted. We ate till we could eat no more, then smoked, then lay down under the overhanging bank where a scrap of shade lay like a blessing, and slept till noon.

We rested there the whole day, guarding that wretched pool as if it were a treasure vault, and not forgetting, in our rough way, to thank the long-dead da Silvestra whose map—scribbled on the tail of a shirt—had set down the place so exactly. The wonder was that such a pan had lasted. The only explanation I could find was some hidden spring deep in the sand feeding it drop by grudging drop.

At moonrise we filled ourselves and our bottles as far as they would go, and started again, spirits raised. That night we covered near five-and-twenty miles. We found no more water, though next day we were lucky enough to find a little shade behind ant-heaps—poor shelter, but even a hand’s breadth of shadow is a mercy when the sun is a hammer.

When the sun rose and for a while cleared the mist, Suliman’s Berg with the two white Breasts, now perhaps twenty miles away, towered over us grander than ever, as if each mile we walked did not bring us nearer so much as make them grow. Toward evening we marched again; and by daylight next morning we stood upon the lower slopes of Sheba’s left breast, the very mountain we had been steering for.

By then the water in our bottles was gone once more. Thirst came back with teeth. We could see no relief until we reached the snow line, far above us—white as a promise and as distant as a lie.

After an hour’s rest—if you can call it rest when your tongue feels like a strip of hide—we began to toil upward in the blazing heat. The mountain’s vast base was built of lava beds, ancient vomit of the earth hardened into rough, dark clinker. It tore at our feet through our worn soles, and each step was a grind of pain. By eleven o’clock we were finished—weak, dizzy, mouths bitter, throats raw. We aimed for some big lumps of lava ahead, hoping for shade.

We reached them, and on a little ridge close by saw something that looked like a miracle: a dense patch of green growth spreading over the black clinker. Soil, made from decomposed lava, had lodged there, and birds had sown it with seed. But a hungry man cannot eat grass like Nebuchadnezzar; that needs special digestive machinery and, perhaps, special madness. So we sat in the shade and groaned, and I, for one, cursed the day I had ever listened to treasure tales.

Then Umbopa rose, and, limping a little, went toward the green patch. A few minutes later that usually dignified man was dancing and shouting like one possessed, waving something green.

We scrambled to him as fast as we could, hope dragging us by the neck.

“What is it, Umbopa, son of a fool?” I shouted in Zulu.

“It is food and water, Macumazahn,” he cried back, holding up his prize.

It was a melon.

And not one only. The green patch was a patch of wild melons—thousands of them, dead ripe, lying like cannon-balls scattered by a careless army.

“Melons!” I yelled, and Good—who could make comedy even with death tapping his shoulder—had his false teeth fixed in one before the word was out.

We ate like men who had forgotten manners and rediscovered childhood. The flesh was pale and watery, the seeds bitter, the rind thick; but the juice ran down the chin and over cracked lips, and it cooled the mouth and throat as if you had poured the moon into them. I think we ate six each before we stopped, and poor as they were, I have rarely tasted anything so exquisite.

They did not satisfy hunger for long—melons are more drink than food—but they quieted thirst. We cut several in halves and set them end on in the hot sun, letting evaporation cool them; it is a simple trick, but it felt like the sort of small domestic cleverness that keeps a family alive through bad seasons. In that desolation, the act of preparing food—even crude food—seemed to stitch a little sanity back into us.

Still, hunger rose again, heavy and empty. We had biltong left, but our stomachs turned from it, and we dared not waste it; who could tell when we would find meat again?

At that moment luck—if luck is only the land’s indifference happening to favour you—sent us a gift. Looking out over the desert below I saw a flock of large birds flying straight toward us.

“Skit, Baas, skit!” whispered Ventvögel, throwing himself flat. We followed suit.

They were a flock of pauw—bustards—and they would pass within fifty yards. I took one of the repeating Winchesters, waited till they were nearly over, then sprang up. As I had hoped, they bunched together on seeing me. I fired twice into the thick of them and brought one down—a fine bird, twenty pounds at least.

In half an hour we had a fire of dry melon-stalks crackling—thin fuel that snapped and hissed, giving off a greenish, sweet smell as it burned. We toasted the bird over it, turning the flesh as best we could, the fat spitting into the flames, the smoke carrying that rich, mouth-watering scent that makes a starving man feel almost cruelly alive. Then we ate, and it was as though warmth spread back through our limbs. We ate that pauw to the bone; nothing was left but leg-bones and beak, and afterwards we could sit and breathe without groaning.

That night we went on again by moonlight, carrying as many melons as we could. As we climbed, the air cooled more and more, a relief so keen it felt like water on the skin. By dawn, as far as we could judge, we were within a dozen miles of the snow line. We found more melons there, and so thirst no longer frightened us; snow would soon be plenty.

But the slope steepened, and we crawled rather than walked, scarcely a mile an hour. That night we ate our last scrap of biltong.

With the exception of the pauw we had seen no living thing upon the mountain. What struck us as odd was that we found no spring or stream, though above us lay a great expanse of snow that must surely melt at times. Later we learned that, for reasons beyond my understanding, all the streams ran down the north side of the range. Then, with water secured by melons and snow, the old terror returned in a different mask: hunger. It began to seem we had escaped death by thirst only to be claimed by starvation.

The next three days I can best give as I wrote them then, for the mind grows blunt with lack of food and records like a machine:

“21st May.—Started 11 a.m., the air being cold enough to travel by day, carrying water-melons. Struggled on all day; no more melons—have passed their district. Saw no game. Halted at sundown, having had no food for many hours. Cold at night.

“22nd.—Started at sunrise, very faint. Only five miles. Found patches of snow and ate it; nothing else. Camped under edge of great plateau. Bitter cold. Drank a little brandy each and huddled together to keep alive. Suffering frightfully from starvation and weariness. Thought Ventvögel would die; he cannot bear cold.

“23rd.—Started when sun had thawed us a little. In dreadful plight. Fear unless we get food this will be our last day. Little brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, Umbopa bear up wonderfully. Ventvögel very bad. Hunger pangs less, but numbness in stomach. Others say same. Now level with the great wall of lava linking the two Breasts; view glorious. Behind us desert rolls to the horizon; before us smooth hard snow, almost level but rising gently, out of the centre of which the nipple of the mountain rises four thousand feet. Not a living thing. God help us; I fear our time has come.”

After that I dropped the journal, because the rest must be told as it happened.

All that day—the 23rd—we struggled slowly up the snow incline, stopping often, lying down to rest, then forcing ourselves up again by will alone. We must have looked like a band of ghosts, loaded with rifles and blankets, dragging feet that no longer belonged to us, our eyes sweeping the whiteness for anything that moved, anything that could be eaten. There was nothing. The snow threw the light into our faces so that the world seemed to glare at our misery.

We made perhaps seven miles. Just before sunset we found ourselves directly under the nipple of Sheba’s left Breast, a vast smooth dome of frozen snow towering thousands of feet above. Weak as we were, the beauty struck us. The setting sun threw flying rays that stained the snow in places blood-red, and set a kind of crown upon the great curve—glory laid on death’s doorstep.

“I say,” gasped Good at last, “we ought to be somewhere near that cave the old gentleman wrote about.”

“Yes,” I said. “If there is a cave.”

“Don’t talk like that,” groaned Sir Henry. “I have faith in the Dom. Remember the water! We shall find it soon.”

“If we don’t find it before dark we are dead men,” I answered; it was not courage, only arithmetic.

We trudged on in silence. Umbopa marched beside me wrapped in his blanket, a leather belt drawn so tight round his stomach to “make his hunger small” that his waist looked like a girl’s. Suddenly he caught my arm.

“Look,” he said.

Two hundred yards away, on the springing slope of the dome, there was a dark hole in the snow.

“It is the cave,” he said.

We made for it, and found that it was indeed the mouth of a cavern—no doubt the same that da Silvestra had spoken of. We had reached it just in time. The sun dropped with startling speed; in these latitudes twilight is brief, and night falls like a curtain. We crept inside.

The cave was not large—perhaps twenty feet long—and smelled of cold stone and old air, a dry, dead smell that had never known smoke or cooking. We huddled together for warmth, shoulder against shoulder, the way men do who have nothing left but each other’s bodies. We swallowed what remained of the brandy—barely a mouthful each—and tried to sleep.

But the cold was a living thing. At that altitude I am sure the temperature fell to fourteen or fifteen degrees below freezing. We had come from a desert that baked the blood, and now the frost moved among us like a thief, taking feeling first from finger, then toe, then cheek, nibbling here, then there. We drew closer, but there was no heat in our starved carcasses. Sometimes one of us slid into a few minutes of uneasy sleep, and perhaps it was well we did not sleep longer; I doubt if we would have woken. It seemed to me that only sheer will kept our hearts beating.

Through the stillness I could hear small sounds—the hiss of breath, the creak of leather, the thin chatter of teeth. Even that mattered: sound is life’s proof in darkness. Ventvögel’s teeth had chattered all night like castanets. Not long before dawn I heard him give a deep sigh, as if letting go of something heavy.

Then his teeth stopped.

I did not think much of it at once; I supposed he had fallen asleep. His back rested against mine, and it grew colder and colder until it felt like ice itself.

At last the air turned grey, then pale, then the first golden arrows of sun slid across the snow outside, and the sun peeped above the lava wall and shone into our cave—cold rays even in their brilliance.

It shone on Ventvögel too. He sat among us with his arms clasped about his knees, and he was stone dead. When I had heard him sigh he had died, and the cold had already stiffened him, freezing him into a posture of patience.

We dragged ourselves away from the body, shocked beyond measure. It is strange—men who can watch a beast die without flinching feel horror at the companionship of a dead man. We left him sitting there, his poor frozen shape turned into a thing of the mountain.

As we moved, sunlight reached farther into the cavern. Someone—one of us—gave a cry of fear. I turned, and saw what he had seen.

At the far end of the cave, where the rock darkened again, sat another form. The head lay fallen on the chest; the long arms hung down. It did not move. It was a dead man too—and, what was worse in that place, a white man.

The sight was more than our shattered nerves could bear. One and all we scrambled out into the cold morning as fast as our half-frozen limbs would take us.
