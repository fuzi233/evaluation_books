A little before our man Foker hightailed it out of Oxbridge, a new bloke turned up at Boniface College by the name of Horace Bloundell. Word leaked pretty quick he’d gotten the boot from rival university Camford after a very heated falling-out with the tutors and campus authorities, and the undergrad wags immediately dubbed him the heir to the mythical Bloundell-Bloundell estate of Bloundell-Bloundell Hall, Bloundell-Bloundellshire. The only reason Boniface took him on, after half a dozen other colleges had slammed their doors in his face? The Master, Dr. Donne, was a Suffolk local, vaguely related to the Bloundells, and Bloundell had a very cozy family church living coming his way if he just scraped through his degree. He’d previously been in a dragoon regiment, all crimson pantaloons and tall fuzzy shakos, until his third brother (the guy who was supposed to take that church job) conveniently dropped dead. Bloundell promptly traded his spurs for a clerical black coat and starched white neckcloth, and wasn’t about to let a silly little ban from Camford derail his very comfortable life plan.

By Pen’s second year at Oxbridge, Major Pendennis came to visit his nephew for a long weekend, and got introduced to Pen’s whole crew: soft-spoken, nice-as-pie Lord Plinlimmon, gallant open-hearted Magnus Charters, snarky quote-a-minute Harland, reckless Ringwood (nicknamed Rupert in the Union Debating Club for his absolutely unapologetic terrible takes and bold, unbacked arguments), and Barebones Broadbent, the firebrand republican Bristol dissenter whose debate rants could make even the most apathetic audience cheer so hard they spilled their port. Last but far from least was Horace Bloundell-Bloundell, who’d already muscled his way right into the top tier of the university’s cool kids club.

The Major might not have understood half of Harland’s Greek references, or approved of Broadbent’s scuffed thick-soled shoes and perpetually ink-stained hands, but he thought Pen’s friends were a delight—all except for the one guy who strutted around like he owned every inch of the quad, acting like he was the most worldly, well-traveled man within 100 miles of the campus.

Over breakfast the morning after the raucous dinner Pen had hosted for the crew, the Major was in excellent spirits, handing out hot takes left and right. He’d trotted out all his best old club anecdotes the night before—stories that were so stale in London that the waiters at White’s could recite them backwards for tips—but they were brand new to the Oxbridge lads, who’d laughed so loud they’d woken up the skip on the next staircase, and hung on every word like they were gospel. Only Bloundell had rolled his eyes every few minutes, making it very obvious he’d heard every single one of those stories a hundred times before. At one point he even had the gall to correct a tiny detail of the Major’s anecdote, saying “actually old boy, I heard the whole story at the club from two blokes who were there, and that part didn’t happen like that at all.” The lads all stared at him like he’d grown a second head, shocked anyone would dare interrupt the charming old major. Bloundell later went around the common room sneering that “old Pendennis is a total bore, eats like a trencherman at Gaunt House, and is the most fuddy-duddy old fogy I’ve met in years.”

The Major, naturally, took an instant dislike to Bloundell. This sort of thing never fails to be mutual, right? You don’t even need someone to tell you they’ve been calling you a bore; you just smell that Dr. Fell vibe the second they walk in the room, and you know you hate each other before anyone says a word.

“Pen my boy, your dinner went off perfectly,” the Major said, chipping the top off his boiled egg that morning. “You carved the roast beautifully—glad you learned that trick, everyone’s doing carving at the sideboard now but it’s still a handy skill to have later in life. Young Plinlimmon’s a total sweetheart, the spitting image of his mother Lady Aquila Brownbill, who I knew back in the day. Broadbent’s got a great set of lungs and a head full of books, and Foker’s always an absolute delight. But that new mate of yours Bloundell? Total bad news. Not eligible for your friendship in the slightest.”

“Wait, sir, Bloundell-Bloundell?” Pen laughed, confused. “He’s the most popular guy in the whole university! We called an emergency special meeting of the Barmecides drinking society just to vote him in the week he arrived! He’s from super old Suffolk gentry, descended straight from Richard the Lionheart’s Blondel! His family coat of arms has a harp on it! Motto’s O Mong Roy!”

“Fancy coat of arms doesn’t stop a bloke from being a total scammer, my lad,” the Major said, popping a bit of egg in his mouth. “Mark my words, that man is a tiger. I’ll lay you any wager you want he got kicked out of that dragoon regiment (and his old C.O. Lord Martingale is one of the most decent men I’ve ever met) for something so ungentlemanly the whole regiment still won’t talk about it. Look at that stack of cheap rings he wears, the reek of low billiard halls and third-rate gambling clubs oozes off him. He’s got ‘scamp’ written all over his face in big neon letters. Avoid him like he’s got the plague. Also, the dinner was a little fancier than strictly necessary, but I don’t object to you splashing out a little for friends, as long as you don’t make a habit of it and stay within your allowance. The cutlets were excellent, the soufflé so light it practically floated off the plate, though that third bottle of champagne was completely unnecessary.”

Poor Pen! His well-meaning uncle had no idea these fancy dinners were happening every other night, because Pen was dead set on acting like a seasoned gourmand and man of the world, the sort of guy who could rattle off vintage dates and critique a salmi like he’d been born with a napkin tucked under his chin. The college cook absolutely worshipped him, and by the start of his third year at Oxbridge, Pen’s staircase was no longer full of waiters carrying silver platters and popping iced champagne. Instead, it was full of scowling tradesmen, debt collectors, and laundresses lingering by his outer oak door waiting to corner him the second he left his rooms.

Naturally, Pen completely ignored his uncle’s warning about Bloundell. What 20-something boy doesn’t think the guy who swears he’s seen every wild party in Paris and pulled off a dozen rakes’ tricks is the coolest person alive? Half the public school boys at Oxbridge had a tattered copy of *Don Juan* hidden under their mattresses they’d read a dozen times, and they all ate Bloundell’s “I’ve seen the world” schtick right up.

It only took one of these walking bad influences to tank the whole college vibe. The Boniface tutors were tearing their hair out within months of Bloundell’s arrival—suddenly their formerly mild-mannered undergrads were skipping chapel, sneaking out at night, and being rude to the dons. The cool kids over at St. George’s College weren’t fooled though: Broadbent had dubbed him Captain Macheath, and swore he’d end up hanged one day. Cautious old Foker wouldn’t say a bad word about him out loud, but pulled Pen aside to mutter “Look, if you have to play cards with him, make him your partner, don’t play against him. That guy cheats at every game going, he’s got the sleight of hand down pat. And don’t take any weird odds from him, yeah? I swear the man hasn’t had spare cash in his pocket for three months.”

But Pen was too headstrong to listen. He was hell-bent on “seeing life,” quoting Terence and Horace out of context to justify skipping tutorials to party, convinced he’d turn into a fully-fledged legendary roué by the time he turned 22 if he kept up the pace.

One night after a supper party in his rooms, when everyone was getting ready to head home after a low-stakes game of vingt-et-un, Bloundell playfully grabbed an empty green wine glass off the table, pulled a pair of dice out of his waistcoat pocket, and waved the glass around like he’d done it a thousand times before. He started belting the chorus from the hit opera *Robert the Devil*, the rest of the lads joined in, and before anyone knew it they were all huddled around the table rattling dice for a game of hazard, the green glass getting passed from hand to hand until Pen smashed it after throwing six winning mains in a row.

After that night, Pen was addicted. Bloundell even rigged up a special felt-lined dice box so the rattle wouldn’t carry out the window and tip off the sharp-eared tutors. They almost got caught once when tutor Mr. Buck was wandering through the quad and heard someone yell “Two to one on the caster!” through Pen’s open window. When he burst in to catch them, the lads had three open copies of Homer spread out on the table, and Pen dead-faced asked him “Sir, quick, do you happen to know if the River Scamander is still navigable? We’re trying to work it out for our tutorial.” The confused tutor left without a single penalty.

Bloundell had a million other wild made-up stories to entertain Pen, too. He ranted about his wild days in garrison, his trips to the Paris casinos, the night he stumbled back to his rooms blackout drunk after a dinner at the Café de Paris and woke up with 12,000 francs stuffed under his pillow and 149 Napoleons tucked into his boot. Pen’s eyes practically sparkled hearing that—he thought Bloundell was the luckiest, most badass man alive.

When Pen told him all about his old grand passion for the actress Miss Fotheringay, Bloundell laughed his head off, and proceeded to slander her so thoroughly Pen believed every made-up story he spun: that she had no reputation left at all, that twenty different rich men had spent hundreds of pounds on her, that Pen was a total idiot for ever wanting to marry her. Sweet, trusting Pen never once doubted the word of the man who never had a good thing to say about any woman, and was left mortified, convinced his great grand love had all been a stupid fool’s delusion.

One Easter, Pen lied straight to his mother and uncle’s face, saying he was staying back at Oxbridge to cram for his exams. Instead he snuck off to London with Bloundell, who had a running “tick” (read: unpaid tab) at a dodgy Covent Garden hotel. They hung out at Bloundell’s old military club, dined with a bunch of spurred, mustachioed ex-dragoons, drank pale ale for breakfast and hit every theatre and singing den in the city. They very nearly ran straight into Major Pendennis on Pall Mall once, and the closest call of all was at the Museum Theatre, where Pen and Bloundell were in the stalls box, separated from the Major (who was in Lord Steyne’s private box) by nothing but a single thin plank of wood.

Pen watched Miss Fotheringay perform that night, and all the magic was gone. He could hear her thick Irish brogue under the fake stage accent, all her sweeping dramatic gestures were the exact same rehearsed schtick he’d seen a hundred times before, and he cringed thinking that once he’d thought that loud overacting was pure genius. He bailed on going backstage to see her, and went off to watch Taglioni dance and *Don Giovanni* at the Opera instead. They went out for a roaring, raucous supper after, Pen woke up with a splitting headache the next morning, and trundled back to Oxbridge completely skint.

He was soon so deep in debt he could barely keep track of how much he owed. Even the money lender Bloundell introduced him to, the infamous Finch, only charged him a 100% interest rate, which was basically a kindness by London usurer standards. The college cook who adored Pen even offered to send him free dinners for as long as he needed them, no pressure to pay the bill. For all his foolishness, Pen was impossibly likable: he’d leave the raunchiest party mid-rager to go sit with a sick friend, he’d split his last guinea with any mate who was broke, and no one could stay mad at him for long even as he careened straight towards total ruin.
