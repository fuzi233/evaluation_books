The equilibrium of good and evil, that ancient and relentless accountant, balances its ledger with a precision that borders on the divine. Yet, in the chaotic theater of the present moment, the scales tipped violently toward survival. The steel bow, strapped to the arbalestrier's back and raised high above his head, had become the instrument of his undoing. It was precisely that elevated position that rendered the weapon accessible to Gerard. One hand seized the cold metal, while the other slipped deftly between the bow and the cord, securing the prize. With a single, eager jerk of his crossbow, Denys was hauled from the churning depths until half his torso broke the surface, gasping for air.

"Now, grip me not! Grip me not!" Gerard screamed, his voice cracking with the mortal terror of a man who knew that a single misjudgment could mean drowning forever.

"Pas si bete," Denys gurgled, the words bubbling up like foam from a broken dam.

Seeing the raw, animalistic stuff of which his companion was made, Gerard's fear evaporated, replaced by a steely calm born of necessity. "On thy back," he commanded sharply. Seizing the arbalest, he drove a powerful stroke forward, aiding the desperate maneuver. "Hand on my shoulder! Slap the water with the other! No—with a downward motion. Just so. Do nothing more than I bid thee."

Gerard had already latched onto Denys's long, sodden hair, twisting it tight and biting the end firmly between his side teeth. With the formidable strength of his youthful neck, he held the soldier's head aloft, striking out lustily against the roaring current. For a fleeting second, hesitation paralyzed him—a terrible indecision regarding which bank to seek—but then the west shore appeared, slightly nearer. He committed to it, choosing not to swim safely to jail like a dutiful child, but rather to forge a path that would offer a novel incident to history.

Driven by the river's fury, they slanted dangerously across the flow. Having covered nearly a hundred yards, Denys murmured uneasily, "How much more of this?"

"Courage," Gerard mumbled, his voice steady despite the chaos. "Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows; you are safe as in bed."

And indeed, the next moment brought shock and relief: they found themselves in shallow water, wading ashore onto solid ground. Once on *terra firma*, they stared at one another from head to toe, as if eyes alone could devour the magnitude of their deliverance. Then, by a singular, overwhelming impulse, they flung arms around each other's necks, panting there, hearts swelling too full for speech.

At this sacred threshold, life tasted sweeter than heaven. Perhaps most intensely so for the poor exiled lover, who had just secured the life of his friend. Oh, the joy to whose heights had any poet yet soared, or dared attempt to soar? To save a human life—and specifically, a beloved one—is a reward worth living for, yes, three score years and ten.

Calmer now, they clasped hands and walked along the bank, hand in hand like a pair of sweethearts, scarcely knowing or caring whither their feet led them. Behind them, the boatmen were all secure on the late-concave, now-convex craft. Herr Turnip-face, the "Inverter of things," stood in the middle, seemingly untouched by the fray. At least, he greeted them cheerfully when they shot into the Rhine and again when they emerged.

"Shall we wait till they right the boat?"

"No, Denys. Our fare is paid; we owe them naught. Let us move on, and briskly."

Denys assented, noting that they could walk the entire way to Cologne along this bank.

"I fare not to Cologne," came the calm reply.

"Why, whither then?"

"To Burgundy."

"To Burgundy? Ah, no! That is too good to be true."

"Sooth 'tis, and sense into the bargain. What matters it to me how I go to Rome?"

"Nay, nay; you but say so to please me. The change is too sudden. Think me not so ill-hearted as to take you at your word. Besides, did I not see your eyes sparkle at the wonders of Cologne? The churches, the images, the relics..."

"How dull art thou, Denys; that was when we were to enjoy them together. Churches! I shall see plenty, going Rome-ward however I will. The bones of saints and martyrs; alas! the world is full of them. But a friend like thee—where on earth's face shall I find another? No, I will not turn thee farther from the road that leads to thy dear home, and her that pines for thee. Neither will I rob myself of thee by leaving thee. Since I drew thee out of the Rhine, I love thee better than I did. Thou art my pearl: I fished thee, and must keep thee. So gainsay me not, or thou wilt bring back my fever; but cry courage, and lead on; and hey for Burgundy!"

Denys gave a joyful caper. "Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne. Oh! Soyez tranquille! Cette fois il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là."

And they turned their backs on the Rhine.

As this decision crystallized, a commotion rippled across the river among the small party that had been watching the discussion. The friends had not taken many steps before a voice cut through the air from the water.

Gerard turned, seeing one of the four figures holding out a badge of office and a parchment slip. His heart sank; he was a good citizen, accustomed to obeying the voice that now bade him turn back toward Düsseldorf—the Law's domain. Denys shared none of his scruples. He was a Frenchman, despising every other nation, its laws, inhabitants, and customs alike. He was a soldier, viewing the situation through a strictly military lens: superior force opposed, a river barrier, the rear open—it was a retreat made easy. He saw at a glance that the boat still drifted in mid-stream and that no ferry lay nearer than Düsseldorf.

"I shall beat a quick retreat to that hill," he declared, "and then, being out of sight, quick step."

They sauntered off casually.

"Halt! In the bailiff's name!" cried a voice from the shore.

Denys turned and ostentatiously snapped his fingers at the bailiff, proceeding undeterred.

"Halt! In the archbishop's name!"

Denys snapped his fingers at his Grace and moved on.

"Halt! In the emperor's name!"

Denys snapped his fingers at his Majesty and continued.

Gerard watched this needless pantomime with regret. As soon as they cleared the brow of the hill, he said, "There is now but one course: we must run to Burgundy instead of walking." He set off, running the best part of a league without stopping. Denys was fairly blown, inquiring what on earth had become of Gerard's fever.

"I begin to miss it sadly," he said drily.

"I dropped it in the Rhine, I trow," was the reply.

Presently, they reached a little village. Here, Denys purchased a loaf and a huge bottle of Rhenish wine. "For," he explained, "we must sleep in some hole or corner. If we lie at an inn, we shall be taken in our beds."

This was no mere common prudence on the old soldier's part. The official network for catching lawbreakers, especially plebeians, was incredibly dense in that age, though the cooperation of the public was almost null, particularly on the Continent. Innkeepers were everywhere under close surveillance regarding their travelers, for whose acts they were even in some degree responsible, more so it would seem than for their sufferings.

Both friends were glad when the sun set, and delighted when, after a long trudge under the stars—for the moon, if memory serves, did not rise until about three in the morning—they arrived at a large barn belonging to a house some distance away. A quantity of barley had recently been thrashed; the heap of straw on one side of the floor was almost as high as the unthrashed corn on the other.

"Here be two royal beds," said Denys. "Which shall we lie on, the mow, or the straw?"

"The straw for me," said Gerard.

They sat on the heap, eating their brown bread and drinking their wine. Then Denys covered his friend in straw, heaping it high above him, leaving only a breathing hole. "Water, they say, is death to fevered men; I'll make warm water on it, anyhow."

Gerard bade him make his mind easy. "These few drops from the Rhine cannot chill me. I feel heat enough in my body now to parch a kennel, or boil a cloud if I were in one."

With this epigram, his consciousness faded so rapidly that he might truly be said to "fall asleep." Denys, lying awake for a while, heard something that made him nestle closer. Horses' hooves rang up from Düsseldorf, and the wooden barn vibrated as they rattled past, howling in a manner too well known and understood in the fifteenth century, but as unfamiliar in modern Europe as a Red Indian's war-whoop.
