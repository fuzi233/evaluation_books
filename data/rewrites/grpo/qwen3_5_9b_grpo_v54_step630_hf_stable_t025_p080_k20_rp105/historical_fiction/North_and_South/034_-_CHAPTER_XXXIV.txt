"Truth will fail thee never, never!
Though thy bark be tempest-driven,
Though each plank be rent and riven,
Truth will bear thee on for ever!"

The burden of "bearing up better than likely" pressed upon Margaret with a weight that threatened to crush her spirit. In moments of quiet, the sudden, sharp realization that she was alone in the world, bereft of her mother, would seize her throat, forcing a cry of pain despite her efforts to maintain cheerfulness before her father. Her mind was equally tormented by the silence from Frederick. The Sunday post had failed to deliver their London correspondence, and by Tuesday, the absence of a letter left her in a state of disheartened darkness. She was adrift without knowledge of his plans, a void that drove her father into a frantic restlessness. He abandoned his recent habit of lingering in one chair, pacing the room, darting out of it, and haunting the landing, opening and shutting bedroom doors with a nervous energy that tore through the house's fragile peace. Margaret found solace only in reading aloud to him, though his attention could not hold, and she felt a profound gratitude that Mr. Thornton's arrival would inevitably shift her father's focus elsewhere.

Mr. Thornton entered with a directness that spoke volumes. He approached Mr. Hale, seizing his hands and wringing them tightly, holding them for a moment in a silence that conveyed a depth of sympathy no words could match. Turning to Margaret, he found her far from "better than likely." Her stately beauty was dimmed by sleepless nights and weeping; her face bore the marks of gentle, patient sorrow, bordering on acute suffering. He had intended to greet her with the studied coldness he had practiced, yet he could not resist stepping toward her as she stood apart, rendered timid by his recent aloofness. In a voice tender enough to break her resolve, he offered the few necessary pleasantries, causing her eyes to fill with tears as she turned away to hide her emotion. She retreated to her work, sitting in a heavy, silent quiet. Mr. Thornton's heart hammered against his ribs, the memory of Outwood Lane momentarily erased. He attempted to converse with Mr. Hale, finding in the older man's presence a familiar anchor of safety and decision, a dynamic that pleased Mr. Hale deeply, as Margaret observed.

Suddenly, Dixon appeared at the door, his manner so flustered that it sent a wave of sickness through Margaret's heart. Something had happened to Frederick; she knew it with absolute certainty. Fortunately, her father and Mr. Thornton were engrossed in conversation.

"What is it, Dixon?" Margaret asked the moment she had closed the drawing-room door.

"Come this way, miss," Dixon said, ushering her into what had been Mrs. Hale's chamber, now Margaret's sanctuary since her father refused to sleep there following his wife's passing. "It's nothing, miss," he choked out, struggling for composure. "Only a police-inspector. He wishes to see you. But I dare say, it concerns nothing serious."

"Did he name—" Margaret began, her voice barely audible.

"No, miss. He merely asked if you resided here and if he might speak with you. Martha admitted him; he is currently in Master's study. I attempted to intervene, but no—it is you, miss, he requires."

Margaret remained silent until her hand rested on the study door's lock. Turning, she warned, "Ensure Papa does not descend. Mr. Thornton is with him."

The inspector was initially intimidated by the haughty demeanor she projected upon entering. There was a suppressed indignation in her gaze, restrained with such control that it radiated a superb air of disdain. She showed no surprise, no curiosity, simply waiting for the interrogation to begin. She asked no questions.

"I beg your pardon, ma'am, but my duty compels me to ask a few plain questions," the inspector stated. "A man died at the Infirmary following a fall sustained at Outwood station between five and six o'clock on Thursday evening, the twenty-sixth. Initially, the fall seemed minor, but physicians attribute the fatality to an underlying internal condition exacerbated by the deceased's habitual drinking."

Her large, dark eyes widened slightly as they locked onto his face, yet her body remained still. Her lips curved into a tighter, richer line due to the tension of her muscles, betraying a sullen defiance she did not realize she displayed. She neither flinched nor trembled, fixing him with an unblinking stare. When he paused, she encouraged him, almost sleepily, "Well—go on!"

"It is anticipated that an inquest will be held. There is slight evidence suggesting the blow, push, or scuffle that precipitated the fall was provoked by the deceased's half-intoxicated insolence toward a young lady. This lady was walking with the man who pushed the victim off the platform. This was witnessed by someone on the platform, who dismissed it as trivial at the time. Furthermore, there is reason to identify this lady as yourself. If so—"

"I was not there," Margaret replied, her expressionless eyes fixed on his face with the vacant intensity of a sleepwalker.

The inspector bowed but said nothing. The woman before him exhibited no fear, no fluttering anxiety, no desire to conclude the interview. The information he possessed was vague: a porter, rushing to catch a train, had seen a scuffle at the far end of the platform between Leonards and a gentleman accompanied by a lady, hearing no sound. Before the train gained speed, the enraged, half-drunk Leonards, swearing and cursing, had nearly knocked the porter down. The porter had not dwelled on it until the inspector unearthed his testimony. Subsequent inquiries at the station revealed that a remarkably handsome young lady and gentleman had been present around that hour; a grocer's assistant identified the lady as Miss Hale of Crampton. While certainty was lacking, the probability was high. Leonards, driven mad by rage and pain, had fled to a gin-palace, where waiters recalled him cursing himself for not utilizing the electric telegraph sooner, before leaving to seek further comfort. Overcome by pain or drink, he collapsed in the road, where police found him and transported him to the Infirmary. He never regained sufficient consciousness to provide a coherent account, though brief lucid moments led authorities to summon a magistrate. However, the magistrate found the dying man rambling, mixing names of captains and lieutenants with railway porters, his final words a curse on the "Cornish trick" that had cost him a hundred pounds.

The inspector reviewed this tapestry of vagueness and Margaret's unflinching, calm denial. She awaited his next word with a composure that seemed supreme.

"Then, madam, I have your denial that you were the lady accompanying the gentleman who struck the blow or delivered the push that caused this poor man's death?"

A sharp, searing pain lanced through Margaret's brain. *Oh God! that I knew Frederick were safe!* A deep observer might have witnessed the momentary agony flash through her gloomy eyes, like a creature trapped and tortured. Yet, the inspector, though keen, lacked the depth to perceive it fully. He was nonetheless struck by the mechanical repetition of her answer, unchanged and unmodified to address his specific query.

"I was not there," she said slowly and heavily. Throughout, she never closed her eyes or ceased that glassy, dream-like stare. His suspicions sharpened; it was as if she had forced herself into a single falsehood and been stunned into an inability to vary it.

He deliberately closed his notebook of notes and looked up. She had not moved, resembling a great Egyptian statue.

"I hope you will not deem me impertinent when I say I may need to call upon you again. I may have to summon you to the inquest to prove an alibi should my witnesses persist in testifying to your presence at this unfortunate event." He looked at her sharply. She remained perfectly quiet, her proud face showing no change of color or shadow of guilt. He thought he saw a wince, but he did not know Margaret Hale. Abashed by her genuine composure, he concluded it must be a mistake of identity.

"It is highly unlikely, ma'am, that I shall have to proceed further. I hope you will excuse me for performing my duty, even if it appears impertinent."

Margaret bowed her head as he turned toward the door. Her lips were stiff and dry; she could not muster the common words of farewell. Suddenly, she stepped forward, opened the study door, and preceded him to the house's entrance, throwing it wide for his exit. She kept her dull, fixed eyes upon him until he was safely outside. She closed the door, walked halfway into the study, then turned back, moved by some passionate impulse, and locked it from the inside.

She entered the study, paused, tottered forward, paused again, swayed for an instant, and fell prone upon the floor in a dead swoon.
