During the sweltering months of July and August, Ethne's anxieties festered until they finally forced themselves from her lips. One morning, standing bathed in sunlight at an open window of Mrs. Adair's cottage on the Salcombe estuary, she voiced her fear.

"I am afraid," she said.

Behind her, Mrs. Adair offered a quiet, unreadable smile from within the room.

"Afraid of what? That some accident befell Colonel Durrance yesterday in London?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly. "Not of that. For he is at this very moment crossing the lawn toward us."

Again, Mrs. Adair smiled, though she kept her gaze fixed on her book, allowing the amusement to play solely on her features. "I thought so," she murmured, her voice so low it barely registered in Ethne's hearing. The words failed to penetrate Ethne's mind, for as she scanned the stone-flagged terrace and the broad steps leading to the lawn, she asked abruptly, "Do you think he has any hope whatsoever of recovering his sight?"

The question caught Mrs. Adair off guard. She dismissed it lightly. "Would he travel to town so frequently to consult an oculist if he held no hope?" she countered. "Of course he hopes."

"I am afraid," Ethne insisted, turning suddenly to face her friend. "Laura, haven't you noticed how acute he has become? How quick he grows to interpret your silences, to deduce your unspoken thoughts from your gestures, to complete your sentences, to make your movements the subtext of your words? At times, I feel the very corners of my mind are laid bare to him. He reads me like a child's primer."

"Yes," admitted Mrs. Adair. "You are at a disadvantage. You no longer possess the shield of your face to hide your thoughts."

"And his eyes no longer reveal anything to me," Ethne added.

Both statements were true. While Durrance once relied on Ethne's expressive countenance and steady gray eyes to gauge her intent, his blindness had stripped her of that visual anchor. Conversely, she could no longer read his expressions. His condition had inverted their dynamic; where she once knew him perfectly, she now navigated a fog of uncertainty.

Mrs. Adair, however, was less concerned with the psychological shift and more intrigued by Ethne's sudden vulnerability. The girl, formerly noted for her quiet candor, was descending into agitation. "Then there is something you intend to conceal from him?" Mrs. Adair asked softly.

"Yes."

"Something significant?"

"Something I must conceal at all costs," Ethne exclaimed, unsure even as she spoke whether Durrance had already discovered the secret.

Stepping onto the terrace, Ethne surveyed the scene. The lawn stretched to a hedge, beyond which lay rolling fields and, further still, the chimney smoke of Colonel Durrance's house. To her left, the land dipped toward the creek, bordered by tall beeches and oaks. A gap in the tree line allowed a view of the water, where a small sailing-boat tacked lazily against the breeze.

Ethne gathered her resolve, composing her thoughts for the confrontation ahead. Yet, as she prepared to descend, she noted a stark contrast: while she hesitated, the blind man moved with absolute certainty. His cane tapped rhythmically against the grass, tracing his path with habitual precision.

As Ethne began her descent, Mrs. Adair abandoned her pretense of reading. Dropping her book, she rushed to the window, hidden by the curtains, watching with a fierce, hungry light in her eyes. A cruel satisfaction warmed her heart. *Blindness has reserved him for me,* she thought, despising Ethne's timidity. *If my chance comes—and it surely will—his dependence will be my ultimate pleasure.*

Below, Ethne and Durrance met on the lawn. Ethne appeared to plead, and Mrs. Adair, longing to eavesdrop, strained to listen.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" Ethne asked immediately upon meeting him.

"Well, what did he say?"

Durrance shrugged. "One must wait. Time alone will reveal if a cure is possible."

"But must *we* wait?" Ethne pressed. "Surely, it would be wiser..."

At that, Durrance posed a question she hadn't anticipated. "Was it Mrs. Adair who arranged for me to return to Guessens and for you to stay with her here?"

Puzzled but honest, Ethne explained her distress upon hearing of his accident and how Laura had suggested the arrangement, adding a note of desperate pleading. Durrance smiled gently. "I know that very well, Ethne. It was kind of Mrs. Adair, but it is hard on you, who wishes to return to your own country. I recall a sentence Harry Feversham spoke long ago in London, just before I departed for Egypt. He said of you: 'She is of her country and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy anywhere outside the reach of Donegal.' Remembering that, it seems selfish of me to keep you here at such a cost to you."

"I wasn't thinking of that," Ethne interjected, realizing her own selfishness. "I was merely wondering why you insist on waiting. For, of course, while we pray for your recovery, the outcome shouldn't matter."

Her voice trembled with emphasis. "It can make no difference."

Suddenly, Durrance snapped out of his reverie. "I beg your pardon, Ethne. I was thinking of Harry Feversham. There is something I wish to know. Years ago at Glenalla, you mentioned you might one day tell me what happened. Now, I would like to know. What transpired that night at Lennon House that ended our engagement and cast Feversham out?"

Silence stretched between them. Then, gently, Ethne refused. "I would rather not. It is all over. I do not wish to discuss it."

Durrance accepted this gracefully. "Very well. It might hurt you, and I wish to avoid causing pain."

"It is not out of fear of pain," Ethne clarified earnestly. "Mr. Feversham is a man I have known well, and now he is dead. The past is closed."

They walked toward the gap in the trees bordering the creek. As Ethne spoke, she glanced up and spotted the small boat that had earlier been tacking up the stream. It had grounded on the shore, its mast sticking upright. Standing near the bank was a man, looking vaguely uncertain of his footing.

"A stranger has arrived from the creek," Ethne announced, seizing the interruption as a lifeline. "He appears lost. I will guide him."

Running forward, she approached the intruder. He was a man of medium build with a short, snub nose, vacant protruding brown eyes, and a ferocious moustache. He wore a round, balding forehead beneath his hat.

"I have sailed down from Kingsbridge," he said, removing his hat. "Never having visited this region, may I ask if this is The Pool?"

"Yes," Ethne replied. "Go up the steps to the terrace to find Mrs. Adair."

"I came to see Miss Eustace."

Turning back with surprise, Ethne introduced herself. The stranger studied her in silence. "So I surmised." He twisted his moustaches before continuing. "I have had difficulty finding you, Miss Eustace. I traveled all the way to Glenalla—for nothing. Hard on a man with limited leave!"

"I am very sorry," Ethne smiled politely. "But why the trouble?"

"You have forgotten my name, no doubt," he said, his eyes lingering vacantly on her. "Though I suspect you heard it five years ago. I am Captain Willoughby."

Ethne recoiled sharply. Color drained from her cheeks, her lips tightened, and her eyes hardened into ice. She glared at him silently.

Willoughby remained unfazed, speaking with the casual forgiveness of someone absolving a social slight. "I understand your lack of welcome, Miss Eustace. None of us foresaw that you would be present when the three white feathers reached Feversham's hands."

"How do you know I was present?" she demanded.

"Feversham told me."

The cry tore from her throat—a raw, vocal thrum of the heart. It startled her as much as it surprised Willoughby. She had trained herself to excise Harry Feversham from her thoughts, to erase him from her affections, yet this sound proved her failure. Moments prior, she had spoken of him as dead; now, the reality crashed down.

"You have actually seen him?" she whispered, gazing at her stoic companion with envy. "Spoken to him? When?"

"A year ago, at Suakin. Else, why should I be here?"

The question struck Ethne like a blow. She feared the answer regardless of what it might be. Slowly, reluctantly, she nodded. "Yes."

"Why are you here?"

Willoughby produced a letter case, withdrew a tiny, soiled white feather, and held it out to her. "I have come to give you this."

Ethne shrank back. "Why?"
