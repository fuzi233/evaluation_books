"Ah," sneered Zat Arras, "to what benevolent turn of fortune do I owe the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?"

As he spoke, one of my guards removed the gag from my mouth. Yet, I offered no reply to the treacherous Jed of Zodanga. Instead, I stood silent, my gaze leveled and burning with contempt upon the man who dared stand before me.

The eyes of everyone in the chamber shifted first to me, then to Zat Arras, until a slow, crimson flush of rage spread across the traitor's face.

"You may go," he commanded the men who had brought me.

Once only his two companions, myself, and the guards remained, Zat Arras addressed me again. His voice was like ice, delivered with agonizing slowness and deliberate pauses, as if he were carefully selecting each poisonous syllage.

"John Carter," he intoned, "by the edicts of custom, by the laws of our religion, and by the verdict of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone hold the power to execute that sentence. You are entirely in my grasp; I may kill you instantly, or I may grant you freedom. Should I choose to kill you, no one would be the wiser."

He paused, letting the threat hang heavy in the air.

"If you were released in Helium for a year, per the conditions of your reprieve, there is little fear the populace would ever demand the execution of the sentence imposed upon you."

His tone sharpened.

"You may walk free within two minutes, upon one condition: Tardos Mors will never return to Helium. Neither will Mors Kajak, nor Dejah Thoris. Helium must select a new Jeddak within the year. Zat Arras shall be that Jeddak. Say that you will espouse my cause. This is the price of your freedom. I am finished."

I knew the cruelty inherent in Zat Arras' heart; he possessed the capacity to destroy me utterly. If I were dead, I doubted he would hesitate to seize the throne of Helium. Free, however, I could pursue the search for Dejah Thoris. Dead, my brave comrades might fail to carry out our plans. Thus, by refusing his offer, I risked not only failing to prevent his rise but actively sealing Dejah Thoris' fate—condemning her, through my defiance, to the horrors of the Arena of Issus.

For a fleeting moment, confusion clouded my mind. But it lasted only an instant. The proud daughter of a thousand Jeddaks would choose death rather than a dishonorable alliance, and John Carter could do no less for Helium than his Princess would.

Turning sharply to Zat Arras, I declared, "There can be no alliance between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is dead."

Zat Arras merely shrugged.

"It will not be long, John Carter," he replied smoothly, "before your opinions matter even to yourself. Make the best of them while you can. Zat Arras will allow you time to reflect upon this magnanimous offer. Tonight, you will descend into the silence and darkness of the pits, knowing that should you fail to agree to the alternative within a reasonable time, you shall never emerge from the dark and silence again. Nor will you know the precise moment when a hand reaches out through the gloom with a keen dagger to steal your last chance at warmth, freedom, and joy."

Clapping his hands, Zat Arras dismissed the guards.

"To the pits!" he barked.

Four men escorted me from the chamber, a radium hand-light cutting through the gloom, leading me through seemingly endless tunnels downward, ever deeper beneath the city of Helium.

They halted in a spacious cavern. Rings were embedded in the rocky walls, from which chains hung. At the ends of many chains lay human skeletons. One of the guards kicked a skeleton aside, unlocked the massive padlock securing a chain around what had once been a human ankle, and snapped the iron band tight around my own leg. Then they departed, extinguishing the light behind them.

Utter darkness swallowed me whole. For a few moments, I heard the clanking of armor fading away, until silence became absolute, matching the depth of the blackness. I was alone with my gruesome companions—the bones of the dead, whose fate served as a grim index of my own.

Hours seemed to pass in the void. Finally, I sank to the hard stone floor, leaning my head against the cold wall, and slept.

When I awoke, a young man stood before me. In one hand he held a light; in the other, a vessel containing a gruel-like mixture—the standard prison fare of Barsoom.

"Zat Arras sends his greetings," the youth said, "and commands me to inform you that though he knows fully of the plot to make you Jeddak of Helium, he remains unwilling to withdraw his offer. To gain your freedom, you need only instruct me to tell Zat Arras that you accept his terms."

I shook my head. The youth said nothing more, placed the food on the floor beside me, and retreated up the corridor.

For many days, twice daily, this youth returned with food and the same greeting from Zat Arras. I attempted to converse with him on various topics, but he refused to speak, forcing me to desist.

Months passed as I devised ways to inform Carthoris of my location. I scraped frantically at a single link of my massive chain, hoping to wear it through so I could follow the youth back to the surface and break free. Beside myself with anxiety regarding the expedition to rescue Dejah Thoris, I feared Carthoris might also be a prisoner in Zat Arras' pits.

Knowing that Zat Arras' spy had likely overheard our discussions regarding the selection of a new Jeddak—and indeed, we had barely spoken of the rescue plan minutes before—he surely knew of our intentions. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar might already be victims of assassination or captivity.

Determined to make one final attempt to learn something, I adopted a new strategy when the youth visited. Noting his handsome features, his age similar to Carthoris', and the stark contrast between his shabby clothes and his noble bearing, I began my negotiation.

"You have been very kind during my imprisonment," I told him gently. "Since I feel I have but a short time left, I wish, ere it is too late, to show my profound gratitude for making my confinement bearable. You have brought my food promptly, ensuring it was pure and sufficient. You have never abused my defenseless state with insults or torture. Your uniform courtesy is what moves me to offer you a token of thanks.

"In the guard-room of my palace are many fine trappings. Go there and select the harness that pleases you most—it shall be yours. All I ask is that you wear it, so I may know my wish is granted. Promise me you will do it."

The boy's eyes lit with pleasure. He glanced from his rusted gear to the magnificence of my own. After a moment of deep thought, he spoke.

"And if I went to the Palace of the Prince of Helium with such a demand, they would laugh at me and likely cast me headlong into the avenue. No, it cannot be, though I thank you. If Zat Arras even suspected I contemplated such a thing, he would have my heart cut out."

"There can be no harm in it, my boy," I urged. "Go to my palace by night with a note from me to Carthoris, my son. Read it first to ensure it contains nothing harmful to Zat Arras. My son will be discreet; only we three need know. It is simple and harmless."

He pondered silently again.

"And there is a jeweled short-sword I took from a northern Jeddak. Once you obtain the harness, bring that sword to Carthoris as well. With it and the harness, you will be the most splendidly accoutered warrior in all Zodanga. Bring writing materials next time, and within hours you shall be dressed befitting your birth."

Silently, he turned and left. I fretted for hours, unsure of his decision.

If he accepted the message, Carthoris lived and was free. If he returned wearing the harness and sword, Carthoris had received my note and knew I lived. A Zodangan messenger explained my captivity perfectly.

With excited anticipation, I awaited his next visit. As he placed the food down, he also set out writing materials. My heart leaped. Feigning surprise, I quickly penned a brief order to Carthoris: *Deliver to Parthak a harness of his choice and the short-sword described.* I laid the note open. Parthak picked it up and, without a word, vanished.

I estimated I had been in the pits for three hundred days. If Dejah Thoris was to be saved, it must be done quickly; those chosen by Issus lived but a year.

When footsteps approached, I eagerly awaited to see if Parthak wore the gear. My chagrin was immense when I saw the bearer was not Parthak.

"What has become of Parthak?" I demanded. The fellow ignored me, deposited the food, and left.
