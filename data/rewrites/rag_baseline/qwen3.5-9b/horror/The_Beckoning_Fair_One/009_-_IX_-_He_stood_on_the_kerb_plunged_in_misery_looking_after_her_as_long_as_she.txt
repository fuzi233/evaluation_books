IX He stood upon the curb, submerged in a mire of misery, tracking her retreating figure until the silhouette dissolved into the twilight. Yet, the moment she vanished, a peculiar alchemy occurred within him; the crushing weight lifted, replaced by a sudden, buoyant clarity. She had granted him his liberty—a truth undeniable, even if he had never truly forfeited it prior. Now, however, was not the season for semantic nitpicking. He was free to act, and the path forward blazed with stark illumination. Lightness bloomed swiftly, swelling into a jubilant celebration of autonomy. Before he had traversed half the distance home, the blueprint for his next move crystallized in his mind. The vicar of the parish, where his dwelling stood, resided merely ten minutes' walk from the square. Oleron turned his steps toward the rectory. He required every scrap of intelligence regarding the ancient edifice—the insurance markings, the sagging "To Let" signs—and the vicar was the most probable source of such lore. With this preliminary hurdle cleared, Oleron chuckled, a sound vibrating with anticipation; things were poised to unfold.

Yet, the harvest yielded less than he had anticipated. The house, the vicar noted, was ancient—a fact requiring no priest to confirm—but it bore a reputation for haunting. Oleron pricked his ears at this, though he knew few antiquities escaped such whispers among the credulous. The vicar, seemingly troubled by the spiritual vacuity of the modern age, offered a soothing demeanor, carefully calibrating his words to avoid offense. Oleron smiled, perceiving the tact. "You may leave my nerves out of the equation," he remarked smoothly. "For how long has the property lain vacant?" "Twelve years, I'd wager," the vicar replied. "And the final tenant? Did you know him—or her?" A subtle tingle ran through Oleron's nerves as he presented the vicar with a binary choice of gender. "Him," the vicar confirmed. "A man. If memory serves, his name was Madley; an artist. A profound recluse, rarely venturing beyond the premises..." The vicar paused, then burst into a candid confession. "Since you seek this knowledge, and since truth ought to supersede garbled rumors, I shall speak plainly: Madley died here under circumstances of considerable peculiarity. The post-mortem revealed an entirely empty stomach, despite the presence of funds. His frame was utterly consumed. Suicide was considered, yet one must concede that deliberate starvation represents an exceptionally rare modality of self-destruction. Consequently, an open verdict was rendered."

"Aha!" Oleron observed. "Do you possess a comprehensive chronicle of this parish?" "None exists; only fragments. I maintain private notes on the ecclesiastical history, registers, and the like, which I would gladly exhibit if your interest warrants it. However, the parish is vast, I am attended by but a single curate, and my leisure is correspondingly limited..." The discussion devolved into the geography of the diocese and the vicar's scarcity of time. Oleron expressed gratitude, took his leave, and ambled slowly homeward. His pace was deliberate; twice within a stone's throw of the gate, he veered away, looping a circuit of twenty minutes. He faced a perilous undertaking now, demanding supreme mental focus: to recalibrate his consciousness into a state of unpreoccupied receptivity, mirroring the morning when, having completed his removal, he had seated himself to draft the sixteenth chapter of the first *Romilly*. Could he resurrect that initial impression, he hoped to extract far greater potency from the site. Previously, his mind had been cluttered with intellectual debris; before the place could reveal itself, it had to surmount the inertia of those dreary chapters. No results had emerged. The process had been one of slow saturation, a gradual filling to the brim. But now, he was light, unencumbered, liberated at last from both the *Romilly* and its prototype. Now, for the new unknown: coy, jealous, bewitching, the Beckoning Fair One!

At half-past two in the afternoon, he inserted his key into the Yale lock, stepped inside, and sealed the door behind him. His fantastical experiment succeeded instantly and astonishingly. He could have screamed in triumph as he entered the room; it felt as though he had escaped *into* it. Once more, as in the golden days when his writing possessed a daily freshness, wonder, and promise, he sensed a renewed ease, mastery, exhilaration, and release. The air of the place seemed charged with extra oxygen; his specific gravity appeared altered, his tread less ponderous. The flowers in the bowls, the elegant proportions of the meadowsweet-colored panels and moldings, the polished floor, and the lofty, faintly starred ceiling seemed to laugh in welcome. Oleron laughed back, speaking aloud. "Oh, you're pretty, pretty!" he cooed, flattering the space. Then he collapsed onto his couch. He spent that afternoon as a convalescent awaiting a cherished guest—in a delicious vacancy, smiling intermittently as if in a dream, ever raising drowsy, contented eyes to his seductive surroundings. Thus he lay until darkness descended, bringing with it the nocturnal symphony of the old house.

But if he awaited a specific eventuality, he waited in vain. Similarly futile was his vigil the following day, maintaining, albeit with diminishing ease, that sensitized-plate-like state of his mind. Nothing occurred to register an impression. Whatever entity he so patiently courted seemed both shy and exacting. Then, on the third day, comprehension dawned. A look of gentle drollery and cunning softened his features, and he chuckled. "Oho, oho! ... Well, if the wind blows from *that* quarter, we shall discover what else remains to be done. What is there, now? ... No, I shall not summon Elsie; we require no wheel to crush the butterfly upon. We shall not resort to such extremes, my butterfly...." Standing in contemplation, thumbing his lean jaw, he looked askance. Suddenly, he crossed to the hall, retrieved his hat, and departed. "My lady is coquettish, is she? Well, let us observe the efficacy of a little neglect," he chuckled, descending the stairs. He sought a railway station, boarded a train, and spent the remainder of the day immersed in the countryside. Ah, yes: Oleron believed *he* was the man destined to negotiate with Fair Ones who beckoned, invited, and then retreated into shyness and hesitation! He did not return until past eleven that night.

"Now," he murmured, pacing the alley and feeling for his keys, "my Fair Beckoner!" Inside his flat, he was perfectly composed, deliberately calm, exceedingly cautious lest he betray himself. To signal an intention to retire immediately, he lit but a single candle; as he set out on his nightly rounds, he feigned a yawn. First, he entered the kitchen. A full moon hung overhead, casting a lozenge of moonlight, almost peacock-blue in contrast to the candle's frame, upon the floor. The window stood uncurtained, allowing him to see the reflection of the candle and, faintly, his own visage as he moved. The door of the powder-closet stood slightly ajar; he closed it before sitting to remove his boots on the chair cushioned with a folded harp-bag. Passing from the kitchen to the bathroom, another slant of blue moonlight sliced across the windowsill, resting upon the pipes. He visited his seldom-used study, pausing to gaze at the silvered roofs across the square. Then, walking silently through his sitting-room, his stockinged feet making no sound, he entered his bedroom and deposited the candle upon the chest of drawers. Throughout this ritual, his face wore no expression save that of fatigue. He had never been wiler nor more alert. His small bedroom fireplace stood opposite the chest of drawers where the mirror rested, while his bed and the window claimed the remaining sides of the room.

Oleron drew down the blind, shed his coat, and stooped to retrieve his slippers from beneath the bed. He could offer no rational justification, yet he never doubted for an instant that the manifestation, withheld for two days, hovered imminently. Nor, though he could not conjecture its form, did he feel fear. Startling or surprising it might be; he was prepared for that. But that was all; his scale of sensation had been depressed. His hand moved hither and thither beneath the bed in search of his footwear. Yet, despite his caution, method, and preparedness, his heart gave a leap and a pause that was almost horrific. His hand had grasped the slippers, but he remained on his knees; absent this circumstance, he would have toppled. The bed was low; the groping necessitated a turn of his head to one side; he maintained the posture until he partially regained his composure. When he eventually rose, a drop of blood stained his lower lip where he had bitten it, and his watch had dislodged from his waistcoat pocket, dangling by its short leather guard.

Then, before the watch ceased its tiny oscillations, he was himself again. Upon his mantelpiece stood a portrait of his grandmother. He positioned himself before it, utilizing the glass to view the steady flame of the candle burning behind him on the chest of drawers.
