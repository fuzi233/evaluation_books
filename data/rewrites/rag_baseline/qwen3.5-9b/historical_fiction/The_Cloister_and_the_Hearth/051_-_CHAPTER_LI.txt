To truly measure the weight of this day, one must first understand the architecture of Margaret's solitude. It was a paradox of sweetness and bitterness, a day that simultaneously gifted her a soulmate and delivered the crushing verdict of abandonment. For the first time, the truth settled upon her like a physical blow: Gerard was alone in that strange, hostile land. The thought shattered any lingering hope she might have harbored; he would not be returning. Denys, however, served as a balm to this fractured spirit. He claimed her as his *she-comrade*, flooding her days with formulas of cheer and hilarities that momentarily suspended the gravity of her grief. She doted on him, treating him with a fierce protectiveness she extended equally to Martin, refusing to let either consume a single meal outside her sanctuary. With a feebly authoritative voice, she policed their tongues, forbidding the intrusion of vulgarity. "It spoils you, Denys," she chided, her tone shifting from affectionate to stern. "Good lack, to hear such ugly words emerge from so comely a head! Forbear, or I shall be angry; so be civil." Thus, Denys adopted a posture of exaggerated propriety, creating a ludicrous tableau where his innate politeness warred with the acquired ruffianism of his upbringing. Yet, as the proverb suggests, when it does not rain, it pours. Other forces soon sought to claim her friendship.

Margaret had written to Margaret Van Eyck, a letter steeped in humility and regret. She confessed that she knew she was no longer the favored daughter-in-law she once was, promising to keep her distance, yet unable to erase the memory of her benefactress's past kindness. She recounted the myriad battles she had fought merely to survive, concluding with a desperate plea: that her current residence remain hidden, especially from Gerard's people. "I do hate them," she wrote, her pen trembling with the residue of trauma. "They drove him from me. And even when he was gone, their hearts turned not to me as they would have, had they repented their cruelty to him." The Van Eyck household was thrown into perplexity. Finally, they made Reicht a confidante, allowing the secret to travel through him like fluid through a cylinder until it reached Catherine. "Ay, and is she turned that bitter against us?" the good woman asked, her voice thick with sorrow. "She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for not rushing into her arms. Nevertheless, it is a blessing she is alive and no farther away than Rotterdam." Meanwhile, the English princess, now the Countess Charolois, made a stately progress through the northern duchy, accompanied by her stepdaughter, the young heiress of Burgundy, Marie de Bourgogne. The old Duke, the most magnificent prince in Europe, unleashed his full splendor. Troops of dazzling knights and bevies of fair ladies, gorgeously attired, trailed behind the princesses, while minstrels, jongleurs, bards, musicians, actors, and tumblers filled the streets. Fencing matches, dances, and unbridled joy erupted in every town they touched. Richart invited all his people to gather in Rotterdam to witness the pageant.

Days passed in Rotterdam before fate intervened. Denys encountered Catherine accidentally in the street. After a warm exchange of greetings, he urged her to rejoice, boasting that he had finally located the *she-comrade* and crowed with triumph. But Catherine quickly cooled his ardor, reminding him that he would have found her far sooner by remaining quietly at Tergou rather than vagabondizing across Holland. "And being found, what the better are we?" she asked, her voice hardening. "Her heart is set dead against us now." "Oh, let that flea stick," Denys retorted impulsively. "Come with me to her house." No, she refused to enter a place where she expected an ill welcome. "Those who come unbidden sit unseated." Instead, she proposed that Denys act as mediator, bringing the estranged parties to a reconciliation. He accepted the office immediately, proceeding with great pomp and confident swagger. He trotted to Margaret's door and announced, "*She-comrade*, I met today a friend of yours." "You looked into Rotterdam, then, and saw yourself?" Margaret replied, her tone sharp. "Nay, 'twas a female, and one that seeks your regard; 'twas Catherine, Gerard's mother." "Oh, was it?" Margaret said, her expression closing off. "Then you may tell her she comes too late. There was a time I longed and longed for her; but she held aloof in my hour of most need. So now, we will be as we have been." Denys attempted to shake this resolution, coaxing her gently, but she remained bitter and sullen, impervious to persuasion. Then he scolded her sharply. At that, she dissolved into hysterics. Terrified by the result of his own eloquence and caught off guard, he allowed himself to be entrapped into a solemn promise never to revisit the subject. He returned to Catherine crestfallen and relayed the rejection. She flared with anger, informing the family how her overtures had been received. In response, the family flared back; the rift widened into a feud that burned fiercer each passing day. Only little Kate dared make excuses for Margaret.

The following day, another visitor arrived. Margaret found her household enslaved and degraded: Martin buried to his elbows in soapsuds, Denys ironing with clumsy ineptitude, and herself plaiting ruffs, though her eyes retained the sharp vigilance of a mistress surveying her raw levies. Into this scene stepped an old man, venerable at first glance but, upon closer inspection, keen and wizened. "Ah," cried Margaret, her reaction instantaneous. Swiftly, she turned her back on him, hiding her face with invincible repugnance. "Oh, that man! That man!" "Nay, fear me not," said Ghysbrecht calmly. "I come on a friend's errand. I bring you a letter from foreign parts." "Mock me not, old man," she retorted, turning slowly around. "Nay, see;" and he held out an enormous envelope. Margaret darted forward, snatching it with trembling hands and glistening eyes. It bore Gerard's handwriting. "Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this," she whispered, forgiveness washing over her features. "I forgive you all the ill you ever wrought me." Pressing the letter to her bosom with one hand, she glided swiftly from the room, leaving Ghysbrecht behind. He departed without delay, casting a final scowl at Martha. Margaret was left alone with her letter for hours, the weight of the world suspended in those few folded pages.
