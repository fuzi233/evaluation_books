Sometimes, the struggle against one’s deepest nature seems futile—like a storm battling the sea, with no end in sight. Yet even such efforts leave their mark. They nudge actions toward reason, even when feeling resists; they shape lives into something more balanced, quieter, more composed on the surface. And it is only the surface that most eyes see. What lies beneath—the hidden currents of sorrow, longing, and quiet prayer—belongs not to human judgment but to God. Man, weak as you are, cannot fathom your soul’s depths. But He can. Bring your secrets to Him. Show Him the pain You carry, the wounds You’ve endured, the questions You cannot answer. Kneel before Him, and ask for light in darkness, strength in weakness, patience in suffering. For though the waiting may be long, though the healing may come in ways you never dreamed, it will come. The waters will stir. The messenger will descend—not in the form you hoped, perhaps, nor at the moment you prayed for—but come He will. And those who wait by the pool, weeping through years of silence, will finally see the dawn.

Heaven’s time moves slowly. To mortal eyes, the flight of angels seems endless. Their journeys may span ages. One cycle of departure and return might embrace generations. Dust rises, breathes briefly, suffers, and returns to dust—forgotten again and again—while divine messengers circle far above. To many broken hearts, the first and only visitor is Azrael, the angel of death. But hope remains.

The next morning, I tried to rise. As I dressed, sipping cold water from the carafe on my washstand to steady my trembling hands, Mrs. Bretton entered. “Here is an absurdity!” she declared, her voice sharp and brisk. But she didn’t linger on words. In two minutes, with her usual decisive energy—once so familiar in how she managed her son, whom he often resisted—she had me back in bed. “There you stay till afternoon,” she said firmly. “My boy left strict orders, and I assure you, my son is master. You’ll have breakfast soon.”

And soon she brought it herself—her own strong hands carrying the tray, refusing to leave it to servants. She sat beside me while I ate. Not everyone, even among our closest friends, brings comfort in sickness. Some make us feel watched, exposed, burdened. But Mrs. Bretton was different. Her presence was like sunlight in a dim room. Her black eyes sparkled, her cheeks glowed with health, her hands moved with purpose. Her confidence, her clear mind, her quiet strength—it all lifted me. She was like a breath of clean air. Her son called her “the old lady,” yet she carried the vigor of youth. I marveled at how twenty-five years still seemed to live in her.

“I’d bring my work here,” she said, taking the empty teacup from me, “and sit with you all day—if only John Graham hadn’t forbidden it. ‘Now, mamma,’ he said, ‘don’t wear out your goddaughter with gossip.’ He insisted I stay in my own quarters and spare you my company. He says, Lucy, he thinks you had a nervous fever—judging by your look. Is that true?”

I hesitated. “I’m not sure what I had,” I replied. “But I suffered deeply—especially within.” I did not wish to speak further. The details belonged to a part of my life I could never share with her. To open up would be to invite her into a world she could not understand—a world of shadows and solitude. The difference between us could be likened to a stately ship sailing calm seas, crewed and guided, and a lifeboat resting dry in a dark shed—only launched when storms rage and danger calls. My godmother had never been cast upon such a sea. Her crew wouldn’t believe it. So the half-drowned sailor keeps his story to himself.

She left, and I lay back, content. It was kind of Graham to remember me before leaving. The day passed quietly, but the thought of evening made it bearable. I felt weak, and rest was welcome. After the morning’s restless energy—the invisible tasks, the sense of duty—afternoon settled like a soft blanket. The house grew still. The maid’s steps faded. And I drifted into a dreamy state, not unpleasant.

My small room seemed transformed—a cave beneath the sea. The colors were pale: white and green, like foam and deep water. The cornice bore shell-like ornaments; dolphins curved in the ceiling angles. Even the red satin pincushion looked like coral. The dark glass might have held a mermaid’s reflection. When I closed my eyes, I heard the wind subside—like a swell retreating from a rock. Far off, the tide withdrew from a distant shore. A world above, high and bright, sent down only murmurs and lullabies.

Evening came. Martha brought a light. With her help, I dressed quickly. Stronger now than in the morning, I walked unaided to the blue saloon.

Dr. John had finished his rounds earlier than usual. His figure stood in the window recess opposite the door, reading a newspaper by the fading light. The fire burned brightly, but the lamp remained unlit. Tea had not yet arrived. Mrs. Bretton, after a full day outdoors, lay half-reclined in her chair, asleep.

He saw me enter and stepped forward, moving carefully so as not to wake her. His voice was low, gentle—always smooth, never harsh. “This is a quiet little château,” he said, inviting me to sit near the casement. “Have you noticed it on your walks? From the road, it’s not visible. Just beyond the Porte de Crécy, you turn down a lane that becomes an avenue—through meadow and trees—to this very door. It’s not modern, but built in the old style of the Basse-Ville. More manoir than château. They call it ‘La Terrasse’ because its front rises from a broad grassy walk, with steps leading down to the avenue. See there—the moon is rising. She looks well through the trees.”

Where doesn’t the moon look well? Every scene, every landscape, is hallowed by her light. Now, she rose above a nearby bank—rosy at first, then flaming, then clearing to gold. In moments, she floated pure and bright into the calm sky.

Did the moonlight soften Dr. Bretton? Did it touch him with romance? I think it did. Though he was not given to sentiment, he sighed softly—quietly, to himself. No need to guess why. I knew it was beauty that stirred him—and Ginevra.

That knowledge pressed upon me. Perhaps it was my duty to name her. He was ready—I saw it in his face. A wealth of thoughts, questions, emotions waited behind his restraint. To spare him the awkwardness of beginning, I could simply speak her name. Love would then flow freely.

I had almost found the right words: “You know Miss Fanshawe has gone on tour with the Cholmondeleys…” But before I could speak, he shifted the subject.

“The first thing this morning,” he said, turning from the moon and sitting down, “I went to Rue Fossette and told the cuisinière you were safe. Do you know she hadn’t realized you were missing? She thought you were still in the dormitory. How carefully you must have been tended!”

“Oh, that’s easy to imagine,” I replied. “Goton could do nothing but bring me tisane and bread. I refused them so often that she gave up coming daily. Only once a day at noon to make my bed. But I believe she’s kind-hearted—would have cooked me mutton chops if I’d eaten them.”

“What did Madame Beck mean by leaving you alone?”

“She couldn’t foresee I’d fall ill.”

“Your nerves took a heavy toll?”

“I’m not sure what my nervous system is, but I felt utterly drained.”

“That makes treatment difficult,” he said. “Medicine can’t give good spirits. Hypochondria stands at the threshold—medicine sees the torture chamber but can do little. You need cheerful company. You should be with people. Exercise, too.”

I nodded. His advice sounded sensible, safe, conventional.

“Miss Snowe,” he continued, shifting gears, “may I ask—what is your religion? Are you Catholic?”

I looked up, surprised. “Catholic? No! Why would you think that?”

“The way you were handed over to me last night made me wonder.”

“Handed over? I don’t recall. I still don’t know how I came to be here.”

“Well,” he began, “under strange circumstances. Yesterday, I was attending a critical case—a rare illness, uncertain treatment. I saw a similar case in Paris, but that won’t interest you. Finally, the patient’s worst symptoms eased, and I left. My shortest route took me through the Basse-Ville. It was a wild, wet night. As I rode past an old church belonging to the Béguines, I saw a lamp burning over the entrance. A priest stood there, lifting someone in his arms. The light revealed his face—I recognized him. A man I’ve met at many sickbeds, rich and poor alike. A good man, better than most. Our eyes met. He called me to stop. He was carrying a woman—fainting or dying.”

“‘This is one of your countrywomen,’ he said. ‘Save her, if she’s not dead.’”

I was examined—unconscious, bloodless, nearly cold. “Who is she?” I asked.

He told a curious tale. That evening, you had gone to confession. Your appearance—exhausted, suffering—combined with what you’d said—”

“Things I said? I wonder what they were!”

“Awful crimes, no doubt,” he said, “but he couldn’t tell me. The seal of confession silenced him. Still, your confessions didn’t make him your enemy. On the contrary—he was so moved, so sorry you were out alone on such a night, that he felt it a Christian duty to follow you, unseen, until you reached home. Perhaps, unconsciously, he also wanted to learn where you lived—did you tell him?”

“No,” I said. “I avoided any hint. And as for my confession… Dr. John, you’ll think me mad, but I couldn’t help it. It was my ‘nervous system,’ as you say. I couldn’t express it—but my days and nights were unbearable. A terrible loneliness gnawed at me. A force inside me needed release—like the blood flowing through the heart, which, if blocked, seeks another way out. I needed companionship, friendship, counsel. I found none in my rooms. So I went to church. I didn’t confess sins—I had none. My life hasn’t been dramatic enough for that. All I poured out was despair.”

“Lucy,” he said gently, “you should travel—for six months. Your calm nature is becoming excitable. Confound Madame Beck! Does that little widow have no compassion, to leave her best teacher to suffer alone?”

“It wasn’t her fault,” I said. “No one’s fault. I won’t blame anyone.”

“Then who is to blame?”

“Me,” I said. “And Fate.”

“You must take better care,” he smiled, perhaps at my grammar.

“Change of air—change of scene,” he added. “Now, about our earlier question—Père Silas, despite his skill—still knows nothing. You didn’t return to Rue Fossette. Your wanderings—there must have been fever—”

“No,” I corrected. “The fever came later. Don’t pretend I was delirious. I was perfectly aware.”

“Good. Then you were as collected as I am now. You wandered in the opposite direction. Near the Béguinage, caught in the storm and darkness, you fainted. The priest found you. I followed. Together, we got a fiacre and brought you here. Père Silas, though old, insisted on carrying you upstairs himself. He would have stayed until you revived. I had to leave—my other patient was dying. Extreme unction couldn’t wait. We left you in Martha’s care. Now—will you convert?”

“No,” I said, smiling. “And don’t let Père Silas know where I live. He’ll try to convert me. But thank him for me. If I ever grow wealthy, I’ll send money for his charities. Look—your mother wakes. Ring for tea.”

He did. Mrs. Bretton sat up, startled, indignant at having slept. “I didn’t sleep!” she protested. “Only a momentary doze!”

“Hushaby, mamma,” he teased. “Sleep again. You look like innocence itself.”

“My slumbers? What nonsense! I never sleep by day.”

“Exactly—a seraph’s lapse, a fairy’s dream. Mamma, you always remind me of Titania.”

“That’s because you’re so like Bottom.”

“Miss Snowe—have you ever heard anything like mamma’s wit? She’s sprightly for her size and age.”

“Keep your compliments to yourself,” she snapped. “And don’t ignore your own size. It’s increasing. Lucy, does he look like an incipient John Bull? He used to be slender as an eel. Now, I see a heavy dragoon—beef-eater tendencies. Graham, take notice! If you grow fat, I disown you.”

“As if you could disown yourself! I’m indispensable to the old lady’s happiness. Without my six feet of iniquity, she’d pine in green melancholy. It keeps her lively—it maintains the ferment of her spirit.”

They stood on either side of the fireplace, arguing playfully. Their words weren’t tender, but their eyes spoke volumes. The greatest treasure of Mrs. Bretton’s life lived in her son’s heart. And he—though bound by love for her—had another passion, newer, stronger, claiming his soul. Ginevra. Ginevra. Did Mrs. Bretton know? Would she approve?

I couldn’t tell. But I suspected—if she saw Ginevra’s games, her coldness and coyness, her cruel teasing and sudden kindnesses—if she knew how she had hurt Graham, how she had humiliated him, preferred others over him—then she would call her foolish, or wicked, or both. I thought so too.

That second evening passed more sweetly than the first. We talked easily, without returning to old sorrows. Friendship deepened. I felt more at ease, more at home. That night, instead of crying myself to sleep, I slipped into dreams along a path lined with peaceful thoughts.
