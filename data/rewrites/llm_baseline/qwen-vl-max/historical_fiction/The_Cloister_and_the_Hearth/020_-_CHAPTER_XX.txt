The courage of ordinary men, like their talents, is confined to narrow limits. Push them just an inch beyond that, and they falter. Martin’s courage was unshakable within its bounds. He had faced danger many times in his rough life—dangers he knew well—and met them with a grim resolve, almost without fear. But never before had he been hunted by a bloodhound, nor had he seen such relentless instinct defeated by human wit. Now, the unknown combined with a sense of the uncanny to shake his spirit. After walking only a few steps, he leaned heavily on his bow, and strength and hope drained from him.

Gerard, who saw the peril as distant and therefore less immediate, urged him forward. “What good will it do?” Martin said wearily. “If we escape the wood, we die cheaply. But here, close at hand, I know a place where we may die dearly.”

“Alas, good Martin,” Gerard replied, “do not despair so soon. There must be some way out.”

“Oh, Martin!” Margaret pleaded, her voice trembling. “What if we split up? Only Gerard’s life is forfeit. Can’t we draw the pursuit upon us two and let him go free?”

“Girl,” Martin said harshly, “you don’t understand the nature of a bloodhound. It does not track one man or another—it tracks blood. I swear by my life they’ve led it to where Ghysbrecht fell. From the dead man’s blood, it will follow straight to the man who shed it. That cursed hound will find him, even if Gerard ran through an army or swam the Meuse.”

He sank again upon his bow, head bowed, defeated.

Then came the hound’s deep, resonant bay—a sound more melodious than any horn ever blew in Crete, Sparta, or Thessaly. Yet beauty could be deadly. The boa-constrictor’s eye, lovely though it is, holds a spell over its prey. No crown holds a gem so rich: a ruby glowing with emerald light, ever shifting. And yet the deer who sees it freezes, helpless, awaiting death. So too did this sweet, mellow cry strike Martin—fascinating, paralyzing, draining his will.

Gerard was no better. Martin’s words had struck him hard. He had struck down a man, spilled his blood—and now, through that very blood, the four-footed avenger of justice pursued him. Was this not divine judgment?

While the men faltered, Margaret’s mind raced. Her beloved was in danger. “Give me your knife,” she said to Martin.

He handed it to her. “But what use will it be in your hands?”

She stepped behind Gerard and, swiftly, drew the blade across her arm. Blood welled freely. She stooped, smearing her hose and shoes with it, letting the stream run while she worked—so quietly, so cleverly, that neither man noticed. Then she seized Gerard’s arm.

“Come,” she said firmly, “be a man! Let this end. Take us to a thick place where numbers won’t help our enemies.”

“I’m going,” Martin muttered, sullenly. “Hurry means nothing—we can’t outrun the hound, and the spot is near.” He turned left and led the way, like a man marching to execution.

Soon they reached a dense hazel thicket—just like the one that had sheltered them earlier. “Here,” Martin said, “it’s barely a furlong wide, but it will serve.”

“What are we to do?”

“Get through it, wait on the other side. When they come straggling through, shoot three, knock two down, and the rest will kill us.”

“That’s all you can think of?” Gerard asked.

“That’s all.”

“Then, Martin Wittenhaagen,” Gerard said, “I take the lead. You’ve lost your wits. Come—can you obey a younger man than I?”

“Oh, yes, Martin,” Margaret cried, “don’t defy Gerard! He knows more than his years.”

Martin grumbled but gave in. “Do as I do,” Gerard said, and drawing his great knife, cut low hazel shoots at every step, twisting them upright behind him among the standing branches. Martin followed suit, but with dull resignation.

As they struggled through most of the coppice, the hound’s bay grew closer—less musical, deeper, fiercer. Margaret trembled. Martin dropped to his stomach and listened.

“I hear horse’s hooves.”

“No,” Gerard said. “It’s a mule. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive—no other would pursue me so fiercely.”

“Never strike your enemy unless you mean to kill him,” Martin muttered.

“I’ll hit harder this time—if Heaven gives me the chance,” Gerard said.

At last they emerged into open woodland. Trees stood far apart, offering no escape. And now, mingled with the hound’s cry, came a chorus of voices—hooping, shouting.

“The whole village is after us,” Martin said.

“I care not,” Gerard replied. “Listen—Martin, I’ve made the path easy for the dog, but rough for the men. We’ll face them one at a time. The hound will reach us first. As soon as it comes out, we kill it.”

“The hound? There are more than one.”

“I hear only one.”

“Ay,” Martin said, “one barks, the others run silent. But if the lead hound loses the scent, another will give tongue. There will be at least two—perhaps devils in dog-skins.”

“Then we kill two instead of one. The moment they’re dead, back into the coppice and retreat.”

“That’s a good thought, Gerard,” Martin said, lifting his spirits.

“Hush—the men are in the wood.”

Gerard whispered orders. “You, stand by the edge of the coppice, in the ditch—with your bow. I’ll go to that oak tree, hide behind it. The dogs will follow me. As they emerge, shoot as many as you can. The rest I’ll brain when they round the tree.”

Martin’s eyes lit with fire. They took their positions.

The shouts drew nearer. Rustling leaves, snapping twigs. Then, once—twice—the hound gave a single, chilling bay. The moment felt endless. Every breath was a battle. Death approached, heralded by that mournful cry.

A trembling hand touched Gerard’s shoulder. He jumped—tense, coiled like a spring.

“Martin says—if we must part, make for the high ash-tree we came in by.”

“Yes! Yes! But go back—please, for Heaven’s sake—don’t come here, out in the open!”

She turned to run toward Martin—but before she could reach him, a massive hound burst from the thicket, standing tall for a moment. Margaret cowered. But the beast ignored her. To it, scent was sight. It lowered its nose, then—suddenly, with a terrible roar—lunged straight at Gerard’s tree. A moment later, it lay dead, impaled by an arrow that sang from Martin’s bow.

At once, another hound appeared, sniffing the corpse. Gerard charged. But before he could swing his cudgel, a flash of white lightning struck—his own weapon, flung like a dart. The hound collapsed, wounded but not dead, howling in agony.

Gerard had no time to finish it—the coppice rustled violently, alive with movement. He pointed wildly at Martin, signaling to retreat, then darted right, crawling into the thickets just as three men burst out.

These were ahead of the others; the rest followed at intervals. Gerard crept back on all fours. Instinct guided Martin and Margaret to do the same—along their line of flight. Within yards, pursuers and fugitives passed each other, unseen.

A cry rang out—then chaos: voices rising, swelling as more arrived. The hunters, stunned by surprise, wasted precious moments. The hunted used every second.

“I hear no more hounds,” Martin whispered to Margaret, regaining his composure.

Now it was her turn to tremble. “Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They’ll kill him—and I’m not there!”

“Nay, nay!” Martin insisted. “No man among them has the wit to catch him. You told him to meet us at the ash-tree?”

“And so I did. Bless you, Martin, for remembering. To the ash-tree!”

“Ay—but quieter.”

They were nearly at the edge when suddenly—behind them—came hooping and hallooing. The men had confirmed the fugitives were in the coppice and were turning back.

“Doesn’t matter,” Martin whispered, calm again. “We’ll have time to clear it and vanish, running hard. Ah!”

He dropped abruptly. Just as he neared the brushwood’s edge, his eye caught a figure: Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, seated on his mule. A bloody bandage crossed his nose—broken bridge, but his eyes burned with sharp suspicion. He had heard them rustle. He was waiting.

Martin swore under his breath. Slowly, carefully, he strung his bow, fitted the last arrow. Margaret pressed her hands to her face, silent. She knew this man must die—or Gerard would.

The bow rose. The arrow drew taut. At that instant, a swift figure leaped onto Ghysbrecht from behind—like a hawk striking a pigeon. A kerchief flew over the burgomaster’s head. In a flash, his face was muffled, and he was thrown from the mule, crashing to the ground, groaning in terror. Gerard landed beside him.

“Hist, Martin! Martin!” Gerard called.

They emerged—Martin gaping. “Now fly! Fly! While they’re all tangled in the thicket—we’re saved!”

Just as safety seemed within grasp, fate intervened. Margaret, who had held firm until now, began to weaken—her wound bleeding, her strength fading.

“Oh, my beloved,” she gasped, “fly! Leave me—I am faint.”

“No! No!” Gerard shouted. “Death together, or safety. Ah—the mule! Mount her—you, and I’ll run by your side.”

In a heartbeat, Martin was on the mule. Gerard lifted the swooning girl into his arms and placed her on the saddle, taking Martin’s bow.

“Help! Treason! Murder! Murder!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, rising on his heels.

“Silence, cur!” Gerard roared, crushing him down by the throat like a serpent.

“Now—have you got her steady? Then fly! For our lives! For our lives!”

But even as Martin kicked the mule into motion, scattering flints beneath its hooves, and even as Gerard pulled his foot from Ghysbrecht’s throat to run—Dierich Brower and five men, returning for orders and hearing the cries, burst roaring from the coppice—upon them.
