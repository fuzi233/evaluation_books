Margaret woke to find her head resting upon Catherine's arm. Around her stood the sturdy, honest members of the household, speaking in rough, homely tones of encouragement. Giles, in particular, roared that she must not take on so; Gerard was alive and well, for he could not have penned such a magnificent letter—the largest, most beautiful, and most moving document the world had ever seen.

"Ay, good Master Giles," Margaret sighed weakly. "He was alive. But how can I know what has happened since? Oh, why did he leave Holland to wander among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not drive him from me sooner, rather than part him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me, you who are his mother!"

She gently dislodged Catherine's arm and made a feeble attempt to slide from the chair onto her knees. After a brief struggle against gravity, she found herself settled on Catherine's lap. Then, holding out the letter to Eli, she spoke faintly but sweetly, "I will entrust it to your hands now. Truly, I am unfit to read any more—and loath to leave this comfort." She wound her free arm tightly around Catherine's neck.

"Read it, Richart," Eli instructed. "Thine eyes are younger than mine."

Richart accepted the letter. "Well," he remarked, "I have never seen such handwriting. It is written with a needle's point, and remarkably clear. Why is he not in my counting-house in Amsterdam instead of vagabonding abroad?"

"When I regained consciousness," Margaret began, "I was seated in the litter, with my good merchant holding my hand. I babbled nonsense, then shivered in silence for a while. He pressed a horn of wine to my lips."

"Bless him! Bless him!" Catherine exclaimed.

"Quiet," Eli whispered.

"I told him what had befallen. He examined my leg; it was severely sprained and swollen at the ankle. All my stays were broken; I could scarcely keep my hose up. I said, 'Sir, I fear I shall be but a burden to you, and can offer you no harmony now; my poor psaltery is broken.' I grieved deeply over my broken instrument, a companion of so many weary leagues. But he patted my cheek, bidding me not fret. He propped my leg on a pillow and tended me like a kind father."

"January 19.—I sit all day in the litter, for we push forward with haste. At night, the good, kind merchant sends me to bed and forbids me to work. Strange! Whenever I encounter men like fiends, the very next moment God sends me some good man or woman, lest I despair of humanity. Oh, Margaret! How strangely mixed they are, and how aged I feel compared to who I was three months ago. Lo! If good Master Fugger has not already bought me a new psaltery."

"Eli, my man," Catherine interrupted, "if that merchant passes our way, let us buy a hundred ells of cloth from him, and haggle not."

"That will I do," Eli promised, "take your oath on it!"

As Richart prepared to continue reading, Kate glanced at her mother. With a faint blush, she withdrew a piece of embroidery from under her apron and began to sew, her head bowed slightly lower than necessary. Her mother followed suit, drawing a similar project from her pocket. As Richart read aloud, both women worked diligently. The specimens they displayed were baby caps, more than half finished, telling a silent tale. Horror! They resembled little monk's cowls in shape and delicacy.

"January 20.—Confined to the litter, nearly blind, yet limping along, the Lombardy plains burst upon me. Oh, Margaret! A land flowing with milk and honey; all sloping plains, noble rivers, cheerful meadows, delightful orchards, and blooming gardens. Though winter, it looks warmer than poor beloved Holland at midsummer, making the wanderer's face shine and his heart leap for joy to see earth so kind and smiling. Here are vines, cedars, olives, and cattle in abundance, though there are three goats to every sheep. The draft oxen wear white linen on their necks, and standing beside dark green olive trees, each one is a picture. The folk, especially the women, wear delicate straw hats adorned with flowers and leaves faithfully imitated in silk, mixed with silver. Today we crossed a river prettily in a chained ferry-boat. On either bank stood a windlass, and a single man, by turning it, drew our entire company to shore—a sight I admired greatly as a stranger. Some country folk passed with us. An old woman, watching a young girl, hid her face with her hand and held out her crucifix like a knight brandishing a sword in a tournament, fearing the evil eye."

"January 25.—Safe at Venice. A place whose strange and surpassing beauty is well known to thee by the reports of our mariners. Do you remember how Peter would often fill our ears with tales of this sea-enthroned, peerless city, shaped like a bow, with its great canal and palaces built on piles, its watery ways plied by dozens of gilded boats? Of that marketplace of nations, *orbis*, non *urbis*—St. Mark's Square, his place? His statue with the peerless jewels in his eyes, and the lion at his gate? But I, lying at my window in pain, can see none of these beauties yet, only a fairly paved street which seems dull, and houses covered with oiled paper and linen instead of glass, which appears crude. The passers-by, with their habits and gestures, seem superfluous. Therefore, to avoid missing my daily comfort of whispering to thee, I will turn my eyes inward and bind my sheaves of wisdom reaped by travel. For I love thee so that no treasure pleases me unless shared with thee; and what treasure is so good and enduring as knowledge? Thus, Sir Footsore, I have learned that each nation possesses its proper wisdom and its proper folly. Methinks, could a great king or duke tramp like me and see with his own eyes, he might pick the flowers and eschew the weeds of nations, returning home to set his own folk upon Wisdom's hill. The Germans in the north are churlish, yet frank and honest; in the south, they are kindly and honest too. Their general failing is drunkenness, carried even to a dislike and contempt of sober men. They commonly say, 'If you cannot drink and eat, you cannot serve any lord well.' In England, the vulgar drink as deeply, but the respectable regard excess as a reproach, drinking a toast or two for courtesy, not gluttony, and always sweetening the wine. In their cups, the Germans use little mirth or conversation, but ply their business seriously, crying 'Seid frolich!' Their finest drunken sport is 'Kurlemurlehuff,' a method of drinking involving deftly touching the glass, the beard, and the table in turn, interspersed with whistlings and finger snaps so curiously ordered that it is a labor of Hercules, yet to the observer, right pleasant and merry. Their drinkers, advised by German physicians, sleep with pebbles in their mouths. For, as the lid of a boiling pot must be set ajar, so with these fleshy wine-pots, to vent the heat of their inward parts; despite which, many die suddenly from drink. Yet it is a matter of religion to gloss over this, attributing it to some innocent disease. Still, it is more a custom than nature, for their women mingle among the tipplers, standing but a moment to kiss the wine-cup, and are indeed most temperate in eating and drinking, and of all women, the most modest, virtuous, and true spouses and friends to their mates; far surpassing our Holland lasses, who, as maids, ask the men questions, and as wives, rule over them. Indeed, there is a wife in Tergou, not far from our door. One came to the house seeking her husband. She said, 'You will not find him: he asked my leave to go abroad this afternoon, and I granted it.'"

"'Tis true! 'Tis true!" Catherine declared. "'Twas Beck Hulse, Jonah's wife. This comes of a woman marrying a boy."

"In the south, where wine flows, the gentry drink themselves bare; but not in the north: for with beer, a nobleman will sooner burst his body than melt his lands. They are quarrelsome, but it is the liquor, not the mind; for they are not vindictive. When they have made a bad bargain drunk, they stand by it sober. They keep their windows bright and judge a man by his clothes. Whatever fruit, grain, or herb grows by the roadside, gather and eat. The owner, seeing you, will say, 'Welcome, honest man.' But if you pluck a wayside grape, your very life is in jeopardy. 'Tis eating of that Heaven gave to be drunken. The French are much fairer spoken, yet not nearly so true-hearted. Sweet words cost them nothing. They call it *payer en blanche*."

"Those scoundrels!" Denys laughed.
