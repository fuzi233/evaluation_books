It would be useless to pretend I can portion out, neatly and coldly, the horror of that night, as though it were a thing that rose and fell like the heat of an oven and then was done. It did not pass off like the terrors of common dreams. It thickened with the daylight; it took possession of the very room, as if the boards and hangings had drunk it in, and even now, in remembering, I see again the familiar objects—chair, curtain, mirror—tainted, as provisions can be tainted, not by sight but by a foul, secret change that makes them unfit for the mouth.

Next day I could not endure solitude for an instant. I should have spoken to my father; yet two opposite fears held my tongue. In one mood I imagined he would laugh, and I could not bear to have that dread handled like a trifle and tossed back at me with a smile. In another I dreaded that he would connect my story with the mysterious sickness that had crept about our neighborhood, house by house, like a miasma that chooses its victims. I had no suspicion of such an illness in myself—none then—and since my father had been an invalid of late, I shrank from alarming him. So I kept close to company, as one keeps close to a hearth when the wind has begun to mutter strangely in the chimneys.

Madame Perrodon was with me, kind and steady as good bread; Mademoiselle Lafontaine too, lively as a spoon tapping a glass. They saw at once that my spirits were low and my nerves raw, as if every sound in the house were too sharp. At last the weight at my heart pressed out of me, and I told them.

Mademoiselle laughed in spite of herself; her laughter had the lightness of a kitchen song, meant to keep hands moving. But Madame Perrodon’s face tightened, as if she had caught, beneath the words, an odor she did not like.

“By-the-by,” cried Mademoiselle, still laughing, “the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla’s bedroom window is haunted!”

“Nonsense!” Madame exclaimed, quickly—too quickly, I thought, as a person slaps a lid down on a pot that threatens to boil over. “And who tells that story, my dear?”

“Martin. He says when the old yard-gate was being repaired he came up twice before sunrise, and twice saw the same female figure walking down the lime-tree avenue.”

“So he well might,” said Madame, with a dryness that tried to turn the tale into mere household arithmetic, “as long as there are cows to be milked in the river fields.”

“I daresay. But Martin chooses to be frightened, and never did I see fool more frightened.”

“You must not say a word of it to Carmilla,” I interposed. “She can see down that walk from her window; and she is—if possible—a greater coward than I.”

Carmilla came down later than usual that day, moving as though the air itself were heavy. The house had its ordinary sounds: distant clatter from the servants, a door closing far off, the soft tread on corridors. Yet I listened as one listens for a wrong note in music, for the smallest disharmony that spoils an entire meal.

“I was so frightened last night,” she said the moment we were alone together, her voice lowered, as if afraid the walls might carry it. “And I am sure I should have seen something dreadful if it had not been for that charm I bought from the poor little hunchback whom I called such hard names. I dreamed of something black coming round my bed, and I awoke in a perfect horror. I really thought—for some seconds—I saw a dark figure near the chimneypiece. But I felt under my pillow for my charm; and the moment my fingers touched it, the figure disappeared. I felt quite certain that only for it something frightful would have shown itself—perhaps throttled me, as it did those poor people we heard of.”

Her words fell into my mind and lay there, heavy as a stone sunk in broth. I tried to swallow.

“Well—listen to me,” I said, and I told her what I had seen and suffered. At my recital she grew pale and seemed truly horrified.

“And had you the charm near you?” she asked, urgently, leaning toward me as if the answer were life or death.

“No. I had dropped it into a china vase in the drawing-room. But I shall take it with me tonight, since you have such faith in it.”

At this distance of time I cannot tell you—cannot even understand—how I overcame my horror so thoroughly that I lay down alone in my room that night. I remember only the exactness of the act: I pinned the charm to my pillow, as carefully as one ties up herbs in muslin so their scent will steep without escaping. Then I slept almost at once—slept, indeed, more soundly than usual, as though my senses had been lulled by some invisible draught.

The next night I slept as well. My sleep was deep and dreamless, like a pot kept at a slow, steady simmer. Yet when I woke there was a lassitude in me, and a melancholy—not sharp, not painful, but insinuating, almost luxurious, as if my mind had been dipped in some sweet, dark cordial that weakened and pleased at once.

“Well, I told you so,” said Carmilla when I described my quiet night. “I had such delightful sleep myself. I pinned the charm to the breast of my nightdress. It was too far away the night before. I am quite sure it was all fancy—except the dreams. I used to think evil spirits made dreams; but our doctor told me it is no such thing. Only a fever passing by, or some other malady, as they often do, knocks at the door; and not being able to get in, passes on—with that alarm.”

“And what do you think the charm is?” I asked.

“It has been fumigated or immersed in some drug,” she answered, “and is an antidote against the malaria.”

“Then it acts only on the body?”

“Certainly. You don’t suppose evil spirits are frightened by bits of ribbon, or the perfumes of a druggist’s shop? No—these complaints, wandering in the air, begin by trying the nerves, and so infect the brain; but before they can seize upon you, the antidote repels them. That, I am sure, is what the charm has done for us. It is nothing magical. It is simply natural.”

I wished I could agree with her entirely. I tried, as a person tries to accept a substitute ingredient when the true one cannot be had. The impression of my first terror did weaken a little; the house resumed its customary domestic rhythm—doors opened, trays were carried, linens folded, footsteps measured along passages. Yet my own body began to fall out of tune with that ordinary music.

For some nights I slept profoundly. But each morning the same languor met me, settled on me, clung, and all day a weight lay upon my spirits. I felt myself a changed girl. A strange melancholy stole over me, and I did not wish it interrupted, as if it were a song in a dialect I could not quite speak but longed to hear to the end. Dim thoughts of death began to unfold; the notion that I was slowly sinking took gentle possession of me, and—horrible confession—was not unwelcome. If it was sad, it was also sweet; my soul, in some secret corner, consented.

I would not admit I was ill. I would not consent to tell my father or to have the doctor sent for. I had no pain; there was no complaint I could name with the plain words of household sickness. It seemed a malady of the nerves, of the imagination; and with a reserve that now astonishes me, I kept my sufferings almost entirely to myself, as if speaking them aloud would give them a vocabulary and so make them truer.

Carmilla, meanwhile, became more devoted to me than ever. Her fits of languid adoration grew more frequent. She would gaze at me—gloat upon me—with an ardor that increased as my strength and spirits waned, as though my weakness were a spice that sharpened her appetite. The look always shocked me, a sudden glare like insanity. I did not know it then, but I was already far advanced in the strangest illness under which mortal ever suffered.

There was, at first, an unaccountable fascination in the early symptoms that more than reconciled me to their disabling effect. That fascination increased for a time until it reached a point; then, little by little, a sense of the horrible began to mingle with it, deepening—as you shall hear—until it discolored and perverted the whole state of my life. The first change was even agreeable. It was very near the turning-point from which began the descent of Avernus.

Certain vague sensations visited me in sleep. The chief was a pleasant, peculiar cold thrill, like the moment in bathing when you move against a river’s current and feel the living chill press and slide along your skin. Then came dreams that seemed interminable, so vague I could never recover their scenery, their persons, or any connected action. They left, however, an awful impression and a sense of exhaustion, as if I had passed through a long period of mental exertion and peril.

After these dreams, on waking, there remained a remembrance of having been in a place almost dark; of having spoken to people whom I could not see; and especially of one clear voice—a woman’s, very deep—speaking as if at a distance, slowly, always producing the same sensation of solemnity and fear, as though it used a language whose meaning could not be translated into mine, but whose command my nerves understood perfectly.

Sometimes there came a sensation as if a hand were drawn softly along my cheek and neck—light as flour sifted over warm skin. Sometimes it was as if warm lips kissed me: longer, longer, more lovingly as they reached my throat, and then the caress fixed itself there. My heart beat faster; my breathing rose and fell rapidly, full-drawn. A sobbing grew into a sense of strangulation; then came a dreadful convulsion, in which my senses left me, and I became unconscious, as if some unseen pressure had extinguished me like a lamp.

Three weeks had passed since this unaccountable state began. During the last week my sufferings had printed themselves on my face. I had grown pale; my eyes looked dilated, with darkness beneath; the long languor that had inhabited me began to show itself openly, like a slow-spreading stain.

My father asked often whether I was ill. But with an obstinacy that now seems incomprehensible I persisted in assuring him I was quite well. In a sense it was true: I felt no pain; I could point to no bodily derangement. Yet what was happening was more alarming than pain, and the more terrible because it slipped between the customary words. I told myself it could not be that dreadful complaint the peasants called the oupire; for I had now been suffering three weeks, and those poor people were seldom ill longer than three days before death ended their misery.

Carmilla complained sometimes of dreams and feverish sensations, but by no means so alarming as mine. Mine were, in truth, terrifying; had I been capable of comprehending my condition, I would have invoked aid and advice on my knees. But some narcotic influence, unsuspected, acted upon me; my perceptions were dulled, my judgment muffled, as if the world’s sounds had been padded—no crisp clink, no clear call—only a softened, treacherous hush.

I am going to tell you of a dream that led at once to an odd discovery.

One night, instead of the deep voice I had come to expect in the darkness, I heard one sweet and tender, and at the same time terrible, which said: “Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.”

At that instant a light sprang up unexpectedly, and I saw Carmilla standing near the foot of my bed in her white nightdress, bathed from chin to feet in one great stain of blood.

I awoke with a shriek, possessed by the one idea that Carmilla was being murdered. I remember springing from the bed; and my next recollection is of standing on the lobby, crying for help. Madame and Mademoiselle came scurrying from their rooms, startled into harsh breath and hurried footfalls. A lamp burned always on the lobby; by that steady light they saw my terror and quickly learned its cause.

I insisted we knock at Carmilla’s door. We knocked—there was no answer. The knocking became pounding; the quiet hallway filled with an uproar that seemed to shatter the house’s accustomed night-silence. We shrieked her name, but all was vain. The door was locked.

We hurried back in panic to my room and rang the bell long and furiously. If my father’s room had been on that side of the house we would have called him up at once; but he was quite out of hearing, and to reach him would have meant an expedition through dim passages none of us had courage for. Soon, however, servants came running up the stairs; meanwhile I had pulled on my dressing-gown and slippers, and my companions had done the same.

Recognizing the servants’ voices on the lobby—those familiar human sounds that ordinarily restore order as surely as the rattle of keys—we sallied out together. We renewed our summons at Carmilla’s door, still fruitlessly. Then I ordered the men to force the lock. They did so.

We stood, holding our lights aloft in the doorway, and stared into the room. We called her by name; there was no reply. We looked round. Everything was undisturbed—exactly as I had left it when I bade her good night.

But Carmilla was gone.
