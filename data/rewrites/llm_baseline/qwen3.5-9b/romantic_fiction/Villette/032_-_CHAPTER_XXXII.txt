Where, indeed, was Paulina Mary? How had my dealings with the magnificent Hôtel Crécy proceeded? Those interactions had recently been interrupted by their departure; Monsieur and Mademoiselle de Bassompierre had spent several weeks traveling between the French provinces and the capital. Fortune informed me of their return almost immediately upon their arrival.

One mild afternoon, strolling along a quiet boulevard, I wandered leisurely, basking in the benign rays of an April sun, lost in pleasant reverie. Suddenly, ahead of me, a group of horsemen halted as if caught mid-conversation, exchanging greetings upon the wide, smooth path lined with linden trees. On one side rode a middle-aged gentleman and a young lady; on the other, a young and strikingly handsome man. The lady's demeanor was exquisitely graceful, her attire impeccable, her entire bearing delicate yet stately. Yet, as I observed them, a familiar sense washed over me. Drawing closer, I recognized them instantly: Count Home de Bassompierre, his daughter, and Dr. Graham Bretton.

How animated was Dr. Graham's countenance! What genuine, warm, yet restrained joy illuminated his face! Such was the convergence of circumstances designed to captivate and bind, to humble and exhilarate Dr. John. The pearl he admired possessed immense value and purity, yet he was not the sort to appreciate a gem while ignoring its setting. Had he seen Paulina with equal youth and beauty, but on foot, alone, unprotected, and clad in simple working garb—a dependent laborer, a *demi-grisette*—he might have found her a charming creature, loving her movements with his eyes. But it required more than mere appearance to conquer him as he was now subdued. To bring him safely under her dominion, preserving his honor while enhancing it, one saw clearly that he was conquered. About Dr. John burned the spirit of the worldly man; personal satisfaction was insufficient. Society's approval was mandatory; the world must admire his choices, or he deemed his actions false and futile. In his victor, he demanded everything visible here: the mark of high culture, the assurance of careful, authoritative protection, the accoutrements dictated by fashion, purchased by wealth, and refined by taste. These conditions were fulfilled to the utmost, and now, proud, passionate, yet fearful, he paid homage to Paulina as his sovereign.

As for her, a smile of feeling, rather than conscious power, rested softly in her eyes.

They departed. He passed me at a swift pace, barely touching the ground beneath him, oblivious to his surroundings. He looked remarkably handsome; steel and purpose surged within him. "Papa, there is Lucy!" cried a musical, friendly voice. "Lucy, dear Lucy—*please* come here!"

I hurried toward her. She tossed back her veil and leaned from her saddle to kiss me. "I intended to visit you tomorrow," she said, "but now, you must come and see me." She specified the hour, and I promised to comply.

That evening found me with her, locked away in her private chamber. I had not seen her since the occasion when her claims were weighed against those of Ginevra Fanshawe, and she had so decisively prevailed. She had much to recount regarding her travels. In intimate conversation, she was an animated, rapid speaker, a vivid narrator; yet, with her artless diction and clear, soft voice, she never seemed to speak too quickly or say too much. My attention might not have wavered easily, but eventually, she seemed to require a shift in topic. She hastened to conclude her narrative briefly. Why she ended with such a concise summary was not immediately apparent. Silence followed—a restless silence, tinged with abstraction.

Then, turning to me in a diffident, half-appealing tone, she said, "Lucy—"
"Well, I am at your side."
"Is my cousin Ginevra still at Madame Beck's?"
"She is still there; surely you are eager to see her."
"Not particularly."
"You wish to invite her for another evening?"
"No... I suppose she still speaks of marriage?"
"To someone you care for?"
"But of course she still thinks of Dr. Bretton? She couldn't have changed her mind so soon, given how fixed it was two months ago."
"Why, you know, it doesn't matter. You saw the terms on which they stand."
"There was a slight misunderstanding that evening, certainly; does she seem unhappy?"
"Not at all. Let us change the subject. Have you heard or seen anything of, or from, Graham during your absence?"
"Papa received letters from him occasionally regarding business, I believe. He managed an affair requiring attention while we were away. Dr. Bretton appears to respect Papa greatly and takes pleasure in assisting him."
"Yes: you met him yesterday on the boulevard; judging by his aspect, his friends need not worry excessively about his health?"
"Papa agreed with you. I couldn't help smiling. He isn't particularly observant, you know, as he is often preoccupied with other matters. But as Dr. Bretton rode away, he said, 'Truly, it does a man good to witness the spirit and energy of that boy.' He called Dr. Bretton a boy; I believe he regards him almost as such, just as he views me as a little girl. He wasn't speaking to me, but muttered the remark to himself. Lucy..."

Again, the appealing accent returned, and simultaneously, she left her chair and sat on the stool at my feet. I liked her. It is rare I make such a declaration regarding acquaintances throughout this book, but please bear with me this once. Close inspection revealed in Paulina only what was delicate, intelligent, and sincere; thus, my regard for her ran deep. A more superficial admiration might have been more demonstrative; mine, however, remained quiet.

"What do you wish to ask of Lucy?" I said. "Be brave and speak plainly."

Yet, there was no courage in her gaze; as it met mine, it lowered. Her cheeks showed no fleeting blush, but a gathering inward excitement flushed them and warmed them.

"Lucy, I truly wish to know your thoughts of Dr. Bretton. Please, give me your real opinion of his character and disposition."
"His character stands high, and deservedly so."
"And his disposition? Tell me about it," she urged, "you know him well."
"I know him fairly well."
"You know his domestic side. You've seen him with his mother; speak of him as a son."
"He is a fine-hearted son; his mother's comfort, hope, pride, and delight."

She held my hand between hers, giving it a gentle, caressing stroke with each favorable word. "In what other way is he good, Lucy?"
"Dr. Bretton is benevolent—humanely disposed toward all mankind. Dr. Bretton would extend kindness to the lowest savage or the worst criminal."
"I heard some gentlemen, friends of Papa, discussing him similarly. They say many poor patients at the hospitals, who tremble before pitiless, selfish surgeons, welcome him."
"They are correct; I have witnessed it. Once he took me over a hospital; I saw how he was received. Your father's friends are right."

The softest gratitude lit her eyes as she lifted them for a moment. She had more to say but hesitated regarding time and place. Twilight was falling; the parlor fire glowed with a reddish hue, yet I sensed she wished the room dimmer, the hour later.

"How quiet and secluded we feel here!" I remarked, to reassure her.
"Do we? Yes; it is a still evening, and I shall not be summoned for tea; Papa is dining out."

Still holding my hand, she unconsciously played with my fingers, adorning them with her own rings, then circling them with strands of her beautiful hair. She pressed the palm against her hot cheek, and finally, clearing her throat—which was naturally liquid as a lark's song—she said:

"You must think it somewhat strange that I discuss Dr. Bretton so much, ask so many questions, and take such an interest—but—"
"Not at all strange; perfectly natural; you like him."
"And if I did," she said with slight urgency, "is that a reason to speak? I suppose you think me weak, like my cousin Ginevra?"
"If I thought you one whit like Madame Ginevra, I would not sit here awaiting your words. I would rise, pace the room at ease, and anticipate everything you have to say with a thorough lecture. Continue."
"I intend to continue," she retorted, sounding petulant and sensitive—the little Polly of Bretton. "If," she emphasized, "I liked Dr. John until I was dying of affection for him, that alone could not license me to remain silent—silent as the grave—silent as you, Lucy Snowe—you know it—and you know you would despise me if I lacked self-control and whined about some fragile liking existing solely on my side."
"It is true I hold little respect for women or girls who boast of triumphs or lament mortifications of feeling. But as for you, Paulina, speak, for I earnestly desire to hear you. Tell me all that brings you pleasure or relief; I ask no more."
"Do you care for me, Lucy?"
"Yes, I do, Paulina."
