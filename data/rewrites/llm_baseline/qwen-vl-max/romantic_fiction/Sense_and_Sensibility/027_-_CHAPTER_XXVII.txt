“Should this pleasant weather continue much longer,” Mrs. Jennings remarked at breakfast the next morning, “Sir John will be loath to leave Barton next week. It’s such a pity for sportsmen to miss a day of sport. Poor things! I always feel for them when they do—they take it so deeply to heart.”

“That’s true,” Marianne replied cheerfully, stepping toward the window to gaze out at the sky. “I hadn’t thought of that. This kind of weather will keep many hunters in the country.”

It was a fortunate thought—her spirits instantly lifted. “It’s lovely weather for them, indeed,” she continued, settling into her seat with a bright smile. “How they must enjoy it! But”—a flicker of unease crossed her face—“it won’t last long. At this time of year, after all that rain, we can’t expect much more of it. Frost will soon come, and likely with force. Perhaps in another day or two; this warmth can’t endure much longer—nay, it might even freeze tonight!”

“At any rate,” Elinor said quickly, hoping to divert Mrs. Jennings from reading too closely into her sister’s thoughts, “I’m sure Sir John and Lady Middleton will be in town by the end of next week.”

“Oh, yes, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings assured her. “I’ll wager they will. Mary always gets her own way.”

And now, Elinor silently mused, “She’ll write to Combe today, no doubt.”

But if she did, the letter was sent off so quietly that Elinor could not catch sight of it. Whatever the truth, though she remained far from content, seeing Marianne in high spirits eased her own unease. And Marianne *was* in high spirits—delighted by the mildness of the air, and even more thrilled by the prospect of frost.

The morning passed mostly in calling on Mrs. Jennings’s acquaintances to announce their arrival in town. Marianne spent every moment watching the wind, studying the clouds, sensing the subtle shifts in the atmosphere. “Don’t you feel it’s colder than earlier?” she asked Elinor. “There’s a definite change. I can barely warm my hands in my muff. It wasn’t like this yesterday. Look—the clouds are parting. The sun will break through soon. We’ll have a clear afternoon!”

Elinor found herself alternately amused and pained by her sister’s obsession. But Marianne persisted, finding signs of approaching frost in every crackling fire and every morning breeze.

The Dashwood sisters had little reason to complain about Mrs. Jennings’s lifestyle or circle of friends. Her hospitality was generous, her manners kind, and she carefully avoided introducing them to anyone who might unsettle their sensibilities. Elinrna, grateful for this comfort, accepted the limitations of their evenings—most of which revolved around cards—with quiet resignation.

Colonel Brandon, who had been invited regularly, visited nearly every day. He came to see Marianne and talk with Elinor, whose conversations with him were often the most meaningful part of her day. Yet she could not help noticing how deeply he seemed to care for her sister—how his eyes lingered on Marianne, how his mood grew heavier since leaving Barton. She feared his feelings were only growing stronger.

About a week after their arrival, it became clear that Willoughby had also reached London. His card lay on the table when they returned from their morning drive.

“Good heavens!” Marianne exclaimed. “He’s been here while we were out!”

Elinor, relieved to know he was in town, ventured, “I’m certain he’ll call again tomorrow.”

But Marianne barely heard her. As Mrs. Jennings entered, she snatched up the card and retreated with it.

This news stirred Elinor’s hopes—and reignited Marianne’s restless longing. From that moment, her mind was never still. She waited for him every hour, unable to focus on anything else. The next morning, she insisted on staying behind while the others went out.

Elinor’s thoughts raced—what was happening in Berkeley Street? But one glance at her sister upon their return told her: Willoughby had not returned.

Just then, a note arrived and was placed on the table.

“For me!” Marianne cried, rushing forward.

“No, ma'am,” the servant corrected. “For my mistress.”

Marianne, unconvinced, seized it anyway. “It *is* for Mrs. Jennings—how annoying!”

“You’re expecting a letter?” Elinor asked, unable to stay silent.

“Yes—just a little—not much.”

After a pause, Elinor softly said, “You have no confidence in me, Marianne.”

“Nay, Elinor,” Marianne replied sharply, “this accusation from *you*—you who trust no one?”

“Me?” Elinor stammered, embarrassed. “Indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to conceal.”

“Nor I,” Marianne declared with conviction. “Our situations are alike. We have neither of us anything to tell—you because you don’t share, and I because I hide nothing.”

Elinor, wounded by the charge of secrecy, felt helpless to press further. Mrs. Jennings soon appeared, and the note was handed over. She read aloud:

> *Dear Mother,*
> *We arrived in Conduit Street last night. We hope you and your dear cousins will join us this evening. Sir John has business, and I am suffering from a violent cold, so we could not call on you.*
> *With love,*
> *Lady Middleton*

The invitation was accepted. But as the appointed hour approached, Elinor struggled to persuade her sister to go. Marianne still hadn’t seen Willoughby, and though she wasn’t eager for company, she dreaded missing him should he come again while she was away.

That evening, Elinor discovered that changing homes doesn’t change human nature. Despite being newly settled, Sir John had already gathered nearly twenty young people for a ball—a gathering Lady Middleton disapproved of. In the country, such an impromptu dance was acceptable, but in London, where elegance mattered and reputations were fragile, hosting a small affair with just two violins and a sideboard supper risked ridicule.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer were among the guests. Mr. Palmer, who had taken care to avoid appearing attentive to his mother-in-law, gave them only a brief nod from across the room. Marianne glanced once around the hall—then sat down, clearly uninterested in pleasure or conversation.

After about an hour, Mr. Palmer drifted over. “I didn’t expect to see you both in town,” he said. “Colonel Brandon told me first, and I made a joke when I heard you were coming.”

“I thought you were both in Devonshire,” he added.

“Did you?” Elinor replied. “When do you return?”

“I don’t know.”

Their exchange ended there.

Never before had Marianne been so reluctant to dance—or so exhausted by it. On the way back, she complained bitterly.

“Aye, aye,” Mrs. Jennings chuckled. “We know why. If a certain person—let’s say no names—had been there, you wouldn’t have tired at all. And honestly, it wasn’t very polite of him not to meet you, even though he was invited.”

“Invited?” Marianne gasped.

“So my daughter Middleton told me,” Mrs. Jennings explained. “Sir John met him this morning in the street.”

Marianne said nothing—but her face flushed with hurt.

Elinor, desperate to ease her sister’s torment, decided the next morning to write to their mother. She hoped to stir her fears for Marianne’s health and finally prompt the inquiries that had long been delayed. Her resolve deepened when she saw Marianne writing—surely to Willoughby.

Later that day, Mrs. Jennings left for errands, and Elinor began her letter immediately. Marianne, restless and anxious, paced between windows or sat by the fire lost in thought.

Elinor wrote with urgency, detailing everything she suspected—Willoughby’s shifting affections—and pleaded with her mother to ask Marianne directly about her relationship with him. Duty and love demanded it.

She had scarcely finished when a knock announced a visitor. Colonel Brandon.

Marianne, having seen him from the window, fled the room before he entered.

He looked unusually grave. Though he expressed relief at finding Elinor alone—as if he had something important to say—he sat in silence for several minutes.

Elinor, convinced he had something urgent to tell her about her sister, waited anxiously.

At last, he spoke, his voice trembling slightly. “When may I congratulate you on gaining a brother?”

Elinor was stunned. “I don’t understand,” she said simply.

He tried to smile. “Your sister’s engagement to Mr. Willoughby is widely known.”

“It cannot be,” Elinor protested. “Not even our family knows.”

He looked surprised. “I beg your pardon—I fear I’ve intruded. But I assumed no secrets were meant, since they correspond openly, and their marriage is the talk of everyone.”

“Who has spoken of it?” Elinor asked.

“Many—some you don’t know, others close to you: Mrs. Jennings, Mrs. Palmer, the Middletons. Still, I might have doubted… until today, when the servant let me in and I saw a letter in his hand—addressed to Mr. Willoughby, in your sister’s handwriting. I came to ask—but I knew already. Is it final? Is there no chance—?” He stopped himself. “I have no right. No hope. Excuse me, Miss Dashwood. I believe I’ve said too much. But I don’t know what to do. I rely on your wisdom. Tell me—it’s all settled? That concealment, if possible, is all that remains?”

These words revealed his love plainly. Elinor was deeply moved. She could not speak at first. When she recovered, she wrestled with how best to respond. She knew so little of the true state between Marianne and Willoughby—she might say too much or too little.

Yet she understood that Marianne’s devotion left no hope for Colonel Brandon. Still, to protect her sister from blame, she chose kindness over truth.

She said, “Though I’ve never been told directly, I have no doubt of their mutual affection. And their correspondence—well, I’m not surprised to hear of it.”

He listened in silence. Then rose, voice thick with emotion. “To your sister, I wish every happiness. To Mr. Willoughby, I pray he deserves her.”

And with that, he left.

Elinor was left not with comfort, but with a heavy sorrow—for Colonel Brandon’s pain, and for the certainty that nothing could heal it.
