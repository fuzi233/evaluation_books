Pen was not sorry when his Mentor took himself off on the second day after their arrival at Oxbridge; and the Major, for his part, wore the air of a man who has at last set down a weighty dish he never meant to carry farther than the kitchen door. More than three months of his valuable leisure had that heroic martyr bestowed upon his nephew—three months in which the Major might have been improving his complexion at Cheltenham, or improving his acquaintance at great houses where the pheasants were fat and the people fatter. We are all ready enough to risk our heads for “honour,” which is a light thing to pack; but ask a man to give up his morning, his nap, his dinner, his favourite chair, his favourite talk, his favourite sauce—and see how soon his courage falls flat like a soufflé taken too early from the oven.

So Foker and Pen saw the Major off in the coach; and Foker, whose benevolence always expressed itself in the imperative mood, gave the coachman such particular instructions to “take care of that gentleman inside,” that it was wonderful the man did not climb in too and sit on the Major’s knees by way of security. The elder Pendennis was pleased: his boy, he fancied, was in the hands of a young fellow who could usher him into the very best set—like a well-trained waiter who knows which door opens upon the great dining-room. The Major rushed to London and thence to Cheltenham; and from that watering-place—where they give you water with an expression as if it were claret—he descended upon neighbouring mansions not yet migrated abroad, where society and shooting were still in season, and noise in the proper places (a crack of a gun, the crunch of boots on gravel) drowned the improper noises (the clink of small talk, the rattle of vanity).

If I were to tell every step of Pen’s university life at the pace of this chapter, some descendant of mine would have to take up the ladle when I drop it, and keep stirring the pot for the entertainment of a future generation. Besides, the daily life of boys—alas!—does not bear inspection like a well-browned joint. I wish it did. I ask you, does yours? So long as what we call our “honour” looks fair on the outside, we are content to let the inside be—how shall I put it—less wholesome than the cook pretends. Women keep their milk sweet longer than men; we have trained them to self-denial as we train orchards to yield: pruning, tying, and expecting fruit. But you, my good sir, with your whiskers and your opinions, what sum of sacrifices will you truly carry to your credit? There now—let us not poison the soup by talking of such things.

During Pen’s first term he attended classical and mathematical lectures with tolerable assiduity; and then discovered two facts. The first was that the exact sciences were a dish for which he had no appetite, no teeth, and no patience; the second, that one or two very vulgar young men—fellows who wore their trousers without straps, leaving their ankles exposed like raw joints in a butcher’s window above thick coarse shoes and stockings—beat him completely in the lecture-room. Pen could endure a great many things; but he could not endure being outdone by a man who looked as if he had never heard a spoon clink against china in his life. So he gave up mathematics and announced to his fond mother that he would devote himself exclusively to Greek and Roman literature.

Mrs. Pendennis was delighted that her darling should pursue the branch of learning to which he had the greatest inclination, and only besought him not to ruin his health by too much study; for she had heard frightful histories of young men who, by over-fatigue, brought on brain-fevers and died in the very midst of their career—like custards that curdle at the moment of triumph. Pen, whose health had always been handled by his mother as delicately as a thin-stemmed wineglass, promised he would not sit up too late; and in this particular he kept his word with more steadfastness than he showed in some other small vows a young gentleman will make when his conscience is warm and his head cool.

Presently he found, too, that the classical lecture was no better than the mathematical—only in a different manner. In one, his companions were too clever for him; in the other, they were too dull. Mr. Buck, the tutor, could, no doubt, parse and count feet with a humdrum conscientiousness—like a man weighing out flour who has never smelt fresh bread—but of poetry he had no more sense than Mrs. Binge the bed-maker. Pen grew tired of hearing the tutor and the students blunder and stumble through a few lines of a play, when he could read it in a tenth of the time, and taste the flavour at once, without having it chopped and served cold. Private reading, he discovered, was the only study that nourished; and he informed his mamma he should read much more by himself and much less in public. She knew no more about Homer than about Algebra; but she had faith in her boy as devoutly as she had faith in the rector, and was perfectly sure Arthur would earn the place he merited.

He did not come home until after Christmas, to the disappointment of his mother and of Laura, who had been longing for him to build a fine snow-fort as he had done three winters before—a domestic epic of white walls and frozen fingers, with the sound of laughter muffled by falling flakes. But Pen was invited to Logwood, Lady Agnes Foker’s, where there were private theatricals and a gay party of very fine folks—some of whom Major Pendennis would on no account have his nephew neglect. However, he spent the last three weeks of the vacation at home, and Laura could remark what a quantity of fine new clothes he brought, and his mother admired his improved appearance and his manly decided tone, as if a tailor had stitched courage into the seams.

At Easter he did not come home at all; but when he arrived for the long vacation, he brought more smart clothes still: wonderful shooting-jackets in the morning, with buttons that seemed to have been invented solely to catch the sun; and at night velvet waistcoats gorgeous enough to make a modest man blush for his own plain linen, with richly embroidered cravats and curious finery which had the air of being half raiment, half confectionery. His mother, in her gentle prowling about his room (a mother’s feet make no more noise than a cat’s when curiosity is pious), discovered a dressing-case with silver mountings, rings and jewellery, and—crowning insult to the dead—an elegant French watch and gold chain in place of the big old chronometer which had once hung from Dr. John Pendennis’s fob, jingling with seals, and measuring time while that good man measured pulses. It was but a few months since Pen had longed for that very chronometer, deeming it the most august timepiece in the world; and just before he went to college his mother had taken it from her trinket-box—where it had lain unwound since her husband’s death—and given it to him with a solemn little speech about his father’s virtues and the proper use of time. Pen now pronounced it “out of date,” and even compared it to a warming-pan, which Laura, who loved relics as she loved old receipts, thought downright irreverent. He left the watch in a drawer among soiled primrose gloves, abandoned cravats, and that other school watch of his which has already played its part in this history.

Rebecca, the old horse—an honest creature whose hoofbeats were part of the household music—was declared “no longer up to his weight,” and swapped for a more powerful animal at a heavy figure. Mr. Pendennis supplied the money; Laura cried when Rebecca was led away; and the stable yard, which had known her familiar snort and the soft munch of her hay, sounded suddenly emptier, as if a note had been removed from the farm’s daily tune.

Pen also brought down a large box of cigars with names that seemed invented by foreign cooks—Colorados, Afrancesados, Telescopios, Fudson Oxford Street, and the like—and began consuming them about the stables and greenhouses, where the smoke, he declared, was “very good for the plants,” and in his own study, which his mother at first disapproved. But he was at work upon a prize-poem, he said, and could not compose without a cigar; and he quoted Lord Byron in defence of the practice, as a man quotes a recipe by a celebrated chef when asked why he uses so much pepper. Since he smoked “to such good purpose,” Mrs. Pendennis could not refuse permission. Indeed, she came in one day and found him on the sofa in the midst of his labours—his “labours” consisting partly in consulting a novel recently appeared, for the cultivation of light literature had become, in his view, as necessary to genius as butter to toast. Rather than disturb him, she went into his bedroom, fetched the light-box and cigar-case, put the cigar into his mouth with her own hand, and lighted it for him. Pen laughed, kissed her hand as it hovered fondly over the sofa-back, and said, “Dear old mother, if I were to tell you to burn the house down, I think you would do it.” And it is likely he was right; her devotion was that domestic kind which will season any dish, even when the dish itself is questionable.

Along with English “light literature,” this diligent student brought boxes of French light literature too; and when Helen dipped into the leaves, she found things that made her open her eyes as if she had tasted vinegar when expecting cream. Pen explained—very gravely—that he did not write the books; that it was necessary to keep up his French by acquaintance with the most celebrated writers of the day; that it was as clearly his duty to read the eminent Paul de Kock as to study Swift or Molière. Mrs. Pendennis yielded with a sigh of perplexity, feeling that modern learning, like modern cookery, was full of spices she could not name. Miss Laura was warned off those volumes—by her anxious mother, and by that rigid moralist Mr. Arthur Pendennis himself; for, though he might be called upon to sample every dish in the intellectual kitchen in order to form his mind and perfect his style, he would by no means prescribe such tasting to a young lady whose business in life, he said, was “very different.”

In the course of this long vacation Mr. Pen drank up the bin of claret which his father had laid in—a claret of which Pen had once remarked that there was not a headache in a hogshead; and when it was exhausted, he wrote for more to “his wine merchants,” Messrs. Binney and Latham of Mark Lane, London. Old Doctor Portman had recommended that house when Pen first went to college, advising him to get port and sherry from honest London merchants rather than have recourse to Oxbridge tradesmen whose liquor, if memory served, was both dear and deleterious. “You will, no doubt, have to entertain your young friends at Boniface with wine-parties,” the worthy rector had said, remembering the customs of his own time, when young gentlemen thought hospitality began in the cellar and ended in the head.

So Pen ordered a stock for the cellars at Fairoaks, and hinted that Messrs. Binney and Latham might send in his university account for wine at the same time with the Fairoaks bill. Mrs. Pendennis was frightened at the amount; it struck her like the sudden clatter of a factory cart passing a quiet lane—too loud, too heavy, too new for comfort. Pen laughed at her old-fashioned views, said the bill was moderate, that everybody drank claret and champagne now; and finally the widow paid, feeling dimly that the household expenses were swelling like dough left too near the fire, and that her narrow income would scarcely suffice. But the extravagance, she told herself, was only occasional. Pen came home but for weeks at a time. Laura and she could pinch when he was gone. In the brief time he was with them, ought they not to make him happy? So the house, which kept its country quiet all year—the creak of the pantry door, the caw in the elms, the soft rural hush—was for a few weeks invaded by the pop of corks and the bustle of a young gentleman’s pleasures, as if some travelling fair had pitched its tents on the lawn.

Arthur’s own allowances were liberal—indeed, more liberal than those of the sons of far wealthier men. Years before, the thrifty affectionate John Pendennis, whose darling project had been to give his boy a university education denied to himself by his own father’s extravagance, had begun laying by money which he called Arthur’s Education Fund. In his books the executors found sums vested year after year as A. E. F.; and after her husband’s death, before Pen went up, the widow added to it too, so that when he entered Oxbridge it had reached no inconsiderable amount. “Let him be liberally allowanced,” was Major Pendennis’s maxim. “Let him make his first entrée into the world as a gentleman, take his place with men of good rank: after giving him the means, it will be his duty to hold it. There is no such bad policy as stinting a boy—or putting him on a lower allowance than his fellows.” Arthur would have to face the world and fight for himself presently; meanwhile they should procure him good friends, gentlemanly habits, and have him well backed and trained against the time when the real struggle came. These opinions were, no doubt, partly just, and partly convenient; for it is easier to be generous with other people’s money than with one’s own.

Thus young Pen—the only son of an “estated” country gentleman, with a good allowance and a gentlemanlike bearing—looked to be a lad of much more consequence than he truly was; and Oxbridge authorities, tradesmen, and undergraduates treated him as quite a young buck, a sort of aristocracy in embryo, to be handled carefully like a delicate glass and charged accordingly. His manner was frank, brave, and perhaps a little impertinent, as becomes a high-spirited youth. He was generous and free-handed with money that seemed plentiful. He loved joviality, and had a good voice for a song; and though boat-racing had not yet risen to the frenzy it has since attained, riding and tandem-driving were the fashions of ingenuous youth. Pen rode well to hounds, appeared in pink as became a buck, and though not outrageously extravagant in any single amusement, contrived to run up a fine bill at Nile’s livery-stables and in a number of other quarters. It is not one blazing kitchen-fire that burns down a house, but a dozen little candles left unattended.

For this lucky young gentleman had almost every taste to a considerable degree. He loved books of all sorts: Doctor Portman had taught him to relish rare editions, and Pen’s own palate ran to handsome bindings. It was marvellous what tall copies, what gilding, marbling, and blind-tooling, the booksellers and binders contrived to pile upon his shelves. He had a fair taste in art too, and a keen relish for high-school prints—none of your French opera-dancers or tawdry racing prints, such as had delighted the simple eyes of Mr. Spicer before him, but your Stranges and Rembrandt etchings and Wilkies before the letter; and his rooms became furnished with what the university allowed to be “perfect good taste.” He had a partiality for rings, jewellery, and fine raiment; and it must be owned that during his time at Oxbridge Mr. Pen was rather a dressy man and loved to array himself in splendour. He and his polite friends would dress themselves with as much care to dine at each other’s rooms as other folks would use who were going to enslave a mistress. They said he wore rings over his kid gloves—a charge he always denied; but youth will commit follies with admirable gravity. That he took perfumed baths is true; and he used to say he required them after meeting certain men of a very low set in hall—as if the sound of their broad vowels and heavy boots left a smudge upon his ears and skin alike.

In Pen’s second year, when Miss Fotheringay made her chief hit in London, and prints of her were published by the score, Pen hung one of them in his bedroom and confided to the men of his set how awfully, wildly, madly, passionately he had loved that woman. He showed them, in confidence, the verses he had written to her; his brow would darken, his eyes roll, his chest heave as he recalled that “fatal period” and described the woes and agonies he had suffered. The verses were copied out, handed about, sneered at, admired, passed from coterie to coterie like a favourite dish whose recipe everyone steals while pretending disdain. There are few things that elevate a lad in the esteem of other lads more than the reputation of a great romantic passion. Perhaps it is noble at any time; among very young men it is heroic. Pen was pronounced a tremendous fellow. They said he had almost committed suicide; that he had fought a duel with a baronet about her. Freshmen pointed him out. At promenade time, at two o’clock, when he swaggered out of college surrounded by his cronies, he was famous to behold—elaborately attired, ready to ogle the ladies who came to lionise the university, and to give his opinion upon their charms or their toilettes with the gravity of a critic whose experience entitled him to speak. Men said they had been walking with Pendennis, and were as pleased to be seen in his company as some of us would be if we walked with a duke down Pall Mall. He and the Proctor capped each other as they met, like rival powers; and the undergraduates scarcely knew which was the greater authority—law in a gown, or fashion in a velvet waistcoat.

In truth, during his second year Arthur Pendennis became one of the men of fashion in the university. It is curious to watch the easy admiration and simple fidelity of youth: how they cluster round a leader, wonder at him, love him, imitate him. No generous boy ever lived who did not worship another boy a little. Monsieur Pen at Oxbridge had his school, his faithful band, and his rivals. When young men heard at the haberdashers’ that Pendennis of Boniface had ordered a crimson satin cravat, you might see two dozen crimson satin cravats in Main Street within the week; and Simon the jeweller was known to sell no less than two gross of “Pendennis pins,” from a pattern the young gentleman had selected—so that Pen’s taste, like a dominant dialect, spread until everybody spoke it, even those who could not pronounce it properly.

Now any person with an arithmetical turn may calculate what it costs a young man to indulge freely in all these propensities, and it will be seen that, in two or three years, a lad of such tastes must spend or owe a handsome sum. Pen had no calculating turn. No one of his tastes was outrageously extravagant in itself; and certainly Paddington’s tailor’s account, Guttlebury’s cook’s bill for dinners, Dillon Tandy’s score with Finn the print-seller for Raphael-Morghens and Landseer proofs, Wormall’s dealings with Parkton the great bookseller for Aldine editions, black-letter folios, and richly illuminated missals of the sixteenth century, and Snaffle’s or Foker’s score with Nile the horse-dealer—each and all were incomparably greater than any single little bill Pendennis ran up. But Pen had what his friends admired as catholicity: he wanted everything. Whereas Lord Paddington did not care twopence for the most beautiful print unless it had a mirror in it; whereas Guttlebury did not mind how he was dressed and had a terror of horses; whereas Snaffle never read any printed work but the Racing Calendar or Bell’s Life, nor cared for any manuscript save the greasy scrawl of his betting-book—our universal-minded young friend occupied himself in every branch of pleasure and “science” above mentioned, and distinguished himself tolerably in each; and that universality, like a kitchen where every sauce is simmering at once, made the whole establishment dear.
