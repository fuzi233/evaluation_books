Cast one last lingering glance over the burnished golden tropical sea, the honeyed sunsets that bleed tangerine and rose over the shore of New Granada, along the fabled, treasure-laden Spanish Main.

On the bay of Santa Marta, the land breeze ripples the surface into sheets of living flame. The vast, unbroken rainforests glitter with tens of thousands of drifting fireflies, and the wispy lazy mist coiled around the inland hills glows molten gold in the last of the sunset rays. Nineteen thousand feet above the shore, the jagged, snow-capped peak of Horqueta pierces the dark blue abyss of the sky, dyed soft rose-red against the fading twilight. The rosy cone fades to dull lead for only a breath, before the stars prick through the dark one by one, and Venus hangs bright as a second moon, gilding the eastern snowfields and stretching a long shimmering ribbon of rippling yellow light across the bay. Everywhere there is richness, unearthly glory. Is it any wonder the very soil of this enchanted land is as wealthy in its depths as it is on the surface? The sky, hills, and sea weave together like a garland of scattered jewels — why would not the earth beneath match? Why not every stream and earthen bank spangled with emeralds, rubies, grains of raw gold, and lacy wreaths of native silver?

That was the whimsical, poetic thought drifting through the Bishop of Cartagena’s mind as he sat in the grand state cabin of the mighty galleon *The City of the True Cross*, staring pensively out the window toward the shore. The good man was wrapped in a state of soft, holy calm: his ample frame propped on one easy chair, his stout ankles resting on a second, beside a table heaped high with oranges, limes, guavas, pineapples, and every other exotic fruit the Indies had to offer.

A young Indigenous girl, draped in bright scarves and strung with cheap gold chains, fended off the swarms of flies with a feather fan. Beside her master, in a pail of fresh ice hauled down from the peak of Horqueta — the gift of a pious Spanish noblewoman who had worked two Indian laborers to death to bring the precious offering down the mountain — sat more than one flask of smooth, vintage Alicant wine. The bishop was far too generous to hoard the ice and wine for himself: at his special invitation, Don Pedro, colonel of the soldiers posted aboard, Don Alverez, the King’s customs intendant for Santa Marta, and Don Paul, master mariner of the galleon, had joined him that evening, alongside two quiet friars sat at the far end of the table, all of them doing their best to distract the prelate from the lingering sting of the recent sack and burning of his cathedral town at the hands of an old familiar rogue: Sir Francis Drake.

“We have suffered greatly, señors — ah, most greatly,” the bishop snuffled, quoting Scripture in the glib, irreverent fashion of the day, twisting verses to suit his grumbles about the town’s ruin, his servant Tita, and the pestering mosquitoes. “Suffered greatly, yes — but there will be a remnant, ah, a remnant as when the olive tree is shaken, and the last grapes are gleaned after the vintage. Gold? Yes, I trust Our Lady’s mercies are not closed off, nor her arm shortened. Look, señors!” He waved a chubby hand grandly at the window. “The very light looks like gold! The air reeks of gold, if I may take a poet’s liberty. The ripples slipping past our hull sing of gold, gold, gold!”

“It is a rare privilege,” the intendant flattered him, “to receive such gentle comfort from a man who is both a churchman and a scholar.”

“And a poet, too,” added Don Pedro. “You have no idea the sweet sonnets he writes —”

“Hush, Don Pedro, hush! If I, a widowed, mateless bird, have spent an idle hour teaching young lovers how to sing, what of it? I am a churchman, yes — but I am still a man, señors. I can feel, sympathize, pardon, excuse. Who knows better than I how much human weakness lingers in all of us fallen sons of Adam? Tita!”

“Um?” the girl mumbled, shifting on her heels with a low, Indigenous grunt.

“Fill his excellency the intendant’s glass. Is there much more treasure due down from the mountains, illustrious señor? Might the poor of Our Lady hope for a few crumbs from their Mistress’s table?”

“Not a single peso, I fear,” the intendant laughed, pointing up at the looming snow peak of Horqueta. “The great white cow up there has been milked dry for this year.”

“Ah.” The bishop sighed, staring up at the magnificent cone. “Good only to cool our wine, eh? And as safe for now as King Solomon’s holy birds.”

“Solomon’s birds? Explain that obscure reference, my lord.”

“Enlighten us, your grace, enlighten us.”

“Ah, that is a tale worth telling. You know the hoopoes that trot about the Prado in Seville, all hooked beaks and cinnamon crests, under the ladies’ feet? Of course you do. The story goes that once, all hoopoes wore solid golden crowns on their heads — and they soon learned the price of that, eh? One day, all the beasts and birds came to pay homage to King Solomon, and the hoopoes came too, with a humble little request. What do you think it was? They begged Solomon to pray that they might trade their golden crowns for any other kind — for mankind hunted them without rest, shooting, trapping, snaring them for that gold, until life was no longer worth living. So Solomon prayed, and their golden crowns turned to feather crests, and ever since then they live as merry as crickets in an oven, and bear the honor of being called his Most Catholic Majesty’s own birds. Tita! Fill the commandant’s glass. Fray Gerundio, what are you whispering about down there, sir?”

Fray Gerundio had just murmured to his fellow Dominican, “O si sic omnia! Would that all gold in the world might turn to feathers the same way!”

“Then,” the other friar replied, sterner of face, “corrupt human nature would only invent some new bauble in a week, to kill and be killed over for no reason.”

“What was that, Fray Gerundio?” the bishop pressed.

“I only said it would be a blessing for the whole world if every man prayed for the same mercy as the hoopoes.”

“The world, sir? What do you know of the world? Go convert your Indians, and leave matters of state to your superiors. You must excuse him, señors,” he added, turning to the Dons and lowering his voice. “A pious, worthy man, but the son of a poor peasant, and — well, you understand. A little soft in the head, I fear, from too much fasting and late night prayers.” He tapped his own forehead knowingly, to signal that Fray Gerundio’s wits were not to be trusted.

The friar heard the insult, and smiled quietly. He was one of those rare, noble souls stirred to courage by the cruelty of his countrymen, like Las Casas and Gasca, who dared protest the evils of their age even before kings. True to his faith, he called the dark-skinned Indigenous and enslaved people his brothers, and defended their God-given freedom at a time when that belief was unheard of. The bishop’s petty insults were old news to him; he took them in silence and sipped his wine, as the conversation turned back to the state of the colony.

“They say,” said the colonel, “that this year’s treasure fleet sailing for Spain will be shamefully small.”

“What else?” the intendant huffed. “What do we have left to send, in the name of all the saints, since those accursed English Lutherans swept the whole place clean?”

“And even if we had treasure to ship,” the master mariner added, “what ships do we have left to carry it? That fiend in human flesh, Drake —”

“Ah!” the bishop winced. “Spare my ears, Don Pedro. Do not speak that man’s name here — it is a Tartarean, devilish name, unfit for polite company. Draco, a dragon, a serpent, the very symbol of Satan himself! He would devour our new Hercules, his Most Catholic Majesty, to get at the golden apples of the West. He deceived Eve with an apple, too — a wicked, wicked name, señors, a name that belongs in hell. Tita!”

“Um!”

“Fill my glass.”

“Nay,” the colonel swore a great oath, “this English serpent is of a far fiercer breed than Eden’s, I’ll warrant.”

“Your reason, señor, your reason?”

“Because this one would have sunk Eve to the bottom of the ocean before he let anyone but himself taste anything that glinted like gold.”

“Ah, very good, very good! But we laugh, brave señors, while the Church weeps. Alas for my poor flock!”

“And alas for their sheepfold! It will take four years to rebuild Cartagena. And Heaven only knows when we will get the blockhouse back up, while the king pours every last coin of the Indies into fending off that supposed English Armada. The town is as bare as an Indian’s back right now.”

“Baptista Antonio the surveyor sent a formal report home to the king with me, laying out how defenseless we are. But writing a report and acting on it are two very different things, as every statesman knows. Heaven grant we get orders to fortify the harbor on the next fleet, or we will be at the mercy of every English pirate who sails by!”

“Ah, that blockhouse!” the bishop sighed. “It was a villainous trick of theirs, demanding a hundred and ten thousand ducats ransom for the town! After they burned and looted half of it already — and made me dine with them, ah! Sit between that serpent Drake and his lieutenant general, drink my own private Xeres wine I had laid down nine years ago, and those shameless heretics even offered to take me back to England if I turned Lutheran, find me a wife, make an honest man of me — ah! And then they demanded even more ransom for the priory and the fort! Perfidious!”

“Well,” the colonel shrugged, “the cunning rascals had us beat on the fine print. We forgot to specify that the ransom only covered the town proper in our agreement. Who would have dreamed they would pull such a trick?”

“So I told the prior when he came to me to borrow a thousand crowns to pay his share. It was Heaven’s will, as sudden as a thunderbolt, to be borne patiently. Every man must bear his own burden. How could I lend him anything?”

“Your grace’s savings were all carried off by them long before,” the intendant said, though he knew perfectly well the bishop had hidden most of his hoard away.

“Just so! All my scanty life savings, stolen from me in my lonely old age. Ah señors, if my dear friend the Marquess of Santa Cruz — whom I pray for daily — had not sent us warning that those wretches were coming, we would have gone down quick into the pit like the sinners of old. I might have saved a little something for myself, but I thought only of others, and forgot my own safety, alas!”

“Warning or no, we had no right to be beaten by a handful of men,” the master mariner grumbled. “It is a shame that will hang over our names for generations.”

“Do you dare cast slurs on the courage and conduct of his Most Catholic Majesty’s soldiers?” the colonel snapped.

“I? No — but we were soundly beaten, even behind our own barricades, that is the plain truth.”

“Beaten, sir? You dare call the fortunes of war a defeat? What more could our governor have done? We lined every road with poisoned caltrops, posted Indian archers to guard the passes, blocked the lanes with earthen ramparts, raked the approaches with culverins and arquebuses! What familiar spirit did we have to warn us those villains would march along the empty sea beach, instead of coming down the high road like proper Christian men?”

“Ah!” the bishop nodded solemnly. “Diabolical intuition, no doubt. Satan himself aids those who serve him, and for my part I can only attribute the disaster to black magic. Those men are possessed by every devil in hell.”

“Well your grace,” the colonel agreed, “there must have been devilry in it. How else would men dare run straight into the mouths of our cannons, fire point blank at our noses, and leap right over those huge earthen ramparts like they were no more than small hurdles?”

“Doubtless driven mad by the fiends inside them,” the bishop confirmed.

“And then they came screaming blasphemies, cutting through us with sword and pike! I saw Lieutenant General Carlisle hew down that noble young ensign, your grace’s sister’s son’s nephew, who was armed cap-a-pie, with a single stroke of his blade. Is that not black magic? And that furious, blaspheming Lutheran Captain Young — I saw him catch our general by the head, even after the illustrious Don Alonzo had given him a grievous wound, throw him to the ground and capture him. Is that not sorcery?”

“Well,” the master mariner cut in, “if you are looking for magic, what do you say to them marching right through the flank fire of our galleys, with eleven of our cannons and two hundred arquebuses firing on them, like it was no more than a swarm of mosquitoes? Some of my men said they fired too high — but that was the English rascals’ trick, lying low on the tidal beach. But señor commandant, even if Satan taught them that trick, did he also teach them to carry pikes a full foot longer than ours?”

“Ah well,” the bishop sighed. “We are sacked. San Domingo is worse off than we are, and St. Augustine in Florida too. All that is left for a poor priest like me is to sail back to Spain, and beg his majesty’s pious clemency to grant a little relief for the poor of Our Lady. Perhaps — who knows? — I might be transferred to a quieter posting, now that I am old and tired after so many labors. Tita! Fill our glasses. I have saved a little something, as you may all have done, from the general wreck. The flock will be well cared for when I am gone, señors. Heaven’s mercies are infinite. New cities will rise from the ashes of the old, new mines pour their treasure into the sanctified laps of the faithful, new Indians flock to the life-giving standard of the Cross to take on the easy yoke of the Church, and —”

“And where will I be then? Ah, where? I long to rest, to depart this world. Tita! Hang my hammock. Señors, you must excuse the weaknesses of old age. Fray Gerundio, go to bed!”

The Dons rose to take their leave, as the bishop drowsily maundered to himself: “Farewell. Life is short. We shall meet in heaven at last. Are there really no more pearls left?”

“Not a single oyster, nor a grain of gold either,” the intendant told him.

“Ah well. A dinner of herbs where love is better than — Tita!”

“My breviary — ah, man’s gratitude is so short-lived, I had hoped — Have you seen anything of Doña Bovadilla?”

“No.”

“Ah, she promised — but no matter. A little keepsake, a gold cross, an emerald ring, nothing fancy — I forget. And why would I care for worldly wealth! Tita! Bring me the casket.”

Once his guests were gone, the old man mumbled prayers from his breviary while he fumbled over a hoard of jewels and gold, his eyes dull and greedy with the covetousness of old age.

“This might buy me a cardinal’s red hat yet! *Omnia Romae venalia* — everything is for sale in Rome. Put it away Tita, and do not stare too hard at it, child. Do not fall into temptation. The love of money is the root of all evil; and Heaven, in his love for the Indian, has made him poor in this world so he may be rich in faith. Ah — ugh — so.”

The old miser clambered into his hammock. Tita drew the mosquito net over him, wrapped a second net around her own head, and curled up on the floor to sleep. Master and slave were soon snoring in perfect unison, a droning bass harmony to the high thin whine of the mosquitoes.

It was long past midnight, and the moon had set. The sentries who had tramped their rounds and challenged passers-by until they thought their officers were fast asleep had slipped away from their posts, out of the dark, and fallen asleep as soundly as the bishop.

Two long, narrow white wherries glided out from behind the isolated rocks of Morro Grande, five hundred yards astern of the galleon. They were nearly invisible on the glimmering surface of the bay, their hulls painted bright white, and even a vigilant sentry would only have spotted them by the faint trail of phosphorescent sparks rippling along their sides.

The bishop stirred, half awake. The wine was wearing off, his shoulders had slipped down in the hammock, his heels were in the air, and his head ached. He sat upright, called for Tita, and pointed out the window.

“Put another pillow under my head, child. What is that out there? A fish?”

Tita glanced out. She did not think it was a fish — but she did not say so. She had her own reasons for not waking the bishop.
