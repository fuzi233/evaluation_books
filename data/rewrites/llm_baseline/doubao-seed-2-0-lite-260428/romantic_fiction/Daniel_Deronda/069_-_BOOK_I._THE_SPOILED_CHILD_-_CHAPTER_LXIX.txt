*The human nature unto which I felt That I belonged, and reverenced with love, Was not a punctual presence, but a spirit Diffused through time and space, with aid derived Of evidence from monuments, erect, Prostrate, or leaning toward their common rest In earth, the widely scattered wreck sublime Of vanished nations.* — WORDSWORTH, *The Prelude*

By early October, Sir Hugo Mallinger’s long-planned autumn stay at his Diplow estate had spread soft, easy cheer across every corner of the neighborhood: from the gilded drawing rooms of Brackenshaw Hall to the neat, linen-draped shop parlors in Wanchester. A genial, open-hearted Liberal of old, well-lined lineage, Sir Hugo loved nothing more than to show himself affable to everyone around him, confident that reform would never erode that quiet, universal English fondness for the comfort of long-established, gentle rank. He turned Diplow into the most welcoming house in the shire, extending invitations to gray-haired Wanchester solicitors and fresh-faced young village curates alike, careful to curate his guest lists so no one ever felt they’d been lumped in with company that would make their invitation feel like a trivial afterthought. Easygoing Lord Brackenshaw would not have minded sharing a dinner table with Attorney Robinson, but Robinson would have been quietly stung to find himself seated beside a crowd of men he’d have seen as his peers rather than his social betters, and Sir Hugo knew the fine lines of local sentiment well enough to win widespread affection for himself while gifting others nothing but warm pleasure.

The rector of Pennicote, Mr. Gascoigne, found a reception at Diplow that was the polar opposite of the haughty, grudging tolerance he’d endured during the late Grandcourt’s reign. It was not that Sir Hugo particularly liked the rector: it was that he was determined to make a public show of friendship to him for the sake of the new Mrs. Grandcourt, whose quiet, unspoken suffering had roused his chivalric protectiveness more and more with every passing month. The main reason he cared for her so deeply was one he could not even bring himself to share fully with his own wife, Lady Mallinger — he would never betray one woman’s secret to another, no matter how loyal his spouse was, a rare delicacy that spoke volumes for his quiet good manners.

When Deronda got engaged to Mirah, he knew he owed Sir Hugo, the fatherly friend who had raised him, full disclosure of his new position and plans, and chose to break the news in a letter. He had more than a hunch the older man would feel at the very least dissatisfied, if not heart sore, at this sharp turn in his life’s path. Reading bad news on a page, rather than hearing it spoken aloud, spared the recipient the risk of blurting out a hot, impatient remark they would regret later — and Deronda dreaded that kind of fleeting verbal clash that could turn an otherwise forgivable hurt into something lasting and ugly.

Sir Hugo, though not completely caught off guard, was thoroughly vexed the second he finished the letter. His first instinct was to carry it straight to Lady Mallinger, who was certain to express the shocked distress he could then argue was unreasonable, splitting the weight of his discontent in two. Sure enough, when she wailed in astonishment that all Daniel’s brilliant talents, and the warm comfort of having him in their home, had led him to throw his lot in so completely with the Jewish people, the baronet was quick to soothe her.
“Oh nonsense, my dear! You can be sure Dan will never make a fool of himself. He’s got grand, wide-reaching views on Judaism — political ideas you can’t possibly follow. He’s far too sharp to ever sink under them.”

But when it came to the upcoming marriage, Lady Mallinger offered him no such easy counterbalance. The gentle, soft-spoken woman said without the slightest trace of bitterness that she’d never dreamed of such an outcome when she’d invited Mirah to sing at her musical soirees, and to give singing lessons to her youngest daughter Amabel. After a long, hesitant pause, she admitted the thought had crossed her mind that after a decent period of mourning, Daniel might marry Mrs. Grandcourt — it had always seemed so unlikely a coincidence that he’d happened to be in Genoa exactly when the late Grandcourt had drowned. Though she’d never been particularly fond of widows, she couldn’t help but murmur that that match would have been far better than Daniel tying his future entirely to the Jewish community.

Sir Hugo agreed so wholeheartedly that he could not dismiss the thought as a silly feminine fancy, and his ill humor at the destruction of his quiet, hopeful vision for Gwendolen had no outlet at all. He made Lady Mallinger swear not to breathe a word of the engagement to anyone, telling himself quietly: “If this is a cruel blow to that poor girl (meaning Gwendolen), the longer she goes without hearing it, the better, in her current fragile, nervous state. And she ought to hear it from Dan himself, not from gossiping third parties.” Between his prior knowledge and his own sharp guesswork, Sir Hugo had convinced himself he understood the full shape of the situation perfectly.

All the while, his family’s residence at Diplow let him keep up his gentle, fatherly attentions to Gwendolen, and Lady Mallinger, despite her small reservations about widows, was happy to help him look after her.

Gwendolen had moved back to Offendene, and had settled into her new home with a calm that far exceeded her poor mother’s most hopeful expectations. She was drifting through a soft, peaceful melancholy that came from renouncing all the old selfish demands she’d once made on life, and learning to treat every small ordinary good — even the loyalty of a stray dog that followed her on her garden walks — as a precious, unhoped-for gift. Who, after nearly falling into a lightless pit, would complain when they stepped out into sweet open air and bright daylight? She had begun to see every new day as a narrow escape, to greet the soft return of dawn and dusk, and far more than that, the star-like glimmer of a kind word or generous impulse cutting through the dark of her old guilt, as a small daily salvation that made even the hardest hardship feel bearable. Anyone who had ever looked inward and railed at their own failures as sharply as Hamlet did would understand that quiet, constant feeling of being rescued, and that was exactly the feeling wrapping around Gwendolen as she re-lived every terrible moment of her past: from the first alluring, false whispers of self-satisfaction that made her fight to throw off the hold of her conscience, to the last desperate, furious wave of hatred that had dragged her toward satisfying her worst impulses, while she’d sobbed and begged for the mercy of that very conscience she’d once abandoned. Now she clung to every single word Deronda had ever spoken to her about being saved from the worst of her own darkness, every phrase that had given her the strength to fight against the urge to sink into total self-loathing.

But she was buoyed even higher by the quiet certainty that she would see him soon. It never crossed her mind that his life might take him far from her: her desperate, all-consuming need for him blinded her to the separate, independent shape of his world, and she filled every corner of her imagined version of his life with his role as her guide and her savior. That passionate, egoistic habit of imagination is not unique to Gwendolen: we all tend to wrap even the people we love, even our idea of God, up in the narrow frame of what we need from them. The future she stepped toward, eager and unafraid, was one where she would spend every day growing into the better version of herself that he would hold out before her. Hadn’t he first appeared to her as a quiet corrective presence, a man she’d resented at first sight, and come to trust and love with every fiber of her being? She could not imagine that reliance ever ending: to her, it felt as steady as the ground beneath her feet, the only firm surface she could walk on without fear of falling.

It was not long before Deronda came to Diplow, far more conveniently located for quick trips back to London than the Abbey. He had been planning to take Ezra and Mirah to a mild, warm spot on the coast while he arranged a new home they could move into as husband and wife, where they could both care for Mirah’s brother together. But Ezra had begged not to be moved anywhere, unless it was to join them on a voyage to the East. All small social demands were growing more of a burden to him every day, but his mind clung to the vision of that journey with a wild, unearthly joy. Deronda, hoping to marry no later than two months out, needed to sit down with Sir Hugo to go over his full resources and arrange all his affairs, and that gave him a reason not to delay his visit. But he had an equally pressing reason to come: his promise to Gwendolen.

His own joy with Mirah carried a sharp, aching edge of anxiety he could not shake, a paradox no romantic trope of the perfectly happy lover could erase. Human experience is always full of such incongruities, after all, and Deronda’s care for Gwendolen was no betrayal of Mirah: it was the very fullness of the nature that made his love for Mirah worth having. For what is love, even for the person you hold dearest? It is an enfolding of endless, tender cares that are far more precious than any trivial, selfish joy you could ever know apart from them.

Deronda visited Diplow twice, and saw Gwendolen both times — but he left for London without ever uttering a word about his engagement, or the massive shift it was making in his future. He blamed himself bitterly, but every heavy conversation that risks inflicting pain seems to wait for the exact right turn of phrase, the exact right mood from the other person, to fall gently. The first time they met, Gwendolen was so full of things she needed to tell him, brimming with questions about how she ought to arrange her life, what she could learn to make herself less ignorant, how she could be kinder to everyone around her, how she could make amends for all her old selfishness and strip that flaw out of her for good, that Deronda could not bear to set aside her immediate, urgent need for his guidance just to talk about his own life, much less drive a sharp wound into her when she was leaning on him so hard for strength. The second time he arrived fully resolved to steer their conversation toward a soft, gradual lead-up to the news, he found her sunk in a deep, crushing depression, overwhelmed by cruel, vivid old memories that felt far more real and tangible than any new, hopeful future she could try to build. She sobbed hysterically, over and over, that he would always despise her, and he could do nothing but speak soft, soothing words of encouragement. When she lifted her head at last, her lashes still beaded with tears, that soft, childlike, curious look back on her face, it was impossible to lay another heavy burden on her shoulders.

As the days slipped by, he knew it was a pressing, unavoidable duty to tell her the truth. Gwendolen had never once thought to ask about his personal affairs, it had never even crossed her mind to wonder why he’d been in Genoa that fateful day — and that total, unknowing innocence would make a sudden revelation that determined the whole shape of his future hit her twice as hard. If he let someone else break the news to her, she would feel he’d treated her with cruel, unthinking disregard. He could not bear to write it in a letter: his tenderness could not stand the thought of her reading his virtual farewell alone, in an empty room, imagining his words full of cold, giddy joy for his own happiness and total indifference to her pain. He set off for Diplow a third time, determined that no amount of discomfort or risk would make him turn back before he told her the truth.

This third time, Deronda found Hans Meyrick installed at Diplow, his easel set up in the drawing room, working on a Gainsborough-style portrait of Sir Hugo’s three daughters sitting on a grassy bank. He broke from painting to ramble over to Pennicote to sketch the village children, and had already grown quite friendly with the Gascoigne family. Hans seemed to have recovered all his old bright vivacity, but Deronda could see it was a little forced, the same way you can spot artificial blush on a lady’s cheek when it sits just a little too bright and unchanging. For all his deep grateful friendship and admiration for Deronda, Hans could not help a flicker of sharp irritation: open, impulsive natures like his always feel a little stung when a friend’s long-held secret comes out, and it turns out to be the exact opposite of what you’d hoped and guessed. For months he’d babbled freely to Deronda about his own crush on Mirah, never once stopping to wonder if Deronda might have feelings of his own, and the realization that Deronda had hidden his quiet rivalry all this time felt like a sharp, unexpected slap. Hans, to his credit, had not let that sore feeling change his behavior toward his fortunate friend — he’d told himself proudly that his own character had rung far truer than he’d ever expected it to, given that he’d always joked he was a dilettante in matters of virtue. But the little edge in his chat remained, and he was so wrapped up in the giddy joy of Deronda’s engagement that he had no idea of the weight of anxiety Deronda carried for Gwendolen, and spoke of her completely without hesitation.

“Hans, when did you get down here?” Deronda asked, joining him in the grounds as he sketched the bank and trees for his portrait background.

“Oh, ten days ago, before Sir Hugo even expected me! I ran down with Rex Gascoigne and stayed a couple of nights at the rectory. I’m up to my ears in all the local gossip now — I know exactly what the wheelwright’s parlor looks like, and I even sat through the infant school exam. Anna Gascoigne, the one with the sweet gentle upper lip, escorted me, else three barefoot urchins and the village idiot would have mobbed me for my long hair and generally un-Pennicote appearance. The whole village is pure idyll, the only flaw being a broad-shouldered, dark-cloaked curate who should have been selling heavy drapery instead of preaching. The Gascoignes are perfect — related to that Vandyke duchess, you know. I caught a glimpse of her, all in black robes, from a distance the other day, though she doesn’t receive visitors.”

“She wasn’t staying at the rectory?” Deronda asked.

“No, but they took me over to Offendene to tour the old house, and naturally I met the duchess and her whole family. I assume you’ve been over there, you know all their business?”

“Yes, I’ve been there,” Deronda said, quiet and even.

“Fine old place, perfect setting for a widow with romantic, dramatic fortunes. I think I’ve worked out there was once a little romance between her and my friend Rex, too.”

“Wait — before she married Grandcourt?” Deronda said, suddenly very interested. “They’d only been at Offendene a year before the wedding. How did you figure that out?”

“Oh, I know what it’s like to be miserable, so I’ve got a sharp eye for the signs of unhappiness in other people. Rex never sets foot on Offendene property, hasn’t seen the duchess once since she came back widowed. And Miss Gascoigne let a tiny little detail slip when we were talking about old charade nights — I was hamming up all my old nonsense to amuse the younger kids — that made it perfectly clear Rex was once hovering around his fair cousin close enough to get properly singed. I don’t know what she felt about it. I suppose the duke swept in and carried her off. That’s always the way it goes for the exceptionally good young men, right? Now I see why Gascoigne’s always talking about making the law his mistress and staying a bachelor forever. But those are young man’s resolutions. Since the late duke didn’t drown for your sake, maybe it turns out he drowned for my friend Rex’s. Who knows?”

“Is it absolutely necessary that Mrs. Grandcourt remarry at all?” Deronda said, already ready to point out that Hans’s track record for inventing love lives for other people didn’t give him the right to plot Gwendolen’s future.

“You monster!” Hans laughed, throwing his sketch pencil down playfully. “Do you want her to wear mourning weeds for you for the rest of her life, burn herself on a perpetual suttee pyre while you’re alive and happy somewhere else?”

Deronda said nothing, but he looked so sharply pained that Hans quickly changed the subject, and when he was alone he shrugged to himself, thinking there really must have been something more serious going on between Deronda and the widowed duchess than Mirah would ever be happy to hear about. “Why didn’t she fall in love with me?” he thought, laughing at his own folly. “She’d have had no rivals. No woman has ever wanted to sit and discuss theology with me.”

No wonder Deronda flinched under that light, lashing joke. It touched on sensibilities already quivering in anticipation of the pain he knew he was about to inflict, the kind of remark that makes the thing you’re already dreading feel twice as real, when someone else voices it out loud. But he had come down this time fully resolved not to evade the trial any longer. The next day, he mounted his horse and rode for Offendene. He had sent word ahead that he planned to call, and ask if she could receive him, and he found her waiting for him in the old drawing room, the very room where so many of the most searing crises of her life had unfolded.

She looked far less sad than he’d seen her since her husband’s death. There was no smile on her face, but a quiet, steady self-possession, a world away from the sobbing, despairing mood he’d left her in last time. She noticed the quiet sadness etched into his face right away, and no sooner had they sat down, him across the room from her at a small distance, than she spoke.
“You were afraid to come see me last time, because I was so full of grief and despair. But I’m not like that today. I’ve been sorry for it ever since. I’ve made it a point to keep my hope up, to be as cheerful as I can, so I never give you pain on my account again.”

The unwonted soft sweetness in her voice and her face at that moment made the cruel task ahead of Deronda feel sharper than a knife. But he knew he had to start.
