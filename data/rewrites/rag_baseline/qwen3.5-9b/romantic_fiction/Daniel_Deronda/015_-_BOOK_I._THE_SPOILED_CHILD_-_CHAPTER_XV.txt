"Festina lente"—celerity tempered with delay.

Gwendolen had spent her time abroad intoxicated by the new thrill of gambling, conjuring herself as an empress of fortune. From her recent experiences, she carried a vague conviction that in this chaotic world, actions held no intrinsic meaning; they were merely diversions. Alongside her, a shadowy entity known as Grapnell & Co. had sought to reign in the realm of luck, equally intent on amusement regardless of cost, thereby orchestrating a painful upheaval in her domestic sphere. Thus, she returned home, dragging behind her, against her will, a necklace she had pawned and which someone else had subsequently redeemed.

As Gwendolen journeyed back to England, Grandcourt was approaching to claim her. Yet he approached in his own distinct fashion—devoid of the frantic haste one might expect via express train from Diplow to Leubronn, where her whereabouts were rumored. Instead, he lingered at Baden-Baden, entangled by the company of Russian acquaintances, making various appointments he ultimately broke when the pull of Leubronn became irresistible. Grandcourt's passions were intermittent, flickering flames that rarely burned hot. Yet vast quantities of life proceed without such fervor: countless cravats are meticulously tied, dinners attended, speeches delivered extolling the virtues of august personages, all devoid of the zest born of intense desire. A man can maintain a formidable appearance in high society, feigning classical knowledge, scientific reserves, political opinions, and the full sentimentality of the English gentleman, all for a negligible expenditure of vital energy. Furthermore, he may display stubborn persistence at a low level, occasionally manifesting sudden impulses that possess a deceptive aura of demonic strength simply because they appear inexplicable. Their secret, however, often lies merely in the absence of regulated channels for the soul to flow; without the ducts of habit, human nature easily devolves into ooze and mud, yielding under pressure only a spurt or a puddle.

Grandcourt was not entirely displeased by Gwendolen's flight from the splendid opportunity he had proffered. The act possessed a certain piquancy. He preferred to believe it stemmed from resentment regarding his careless conduct in Cardell Chase, a behavior which, upon reflection, appeared rather cool. To have drawn her so near to a tender admission, only to watch her walk headlong away from further chances of securing her consent—which he had made clear he was requesting—was enough to provoke a spirited girl. And to be worthy of mastering such a spirit, she required just that spark. Undoubtedly, she expected him to pursue her, and so did he. Yet for a full week, he took no steps toward departure, nor did he inquire of Mr. Lush where Miss Harleth had vanished. Mr. Lush felt a triumph tinged with deep suspicion; Grandcourt had uttered not a word to him about her, maintaining a neutrality akin to an alligator, leaving one unable to predict what might churn within his slowly rotating mind. Nevertheless, postponing a decision created space for the dissipation of Grandcourt's energy.

The guests at Diplow harbored greater curiosity than their host. Silence regarding Miss Harleth was deafening. Could it be credible that she had refused Mr. Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, a vivacious middle-aged woman brimming with inquiry, suddenly felt compelled to embark on a round of calls with Mrs. Torrington, visiting the rectory, Offendene, and Quetcham. Through this circuit, she not only doubled her efforts but also gleaned from the Arrowpoints that Miss Harleth had departed for Leubronn to join old friends, the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The immediate agitation and disappointment of Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes had crystallized into a collective wish that Gwendolen's disappearance should not be construed as eccentric or requiring secrecy. Indeed, the rector's mind entertained the possibility that the marriage was merely deferred; Mrs. Davilow had hesitated to reveal the bitter determination with which Gwendolen had spoken. Despite his practical acumen, some of his experience had hardened into maxims and quotations. As Amaryllis fled, she desired her hiding place remain unknown; yet in this age of steam, the aphorism that love finds a way "over the mountain and over the wave" holds truth without hyperbole. Gwendolen, the rector concluded, was an Amaryllis of excellent sense but coquettish daring; the question remained whether she had dared too much.

Lady Flora, returning laden with news of Miss Harleth, saw no impediment to attempting to electrify Mr. Grandcourt by whispering the rumor of his thwarted adoration during dinner. She dropped a few suggestive hints, and Grandcourt listened with quiet attentiveness. The following day, he instructed Lush to engineer a polite pretext for dissolving the party at Diplow within another week, citing an impending yacht trip to the Baltic or beyond. It was impossible to remain at Diplow like a prisoner on parole amidst a crowd he had never desired. Lush required no clearer signal that Grandcourt was bound for Leubronn, though he might travel with the sluggish momentum of a creeping billiard ball, sticking to the path. Mr. Lush intended to render himself indispensable to accompany his patron, a strategy that succeeded; Gwendolen's repulsion for him was a fact that only amused Grandcourt, rendering him all the more willing to keep Lush perpetually at hand.

Thus, it came to pass that Grandcourt arrived at the *Czarina* on the fifth day after Gwendolen had left Leubronn, discovering there his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, accompanied by his family, including Deronda. It is seldom a pleasure, for either the reigning power or the heir presumptive, when their separate affairs—a touch of gout in one, a streak of willfulness in the other—conspire to bring them to the same locale. Sir Hugo was a man of easy temper, tolerant of differences and defects alike. However, a divergent viewpoint regarding the settlement of family estates fretted him more than disputes over church discipline or the ballot; faults were less forgivable when committed by someone whose existence was inconvenient. In no scenario could Grandcourt have been a nephew to Sir Hugo's liking; yet as the presumptive heir to the Mallinger estates, he embodied the baronet's chief grievance: the lack of a son to inherit the lands, in no portion of which did Sir Hugo hold more than a life-interest. For in the ill-advised settlement dictated by his father, Sir Francis, even Diplow, with its modest acreage, had been left under identical conditions as the ancient, wide inheritance of the two Toppings—Diplow, where Sir Hugo had hunted and lived through many seasons in his youth, and where his wife and daughters ought to have retired upon his death.

This grievance had intensified with the passing years. Lady Mallinger, after bearing three daughters in rapid succession, had remained childless for eight years, now surpassing forty without producing another girl. Sir Hugo, nearly twenty years her senior, stood at an age where, despite the fashionable retardation of everything from dinners to marriages, a man's hopefulness begins to show signs of wear, unless restored by a second childhood.

Indeed, he had begun to despair of a son. Consequently, the confirmation of Grandcourt's interest in the estates rendered his image and presence increasingly unwelcome. Yet, paradoxically, this circumstance inclined Sir Hugo to ensure their relationship remained as cordial as possible. It spurred him to dwell on a plan that had grown alongside his disappointment: to secure Diplow as a future residence for Lady Mallinger and her daughters, preserving this pretty fragment of family inheritance for his own offspring despite the lack of a male heir. His knowledge of his nephew's disposition encouraged the belief that Grandcourt might consent to a transaction granting him a substantial sum of ready money in exchange for his prospective interest in the domain and its attached lands. Should the unhoped-for son eventually be born, the money would have been squandered, and Grandcourt would have been compensated for relinquishing interests that proved worthless; yet Sir Hugo dismissed this risk as nil, having recently husbanded his fortune through mining operations and lease sales, preparing himself for such an outlay.

This objective made him vigilant against quarreling with Grandcourt. Years prior, while improving the Abbey and needing Grandcourt's concurrence to fell an obstructive mass of timber on the demesne, he had congratulated himself on finding no active spite in his nephew's peculiar mind. Nothing had since occurred to deepen their hatred beyond what was compatible with perfect politeness or mutually beneficial accommodation.

On his side, Grandcourt viewed his uncle as a superfluity and a bore, believing the world would improve once Sir Hugo was expunged from the ledger. Yet, informed through Lush—a useful medium—of the baronet's inclinations regarding Diplow, he was gratified to have the option of the money in his mind. Even if he deemed acceptance unlikely, his sense of power would be flattered by the capacity to refuse what Sir Hugo desired. The hinted transaction influenced his motives for requesting a year's tenancy of Diplow, a request that had annoyed Sir Hugo, for the excellent hunting in the vicinity might deter Grandcourt from parting with his chance of future possession; a man possessing two places, one with inferior hunting, naturally desires a third where the game is better. Moreover, Lush had suggested to Sir Hugo the probability that Grandcourt would woo and win Miss Arrowpoint, in which case ready money might lose its allure.
