The sun-drenched lawn unfolded like a canvas of emerald and gold, stretching smoothly towards the vast expanse of the ocean. A delicate border of scarlet geranium and coleus encircled the grass, punctuated by cast-iron vases in a rich, chocolate hue, their petunia and ivy geranium garlands dancing above the neatly raked gravel.

Halfway between the cliff's edge and the charming wooden house – its walls painted a warm, chocolate brown, with the tin roof of the veranda striped in vibrant shades of yellow and brown – two imposing targets stood sentinel behind a lush backdrop of shrubs. Across the lawn, opposite the targets, a colorful tent had been set up, accompanied by benches and garden seats. A gathering of ladies in their finest summer attire and gentlemen in their Sunday best stood on the lawn or sat on the benches, their conversations occasionally interrupted by the sound of a slender girl in starched muslin emerging from the tent, bow in hand, as she aimed her arrow at one of the targets.

Newland Archer stood on the veranda of the house, gazing down at the scene below with a sense of wistfulness. On either side of the shiny painted steps, two large blue china flowerpots perched atop bright yellow stands, each containing a spiky green plant. Below the veranda, a broad border of blue hydrangeas edging red geraniums created a picturesque frame. Through the French windows of the drawing rooms, which he had recently passed through, glimpses of glassy parquet floors, adorned with chintz poufs, dwarf armchairs, and velvet tables laden with delicate trinkets in silver, caught his eye.

The Newport Archery Club's annual meeting had taken place at the Beauforts' residence for years, and today was no exception. The sport, which had once reigned supreme alongside croquet, was slowly being eclipsed by the rising popularity of lawn tennis, deemed too rough and unsophisticated for polite society. Yet, the elegance of the bow and arrow remained unrivaled in showcasing refined beauty and poise.

As Archer watched, he felt a pang of disconnection from the world around him. It was Newport that had first made him realize the extent to which his own perspective had shifted.
