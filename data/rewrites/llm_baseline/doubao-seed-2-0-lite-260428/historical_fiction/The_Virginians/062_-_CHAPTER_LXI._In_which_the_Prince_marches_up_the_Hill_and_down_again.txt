Every loyal Briton of spirit, we may safely say, burns with righteous indignation when they hear the tale of George Warrington’s conduct towards that gallant, gracious Prince—beloved son of the best of monarchs, and Captain-General of all His Majesty’s armies. What inestimable favour the young man spurned! What golden path to rank and distinction he cast underfoot!

Will Esmond, whose coarse tongue was never short of the most blistering oaths, poured out his strongest curses on his cousin’s head, crowing with delight that the accursed young Virginian Mohock was busy cutting his own throat. His cousin Castlewood merely shrugged and drawled that a savage gentleman had every right to scalp himself if he pleased; then added, with a touch of reluctant charity, that no doubt George had heard more than enough of war-whoops and slaughter in Braddock’s disastrous campaign, and had no stomach left for fighting.

Will rejoiced that the younger Warrington brother had long since gone to the dogs, and was chuffed to see the elder following close on his heels. The next time he met George, he swore, he would tell him exactly what every man of sense in London thought of his craven folly.
“If you dare to insult George, you had best make very sure his brother Harry is out of earshot first!” Lady Maria snapped— a retort that earned her a fresh volley of oaths from Will, directed at both the Warrington twins.
“Tut, tut, tut!” my Lord Castlewood cut in, flapping a languid hand. “No more of this squabbling. We cannot all be warriors in the family, after all.”
“I never heard your lordship lay any claim to the title!” Maria shot back.
“Never, my dear, quite the contrary!” he smiled. “Will is our household champion, and one fighting fool is more than enough for the house. I dare say the two Mohocks follow the same rule: George is the bookish one, and Harry is the man born for battle. What a pity it was, Will, that when you were set on picking a quarrel, you did not have George in your grip instead of the other brother!”
“Your lordship’s hand is famous enough—at piquet,” Will’s mother put in dryly.
“It is a very pretty hand,” my lord agreed, holding up his slim fingers to admire them with a simper. “My glove was the exact size of my Lord Hervey’s, if you will credit it. Yes, as you say, mine is far fitter for cards than for war. Yours, my Lady Castlewood, is deuced deft with a scandal too. How I bless the day you bestowed that hand on my lamented father!” He was never loath to indulge in this cut and thrust of sarcasm, his cool head letting him outwit every last member of his family with ease.

When Madame de Bernstein heard of her nephew’s blunder, she flew into a towering rage, stormed through her household scolding every servant within earshot, and would have given George a piece of her mind too—only she found she no longer had the fire of her younger days. What was more, she had grown a little afraid of her steady, quiet nephew, and treated him with a deference she never showed anyone else.
“You will never make your fortune at court now, nephew!” she groaned, when George came to wait on her soon after the incident.
“It was never my wish to do so, madam,” George replied, with a quiet, stately courtesy.
“Your wish was to help Harry? You might have done him a world of good, years from now, if you had accepted the Duke’s offer. Princes do not love to have their favours thrown back in their faces, and I do not wonder his Royal Highness was offended.”
“General Lambert said the very same thing,” George admitted, turning a little red. “I see now I was in the wrong. But you must remember, I had never stepped inside a court before, and I was never likely to shine there.”
“Very likely not, my good nephew,” the old lady said, taking a pinch of snuff. “And what then?”
“I never craved that sort of glory. I can be perfectly happy without it. When his Royal Highness spoke to me—most kindly, I own—all I could think was that I should make a very poor soldier, and Harry would make an excellent one. He has a hundred qualities for the profession I lack entirely, and would serve his commanding officer a thousand times better than I ever could. Suppose the Duke was in battle, and his horse was shot, as my poor colonel’s was on our Virginian frontier: would he not fare far better with a beast bold and strong enough to carry him anywhere, than one too faint-hearted to bear his weight?”
“Faith, his Royal Highness’s charger must be a very strong one, my dear!” the Baroness chuckled.
“Expende Hannibalem,” George muttered with a shrug. “Our Hannibal weighs no trifling amount.”
“I do not follow you and your Hannibal, sir,” she said.
“When Mr. Wolfe and General Lambert remonstrated with me just as you have done, madam,” George laughed, “I gave them the very same defence. I told them I had offered the Prince the best soldier in our family, and they both allowed my absurd blunder had at least some small excuse. Who knows but they may set me right with his Royal Highness? The little taste of battle I saw was enough to show me my genius never runs that way. We all went to see that new Scottish play everyone is raving about the other night, and when young Norval declared he ached to follow some warlike lord to the field, I thought to myself: that is my Harry to the life—except Harry never brags. He is pining for a red coat so badly he will soon enlist for a shilling if we do not watch him. He keeps a map of Germany spread out on his table everywhere he goes, and follows every move the King of Prussia makes. He fears neither man nor devil. As for me, I love my books and my quiet best, and would far rather read of battles in Homer or Lucan than fight them.”
“Then what ever made you turn soldier at all, my dear? Why did you not send Harry with Braddock, instead of going yourself?”
“My mother loved her younger son best,” George said, his face darkening a little. “Besides, with the enemy marching on our borders, it was my duty, as head of the family, to go on campaign. If I had been a Scotsman twelve years ago, I should have been—”
“Hush, sir!” the old lady cut in, feigning fury while her face lit up with perfect pleasure.

George’s explanation might appease Madame de Bernstein, whose principles we fear were never particularly strict, but to the loyal, rigid hearts of Sir Miles Warrington and his lady, the young man’s conduct was a severe shock indeed.
“I should have thought,” Lady Warrington huffed, “from my sister Esmond Warrington’s letters, that my brother’s widow was a woman of sound sense, and had raised her sons to behave as proper gentlemen should. But what, Sir Miles, what, my dear Thomas Claypool, are we to make of an education that has ended in such a lamentable failure for both boys?”
“The elder seems to know a power of Latin, though, and speaks French and German too,” Mr. Claypool put in. “I heard him chatting with the Hanover Envoy at the Baroness’s rout last week. He jabbered French as easy as you please, and when he stumbled over High Dutch, the pair switched to Latin and talked away so fast the whole room stared at them.”
“It is not languages we care about, it is principles, Thomas Claypool!” the virtuous matron exclaimed. “What sort of principles can Mr. Warrington have, when he can reject an offer made him by his own Prince? If he can speak High Dutch, all the more reason he should have accepted his Royal Highness’s condescension and made himself useful in the campaign! Look at our son, look at little Miles!”
“Hold up thy head, Miley, my boy!” Sir Miles prodded his son.
“I trust, Sir Miles, that as a member of the House of Commons, and an English gentleman, you will wait on his Royal Highness’s levee tomorrow, and tell him plainly that if such an offer had been made to us for that child, we would have accepted it at once—though our boy is barely ten years old!”
“Faith, Miley, thou wouldst make a fine little drummer boy, wouldst thou not?” Sir Miles laughed. “Wouldst like to be a little soldier, Miley?”
“Anything, sir, anything! A Warrington ought to be ready at any moment to be cut to pieces for his sovereign!” the matron cried, pointing dramatically at the boy—who, as soon as he grasped what his mother was proposing, set up such a loud, wailing roar that he had to be carried off kicking by his nurse Screwby.

True to his wife’s orders, Sir Miles attended the Prince’s levee the next day, and poured out such a fulsome declaration of his loyalty and devotion that the Prince deigned to smile and reply: “No one ever doubted that Sir Miles Warrington would refuse any place offered to him.” It was a gracious compliment indeed, and Lady Warrington repeated it to every acquaintance she met for months, as proof of how implicitly the royal family trusted the loyalty of the Warrington line.

Accordingly, when the worthy couple next saw George, they greeted him with a ghastly, pitying kindness of the sort that one reserves for friends and relatives who have just committed some fatal public blunder: when they have lost a lawsuit, or walked in on a whole room full of people abusing them, or had their banker go bankrupt, or been forced to appear in the commercial pages of the London Gazette under the list of unfortunate debtors. Who does not know that faint, commiserating smile? Whose dear relations have not deplored him as good as dead, while he is still very much alive? If you have never scraped through such a scrape, if you never sowed a handful of wild oats, if you have always been perfectly fortunate, perfectly good, perfectly careful, and never let an imprudent word slip your lips—then you are a stuffy old wiseacre who would never waste your time on an idle novel, and this tale was never written for you.

It was hardly just of Sir Miles to turn on George for rejecting the Prince’s offer, for he had heartily approved of George’s plan to avoid a military career entirely. It was for that very purpose that Madam Esmond had written to him from Virginia: George had announced his intention of entering the Temple to study the law, and qualify himself for the magisterial and public duties that would fall to him in time as head of his family. No one applauded the resolution more than Sir Miles, who introduced George to a leading barrister of good reputation, under whose guidance the young man settled down to read at a leisurely, steady pace. Madam Esmond wrote back from Virginia to voice her full approval, agreeing wholeheartedly that the British Constitution was the envy of the whole world, and the proper object of every English gentleman’s study. The only part of the plan she objected to was the idea that George would have to sit down to dinner in the Temple’s great hall, carving shoulder of mutton and drinking small beer out of tin pannikins, side by side with rough, shabbily dressed students who wore gowns no better than a parish clerk’s.

Harry shared every bit of that indignation. Anything was good enough for him, he declared: he was a younger son, and prepared to rough it anywhere. But George, togged out in a student’s gown, eating off dirty pewter alongside three nobodies of no family or fortune! Harry could not bear the thought of his brother condescending so far, and fretted that George should never sit anywhere but at the highest table of the hall. He was even disappointed that a plan his mother hinted at in her letters was not feasible: to petition the Master of the Temple, and inform him that Mr. George Warrington was a gentleman of noble birth and vast property in the Virginia colonies, and ought only to be seated alongside the very best company in the hall. When he laid this proposal before their new coffee-house friend Mr. Spencer, the man burst into such roars of laughter that he could barely breathe; no record in the Warrington papers survives of any application ever being made to the Master on that absurd subject.

Alongside his beloved literary and historical studies, George pored over English law, attended the courts at Westminster where he heard great orators like Henley, Pratt, and Murray hold forth, and haunted the public galleries of the two Houses of Parliament, that great school of eloquence and patriotism. Gradually he made the acquaintance of many members of Parliament and distinguished barristers, who came to know him and speak of him as a young man of excellent parts and gentle breeding. Their kind reports were so complimentary that his good uncle’s heart softened towards him, and his cousins Dora and Flora began to smile on him again.

This full reconciliation dated from the news that his Royal Highness the Duke of Cumberland had been defeated by the French at the battle of Hastenbeck, and had signed the notorious convention of Kloster-Zeven, a capitulation King George II flatly refused to ratify. As all the world knows, the Prince flung down his commission in disgrace, laid aside the commander’s baton (which he had never wielded with much luck or skill, it must be said), and never again appeared at the head of an army, or in public life. He would not suffer a single word of complaint against his father and sovereign to pass his lips, but as he retired with his honour smarting, and no longer had any interest or authority, or places to dole out to favourites, Sir Miles’s anger against his nephew faded right along with his respect for the disgraced Prince.

One day, as George and Harry were walking through St. James’s Park with their friend General Lambert, they came across the Prince in plain civilian clothes, no star pinned to his coat, and bowed low. The Prince stopped to speak to them.
He asked Lambert how he liked serving under my Lord Ligonier, his new commanding officer at the Horse Guards, and how he found his new duties. Then, with that remarkable memory for faces that runs in the royal family, he turned to George.
“You did well, sir, not to come with me when I asked you last spring.”
“I was sorry then, sir,” George replied, bowing very low. “And I am far sorrier now.”
“Thank you, sir,” the Prince said, touched his hat, and walked away.

Harry wrote the whole story down in a letter to his mother in Virginia, and the anecdote made such an impression on Madam Esmond Warrington that she repeated it hundreds of times over the rest of her life, until every last one of her friends and acquaintances knew the tale by heart, and no doubt grew heartily tired of hearing it.

The three friends walked on through the park, heading towards the Strand where they had business to attend to. Lambert pointed up at the great stone lion perched on the roof of the Earl of Northumberland’s new house at Charing Cross.
“Harry Warrington!” he called out. “Your brother there is just like that lion.”
“Because he is as brave as one!” Harry grinned at once.
“Because I respect virgins?” George laughed.
“Because he is a stupid old lion,” Lambert went on, chuckling. “He turns his back on the East, and faces the setting sun. What earthly good do you think you will get by being polite to a man in utter, hopeless disgrace? Your uncle will be more angry with you than ever—and so am I, sir.” But his face was crinkled with his usual waggish grin, and he did not look the least bit angry.
