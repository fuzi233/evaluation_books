Elinor began with a steady, though measured tone. “I would be unworthy of the trust you’ve placed in me if I didn’t wish for it to continue—or if I weren’t still curious about what lies behind it. So I won’t apologize for bringing it up again.”

“Thank you,” Lucy replied warmly, her voice brightening. “You’ve eased my mind—because somehow, I’d begun to fear I’d offended you when I told you that Monday.”

“Offended me? How could you think such a thing?” Elinor said sincerely. “Nothing could be farther from my intention than to make you feel that way. Could you possibly have any reason to confide in me that wouldn’t be both an honor and a compliment?”

“And yet,” Lucy answered, her sharp eyes gleaming with quiet meaning, “I felt there was a coldness in your manner—something displeased—that made me uneasy. I was certain you were angry with me. Ever since, I’ve been blaming myself for daring to share such personal matters. But now I’m relieved to know it was only my own imagination, and that you truly don’t hold it against me. If you knew how much comfort it gave me to speak openly about something I carry in my heart every moment of every day, I’m sure your kindness would overlook everything else.”

“I can well believe it brought you relief,” Elinor said gently. “And I assure you, you’ll never regret having trusted me. Your situation is indeed unfortunate—you seem surrounded by obstacles, and you’ll need all the strength your love can give you to endure them. Mr. Ferrars, I understand, is entirely dependent on his mother.”

“He has only two thousand pounds of his own,” Lucy admitted. “To marry on that alone would be madness—though I wouldn’t hesitate to give up every other hope without a sigh. I’ve always lived with very little, and I could bear any hardship for Edward’s sake. But I love him too deeply to become the selfish cause of robbing him of what his mother might offer if he married someone she approves of. We must wait—perhaps many years. With almost any other man, that would be unbearable. But Edward’s love and faithfulness—I know nothing could ever take those away from me.”

“That certainty must mean everything to you,” Elinor said softly. “And he, no doubt, finds the same comfort in knowing yours remains unshaken. If your mutual devotion had weakened, as it so often does after long engagements, your plight would truly be pitiable.”

Lucy glanced up at her, but Elinor kept her expression carefully neutral, guarding against any hint of suspicion.

“Edward’s love,” Lucy continued, “has been tested thoroughly by our long separation—years, really—and it has held firm. I’d be unjust to question it now. I can honestly say he has never given me even a moment’s doubt.”

Elinor hesitated—was she meant to smile or sigh? She chose silence.

Lucy pressed on. “I confess I’m naturally jealous, especially given our different lives—his being so much more involved in society than mine, and us rarely seeing each other. That made me particularly alert. If he’d changed in the least when we met—any hesitation, any sadness I couldn’t explain, any talk of another woman, or less joy at Longstaple than before—I would have known instantly. I may not be especially observant in general, but in this case, I’d be impossible to fool.”

*All this,* Elinor thought, *is charming—but it means nothing to either of us.*

After a brief pause, she asked, “But what are your plans? Or do you have none beyond waiting for Mrs. Ferrars’s death—a prospect that feels grim and terrible? Is Edward truly willing to endure years of uncertainty rather than risk his mother’s anger by revealing the truth?”

“If we could be sure it would only be temporary!” Lucy sighed. “But Mrs. Ferrars is proud and stubborn. In her first rage, she might secure everything for Robert. The thought of that—especially for Edward’s sake—chills any desire I might have for hasty action.”

“And for your own sake, too,” Elinor added quietly. “Your selflessness seems to go beyond reason.”

Lucy looked at her, then fell silent.

“Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?” Elinor asked.

“No,” Lucy replied. “Never seen him. But I imagine he’s nothing like Edward—foolish and vain.”

“A great coxcomb!” Miss Steele exclaimed, catching the words during a sudden break in Marianne’s music. “Oh, they’re talking about their favorite gentlemen, I suppose.”

“No, sister,” Lucy said quickly, “you’re mistaken. Our favorite beaux are not coxcombs.”

“I can vouch for Miss Dashwood’s,” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “Her gentleman is one of the most modest, well-mannered young men I’ve ever met. But Lucy—well, she’s such a secretive little thing, who knows whom she likes?”

“Oh,” Miss Steele said, glancing around with a knowing look, “I daresay Lucy’s beau is just as modest and well-behaved as Miss Dashwood’s.”

Elinor flushed involuntarily. Lucy bit her lip and shot her sister a glare. A quiet tension settled over them.

At last, Lucy broke the silence, lowering her voice despite Marianne’s dramatic concerto filling the room. “I’ll tell you honestly about a plan I’ve recently considered—one that might help move things forward. In fact, I feel I must share it with you, since you’re directly involved. You’ve seen enough of Edward to know he’d prefer the church above all other professions. My idea is this: he should enter the clergy as soon as possible. Then, through your influence—your kindness toward him, and your regard for me—perhaps your brother could be persuaded to give him the Norland living. I understand it’s a good position, and the current holder isn’t expected to live long. That income would be enough for us to marry on, and we could leave the rest to time and fortune.”

“I’d always be glad,” Elinor replied, “to show my respect and friendship for Mr. Ferrars. But don’t you see that my influence would be entirely unnecessary? He is the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood—surely that alone would be enough recommendation to her husband.”

“But Mrs. John Dashwood wouldn’t approve of Edward taking holy orders,” Lucy said.

“Then I suspect my influence would be of little use,” Elinor replied calmly.

They sat in silence for several minutes. Then Lucy sighed deeply. “Perhaps the wisest course would be to end the engagement altogether. We’re trapped on every side. Though it would hurt us now, we might find greater happiness in the long run. But you won’t give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?”

“No,” Elinor answered, smiling faintly, though her heart stirred beneath the surface. “On such a matter, I certainly will not. You know well enough that my opinion would mean nothing to you unless it aligned with your own desires.”

“You misunderstand me,” Lucy said solemnly. “I value your judgment above all others. And I truly believe—if you were to say plainly, ‘I advise you to end your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it would bring greater happiness to both of you’—I would act on it immediately.”

Elinor blushed—not at the compliment, but at the insincerity of the woman who would soon be Edward’s wife. “That kind of praise terrifies me,” she said. “It places me in too powerful a position. To have the power to separate two people so deeply attached—it’s far too much for someone who is merely an outsider.”

“It’s precisely because you’re an outsider,” Lucy replied, a hint of irritation in her tone, “that your judgment would carry real weight. If you were biased by your own feelings, your opinion wouldn’t matter at all.”

Elinor decided not to respond—lest their conversation grow too intimate, too unrestrained. She resolved, silently, never to bring up the subject again.

Another long silence followed, until Lucy spoke once more. “Will you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?” she asked, regaining her usual composure.

“Certainly not,” Elinor replied.

“I’m sorry to hear that,” Lucy said, her eyes lighting up. “It would have been such a pleasure to meet you there! But I’m sure you’ll come anyway—your brother and sister will surely invite you.”

“It won’t be in my power to accept,” Elinor said.

“How unfortunate!” Lucy exclaimed. “I’d counted on seeing you. Anne and I are going to visit relatives in January—invited for years now. But I’m only going for Edward. He’ll be in London in February—otherwise, London holds no charm for me. I lack the spirit for it.”

Elinor was summoned to the card table as the first rubber ended, and thus the private conversation came to a close—neither woman reluctant, for nothing had been said to deepen their regard for one another. Elinor took her seat with a heavy heart, convinced that Edward was not only indifferent to the woman he was to marry, but also doomed to a marriage lacking even the chance of contentment. For only self-interest, she believed, could compel a woman to cling to an engagement she clearly saw as burdensome and unwanted.

From that point on, Elinor never brought up the subject again. When Lucy did—often, and with careful attention to detail, especially when sharing letters from Edward—Elinor responded with calm reserve, ending the conversation as politely as possible. Such exchanges, she felt, were indulgences neither deserved nor safe.

The Miss Steeles’ visit to Barton Park stretched far longer than originally intended. Their presence became increasingly welcome; Sir John refused to let them leave. Despite their commitments in Exeter and the urgent need to return each week, they stayed nearly two months—long enough to fully participate in the festive season, which demanded extra balls and grand dinners to mark its significance.
