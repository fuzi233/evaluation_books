Arthur Pendennis, buoyed by a sudden, intoxicating desire to experience "real" life, threw himself into the chaotic embrace of London's underworld. He fancied himself a grand explorer of human nature, wandering from one eccentric haunt to another. He delighted in observing the raw vitality of coal-heavers in their taverns, watching boxers lounge in inn parlors, and admiring honest citizens enjoying the river breeze. In his heart, he longed to share a pint with a celebrated pickpocket or raise a tankard with a band of burglars, provided fate granted him the chance.

He watched with amusement as Warrington listened intently to the ramblings of the "Tutbury Pet" or the "Brighton Stunner" at the Champion's Arms, and noted the genuine interest Warrington showed toward the rough crowd gathered at the Fox-under-the-Hill. Pen's knowledge of London's pubs and their patrons grew prodigious. He was the favorite guest of landlords and landladies alike, welcomed at the bar as if he belonged in a private club. He confessed a preference for this rough-and-tumble society over his own refined class, finding the manners of the upper crust annoying and their conversations tedious.

"In society," Pen would declare, "everyone dresses the same, eats the same, and says the same things. One young dandy at a club looks exactly like another; one debutante at a ball is indistinguishable from the rest. But *here*? There is character!" He preferred the company of England's strongest man, its finest beer-drinker, or that radical hatter who revered Thomas Hardy Thistlewood as history's greatest hero. "I'd rather drink gin-and-water than claret," he admitted, preferring the grit of a sanded floor in Carnaby Market to the polished marble of Mayfair. "I admit it: I prefer snobs." Indeed, Pen was a self-proclaimed social republican. Yet, while chatting with Jack and Tom, he never felt superior to them, though he likely savored the secret deference they paid him.

Pen followed Warrington to these dives with great enthusiasm. However, being considerably younger, Pen carried himself with a pompous stateliness that Warrington lacked. He was essentially a young prince in disguise, touring the provinces of his father's kingdom. To the locals, he appeared as a high-ranking chap, a fine fellow, a regular swell. He possessed an air of imperious good humor and royal frankness, despite being the heir-apparent to nothing more than twopence-halfpenny and descending from a lineage as humble as a gallypot. Humans are remarkably quick to accept roles assigned to them and eager to assert superiority over equals. Pen's condescension during this phase of his life was a spectacle worth witnessing. Such assumptions pass unnoticed in capable youths, but watching the conceit of a generous, clever lad is strangely touching—a display of early simplicity and folly.

After rigorous mornings spent devouring not just law, but also politics, history, and literature—deemed essential for a young man's education—they would apply themselves diligently to letters, reviews, legal texts, and newspapers until dinner approached. Then, with spirits high and appetites whetted, the duo would sallied forth to enjoy a merry night, following a pleasant afternoon. This was the glorious era of twenty-four, when every muscle of mind and body operated in healthy harmony, the world felt entirely new, and one moved forward spurred by good cheer and the delightful capacity to enjoy oneself. If we ever feel truly young later in life, it is thanks to the companions of those days; the tunes we hum in our twilight years are those we learned then. Occasionally, the festivities of that period resurface in memory, but how dingy the pleasure gardens seem now, how tattered the garlands, how sparse the company, and how many lights have gone out since then! Gray hair arrives like daylight, bringing headaches in its wake. Pleasure herself retires to bed with rouge still on her cheeks. So, friend, let us walk through the day sober and sad, yet friendly.

One wonders what Laura and Helen would have said if they had witnessed, as they might have occasionally, the early morning scenes in London. Imagine them seeing Mr. Pen and Mr. Warrington rattling over the echoing bridge flags toward the Temple after a wild night of carousing. These nights were wild, certainly, but not wicked, for Warrington despised women, and Pen, being too lofty for vulgarity, avoided coarse intrigues. Our Young Prince of Fairoaks could never address a member of the opposite sex without respectful courtesy, shrinking instinctively from anything crude. Though he had undoubtedly fallen in love with fools—as his superiors and inferiors had done—he always viewed the object of his affection as a goddess during the delusion, choosing to wait upon her. As the saying goes: "Men serve women kneeling; when they rise, they depart."

Such sentiments were voiced by an acquaintance of Pen's in his blunt, homely manner—an old friend named Mr. Bows of the Chatteris Theatre, now employed as a pianist accompanying the star attraction at the Fielding's Head in Covent Garden. Here was located the little club known as the Back Kitchen.

Numerous friends of Pen frequented this lively gathering spot. The Fielding's Head had been a house of entertainment almost since the time Henry Fielding presided as magistrate in nearby Bow Street; his seat was still occupied by the president of the evening's entertainment. Usually, this role fell to the worthy Cutts, the landlord, unless gout or illness prevented him. With his jolly appearance and fine voice, Cutts was a familiar figure to many male readers. He sang profusely during the harmonic meetings, performing songs of the "British Brandy-and-Water School"—tunes like "The Good Old English Gentleman" and "Dear Tom, this Brown Jug," blending pathos and hospitality with praise for good liquor and social bonds. Ballads of this school often celebrated women's charms and the heroic deeds of naval commanders. Cutts could work an audience to patriotic fervor, recounting how brave Abercrombie received his death wound, shedding liberal tears himself as he faltered through lines about autumn leaves proclaiming an old man's mortality. Suddenly, Cutts the singer transformed back into Cutts the landlord, and amidst the applause, he would shout, "Now, gentlemen, give your orders! John, a champagne cup for Mr. Green. Did you say sausages and mashed potatoes? John, attend to the gentleman."

"And I'll thank ye for a glass of punch too, John, and take care the water boils," a familiar voice would cry, causing Pen to blush and startle. This was the voice of the venerable Captain Costigan, now established in London and a pillar of the harmonic meetings at the Fielding's Head.

Captain Costigan's manners and conversation attracted many young men. He was a character whose fame spread rapidly after arriving in the metropolis, particularly following his daughter's marriage. He loved to talk endlessly about "my daughter," recounting her wedding, the carriages she kept, Mirabel's adoration for both her and him, and the hundreds of pounds he was entitled to withdraw from his son-in-law whenever needed. He would frequently propose borrowing a half-crown from his friend until the arrival of that mythical Saturday—the fourteenth—which he promised to repay with honor.

Sir Charles Mirabel did not possess the enthusiastic attachment his father-in-law claimed, though Costigan would sometimes tearfully inveigh against the ingratitude of his beloved child and the stinginess of the wealthy old man who married her. Nevertheless, the couple treated Costigan kindly, settling a small pension on him that was paid regularly, often pre-paid by the poor captain himself. The payment dates were well known to his friend at the Fielding's Head, where the honest Captain would appear with banknotes, loudly demanding change amidst the singing. "I think ye'll find that note won't be refused at the Bank of England, Cutts, my boy," Captain Costigan would declare. "Bows, have a glass? Ye needn't stint yourself tonight; a glass of punch will make ye play *con spirito*." For when money flowed his way, he was lavishly generous, rarely buttoning his breeches pocket unless the coins were gone or a creditor arrived.

It was during one such moment of exultation that Pen spotted his old friend swaggering at the singers' table, ordering brandy-and-water for anyone who appeared. Warrington, on confidential terms with the bass singer, made his way to this section of the room, and Pen followed close behind.

Pen started and blushed upon seeing Costigan. He had just returned from Lady Whiston's party, where he had encountered the Captain's daughter again for the first time in many years. He approached with an outstretched hand, greeting the old man warmly, still holding a strong memory of the time when Costigan's daughter meant everything to him. Although Pen was somewhat capricious in his attachments, transferring affections from one woman to another, he always respected the place where Love had once dwelt. Like the Sultan of Turkey, he desired honors be paid to the lady toward whom he had once thrown his royal pocket-handkerchief.

The tipsy Captain returned the handshake with all the strength of a palm weakened by years of lifting heavy pots of brandy-and-water. He stared hard at Pen, muttering, "Grecious Heavens, is it possible?
