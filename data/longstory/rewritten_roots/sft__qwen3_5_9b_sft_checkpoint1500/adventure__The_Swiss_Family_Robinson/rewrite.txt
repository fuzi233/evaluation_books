For seven days and nights, the ocean had been our prison. The darkness had fallen six times upon a scene of wild terror, and each returning light had revealed only greater ruin. By the seventh day, hope had evaporated entirely. Our course was lost beyond recovery, a mystery to any mind capable of conjecture. The crew, stripped of spirit and utterly drained by relentless labor, watched as the ship tore itself apart. Riven masts lay splintered on the deck; leaks gaped in every direction, swallowing the vessel with rushing water.

Gone were the reckless oaths of men who dare the sea; now, frantic cries rang out for mercy, mingled with strange, ludicrous vows promised to any god who would grant deliverance. Every soul aboard alternately surrendered their spirit to the Creator, striving desperately to imagine a means of survival.

My heart sank as I surveyed my family amidst the carnage. Our four young sons were paralyzed by terror. "Dear children," I spoke, my voice trembling but firm, "if the Lord wills, He can save us from this fearful peril; if not, let us calmly yield our lives into His hand, thinking of the joy of finding ourselves forever united in that happy home above. Even death is not too bitter when it does not separate those who love one another."

At these words, my weeping wife lifted her chin with newfound bravery. As the boys clustered around her, she began to soothe and encourage them with calm, loving tones. I rejoiced in her fortitude, though my own heart threatened to shatter under the gaze of my loved ones.

Together, we knelt, praying with deep earnestness. Fritz, in particular, besought help and deliverance for his parents and brothers, seemingly forgetting his own safety in his fervor. Our hearts were soothed by the comforting power of child-like faith, and the horrors of our situation seemed less crushing. "Ah," I thought, "the Lord will hear our prayer! He will help us!"

Amidst the roar of thundering waves, a cry rent the air: "Land! Land!" Simultaneously, the ship struck with a frightful shock, hurling everyone onto the deck and threatening immediate destruction. Dreadful sounds signaled the breakup of the hull, and the roaring waters poured in from all sides.

Then the Captain's voice boomed above the tumult, shouting, "Lower away the boats! We are lost!"

"Lost!" I exclaimed, the word striking me like a dagger. Yet, seeing my children's terror renew, I composed myself. "Take courage, my boys! We are all above water yet. There is land not far off; let us do our best to reach it. Remember, God helps those who help themselves!" Calling out this cheer, I left them and climbed the slippery ladder to the deck.

A wave instantly threw me down, followed by another, and then another, as I struggled to find footing. The ship was shattered in directions, and on one side, a massive hole gaped in the hull.

Forgetting the passengers, the ship's company crowded into the lifeboats. The last man to enter cut the davit ropes, casting each boat into the sea.

Horror seized me as I beheld the last remaining boat depart, the final seamen springing in and pushing off, ignoring my desperate cries and entreaties to allow us to share their slender chance of life. My voice was drowned in the howling blast, and even if they had wished to return, the mountain-high waves made it impossible.

Casting my eyes despairingly around, I gradually realized our position was not hopeless. The stern of the ship, containing our cabin, was jammed between two high rocks, partly raised from the breakers which dashed the fore-part to pieces. Through rents in the mist and rain, I could make out a line of rocky coast. Though rugged, it offered a sign of help.

Yet, the sense of loneliness weighed heavily on me as I returned to my family. Constrained to smile, I said, "Courage, dear ones! Although our good ship will never sail again, she is so placed that our cabin remains above water. Tomorrow, if the winds abate, I see no reason why we should not reach the shore."

These words worked magic on my children's spirits; they trusted my assurances implicitly. The boys immediately regarded our precarious chance of escape as a happy certainty and began to enjoy the relief from the violent pitching.

However, my wife perceived my hidden distress despite my forced composure. I made her understand our real situation, greatly fearing the toll it might take on her nerves. Yet, her courage and trust in Providence never faltered, and seeing this, my own fortitude revived.

"We must find some food and take a good supper," she declared. "It will never do to grow faint by fasting. We shall require our utmost strength tomorrow."

Night descended apace, the storm as fierce as ever, punctuated by crashes announcing further damage to our unfortunate vessel. We feared the lifeboats must have sunk under the foaming waves.

"God will help us soon now, won't He, Father?" asked my youngest child.

"You silly little thing," said Fritz, my eldest son, sharply, "do you not know that we must not settle what God is to do for us? We must have patience and wait His time."

"Very well said," I murmured inwardly, "had it been said kindly, Fritz, my boy. You too often speak harshly to your brothers, though you may not mean to."

A good meal was now ready. My youngsters ate heartily, and retiring to rest, were speedily fast asleep. Fritz, aware of the real danger, kept watch with us. After a long silence, "Father," he said, "don't you think we might contrive swimming-belts for Mother and the boys? With those, we might all escape to land, for you and I can swim."

"Your idea is so good," I answered, "that I shall arrange something at once, in case of an accident during the night."

We immediately searched for materials and fortunately found a number of empty flasks and tin canisters. Connecting them two and two, we formed floats sufficiently buoyant to support a person. My wife and young sons each willingly put one on. I then provided myself with matches, dry tinder, knives, cord, and other portable articles, trusting that if the vessel went to pieces before daylight, we might gain the shore, not wholly destitute.

Fritz and his brothers now slept soundly. Throughout the night, my wife and I maintained our prayerful watch, dreading every fresh sound as a sign of fatal change.

At length, the faint dawn of day appeared. The long, weary night was over, and with thankful hearts, we perceived that the gale had begun to moderate. Blue sky broke above us, and the lovely hues of sunrise adorned the eastern horizon.

I aroused the boys, and we assembled on the remaining portion of the deck. To their surprise, they discovered that no one else was on board.

"Hallo, Papa! What has become of everybody? Are the sailors gone? Have they taken away the boats? Oh, Papa! Why did they leave us behind? What can we do by ourselves!"

"My good children," I replied, "we must not despair, although we seem deserted. See how those on whose skill and good faith we depended have left us cruelly to our fate in the hour of danger. God will never do so. He has not forsaken us, and we will trust Him still. Only let us bestir ourselves, and each cheerily do his best. Who has anything to propose?"

"The sea will soon be calm enough for swimming," said Fritz.

"And that would be all very fine for you," exclaimed Ernest, "for you can swim, but think of Mother and the rest of us! Why not build a raft and all get on shore together?"

"We should find it difficult, I think, to make a raft that would carry us safe to shore. However, we must contrive something, and first let each try to procure what will be of most use to us."

Away we all went to inspect the wreckage. I proceeded to examine the supplies of provisions and fresh water within reach. My wife took her youngest son, Franz, to help her attend to the unfortunate animals on board, who were in a pitiful plight, having been neglected for several days.

Fritz hastened to the arms chest, Ernest to look for tools; and Jack went towards the Captain's cabin. No sooner did he open the door than out sprang two splendid, large dogs, who testified their extreme delight and gratitude by such tremendous bounds that they knocked their little deliverer completely head-over-heels, frightening him nearly out of his wits.

Jack did not long yield to fear or anger. He recovered himself, the dogs seemed to ask pardon by vehemently licking his face and hands, and so, seizing the larger by the ears, he jumped on its back. To my great amusement, he coolly rode to meet me as I came up the hatchway. I laughed at the sight, praising his courage, but warning him to be cautious, for animals of this species might, in a state of hunger, be dangerous.

The night was as chilling as the day had been scorching, and without our makeshift tent, survival would have been precarious. Yet, exhaustion from our labors rendered everyone deeply tired, allowing us to sleep soundly despite the cold.

At dawn, the vigilant crow of our cock signaled the rising moon, waking me. I gently roused my wife, hoping to consult on our dire situation before the children stirred. It was evident to both of us that our immediate priorities were twofold: first, to determine the fate of our shipwrecked companions, and second, to assess the resources of this hostile island.

"We shall depart after breakfast," I decided, "with Fritz and myself leading the way to investigate. You must stay here with the younger boys near our landing place."

I woke the children with cheerful cries, urging them to assist their mother with breakfast. My wife smiled, noting that boiling soup was all they could manage. "Don't forget Jack's lobster!" I recalled. "Where is it?"

"It is safely hidden in a hollow in the rock," Jack explained proudly. "I thought the dogs might appreciate it as much as the agouti."

"A sensible precaution," I agreed. "Jack may yet learn wisdom. That lobster is essential for our journey today."

Mention of an expedition sent the four children into a frenzy of delight, clapping their hands. However, I calmed them down. "Such a trek would be too perilous for you younger ones. This area appears safe. Fritz and I will go alone, accompanied by one of the dogs, leaving the other to guard you."

At the mention of "guns," Fritz blushed crimson, struggling to hold his weapon upright. Seeing his distress, I allowed him to take a second firearm, pleased to see the humiliation spur a sense of responsibility.

Flora, the smaller dog, resisted being tied up, remembering recent blows, and snarled. Turk, fiercer and more independent, refused to approach as well. I had to call to them personally to soothe their tempers. Fritz, overcome with guilt, tearfully begged for a biscuit, promising to make peace. He carried treats to the dogs, stroking them and begging forgiveness. While Flora quickly forgave him, licking his hands, Turk remained wary.

"Just give him a claw of my lobster," Jack offered generously. "I intended it for his journey anyway."

With this final token, Turk relaxed. Armed with guns, game bags, pistols, and a hatchet, we packed the remaining provisions—a flask of water and the rest of the lobster—and prepared to leave.

"Wait!" I paused. "Something vital remains undone."

"What?" Fritz asked.

"Morning prayer," I replied. "Amidst the cares and pleasures of life, we too often forget the God to whom we owe everything."

Commending ourselves to His care, I bid farewell to my wife and children, instructing them not to stray far from the boat and tent. Anxiety lingered on both sides as we departed into the unknown.

The terrain was treacherous; the stream banks were so rocky that only one narrow passage allowed access to the water. Reaching a point where the waters cascaded over large rocks, we successfully crossed the stream. On our left lay the sea, and to the right, rocky heights dotted with trees stretched inland.

Scarcely fifty yards into the tall, tangled grass, a rustling sound alarmed us. The grass waved as if disturbed by a beast. Fritz instantly raised his gun, cool and ready. I admired his presence of mind, trusting him implicitly in times of danger. However, no savage beast emerged; instead, it was Turk, whom we had forgotten, sent back by my thoughtful wife.

I praised Fritz's restraint and caution. This incident highlighted the dangers lurking in our position; if a fierce beast had attacked unawares, escape would have been impossible. Hastening to the open seashore, we found a delightful scene: hills, green grass, and trees reaching the water's edge.

Searching the smooth sands for traces of our hapless companions, we found no footprints whatsoever.

"Shall I fire a shot or two?" Fritz suggested. "To summon them if they are within hearing."

"No," I replied firmly. "Fire would alert any savages here. Let us search diligently but silently."

"But why bother, Father?" Fritz argued. "They abandoned us. I wouldn't wish to see them again."

"You are mistaken, my boy," I corrected. "First, returning evil for evil is never right. Second, they might assist us in building a shelter. Lastly, they left nothing behind and may be starving."

"Why not return to the vessel and save the animals on board?" Fritz countered.

"When duties clash, choose the one offering the most solid advantage," I taught. "Saving a human life is nobler than saving a few quadrupeds, whom we have already fed. Besides, the sea is calm; the ship likely won't sink before we return."

Pushing onward, we reached a pleasant grove bordering the water. Halting under a large tree beside a murmuring rivulet, we rested. Thousands of colorful birds twittered overhead. Suddenly, Fritz jumped, claiming he saw a monkey. Springing aside, he stumbled over a small, round object.

"I thought it was a bird's nest!" he exclaimed.

"You may have read about nests," I laughed, "but this is not one. It is a coconut. Remember, coconuts have a fibrous husk over a hard shell. The outer layer has decayed, revealing the twisted fibers inside."

We split the shell, only to find the kernel dry and inedible.

"Hullo!" Fritz cried. "I always thought coconuts were full of sweet milk like almond milk."

"They are, when young and fresh," I explained. "As they ripen, the milk congeals and solidifies. Eventually, the germ inside swells, bursts through the shell, and sprouts a new tree."

"How does the germ get through such a thick shell?" Fritz wondered.

"Nature provides," I answered, showing him the three round holes near the stalk through which the germ escapes. "Let us find a viable nut."

Coconuts falling naturally are usually over-ripe. After some difficulty, we found one with edible oil, though slightly rancid. Refreshed by the fruit, we deferred our dinner until later, conserving our stock.

Venturing through a dense thicket of lianas, we cleared a path with our hatchets and emerged onto the seashore. Before us stood single trees of remarkable appearance, featuring strange bumps on their trunks. Fritz pointed them out.

"Oh, absurd-looking trees!" he marveled. "Strange bumps everywhere."

Approaching them, I identified them as calabash trees bearing gourds. "Savages are known to craft bowls and bottles from these hard rinds. Indeed, they cook food inside them."

"That's impossible!" Fritz protested. "The rind would burn instantly."

"Not by direct fire," I clarified. "Water is poured in, fish added, and red-hot stones introduced to boil the water. The gourd remains unharmed."

"That is clever and simple," Fritz admitted. "I might have invented it."

"The friends of Columbus thought it easy to stand an egg upright once shown how," I noted. "Suppose we prepare some calabashes for future use."

Fritz attempted to split a gourd equally but failed, cutting it jaggedly. "Nuisance!" he complained. "Spoiled!"

"Too impatient," I advised. "Those pieces aren't useless. Try fashioning a spoon." Meanwhile, I demonstrated how to divide a gourd precisely by tying a string tight around it and striking it with the back of a knife, causing it to split cleanly.

"Fascinating!" Fritz cried. "How did you devise that?"

"A technique learned from travelers' accounts," I replied. "It makes a perfect soup tureen."

"If you wanted a bottle, how would you proceed?" Fritz asked.

"Easier, if possible," I explained. "Cut a round hole at one end, scoop out the interior, and fill it with shot or stones. Shake it to detach remaining pulp, cleaning it thoroughly."

"But that would be a barrel, not a bottle," Fritz observed.

"True. For a shapely vessel, intervene while the gourd is young. Tie a bandage around it on the tree to restrict swelling at specific points, creating a neck."

I filled the gourds with sand and left them to dry, marking the spot for retrieval.

For three hours, we scanned the area for signs of our companions, eventually reaching a bold promontory overlooking the bay. Despite the stunning view—yellow sands, shadowy promontories, and rippling water reflecting the sun—the landscape was utterly devoid of human traces. A sense of loneliness settled over us.

"Cheap up, Fritz," I encouraged. "We chose a settler's life knowingly. Solitude is not ideal, but compared to a barren, inhospitable island, this is a blessing. Let us find shade before the heat overwhelms us."

Descending the hill, we navigated a dense thicket of reeds, fearing venomous snakes. Sending Turk ahead, I cut a reed and tasted the thick, sweet juice exuding from it. I realized we were standing amidst sugar-canes.

Advising Fritz to cut a cane for defense, he sliced one, covering his hand with juice. Tasting it, he confirmed its sweetness and eagerly began sucking the cane, shouting, "Sugar-cane! Sugar-cane!"

"Gently," I cautioned.

"I shall spare you a detailed account of our first day's labors," my wife remarked, "for truth be told, I spent the majority of my time in anxious contemplation, keeping a vigilant eye on your progress and awaiting your signals. I rose with the dawn, and upon sighting your reassuring sign that all was well, I hastened to reply. Then, while my sons still slumbered, I sat and pondered how we might improve our precarious situation.

"For it is plainly impossible," I murmured to myself, "to survive much longer in this exposed spot. The sun beats down relentlessly all day upon this barren rock, and our sole shelter is this wretched tent, where the heat trapped beneath the canvas is far more oppressive than on the open shore. Why should not I and my younger boys lend a hand, just as Fritz and your father do? Why should not we strive to accomplish something useful?"

"If we could trade this gloomy, unhealthy refuge for a pleasant, shaded dwelling, surely our health and spirits would improve. Amidst those delightful woods and groves where Fritz and his father witnessed such wonders, I am certain there exists some hidden retreat where we could establish a comfortable home; there must be one, and I intend to find it."

By this time, the boys had awakened. I observed Jack quietly and industriously working with his knife near the spot where Fritz's jackal lay. Watching his movements, I saw him cut two long, narrow strips from the animal's skin, cleaning and scraping them with great care. He then produced a handful of large nails from his pocket, driving them through the skin points outward. Next, he cut strips of canvas sailcloth, twice as wide as the leather thongs, doubled them, and laid them over the raw side of the skin to cover the protruding nail heads.

At this stage, Master Jack approached me with a polite request that I kindly stitch the canvas and the moist skin together for him. Though I provided him with needles and thread, I could not bear to deprive him of the satisfaction of completing the task himself. However, seeing how cheerfully he persisted with his clumsy, unskilled fingers, I eventually took pity on him. I advised him to devise a method to prevent the skin from shrinking.

Ernest, initially treating Jack's creations with mild ridicule, proposed a practical plan which Jack immediately executed. He nailed the skin flat onto a board and placed it in the sun to dry.

My scheme for an exploratory journey was met with joyful agreement by my young companions. Preparations were made instantly: weapons and provisions were gathered, with the two elder boys carrying their guns and entrusting me with the water flask and a small hatchet. Leaving the tent in as orderly a state as possible, we proceeded toward the stream, accompanied by the dogs. Turk, who had joined you on your first expedition, seemed to intuitively understand that we intended to follow the same path, proudly leading the way.

Glancing at my two young sons, each armed with a gun, I reflected deeply on how crucial their safety was to the party, feeling profound gratitude toward you, dear husband, for teaching them the use of firearms in childhood. Filling our water-jar, we crossed the stream and ascended to the height from which, as you described, a magnificent view could be obtained. Upon beholding it, a pleasurable surge of buoyant hope, long absent from my heart, stirred within me.

A charming woodland in the distance particularly caught my eye, and thither we directed our course. Following the shoreline, we reached a point closer to the wood; abandoning the strand, we made for the grove.

We had not entirely avoided the tall grass, and with utmost fatigue and difficulty, we struggled through the reeds. Suddenly, a tremendous rushing noise terrified us all. A very large and powerful bird sprang upward on the wing. Both boys attempted to take aim, but the bird was already far away before they were ready to fire.

"Oh dear, what a pity!" exclaimed Ernest. "Now, if I had only had my light gun, and if the bird had not flown quite so fast, I should have brought him down directly!"

"Oh yes," I replied, "no doubt you would be a capital sportsman if only your game would always grant you time to prepare comfortably."

"But I had no notion that anything was going to fly up just at our feet like that," he cried.

"A good shot," I answered, "must be prepared for surprises: neither wild birds nor wild beasts will send you notice that they are about to fly or run."

"Oh, it certainly must have been an eagle," answered little Franz, "it was so very big!"

"Just as if every big bird must be an eagle!" retorted Ernest, in a tone of derision.

Jack sprang toward the location, and instantly a second bird, slightly larger than the first, rushed upward with a startling roar.

The boys stood staring upward, perfectly stupefied, while I laughed heartily, declaring, "Well, you are first-rate sportsmen, to be sure! You certainly will keep my larder famously well supplied!"

At this, Ernest blushed and looked inclined to tears, while Jack put on a comical face, pulled off his cap, and bowed low, calling after the fleeing creature, "Adieu for the present, sir! I live in hopes of another meeting!"

Upon searching the ground carefully, we discovered a crude nest, untidily constructed of dry grass. It was empty, though we noted broken eggshells nearby and concluded that the young brood had escaped into the surrounding grass, which we saw waving as the little birds ran through it.

"Now look here, Franz," Ernest remarked, "consider how this bird could possibly have been an eagle. Eagles never build nests on the ground, nor can their young leave the nest and run immediately after hatching. That is a peculiarity of the gallinaceous tribe alone, to which these must belong. The species, I think, is indicated by the white belly and dull red color of the wing coverts I observed, and I believe them to be bustards, especially since I noticed the fine, moustache-like feathers over the beak of the largest specimen, peculiar to the Great Bustard."

"My dear boy!" I said, "your eyes were actively employed, I must confess, if your fingers were unready with the gun. And after all, it is just as well, perhaps, that we have not thrown the bustard's family into mourning."

Thus chatting, we finally approached the grove. Numerous birds fluttered and sang among the high branches, but I discouraged the boys' wish to shoot any of the happy little creatures. We were lost in admiration of the trees of this grove. I cannot describe to you how wonderful they are, nor can you imagine their enormous size without seeing them yourself. What we had called a wood proved to be a group of about a dozen trees only, and strangely, the roots supported the massive trunks elevated high above the earth, forming strong arches and props around each stem, which was firmly rooted in the center.

I gave Jack some twine, and scrambling up one of the curious open-air roots, he succeeded in measuring around the trunk itself, calculating it to be about eighteen yards in circumference. I saw no fruit, but the foliage was thick and abundant, throwing delicious shade on the ground beneath, which was carpeted with soft green herbage, entirely free from thorns, briars, or bushes. It was the most charming resting-place ever seen, and I and the boys enjoyed our midday meal immensely in this glorious palace of the woods, so grateful to our senses after the glare and heat of our journey.

The dogs joined us after a while. They had lingered behind on the seashore, and I was surprised to see them lie down and go comfortably to sleep without begging for food, as they usually do when we eat.

The longer we remained in this enchanting place, the more it charmed my fancy; and if we could manage to live in some sort of dwelling up among the branches of those grand, noble trees, I would feel perfectly safe and happy. The morning dawned crisp and bright, waking the entire household with a vigor that belied the exhaustion of recent days. Down below in the canopy, the children moved with the restless energy of young monkeys, leaping from limb to limb.

"What shall we start with today, Father?" Jack shouted, his voice echoing through the leaves. "What does Dad want us to do?"

"Rest, my boys," I replied gently, descending the ladder to join them. "Rest."

"Rest?" Franz echoed, his brow furrowing. "Why rest? There is so much to fix!"

I knelt beside them, placing a hand on each shoulder. "Remember the law given to Moses? 'Six days shalt thou labour, but on the seventh, thou shalt do no manner of work.'" I smiled. "Today is the seventh day. Therefore, let us rest."

Jack grinned mischievously. "Is it really Sunday? How jolly! I won't do any work. But I'll grab a bow and arrow, and we'll climb the tree and hunt all day!"

"That is not rest," I corrected, shaking my head. "And it is not how you are meant to honor the Lord's Day."

"But there is no church here!" Jack protested, kicking at a fallen branch. "No priest, no organ, nothing!"

"We can worship here as beautifully as anywhere else," I assured him, gesturing to the towering tree that sheltered us. "Look at this green sanctuary. Underneath its leafy roof, we will offer thanks to our Creator."

I guided them down to the ground. "Elizabeth," I called to my wife, "today we dedicate the morning to service. I intend to teach them a lesson through a story—a parable—so they understand the spiritual truth. But without books or games, keeping them quiet all day will be impossible. Once the lesson is done, I shall permit them any innocent play they desire. Later, when the heat breaks, we will take a walk."

Elizabeth nodded in agreement. After breakfast, the family gathered around me in the cool, soft grass beneath the tree. Following songs and sincere prayers, I announced my intention to share a story rather than preach.

"Oh, a parable! I love those stories from the Bible!" Franz cheered. "When can we hear you read from the Book again, Father?"

"A sad confession," I admitted, feeling a pang of guilt. "While I have been busy securing food and safety for our bodies, I have neglected the Bread of Life—the Word of God itself. Before I could retrieve it, however, Elizabeth produced a leather-bound volume from her magical bag. With grateful hands, I accepted it. As I read from its holy pages, weaving a tale of a Great King who ruled a realm of light, I watched the children lean forward, captivated.

I spoke of a desolate island transformed by the King's care, where colonists lived in harmony, bound by love and loyalty. But I warned them of the dangers of pride and rebellion, describing how disobedience could bring ruin and exile. Yet, I emphasized the promise of forgiveness: those who humbled themselves and aligned their lives with the King's will would eventually be restored to glory.

When the story ended, the silence was deep and thoughtful. Each child seemed to internalize the moral. I then prayed briefly for the blessing of the words, and we dispersed to our individual tasks.

I spent some time crafting arrows, sharpening reeds with porcupine quills. Soon, Franz arrived asking for help making a bow, while Fritz consulted me about tanning a tiger-cat skin into armor cases. Jack worked alongside me, driving a sharp spine into a reed and binding it tight with pack-thread.

"Oh, Jack!" Franz squeaked, noticing the mess. "Mummy's soup is stickier than glue! Shall I run and ask for a cake?"

"No, little goose!" I laughed. "Better look for real adhesive in the tool-box."

"There is glue there, surely," I confirmed, turning to Jack. "And while you're at it, do not mock your brother's ideas. Sometimes the greatest discoveries spring from thoughts that seem as silly as a child's wish for sticky soup."

Just then, a sharp crack rang out overhead. Two small birds plummeted to the earth at our feet. Looking up, we saw Ernest high in the branches, his face alight with triumph.

"Well hit! A fine shot, wasn't it?" he cried, sliding down the ladder to collect his prey.

"One was a thrush," he explained, handing me the birds. "The other was an ortolan, a rare delicacy known for its flavor."

Since the figs on which they fed were barely ripening, I surmised that soon the trees would swarm with them. If we could catch enough before the rains, we might preserve them in casks with lard or butter, providing vital sustenance for the coming wet season.

By now, Jack had prepared a batch of arrows and practiced his aim. I finished Franz's bow and quiver, expecting a brief respite. Instead, a younger brother demanded a quiver, forcing me to improvise one from a strip of bark and a cord, completing his kit. He departed looking, as his mother noted, like a tiny Cupid bent on conquest.

Shortly after, dinner was called. During the meal, I proposed a new system for naming our surroundings. Explaining that descriptions like "where we found the dead shark" were cumbersome, I suggested we adopt proper names to feel more at home in this wilderness.

"Safety Bay," Elizabeth proposed for the inlet where we landed, honoring our escape. The suggestion was universally accepted. We quickly christened our campsite Tentholm, the nearby islet Shark's Island, and the marsh Flamingo Marsh. After considerable debate, we named the great tree Falconhurst. Other locations followed: Prospect Hill, Cape Disappointment, and Jackal River.

In the afternoon, the boys resumed their projects. Fritz completed his armor cases, while Jack and I constructed a cuirass from the porcupine skin for their dog, Turk. After cleaning the interior and sewing strings, the resulting coat of mail made Turk look like a miniature knight. Juno, wary of the armored beast, retreated to a safe distance. I also crafted a helmet for Jack from the remaining skin, delighting him immensely.

As evening fell, we enjoyed a leisurely stroll through the fragrant glades, concluding the Sabbath with prayer and a hymn before retiring with peaceful hearts.

The next morning, I planned an expedition to Tentholm via a new route. Armed with guns and game-bags, I, along with Fritz, Jack, and Ernest, set out. Little Franz carried his bow and a full quiver. "Grafting," I continued, "is the art of inserting a slip or twig from one tree into a specific knot or 'eye' on the branch of another. "But," Ernest asked, "where did the slips of good fruit come from, if none grow without grafting?"

"From foreign countries," I explained. "In the cold climate of our region, nature requires this human aid. "When the Romans invaded England," I mused, "they found nothing but crab-apples, nut bushes, and brambles. Yet, through grafting, they produced fine apples, filberts, and raspberries. It was the same story in our beloved Switzerland—all our fruit trees were imports."

Then indeed ensued a happy meeting, and we gave thanks together for the mercy which had spared our lives. Returning joyfully to Rockburg, we changed our drenched garments for warm, dry clothes and, seated at a comfortable meal, considered and described at our ease the perils of the storm.

Afterward, the head of the walrus was conveyed to our workshop. There, it underwent a skilled and thorough process of cleaning, embalming, and drying. Ere long, it was actually fixed on the prow of the *cajack*, presenting a most imposing appearance! The strips of hide, when well-tanned and prepared, made valuable leather.

Much damage had been done by the late storm. Heavy rains had flooded all the streams and injured crops that should have been housed before the regular rainy season. The bridge over Jackal River was partly broken down, and the water tanks and pipes all needed repair. Our time was much occupied in restoring things to order.

On going to work one day near the cascade, we found a great number of dark-red berries scattered on the ground. They were about the size of ordinary hazelnuts, with small leafy coronets at the tip. The boys thought them so inviting that they tasted them at once, but angry exclamations and much spitting and spluttering followed the experiment. Even Knips rejected them, and they would have been cast aside with contempt had not the smell induced me to examine them. I decided this was the fruit of the clove.

Some plants were immediately set in the nursery garden, and my wife was pleased to have this excellent spice wherewith to flavor her boiled rice and other dishes, in lieu of pepper—a very welcome variety to everyone.

Having a good supply of clay brought from the bed near Falconhurst, I proposed using it for making aqueducts. Observing how much the recent rain had promoted the growth of our young corn, I determined to irrigate the fields with the drainage from our crushing mill.

The fishing season was again successful. Large takes of salmon, sturgeon, and herring rewarded our annual exertions, and our store-room again assumed a well-stocked appearance. Though I wished to obtain a constant supply of these fish fresh, I was obliged to reject Jack's naive proposal that we tether a shoal of salmon by the gills to the bottom of the bay as we had secured the turtles.

Ten years have passed since we first settled on this uninhabited island, and as I review the chronicles of our life, I am reminded of King David's words: "We spend our years as a tale that is told." Time has slipped away imperceptibly, chapter by chapter, yet amidst the shadows of reflection, a profound gratitude warms my heart. To see our children grow into sturdy, capable men—fortified by the principles of industry, piety, and unity—is a blessing beyond measure. It is my fervent hope that readers of this journal may find inspiration in the example of a happy, cohesive family, understanding that such a foundation yields characters of strength and purity.

Our domain, which we affectionately termed "New Switzerland," has transformed significantly. Rockburg, once a bleak refuge due to the oppressive heat and lack of greenery, now stands as a verdant sanctuary. Fountains play, trellises climb with fragrant vines, and balconies are draped in flowering creepers. Native and exotic trees intermingle in lush groves, while Shark Island guards the entrance to Safety Bay, its crest adorned with graceful palms and a prominent flagstaff. The once-malarial swamp has been reclaimed, draining into a serene lake teeming with life. Here, stately black swans glide alongside snow-white geese and vibrant ducks, while colorful flamingos, demoiselle cranes, and crested herons wade among the rushes, utterly unafraid of their human masters. Even the giant frogs, Grace and Beauty, have grown to the size of rabbits, waddling obediently at Jack's call to devour insects. Old Hurry, the ostrich, marches with regal dignity beneath the trees, while countless pigeons nest in the rocks, their soft coos filling the air.

Security remains paramount, though our defenses have evolved. A dense impenetrable thicket of orange, lemon, and prickly pear trees now blocks access to the river above Rockburg, reachable only via the bridge. Our economy thrives on the resources of the surrounding islands: Shark Island supplies us with rabbits and fur, Whale Isle serves as a factory for tanning and candle-making, and Woodlands provides abundant meat and milk. The farm at Woodlands flourishes, and my wife's dairy is a bustling hub of activity.

The boys retain their whimsical spirit, naming their new companions with great delight. Blanche, a cream-colored cow, and Stentor, a bull with a booming voice, graze nearby. Two swift onagers, Arrow and Dart, race across the fields, while Jack proudly claims Coco, a jackal descended from Fangs, arguing that "Coco" is the only word that cannot be misheard at a distance.

Health has been remarkably robust for all of us over the decade, despite occasional minor fevers for my wife and trivial accidents for the boys. They are now fine, handsome young men. Fritz, twenty-four, is strong, muscular, and spirited. Ernest, two years younger, is tall and slender, having overcome his youthful indolence to become a man of refined intellect and calm demeanor. Jack, at twenty, mirrors Fritz's vigor with a lighter, more agile build, while Franz, seventeen, combines the wit of his brothers with a steady shrewdness. Though we have lived in seclusion for ten years, a lingering hope persists that civilization awaits us someday. However, the creeping realization of our advancing age fills me with anxiety regarding their future, a burden I bear silently, seeking solace in prayer.

Recently, Fritz embarked on an unauthorized expedition, venturing out in his cajack without permission. We only discovered his absence when his boat was missing at twilight. Anxiously, Ernest, Jack, and I climbed the watchtower on Shark Island to scan the horizon. Against the backdrop of the setting sun, we spotted a small speck—a skiff moving sluggishly toward shore, towing a heavy sack. Upon sighting us, Fritz fired a warning shot, signaling his safe return. As he docked, the burden on his skiff revealed itself as a massive sack of goods.

"Fritz!" I cried, welcoming him back. "Where have you been, and what treasures have you brought?"

"My absence was unauthorized, Father, and I offer my apologies," Fritz admitted, though his face lit with excitement. "However, I have made a voyage of discovery along the coast we had never explored. I prepared thoroughly, provisioning my skiff and arming myself with a rifle, harpoon, and axe. I even took Pounce, my eagle, with me."

He recounted his journey, describing how he navigated past the wrecks of our ship, scattered across the seabed like ghosts in the glassy water, and skirted rugged cliffs teeming with seals and walruses. Eventually, he reached a high, precipitous cape dominated by a magnificent natural archway leading into a vast, vaulted cavern. Inside, thousands of tiny swallows nested on platforms resembling spoons without handles. Fritz had collected these unique structures, which I identified immediately as edible birds' nests. If commerce with China were possible, these would be invaluable; the freshest, whitest nests are prized highly in the East, fetching prices equivalent to their weight in silver. They are renowned for their restorative properties when steeped in soup.

Beyond the cavern, Fritz ventured into a secluded, landlocked bay known as Pearl Bay. Its shores were lined with fertile plains and forests of cedar, and the waters were crystal clear. There, anchored to the rocks, lay beds of enormous oysters. Fascinated by their size, Fritz hooked up clusters and dragged them ashore. Upon examining them, he discovered that inside the shells lay numerous pearly orbs of varying sizes. "Treasure!" I exclaimed. "Though useless to us now, these pearls could bring immense wealth if we ever reconnect with the civilized world. We must revisit this bay immediately."

Later that morning, Fritz confided a startling secret to me alone. While resting on the deck of his canoe, he had noticed an albatross he had knocked unconscious. Upon inspecting it, he found a rag tied to its leg bearing English words: "Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!" His mind raced with possibilities. Could a fellow human inhabit this lonely region? Did the "smoking rock" refer to a volcano, perhaps a dormant one emitting smoke? Fritz was convinced that the message was recent and genuine. We agreed that, pending further investigation and modifications to his canoe to accommodate two passengers, Fritz would eventually embark on a mission to locate the woman in distress. For now, however, the discovery of pearls and the potential for a lucrative fishery captured the imagination of all.

Preparations for an expedition to Pearl Bay began in earnest. We invented machinery to scrape oysters from the rocks, and Fritz modified his canoe to include a spare seat, a move his brothers interpreted as an invitation for one of them to join him. Taking advantage of the delay, Jack and Ernest crafted various tools, while I focused on securing the necessary equipment.

Finally, the day arrived. Leaving my wife and Franz behind, I boarded the yacht with some of the dogs. Fritz piloted his newly fitted canoe ahead of us, with Jack proudly occupying the new seat beside him. We navigated safely through the rocky channels near Walrus Island, entering a sheltered expanse where the sea mirrored the sky. For the first time, we sighted the fairy-like shells of the paper-nautilus gliding lightly over the water, a sight so enchanting that we chased down half a dozen to preserve for the museum, naming the location Nautilus Creek.

Following the promontory of Cape Pug-Nose, we approached the majestic cliffs of a headland extending far into the sea. Guided by Fritz's skill, we found a narrow passage through a series of reefs and sandbanks, leading us to a breathtaking natural cathedral of stone pillars, arches, and pinnacles. A swarm of swallows darted from the cavernous entrance, dividing into flocks before returning in a synchronized display. We harvested dozens of nests, careful to avoid those containing young, marveling at the intricate architecture of the "Gothic" formation. Emerging into the sunlight, we drifted into the calm, romantic waters of Pearl Bay, surrounded by fertile meadows and gently swelling hills.

Seeking a suitable landing spot near the shallow oyster beds, we found a sparkling streamlet. As dusk fell, we erected a watch-fire, partook of a hasty supper, and returned to the yacht, anchoring close to shore. To guard against unseen dangers, we kept Mercury, the little ape, aboard for his keen vigilance. That night, the camp was disturbed by the yelping of jackals, with whom Coco, the jackal, engaged in a raucous conversation.

Dawn broke, and we hurried to the oyster beds, working diligently with nets and scrapers. Within two days, a towering stack of empty shells lay on the beach, awaiting decomposition. We collected seaweed to burn later for alkali production, ensuring our harvest of pearls would be secured. Each evening, we ventured out to shoot game, replenishing our stores. On the final day, intending to explore deeper into the woods, Ernest set off with Floss, followed by Jack and Coco. Fritz and I finished loading the last of our tools onto the yacht.

Suddenly, the silence was shattered by a gunshot, a cry of pain, and another report. Dogs bolted toward the disturbance, and Fritz, calling Pounce to follow, dashed off with his rifle. Before I could arrive, more shots rang out, followed by a shout of triumph. Emerging from the trees, I saw Jack hobbling limply, supported by his brothers.

With the funeral rites complete, the spoils of our hunt—the wild boar and truffles—were delivered to my wife, who promised many savory meals from them. Though she coveted the boar's head, I had pledged it to Ernest's museum collection, and despite my own hunger, I honored my word. The magnificent trophy, alongside the lions' skins, was transported to the tannery on Whale Island for preservation.

Five days passed, yet Fritz remained missing. My anxiety grew unbearable, and eventually, I resolved to track him down. To my delight, everyone supported the plan, including my wife, who agreed to sail in the pinnace.

Under a brilliant sky with favorable winds, we five, accompanied by our loyal dogs, launched into the sea, racing toward Cape Minster. Our small yacht danced merrily over the waves, buoyed by sunshine and a refreshing breeze. As we approached the archway, I spotted a dark, shadowy mass beneath the surface—a submerged rock I had somehow missed before.

Instinctively, I dropped the helm, but disaster loomed. The boat surged forward, striking the hidden obstacle with a violent shock. Yet, miraculously, the danger passed instantly. Glancing backward, I found the rock vanished; where I had seen its ominous shadow moments prior, now lay only clear blue water.

Before we could recover from our shock, Jack pointed urgently to starboard. "Another one!" he cried. Indeed, a second dark shape floated nearby. Franz screamed, "The rock is moving!" Suddenly, a massive black body erupted from the sea, spewing a towering column of water that crashed down around us like a waterfall. The mystery solved itself: it was a sperm whale, or cachalot, enraged by our earlier scratches.

Feeling the beast's fury, I recalled tales of whales destroying entire fleets. Desperation gripped me as I scrambled to mount a cannon. Jack joined me, and together we fired. Two shots rang out, sending the whale into a frenzy. It lashed the water violently before plunging deep, disappearing momentarily.

Refusing to let our valuable prize escape, we scanned the horizon. Soon, the whale resurfaced in shallow waters, thrashing and bleeding. Daredevil courage took hold as we closed in, firing again. The creature's struggles intensified until, with a final, shuddering convulsion, it lay motionless and dead.

Just as the boys prepared to celebrate, a shout cut through the air. Behind a rocky outcrop, they spotted a canoe manned by a tall, muscular savage. As the swarthy native raised his paddle, scanning us warily, an awful thought seized me: a tribe of blacks must lurk here, and Fritz had likely fallen into their hands.

Anxious glances exchanged, we readied our guns. Suddenly, dusky faces peeked from surrounding rocks, vanishing quickly. Then the canoe pushed forward again, probing closer. Even Jack grew uneasy, turning to me for command.

"Hoist a white flag," I ordered, "and hand me the speaking-trumpet." I raised the device, offering peaceful greetings in Malay. But the savage ignored us, poised to retreat. Impatient, Jack grabbed the trumpet, shouting aggressively, "Come here, you black son of a gun..."

"Stop!" I rebuked sharply. "Your wild words will only alarm him."

"No!" Jack countered, pointing. "He's paddling straight for us!"

Sure enough, the canoe sped toward us. Just then, Franz shrieked, "Look! Look! The villain is in Fritz's cayack! I see the walrus-head mask!"

While chaos threatened, Ernest remained calm. Seizing the trumpet, he called out, "Fritz, ahoy! Welcome, old fellow!"

Scarcely had the words left his lips when I recognized the familiar features beneath the painted disguise. Within minutes, the brave boy stood on deck, unmasked and embraced warmly. Despite his soot-stained face, he was greeted with affectionate kisses. Immediately, a storm of questions erupted from us all: Where had he been? How long had he stayed? Why did he don this terrifying guise?

"The last question," Fritz smiled, "is the only one I will answer now. The others await my full account." He explained that hearing gunfire, he assumed Malay pirates were hunting us. Never imagining we were in the yacht, he had disguised himself to scout the area. Addressed in Malay, he had believed us to be enemies, heightening his terror.

Having recounted our whale encounter, I asked if he knew of a suitable anchorage. "Certainly," he replied, his gaze filled with meaning. "I can guide you to an island with splendid harbors, teeming with strange wonders." Removing the stains from his skin, he jumped back into his canoe and piloted us to a picturesque islet within the bay.

Now convinced of Fritz's success, I decided to reveal his secret mission to my wife, preparing her for the surprise awaiting her. Her reaction was immediate and emotional; the prospect of encountering another woman, especially one of her own kind, overwhelmed her.

"But why?" she questioned, her voice trembling. "Why withhold this joyful news until the last moment?"

"I hesitated," I admitted, "fearing false hopes. But now, thanks to heaven, he has succeeded, and secrecy serves no purpose."

The boys struggled to comprehend the air of mystery and suppressed excitement enveloping us all. Curiosity burned in their eyes as the sails furled and anchor dropped. Together, we followed Fritz silently through the dense foliage, emerging into a clearing dominated by a hut constructed from sheltering boughs, where a cheerful fire crackled.

Inside this leafy sanctuary, Fritz slipped away, leaving his brothers outside, awestruck. Moments later, he emerged leading by the hand a slender, handsome youth dressed as a young English naval officer. The pair advanced to greet us, and Fritz, his face radiant with joy, introduced his companion as Edward Montrose.

"And," he continued, looking at his parents, "will you not welcome him as a friend and brother to our family circle?"

"That will we, indeed!" I exclaimed, stepping forward to shake the stranger's hand. "Though our wilderness life may have coarsened our appearances, it has not hardened our hearts."

My wife embraced the apparent youth with heartfelt warmth. The lads, even the dogs, expressed their delight at the arrival of a new companion. However, Fritz's tone suggested that the girl wished to maintain her disguise until my wife could procure clothing more appropriate to her true nature.

Meanwhile, the young men raced down to fetch supplies for supper and prepare a campsite for the night. Once the preparations were complete, my wife served a substantial meal. The boys, eager to make their new acquaintance feel at home, worked tirelessly to entertain her. Soon, the initial sense of strangeness faded, replaced by laughter and lively chatter. She admired our dishes, sipped our mead, and engaged in spirited conversation, utterly forgetting her past isolation.

Meeting any human being after years alone was enough to exhilarate the boys; discovering that this person was so charming, so vibrant, seemed to lift their spirits to dizzying heights. As I signaled the end of the feast, the health of "Edward Montrose" was proposed in fragrant mead, cheered by everyone present.

Once she departed, silence fell over the group. Jack broke the quiet, turning to Fritz. "Now, tell us where you found this jolly fellow. Was he your destination, or did you stumble upon him by chance? Spill the beans about your adventures while we sit by the fire."

Adding fuel to the blaze, Jack settled down expectantly. After a brief pause, Fritz began his tale. "Do you recall when I returned from my recent journey in the cayack? I killed an albatross. Only Father knew what happened to the injured bird, or perhaps he didn't care enough to ask. That albatross, however, led me to the shipwrecked stranger, and I intended to send him back a message of encouragement."

Describing his preparations, Fritz recounted how he modified the cayack to accommodate two passengers, setting off with Pounce, his heroic parrot, perched before him. For hours, he paddled steadily until a freshening wind prompted him to hug the shoreline, seeking shelter from potential storms.

His decision proved wise. Upon reaching a seemingly tranquil cove, the sea transformed into a churning mass of foam, with towering waves threatening even the relative calm of the bay. He spent a night in his fragile craft, surviving on pemmican and water from his flask before venturing forth again the next morning.

Fields and orchards demanded labor, filling the lads with purpose, while Mother and Jenny oversaw the poultry yard and household chores with practiced ease.

Our empire stretched far beyond our immediate home. From Falconhurst to Woodlands, from Prospect Hill to the distant Shark and Whale Islands, every outpost required tending. Jack and Franz had been assigned the honor—and burden—of maintaining the coastal defense line. Almost immediately, the horizon erupted in response. Three distinct booms rolled across the water from the west. The beach fell silent. Was it a phantom? A trick of the ear? Or the unmistakable roar of a ship? Before anyone could speak, Jack and Franz dashed back to shore, paddling with frantic urgency. Their faces were pale with a mixture of terror and exhilaration.

"Hear you?" Jack gasped, clutching the sand. "Hear them? What do we do?"

"It must be a European ship!" Fritz cried, seizing his brother's hand, his eyes shining with hope. "Fatherland! Civilization!"

A wave of emotions swept over us—fear, relief, desperate longing. Were they allies or pirates? Friends or foes? The answer mattered more than life itself. Before we could formulate a strategy, the wind shifted, and the sky darkened. A terrible storm brewed, the waves rising into towering mountains that threatened to swallow everything in their path. For two days and two nights, the hurricane raged, drowning out all sound and rendering travel impossible.

Curiosity and necessity drove us to Shark Island. One cannon, then two, fired into the distance. Moments later, the echo returned. The stranger was still there.

We signaled with our flag—a sign of peace—and retreated to the mainland, where the entire family gathered in a frenzy of anticipation. Fritz and I, armed with muskets, cutlasses, and spyglasses, ventured out in our canoe to investigate. We circled the high cliffs, scanning the shoreline where the gunshots had originated. Then, hidden behind the curve of Cape Pug-Nose, we saw it: a brig-of-war anchored in a secluded cove, flying the English colors.

At first, a shadow of doubt crossed my mind. Why would a naval vessel seek such a remote, treacherous harbor? Could it be a mutiny? A crew of pirates hiding in plain sight? But then I saw the captain speaking with an officer, his demeanor calm and dignified. I peered closer at the decks; they were spotless, the brass polished to a mirror finish. Discipline and order reigned supreme. It was a lawful ship, not a pirate den. Satisfied, we slipped back unnoticed, returning to Rockburg to report our findings.

Jenny's heart leaped at the news of Englishmen nearby, but I counseled patience. We could not approach as beggars. Instead, we worked feverishly to restore our dignity. The yacht, usually a humble craft, was scrubbed until her wood shone like varnish. Her guns were burnished, and the English flag was hoisted high at her peak. We dressed in our finest clothes, discarding our rough workwear for tailored suits and hats that would command respect.

As dawn broke, the family assembled in silence, the air heavy with unspoken emotion. Fritz and Jack returned with baskets overflowing with fresh fruit, gifts to welcome our new neighbors. Under sail, the yacht glided toward the cove, a tiny speck against the vast blue ocean. To the surprise of the crew on the brig, we emerged from behind the cape, a pleasure yacht manned by a family of explorers.

Captain Littlestone, a man of straightforward kindness, welcomed us aboard. He listened intently as I recounted our story—the shipwreck, our survival, and most importantly, our rescue of Miss Montrose. Tears welled in the captain's eyes as he revealed the truth: he had sailed specifically to find traces of her. The disappearance of the *Dorcas*, carrying her father, Colonel Montrose, had been a tragedy for three years. Survivors from a lifeboat had guided him here, leading him to believe that Miss Montrose might be alive on this shore.

"I owe you a debt of gratitude," the captain said, taking Fritz's hand. "Finding her... it is a miracle."

Soon, the rest of our party joined us. Lunch was served, and the captain shared news of his own companions: an invalid gentleman, Mr. Wolston, and his family, who had traveled from Sydney hoping to recover their health in the tropics. Later that afternoon, we visited them in their shaded encampment. Mr. Wolston, frail but hopeful, was charmed by our hospitality. That evening, unable to return to the mainland due to the storm-tossed waters, we stayed on shore, sharing stories until the stars appeared.

Later that night, Father and Mother sat together by the fire, their voices low and serious. The proximity to civilization had sparked a difficult debate. Should we return to Europe? The journey would be perilous, and the island had become a paradise they had built with their own hands. Yet, the pull of home remained. Slowly, gently, the truth surfaced. Neither parent, nor child, harbored a burning desire to leave. The love they had forged in isolation outweighed the allure of the Old World. They wished to stay. They wished to call this island "New Switzerland" forever.

"We must decide who goes," Father said softly, breaking the silence. "Which sons are meant to remain, and which to return?"

Time, it seemed, was needed to reveal the answers. Days passed, filled with preparations and visits. The captain and his crew toured our estates—Falconhurst, the gardens, the workshops. They marveled at the ingenuity of our settlements, the beauty of our homes, and the harmony of our family.
