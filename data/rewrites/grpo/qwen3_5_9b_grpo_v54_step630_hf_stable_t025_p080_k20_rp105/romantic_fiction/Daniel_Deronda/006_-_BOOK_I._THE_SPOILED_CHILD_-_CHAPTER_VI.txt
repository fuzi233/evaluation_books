"Croyez-vous m'avoir humiliée pour m'avoir appris que la terre tourne autour du soleil? Je vous jure que je ne m'en estime pas moins."
—FONTENELLE: *Pluralité des Mondes*.

That lofty rebuke had struck a fresh, stinging blow to Gwendolen's vanity. She would never have admitted to herself, let alone confessed to anyone, that she felt a profound regret for lacking Miss Arrowpoint's musical training. Without that foundation, she could not hope to challenge Herr Klesmer's taste with the authority of true knowledge. Even worse, she could not deny that every encounter with Miss Arrowpoint ignited a sharp, unwonted spark of jealousy within her. It was not merely because the other girl was an heiress; it was because it was infuriating that a woman of slight build, average height, small features, and a sallow complexion possessed a mental superiority that defied explanation. There was an exasperating thoroughness in Miss Arrowpoint's musical command, a fastidious discernment in her general tastes that commanded awe and made it impossible to force her admiration. This seemingly insignificant young lady of twenty-four might secretly believe Miss Harleth's accomplishments were merely common, yet she masked such thoughts beneath a veneer of perfect kindness.

Gwendolen refused to dwell on facts that cast such an unfavorable light on her own standing. The Musical Magus who had so suddenly expanded her horizons was not always present; his constant travel between London and Quetcham soon offered opportunities for her to convert him into a more admiring state of mind. Meanwhile, the evident pleasure her singing brought at Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and elsewhere restored her equanimity. She was inclined to trust approval far more than objection, and she was certainly not one of those rare souls who thirst for a perfection their neighbors do not demand. Perhaps it would have been rash to claim she was exceptional inwardly, or that the unusual in her extended beyond her rare grace of movement and a certain daring that added spice to a very common egoistic ambition. Such ambition often hides under clumsy exteriors, unnoticed by the world. After all, the tilt of the head does not truly determine the hunger of the inner self for supremacy; it only alters the method of attainment and the degree of success, especially for a girl whose passion for being remarkable finds its ideal limit in consistency with high breeding and a complete freedom from the sordid need for income. Gwendolen was inwardly rebellious against the restraints of family conditions, ready to look through obligations to her own fundamental lack of feeling for them, as if sustained by the boldest speculations. Yet, she possessed no such speculations. She would have instantly distinguished herself from any theoretical or practical reformer by satirizing them. She rejoiced in feeling exceptional, but her horizon remained that of the genteel romance: a heroine whose soul pours out in journals full of vague power, originality, and rebellion, while her life moves strictly within the sphere of fashion. If she wanders into a swamp, the pathos lies partly in her wearing satin shoes. This restraint, provided by nature and society, holds captive a soul burning with a sense of what the universe is not, ready to consume all existence as fuel, keeping it prisoner to the ordinary wirework of social forms and preventing it from doing anything particular.

This commonplace result was what Gwendolen found herself threatened with, even amidst the novelty of the first winter at Offendene. She was clear that she did not wish to lead the same life as ordinary young ladies, but she was unclear on how to begin leading another, or what specific acts she would perform to assert her freedom. Offendene remained a good background if anything exciting were to happen there, but on the whole, the neighborhood was at fault.

Beyond the initial impact of her beauty, there was little excitement to be gleaned from her earliest invitations. She returned home after little sallies of satire and knowingness, such as had offended Mrs. Arrowpoint, to fill the intervening days with girlish devices. Her strongest assertion of individual claim was to skip Alice's lessons (on the principle that Alice would excel in ignorance) and employ her with Miss Merry and the maid who waited on all the ladies. Together, they helped arrange various dramatic costumes Gwendolen had prepared for future charades or theatrical pieces, occasions she intended to create by sheer force of will. She had never acted professionally, only posed in *tableaux vivans* at school, but she felt assured of her ability. Having visited the Théâtre Français and heard her mother speak of Rachel, her waking dreams often turned to whether she should become an actress like that thin Jewess, since she was more beautiful. The wet days before Christmas were spent pleasantly preparing Greek, Oriental, and composite costumes. Gwendolen attitudinized and speechified before a domestic audience, even pressing the housekeeper to swell the notes of applause. However, after the housekeeper pointed out that Miss Harleth looked far more like a queen in her own dress than in a baggy outfit with bare arms, she was not invited again.

"Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?" Gwendolen asked one day, displaying herself in a Greek dress to Anna and enacting scraps of scenes with much tragic intention.

"You have better arms than Rachel," Mrs. Davilow replied. "Your arms would do for anything, Gwen. But your voice is not so tragic as hers; it is not so deep."

"I can make it deeper, if I like," Gwendolen said provisionally, then added with decision, "I think a higher voice is more tragic: it is more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic it seems when she does desperate actions."

"There may be something in that," Mrs. Davilow said languidly. "But I don't know what good there is in making one's blood creep. And if there is anything horrible to be done, I should like it to be left to the men."

"Oh, mamma, you are so dreadfully prosaic! As if all the great poetic criminals were not women! I think the men are poor cautious creatures."

"Well, dear, and you—who are afraid to be alone in the night—I don't think you would be very bold in crime, thank God."

"I am not talking about reality, mamma," Gwendolen said impatiently. Then, as her mother was called out, she turned quickly to her cousin. "Anna, do ask my uncle to let us get up some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could act with us—just for practice. Mamma says it will not do to have Mr. Middleton consulting and rehearsing here. He is a stick, but we could give him suitable parts. Do ask, or else I will."

"Oh, not till Rex comes. He is so clever, and such a dear old thing, and he will act Napoleon looking over the sea. He looks just like Napoleon. Rex can do anything."

"I don't in the least believe in your Rex, Anna," Gwendolen laughed. "He will turn out to be like those wretched blue and yellow water-colors of his which you hang up in your bedroom and worship."

"Very well, you will see," Anna said. "It is not that I know what is clever, but he has got a scholarship already, and papa says he will get a fellowship, and nobody is better at games. He is cleverer than Mr. Middleton, and everybody but you calls Mr. Middleton clever."

"So he may be in a dark-lantern sort of way. But he *is* a stick. If he had to say, 'Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,' he would say it in just the same tone as, 'Here endeth the second lesson.'"

"Oh, Gwendolen!" Anna exclaimed, shocked at these promiscuous allusions. "And it is very unkind of you to speak so of him, for he admires you very much. I heard Warham say one day to mamma, 'Middleton is regularly spooney upon Gwendolen.' She was very angry with him; but I know what it means. It is what they say at college for being in love."

"How can I help it?" Gwendolen said, rather contemptuously. "Perdition catch my soul if I love *him*."

"No, of course; papa, I think, would not wish it. And he is to go away soon. But it makes me sorry when you ridicule him."

"What shall you do to me when I ridicule Rex?" Gwendolen asked wickedly.

"Now, Gwendolen, dear, you *will not*?" Anna said, her eyes filling with tears. "I could not bear it. But there really is nothing in him to ridicule. Only you may find out things. For no one ever thought of laughing at Mr. Middleton before you. Every one said he was nice-looking, and his manners perfect. I am sure I have always been frightened at him because of his learning and his square-cut coat, and his being a nephew of the bishop's, and all that. But you will not ridicule Rex—promise me." Anna ended with a beseeching look that touched Gwendolen.

"You are a dear little coz," she said, touching the tip of Anna's chin. "I don't ever want to do anything that will vex you. Especially if Rex is to make everything come off—charades and everything."

When Rex finally arrived, the animation he brought to Offendene and the rectory, along with his ready partnership in Gwendolen's plans, left her no inclination for any ridicule that was not open and flattering. He was a fine, open-hearted youth with a handsome face resembling his father's and Anna's, yet softer in expression and larger in scale. He possessed a bright, healthy, loving nature that enjoyed ordinary innocent things so much that vice held no temptation for him. His knowledge of vice lay too entirely in the outer courts of his mind for him to think of it with great repulsion. Vicious habits were simply "what some fellows did"—"stupid stuff" he liked to keep aloof from. He returned Anna's affection as fully as could be expected of a brother whose pleasures apart from her exceeded the sum total of hers; he had never known a stronger love.

The cousins were continually together, chiefly at Offendene where there was more freedom, or rather where Gwendolen held a more complete sway. Whatever she wished became a ruling purpose for Rex. The charades came off according to her plans, along with other impromptu scenes where her acting shone. It was at Offendene that the rehearsals and presentations took place. Mrs. Davilow saw no objection to Mr. Middleton joining them now that Rex was there, especially since his services were indispensable. Warham, studying for India with a Manchester "coach," had no time to spare and was generally dismal under a cram of everything except the answers needed for his examination, which might disclose the welfare of the Indian Empire to be connected with a quotable knowledge of Browne's Pastorals.

Mr. Middleton was persuaded to play various grave parts, Gwendolen flattering him on his enviable immobility of countenance. At first, a little pained and jealous of her comradeship with Rex, he presently drew encouragement from the thought that this cousinly familiarity excluded any serious passion. Indeed, he occasionally felt that her more formal treatment of him was such a sign of favor as to warrant his making advances before he left Pennicote, though he had intended to keep his feelings in reserve until his position was more assured. Miss Gwendolen, quite aware that she was adored by this unexceptionable young clergyman with pale whiskers and a square-cut collar, felt nothing more on the subject than that she had no objection to being adored. She turned her eyes on him with calm mercilessness, causing him many mildly agitating hopes by seeming always to avoid dramatic contact with him—for all meanings, we know, depend on the key of interpretation.

Some might have thought beforehand that a young man of Anglican leanings, with a sense of sacredness exercised on small as well as great things, rarely laughing save from politeness, and regarding the mention of spades by their naked names as coarse, would not see a fitting bride in a girl who was daring in ridicule and showed none of the special grace required in a clergyman's wife. Or that a young man informed by theological reading would reflect he was unlikely to meet the taste of a lively, restless young lady like Miss Harleth. But are we always obliged to explain why the facts are not what some persons thought beforehand? The apology lies on their side, who had that erroneous way of thinking.

As for Rex, who would possibly have been sorry for poor Middleton if he had been aware of the excellent curate's inward conflict, he was too completely absorbed in a first passion to have observation for any person or thing. He did not observe Gwendolen; he only felt what she said or did, and the back of his head seemed a good organ of information as to whether she was in the room or out. Before the end of the first fortnight, he was so deeply in love that it was impossible for him to think of his life except as bound up with Gwendolen's. He could see no obstacles; his own love seemed a guarantee of hers, since it was one with the unperturbed delight in her image, so that he could no more dream of her giving him pain than an Egyptian could dream of snow. She sang and played to him whenever he liked, was always glad of his companionship in riding, though his borrowed steeds were often comic, and was ready to join in any fun of his, showing a right appreciation of Anna. No mark of sympathy seemed absent. That Gwendolen was the most perfect creature in the world and thus destined for a grand match had not occurred to him. He had no conceit—at least not more than goes to make up the necessary gum and consistence of a substantial personality. It was only that in the young bliss of loving, he took Gwendolen's perfection as part of that good which had seemed one with life to him, being the outcome of a happy, well-embodied nature.

One incident during their dramatic attempts impressed Rex as a sign of her unusual sensibility. It showed an aspect of her nature that could not have been preconceived by anyone who, like him, had only seen her habitual fearlessness in active exercises and her high spirits in society.
