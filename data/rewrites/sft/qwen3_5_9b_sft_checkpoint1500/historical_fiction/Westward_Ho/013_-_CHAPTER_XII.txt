"How three lords sat drinking late yestreen,
And ere they paid the lawing,
They set a combat them between,
To fight it in the dawing"—so ran an ancient Scotch Ballad.

Every traveler who knows Bideford must know Bideford Bridge; it is the very center, the focal point, and the soul around which the town, like a living organism, has organized itself. Just as Edinburgh defines itself by its castle, Rome by its Capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids, so Bideford exists as Bideford solely by virtue of this bridge. Yet, few understand the occult powers that have animated this wondrous structure for nearly five centuries, elevating it, according to Prince and Fuller, to the status of a chief wonder of Devonshire. It was first conceived as an inspired bridge, a soul-saving conduit, an alms-giving haven, an educational pillar, a sentient entity, and finally, though not least, a dinner-providing monument. Few know how, when construction began half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried the stones downstream each night to their final resting place. Nor do they recall how Sir Richard Gurney, parish priest, one night plagued by the fear of an evil spirit haunting his sheepfold, beheld a vision of an angel directing him to build precisely where those materials had been miraculously transported—the only spot offering sure foundation amidst the shifting sands. Even fewer know how Bishop Grandison of Exeter proclaimed indulgences and benedictions throughout his diocese to all who promoted this dangerous ford, urging men to "make the best of both worlds" by securing salvation and crossing safely.

Few, perhaps not even the narrator, know that "though the foundation of the bridge is laid upon wool, yet it shakes at the slightest step of a horse;" or that despite possessing twenty-three arches, a man named William Alford (another Milo) once carried four bushels of saltwater measure on his back for a wager across its entire length. The bridge is a veritable esquire, bearing its own coat of arms—a ship and a bridge on a plain field—and owning lands and tenements in numerous parishes. From these holdings, the miraculous bridge has founded charities, built schools, waged legal suits, and, most pertinently for our story, hosted annual dinners, maintaining for that purpose the finest wine cellar in all Devon.

To one of these banquets, in the year 1583, were invited all the notables of Bideford, alongside Mr. St. Leger of Annery Close, brother of the Marshal of Munster and of Lady Grenville. He was a worthy and hospitable gentleman, one who found riches a snare and parted with them so freely to his neighbors that he effectively shielded his descendants from the temptations thereof. Between Mr. St. Leger and one of the bridge trustees, a debate arose regarding whether a salmon caught below the bridge was superior to one caught above. Since this weighty question required practical experiment, Mr. St. Leger vowed that, having been graciously entertained by the bridge, he would reciprocate. Offering a bet of five pounds, he promised to procure a salmon from the pool below Annery as firm-flaked as the one they had just consumed from Appledore. In the fulness of his heart, he then extended an invitation to the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days hence, requesting that each bring a wife or daughter; and, as fortune would have it, Don Guzman was likewise invited.

Thus, a magnificent feast ensued in the great hall at Annery, scarcely rivaled since Judge Hankford feasted King Edward IV there. While guests ate their fill and drank their fill, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman engaged in a silent pantomime, watching one another with intensity. Rose, however, exercised extreme caution with her glances; not only was her father present at the table, but seated directly opposite her were Messrs. William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in Her Majesty's Irish Army returning on furlough.

Though Rose and the Spaniard had not exchanged a word in six months—having met frequently before—they had maintained a strict distance in her father's house. The Spaniard, however, ensured he appeared oblivious to her presence save for the stateliest salutations at entry and exit. Yet, whenever he was in her company, he laid himself open to the utmost advantage, striving to be wittier, more eloquent, more romantic, and more filled with marvelous tales than ever before. The cunning Don realized his initial tactics had failed; having been foiled by Rose's refusal of his love, he now employed a far more formidable strategy. First, Rose deserved severe punishment for daring to reject a Spanish nobleman. What greater penalty could he impose than withdrawing the honor of his attentions and the sunshine of his smiles? Though conceit fueled this notion, it was underpinned by cunning. The Spaniard knew better than anyone that women, like the world, tend to value a man—at least if there is genuine worth in him—at his own self-assessed price. The higher the price he demanded, the more they would pay.

Now, he intended to set a high price on himself and prick her pride, for she was too accustomed to worship to be won by flattery. He could have achieved this by attending to someone else, but he was too wise to resort to such a crude method, which might spark indignation, disgust, or despair in Rose's heart without ever bringing her to his knees. Instead, he quietly and unobtrusively signaled that he could do without her. The poor fool, as fate intended, immediately began to ask herself why? What hidden treasure, what reserve force rendered him independent of her, while she could not claim similar independence? Did he possess a secret? How delightful it would be to know! Some grand ambition? How sweet to share! Some mysterious knowledge? How delicious to learn! Some capacity for love beyond the common? How exquisite to possess exclusively! He must be greater, wiser, richer-hearted, and better-born than she. Ah, if only his wealth could supply her poverty! Step by step, she was being led to sue *in forma pauperis* to the very man she had spurned when he sued her in like form. The temptation of possessing some mysterious private treasure, of serving as the priestess of a hidden sanctuary, and of thanking Heaven for not being like other women, was becoming too heavy for Rose to bear, as it is for most. For few understood better than the Spaniard how deeply women, by the very laws of their sex, cherish worshipping more than being worshipped, and obeying more than being obeyed; how their coyness and scorn serve as masks to hide a consciousness of weakness, masks they themselves will often grow weary of first.

Rose was utterly exhausted by that same mask as she sat at the Annery table. Don Guzman perceived it in her uneasy, downcast gaze. Thinking himself a conceited coxcomb, he concluded she must be sufficiently punished. Stealing glances at her, he was unashamed when he saw her eyes drop upon contact, noticing how her silence and abstraction increased, and how a blush crept into her cheeks. So he feigned equal downcast abstraction, continuing his glances until he once found her looking at him to verify if he was looking back. Then he knew his prey was secure. He asked her, with his eyes, "Do you forgive me?" Seeing her halt abruptly in conversation with her neighbor, falter, lower her eyes, and lift them again after a minute in search of his, he repeated the pleasant inquiry. What choice did she have but to answer, with every feature and bend of her pretty neck, "And do you forgive me in turn?"

Whereupon Don Guzman erupted like a nightingale on a bough, bursting forth with stories, jests, and repartees. He instantly became the soul of the company and the most charming cavalier of all. Poor Rose knew she was the catalyst for his sudden mood shift, blaming herself for her actions, shuddering and blushing at her own delight, longing for the feast to end so she might hasten home and hide away with sweet fantasies about a love whose reality she felt she dared not face.

Beautiful was the scene on the great terrace at Annery that afternoon. Smart dames in gaudy dresses paraded up and down in twos and threes before the stately house, or looked down upon the park, where old oaks, deer, and a broad land-locked river spread like a lake lay bathed in the glare of midsummer. Others listened obsequiously to the two great ladies hosting the day, Mrs. St. Leger and her sister-in-law, the fair Lady Grenville. All chatted, laughed, admired each other's dresses, and gossiped about husbands and servants. Only Rose Salterne remained apart, longing to retreat to a corner to laugh or cry, knowing not which.

"Our pretty Rose seems sad," observed Lady Grenville, approaching her. "Cheer up, child! We want you to sing for us."

Rose replied vaguely and obeyed mechanically. Taking the lute, she sat on a bench beneath the house while the others gathered around.

"What shall I sing?"

"Sing your old song, 'Earl Haldan's Daughter.'"

Rose shrank from it. It was a loud, dashing ballad ill-suited to her current thoughts; Frank had praised it in happier, long-gone days. She thought of him, of others, of her pride and carelessness.
