As long as there were eager card partners waiting for her at her lodgings and the Tunbridge Wells assembly rooms, Madame de Bernstein settled happily into life at the spa, splitting her days between sharp-tongued lectures for her nephew Harry and notching up steady wins at her regular rubber of whist.
At Harry’s age, nearly every spot feels bright and welcoming, so long as it offers lively company, fresh country air, and your fair share of sport and small diversions. Every pleasure tastes sweeter at twenty. We run out to meet joy at a full sprint, speculate on all the fun it will bring, and once a dance or a picnic or a long-awaited party is scheduled, count the days until it arrives. How coolly, how gently we greet those same delights when we reach the end of life’s long season! Madam, do you not recall your very first ball? Does your memory not wander back to that glowing past sometimes, when you sit resting against the wall watching your own daughters spin across the dance floor? For my own part, I can still remember when I thought it the height of delight to walk three miles out into the country and three miles back just to dine with old Captain Jones. Fancy wanting to tramp six miles now just to sup with Jones and drink his thin half-pay port! No doubt that wine was bought cheap from a tiny village vintner, but it was offered with a full, kind welcome, and youth lent it a richness that no aged bottle or seasoned man could ever match these days. *Viximus nuper* – we lived but recently, and none of us can afford to cast too harsh a eye on young Harry’s stretch of idle pleasure, not even his stern friend the Colonel of the Twentieth Regiment, James Wolfe.
O blessed idleness! Divine, lazy nymph! Hand me a novel as I curl up in my dressing-gown at three o’clock in the afternoon, mix me a sherry-cobbler, and set a cigar at my elbow! Dear, soft-smiling, slatternly enchantress! The world may heap cruel names upon you, swear your character away, and call you the Mother of Evil – but for all that, you are the very best company on earth!
Lord of March soon departed for his northern estates, and Lord Chesterfield found the Tunbridge mineral waters did nothing to ease his worsening deafness, so he retreated back to his quiet solitude at Blackheath. But scores of other fashionable gentlemen stayed behind to make merry, and young Mr. Warrington never lacked for companions at his regular table at the White Horse Inn. He picked up the ways of a man of fashion with remarkable speed: soon he could order an elaborate French dinner every bit as well as the most seasoned dandy out of St. James’s, chatted to innkeeper Monsieur Barbeau far more fluently in the man’s native French than most other spa visitors, and developed a sharp, refined taste for wine that let him tell a Clos Vougeot apart from a Romanee with barely a single sip. He was the uncrowned young king of the Wells, and the easy-going, worldly regulars who frequented the spot were hardly shocked by his growing reputation for gallantry and extravagance – the very reputation that had so alarmed Mr. Wolfe.
Though our Virginian moved full time amongst revellers, swimming in the same pleasure-seeking current as all the other loose, careless visitors, the boy had a natural shrewdness and stubborn core of honesty that kept him clear of the snares and traps so often laid for unwary young men of fortune. He almost never made foolhardy bets with the jolly idle fellows around him, and even the oldest, sharpest card sharps at the spa found they could barely trick him out of a farthing. He joined in every game, indoors and out, because he had a natural knack for athletic and playful contests, and could hold his own against almost any fair competitor. He was scrupulous to only play against men he knew to be honourable, and always settled his own debts on the spot the second a match ended. No, he would have cut a very poor figure at a stuffy university examination – but he possessed prudence, fierce loyalty, sharp common sense, overwhelming generosity, and dauntless personal courage, and he got no shortage of chances to prove what he was made of.
Take for instance the troublesome little opera dancer Cattarina, who had already landed him in so much trouble once before. When her importunate demands for money crossed the line Harry drew for his own generosity, he extricated himself from the opera siren’s advances with perfect coolness and grace, leaving her to cast her nets over some softer, easier mark. The girl’s hysterical mother showed up at his lodgings, swearing that a cruel bailiff had seized all her daughter’s trinkets for unpaid debt, and that her venerable old father was rotting in a London debtor’s prison. Harry told her plainly he had no dealings with bailiffs, that just because he’d enjoyed Cattarina’s company and bought her small trinkets and knick-knacks when she fancied them, he was under no obligation to pay off her family’s longstanding debts. He refused point blank to stand bail for her father in London, or settle her outstanding bills at Tunbridge.
The old woman first screamed at him, calling him a monster and an ungrateful wretch, then leered at him like a veteran of a hundred such scams and asked why he did not demand payment for the little attentions he’d lavished on the girl. At first Harry did not understand what she meant – but when the full, horrifying import of her offer landed, the honest young man rose up in utter revulsion. He told her that in his Virginia homeland even the native Iroquois would recoil from bartering a child’s honour for gold, bowed her ceremoniously to the door, and ordered his servant Gumbo to mark her face well and never let her cross his threshold again. She stormed off swearing vengeance, squawking that no Turk or Persian would ever treat a defenceless lady so, and she and Cattarina fled back to London as soon as their landlord would let them leave.
Harry faced perils of the gaming tables as well, of course. As the old saying goes, any man who plays at bowls has to expect a few rubbers. One evening after dinner at the ordinary, Harry declined to play piquet any longer with Captain Batts, and when the man roughly demanded his reason, Harry told him straight he only played against gentlemen who settled their debts as promptly as he did. He added that he would be more than happy to play any number of matches with Batts the second their outstanding little account was settled. The captain declared himself perfectly satisfied, and snuck out of the Wells the very next day without paying Harry or a single one of his other creditors.
Harry also got the chance to show his mettle when he thrashed a rude sedan chairman who had accosted elderly spinster Miss Whiffler one evening as she made her way to the assembly rooms. And when the malicious gossip about him and Cattarina was repeated by Mr. Hector Buckler, one of the most quarrelsome regulars at the Wells, Harry walked right up to him in the crowded pump room, where Buckler was regaling a whole circle of water-drinkers with the lie, and publicly told him the story was a total falsehood, and he would hold any man who repeated it accountable to him from that day forward.
So even though Harry did as the Romans did, living fully into the pleasure-seeking ways of Tunbridge Wells society, he proved himself a valiant, honourable Roman – running with the wolves, as the French phrase goes, even James Wolfe himself admitted he was as brave and true as the best of the pack. When Wolfe found out his young friend was entirely innocent of the ugly rumours he’d circulated, he lost no time in going to see the Lamberts and retracting every last odious charge he’d made against Harry.
The whole Lambert household burst with joy at Harry’s vindication, that bright, tender pleasure good people feel when a soul they care for is redeemed from unfair slander. Never had that little family been so happy – not even when they got the news that their son Tom had won his university scholarship – as when Colonel Wolfe rode over with the full account of his conversation with Harry Warrington.
“If you’d ridden up here leading a whole new regiment for me, James, I don’t think I could be any more pleased,” Colonel Lambert declared.
Mrs. Lambert called her two daughters in from the garden, kissed them both the second they stepped through the door, and blurted out the good news. Hetty jumped up and down for joy, and that night Theo played a series of dazzling, bright flourishes on the harpsichord that she had never managed to pull off before. When Dr. Boyle dropped in for their usual weekly backgammon game, he could not make heads or tails of the glowing, giddy look on all their faces, until the three women burst into happy chorus telling him how right his recent sermon had been, and how dreadfully they had all wronged that poor, dear, good young Mr. Warrington.
“What say we, my dear?” Colonel Lambert leaned over the table and whispered to his wife. “The hay’s all in, we won’t be cutting the corn for another fortnight, the horses are standing idle in the stables. What if we…”
His wife clapped her hands over his, beaming. “My dearest Martin! That’s the very thing!”
“What’s the very thing, mother?” yelled young Charley, home for his Bartlemytide holidays.
“The very thing is that we’re going in to supper right now,” Mrs. Lambert laughed. “Come along, Doctor! We’re opening a bottle of the best wine tonight, to drink a toast to repentance for every soul that speaks ill of a good young man without proof.”
“Amen to that,” the Doctor smiled, lifting his glass. “With all my heart.”
And so the warm, worthy little family trooped off to their supper, full of joy and bright, unspoken plans.
