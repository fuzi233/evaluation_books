Margaret Brandt had undergone a profound transformation. She moved through her household duties as if drifting in a fevered dream, her senses dulled by a pervasive dread. A mere sharp word from Peter sent her heart hammering against her ribs, her eyes wide with terror as she stared at him. Her visits to her old friend, Margaret Van Eyck, became rare and fraught with discomfort; instead of meeting the elder woman's warm embraces, Margaret would stand passive and trembling, sometimes shrinking back as if burned. Yet, the most striking change was her refusal to venture outdoors in daylight. She would only slip away to Tergou after dusk, returning before the sun could rise again, avoiding even the morning prayers.

Peter, though usually oblivious to such subtleties, eventually noticed her absence and pressed her for an explanation.

"I fear the people all stare at me," she whispered.

One afternoon, Margaret Van Eyck, concerned, asked her directly what troubled her. Margaret offered only a frightened glance and a torrent of tears. The old lady spoke gently, urging her to share her burdens. "What, sweetheart, afraid to confide your sorrows to me?"

"I have no sorrows, madam, but ones of my own making," Margaret replied, her voice thin. "I am treated with more kindness than I deserve, especially within these walls."

"Then why not come to me oftener, my dear?"

"I come oftener than I deserve," she sighed, the weight of her guilt heavy upon her.

"There, Reicht is crying for you," Margaret Van Eyck said, pointing to the child. "Go, child—what on earth can it be?"

Speculating on the cause, Margaret Van Eyck suspected Margaret felt mortified by the contempt shown to her by Gerard's family. Resolved to intervene, she decided to reprimand the women of the household. The following day, hooded and cloaked, accompanied by Reicht, she marched to the hosier's house. Catherine received her with respectful tears, thanking her for her past kindness to Gerard. However, when Margaret Van Eyck attempted to speak of Margaret Brandt, Catherine's demeanor hardened instantly. Her eyes dried, her lips thinned, and she adopted the stubborn, impenetrable expression of an ignorant woman. To try to soften such a face was like attempting to melt a brick wall. Margaret Van Eyck, unused to being thwarted, grew provoked. She finally withdrew with an abrupt, truncated curtsey, which Catherine returned with an air of defiant challenge.

Stepping outside, Margaret Van Eyck found Reicht talking with a pale girl leaning on crutches. As the old lady pushed past them, her face flushed with indignation and her head tossed in scorn toward the entire family, a small, delicate hand suddenly glided timidly into hers. Turning, Margaret saw two dove-like eyes filled with tears, looking up with a gaze that was both grateful and imploring. Reading this complex expression, Margaret's anger dissolved instantly. She stopped, kissed Kate's brow, and said, "I see. Then, I leave it to you."

Returning home, she reflected, "I visited a house today where I witnessed both the common and the uncommon. I saw a stupid, obstinate woman, and I also saw an angel in the flesh—a face so beautiful that if I had my brushes here, I would take them down again and try to paint it."

Little Kate did not disappoint the high opinion Margaret had formed of her. Waiting for a quiet moment, she told her mother what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: that Margaret had shed her very blood for Gerard in the woods.

"See, mother, how she loves him," Kate said softly.

"Who would not love him?"

"Oh, mother, think of it! Poor thing."

"Ay, wench. She has her own troubles, no doubt, just as we have mine. I cannot bear the sight of blood, let alone my own."

This was a significant step forward, but when Kate tried to press further, she was met with silence.

About a month later, a soldier of the Dalgetty tribe, returning from service in Burgundy, delivered a letter to the hosier's house. He was on business elsewhere, but the family sat down to supper. The soldier placed the letter on the table beside Catherine and, refusing any payment, departed for Sevenbergen.

The letter was unfolded. Curiously, though none of them could read the script, they all knew immediately it was Gerard's handwriting.

"And your father must be away," cried Catherine. "Are you not ashamed of yourselves? Not one of you can read your brother's letter."

Though the words were hieroglyphics to them, the letter held a meaning they could decipher. There exists an art that speaks without words, unfettered by the limits of the pen, capable of stealing through the eye straight into the heart and mind of the learned and the unlearned alike. It crosses frontiers and seas without losing its essence, needing no translator, for it writes a universal language.

When they beheld this, a simple sketch of two hands clasped together, drawn by Gerard between the two short paragraphs of his text, they understood. It went straight to their hearts. Gerard was bidding them farewell.

As they gazed upon that simple drawing, recognizing his manner in every turn and line, Gerard seemed present, saying goodbye. The women wept until they could barely see the paper.

Giles murmured, "Poor Gerard!" in a voice lower than seemed natural for him. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt felt a sudden pang of remorse, sitting silent and gloomy.

But how could they get the words read to them? They were reluctant to show their ignorance and their raw emotion to a stranger.

"The Dame Van Eyck?" Kate suggested timidly.

"And so I will, Kate," her father agreed. "She has a good heart. She loves Gerard, too. She will be glad to hear of him. I was short with her when she came here, but I will make my submission, and then she will tell me what my poor child says to me."

Soon, Catherine was at Margaret Van Eyck's house. Reicht led her into a room and said, "Wait a moment; she is at her prayers."

Inside, a young woman sat pensively by the stove. She rose and courteously made way for the visitor.

"Thank you, young lady; the winter nights are cold, and your stove is a treat," Catherine said, warming her hands. As she did so, she inspected her companion furtively from head to toe. The young woman wore an ordinary wimple, but her gown was trimmed with fur—a sign of superior rank or wealth in those days. What struck Catherine most, however, was the candor and modesty of the girl's face. Feeling sure of sympathy from such a good countenance, she began to chat.

"Now, what brings me here, young lady? It is a letter! A letter from my poor boy who is far away in some savage land. And I take shame to say that none of us can read it. I wonder whether you can?"

"Yes."

"Can you, now? It is much to your credit, my dear. I dare say she won't be long, but every minute feels like an hour to a poor longing mother."

"I will read it to you."

"Bless you, my dear; bless you!"

In her unfeigned eagerness, Catherine never noticed the suppressed urgency with which the hand was slowly extended to take the letter. She did not see the tremor as the fingers closed around it.

"Come, then, read it to me, prithee. I am wearying for it."

"The first words are, 'To my honoured parents.'"

"Ay! And he always did honour us, poor soul."

"'God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.'"

Catherine laid her hand on her bosom and sank back in her chair with one long sob.

"Then comes this, madam. It speaks for itself: 'a long farewell.'"

"Ay, go on; bless you, girl, you give me sorry comfort. Still, it is comfort."

"'To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!'"

"What does that mean? Ah!"

"'To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father's house. Be kind to her—for poor Gerard's sake: since grief to her is death to me.'"

Catherine gasped, "Ah!" Nature, resenting the poor girl's struggle for unnatural composure, suddenly gave way. She sank from her chair and lay insensible, the letter still in her hand, her head resting on Catherine's knees.
