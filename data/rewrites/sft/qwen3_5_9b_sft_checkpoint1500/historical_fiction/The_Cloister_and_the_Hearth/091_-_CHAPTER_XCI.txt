News spread swiftly that Margaret Van Eyck had passed, and within days, her funeral rites were concluded. By a will she had drawn up a year prior, she bequeathed her entire estate to her beloved daughter, Margaret Brandt, following the costs of interment and specific gifts to Reicht Heynes. She requested that Reicht remain under Margaret Brandt's roof until he wed, a testament to the bond they shared. Through this inheritance, Margaret acquired a fully furnished dwelling, alongside a collection of sketches and paintings that, in modern times, would constitute a vast fortune. Among these treasures hung a portrait she cherished above all others—a depiction titled "A Betrothal." The canvas captured a solemnity etched into the groom's grave visage and a demure, composed grace in the bride. Though largely executed by Margaret Van Eyck herself, the remainder was the hand of Jan. The details surrounding the couple were rendered with exquisite mastery, remaining a marvel of artistic skill to the present day. Before her passing, Margaret sent word to Reicht to remain in the house until she could summon the courage to re-enter it, asking him to guard the painting carefully, noting it was hidden in a small cupboard—referring to the diptych.

Shortly thereafter, Luke Peterson returned home and learned that Gerard was now a monk. Joy surged within him, threatening to overwhelm his senses. Rushing to Margaret, he declared, "Take heed, mistress. If he cannot marry you, I can."

"You?" Margaret asked, surprised. "I have seen him."

"But he is a friar!" Luke insisted.

"He was my husband and the father of my boy long before he took vows," Margaret replied firmly. "I have seen him, I have seen him."

Luke grew thoroughly puzzled. "Tell you what," he suggested, "I have a cousin who is a lawyer. I will go and ask him whether you are married or single."

"Nay," Margaret chided, "I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. Such inquiries would turn me into town gossip, oh, fie!"

"That rumor is already spreading without a word from me."

"But not by anyone seeking my respect. If you pursue this, never approach me again."

"Ay," Luke sighed, "you are like a dove to everyone else, but a hard-hearted tyrant to me."

"'Tis your own fault, dear Luke, for courting me. That prevents me from being kinder to you than I wish, Luke, my bonny lad, listen. I am wealthy now; I can make friends happy, though not myself. Look around the street, look around the parish. There are many fairer wenches than I, twice told, and not ruined by weeping. Look higher; choose wisely. Speak to the lass yourself, and I will speak to her mother; they shall not refuse you. Take my word for it."

"I understand what you mean," Luke said, turning a deep shade of crimson. "But if I cannot have your affection, I want none of your money. I served you when you were poor as I, and poorer. If you prefer to be a friar's mistress rather than an honest man's wife, you are not the woman I took you for. Let us part without malice; seek your comfort on that road where no woman has ever found it, and for me, I will live and die a bachelor. Good evening, mistress."

"Farewell, dear Luke; and may God forgive you for speaking thus to me."

For several days, Margaret awaited the inevitable meeting with Gerard with a mixture of dread and longing. She reasoned that perhaps he would keep away briefly, as she might, but eventually, the truth of his paternity would surface, and his desire to see their son would compel him. "If," she thought, "this meeting does not kill me, I shall feel better afterward than I do now."

However, as day succeeded day without his appearance, a chilling suspicion began to gnaw at her mind. Was his absence intentional? Had he fled to some ruthless monastic order where they forbade contact with former lovers? The convent, she recalled, had proven as merciless to true love as the grave itself. At the thought, her vitality seemed to drain away.

Deep indignation began to mingle with her grief. "Could he have ever truly loved me?" she wondered, anger rising in her chest. "To flee from me and his child without a single word! Why does this poor Luke think more of me than he does?"

Suddenly, Giles erupted in a roar. "I have hit the mark! Our Gerard is the Vicar of Gouda."

A brief sketch of Giles's transformation at court prepares the reader for his account. Months prior, his intelligence had begun to bud. He dated this awakening to a specific eighth of June, when, swinging by one hand along with a week's washing on a tightrope in the drying yard, something cracked inside his head. Lo! Intellectual powers were unchained. At court, his shrewdness, blunt speech, gigantic voice, and diminutive stature made him a power; without his small size, he feared he would have been condemned to the gallows. Both the Young Duchess of Burgundy and Marie, the heiress apparent, doted on him as great ladies have always favored dwarfs. The court poet melted butter by the six-foot rule, pouring enough down Giles's back to stew Goliath. Even Giles's rough dictations were amplified, versified, and softened by the poet.

The resulting prolixity reached Eli via letter, titled: "The high and puissant Princess Marie of Bourgogne her lytel jantilman hys complaynt of y' Coort, and praise of a rusticall lyfe, versificated, and empapyred by me the lytel jantilman's right lovynge and obsequious servitor, etc."

Yet Giles reached his apex through a fortunate mixture of mind and muscle. The day before a grand joust, he challenged the Duke's giant to a contest of strength. The challenge provoked a grim grin and raised expectations. Giles erected a lofty pole, ascended it like a squirrel at the appointed hour, used his immense arm strength to form a right angle with his body, and remained suspended there. Then, sliding down with terrifying speed, he caused the high and puissant princess to squeak and bury her face in her hands, terrified to witness the demise of her pocket-Hercules. The giant managed only about ten feet, then looked ruefully upward and downward, descending bathed in sweat to justify his failure.

"It was not the dwarf's greater strength, but his smaller body," the giant offered as an excuse.

The spectators received this justification with loud derision. The fact stood clear: the dwarf was mighty at climbing poles; the giant was merely mighty at excuses. In short, Giles had gauged their intellects—with his own body, no doubt.

"Come," Giles said, "if you go that route, I will wrestle you, my lad, if you will allow me to blindfold your eyes."

Smarting from defeat, the giant, believing he could recover honor through this means, readily agreed.

"Madam," Giles addressed the court, "see yonder blind Samson? At a signal from me, he shall make me a low obeisance and unbend his hat."

"How may that be, being blinded?" a maid of honor inquired.

"I'll wager on Giles for one," the princess declared.

"That is my affair."

As wagers were placed on both sides, Giles struck the giant squarely in the bread-basket. The giant doubled over—the obeisance—and his bonnet fell off. The company yelled with delight at this delicate stroke of wit, and Giles fled on his heels. The giant pursued as soon as he could regain his breath and remove his bandages, but it was too late. Giles had prepared a small door in the wall, through which he could pass, but not a giant, and had colored it so artfully it appeared to be solid masonry. He tore it open and plunged through, leaving no trace save a poem written boldly upon the reverse of his trick door:

Long limbs, big body, panting wit
By wee and wise is bet and bit.

After this feat, Giles became a force.

He now spoke for himself. Finding Margaret unable to believe the news, skeptical of the Holy Church being administered by dwarfs, he narrated as follows:

"When the princess summoned me to her chamber, as per custom, to keep her from lethargy, I arrived not merry nor filled with country ditties, as is my wont, but dull as lead.

"Why, what aileth thee?" she asked. "Art sick?" "At heart," I replied. "Alas, he is in love," she remarked. Whereupon five brazen hussies, whom they call maids of honor, laughed loudly. "Not so mad as that," I said, "seeing what I see at court regarding women folk."

"There, ladies," the princess said, "let him be. 'Tis a liberal mannikin, and still giveth more than he taketh of saucy words.'

"In all seriousness," she asked, "what is the matter?"

I told her I was meditating, and what perplexed me was that common folk sometimes kept their word, but princes never did.

"Hayday," she exclaimed, "thy shafts fly high this morn." I told her, "Ay, for they hit the Truth."
