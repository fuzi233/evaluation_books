The Dashwoods, by degrees, were rooted at Barton as a plant is rooted when it has been shifted—at first a little drooping from the change, then, with attentive tending, standing more upright than it ever did in the old border. The cottage—its low ceilings, its honest little rooms, its garden that seemed to keep the weather’s memory in every leaf—grew familiar to them; and the employments which had once made Norland tolerable were taken up again with an enjoyment more keen for having been interrupted by grief. There was comfort in the ordinary: the soft scratch of Elinor’s pencil; the whisper of pages under Mrs. Dashwood’s hand; the scissor’s brisk bite through thread; the kettle’s domestic song as it came up to the boil. Even the sounds outdoors had their order—wind moving in the hedge, rooks arguing in the elms, the thin far-off strike of a farm tool—so different from any harsher clamour that would have belonged to a town. Here, nothing mechanical drowned the living world; each noise had a source you could name, as each herb in the garden had a scent you could crush between finger and thumb.

Sir John Middleton, who in the first fortnight called upon them daily and was little used to finding women at home otherwise than in idleness, could never conceal his astonishment at encountering them always engaged. They were, to his thinking, perpetually doing something—reading, drawing, arranging, mending, planning—when, in his opinion, they might have been doing the far more sensible thing of driving about the neighbourhood and “seeing people.” But though he pressed them, and though he repeated with hearty generosity that his carriage was always theirs, Mrs. Dashwood’s independent spirit—made warmer, perhaps, by misfortune—overruled her desire of society for her children. She was determined to visit no family beyond walking distance.

This resolution, however, did not much enlarge their acquaintance; for there were few within reach, and fewer still to whom they could be readily introduced. On one of their earliest walks they had discovered, about a mile and a half from the cottage, along the narrow winding valley of Allenham (which issued from that of Barton), an ancient, respectable-looking mansion. Something in its aspect—older, graver, shaded by trees of a size that seemed to belong to another century—touched their imagination by reminding them, in a softened way, of Norland. They wished to know it better. Yet on enquiry they learned that its possessor, an elderly lady of excellent character, was unfortunately too infirm to mix with the world, and never stirred from home.

The country, nevertheless, made amends for the want of drawing-rooms. It abounded in walks which were not mere paths, but histories: lanes worn by generations of boots; banks where thyme grew, bruising its sharp, clean fragrance underfoot; little hollows where rain collected and held the sky in a trembling mirror. The high downs that rose so temptingly from almost every window of the cottage offered, when the valleys were clogged with mud, an alternative as wholesome as it was exhilarating. Their summits promised air that tasted of distance—salted by the unseen sea, spiced by gorse, and always carrying, in its sound, the wide liberty of open ground.

It was towards one of these hills that Marianne and Margaret, one memorable morning, directed their steps. Two days of settled rain had kept them within doors, and though the parlour was warm and the tea fragrant, the confinement had grown insupportable to Marianne. Now the sky was showery, but broken—patches of sunshine travelling, like blessings, across the fields; and Marianne, in that eager confidence of hers, declared the day would be lastingly fair, that every threatening cloud would be drawn away from their hills as if commanded.

The weather, however, was not tempting enough to entice the other two from their pencil and their book. Mrs. Dashwood had her reasons for preferring the hearth; Elinor, who observed the sky as carefully as she did a household account, shook her head at Marianne’s prophecy and stayed where she could hear the kettle and watch the light on paper.

So Marianne and Margaret set off together, and for a time were rewarded for their audacity. They gaily ascended the downs, rejoicing in their own penetration at every glimpse of blue; and when the high south-westerly wind met their faces—full of life and damp sweetness, carrying the smell of wet earth and crushed grass—they pitied, with laughing superiority, the fears that had kept their mother and Elinor indoors.

“Is there a felicity in the world,” cried Marianne, breathless with delight, “superior to this? Margaret, we will walk here at least two hours.”

Margaret, who loved motion and adventure chiefly because Marianne loved them, agreed at once. They pushed on against the wind, resisting it with laughing delight—skirts tugged and hair loosened, the sound of it in their ears like a great invisible instrument played over the hills.

For about twenty minutes they kept their triumph. Then, as if the sky had grown tired of being guessed at, the scattered clouds drew together above their heads, united into one heavy purpose, and a driving rain set full in their faces. The change was so sudden, so complete, that it felt like a rebuke. Chagrined and surprised, they were obliged—though unwillingly—to turn back; for no shelter was nearer than their own house.

One consolation remained, and in their present exigence it appeared not only proper but irresistible: they could run. The steep side of the hill led directly to their garden gate; and speed, if it could not stop the rain, might at least shorten the humiliation of enduring it.

They set off. The world became sound and sensation: rain striking their bonnets and lashes; the wind snatching at breath; the wet turf slick underfoot. Marianne, lighter and more impetuous, had at first the advantage. But a false step—no more than the smallest treachery in the grass—brought her suddenly to the ground. Margaret, unable to stop herself, was involuntarily hurried along and reached the bottom in safety, turning only when she had breath enough to cry out.

At that very moment a gentleman carrying a gun, with two pointers playing round him, was passing up the hill within a few yards of Marianne. He put down his gun at once and ran to her assistance. Marianne had raised herself from the ground, but her foot had been twisted in the fall, and she was scarcely able to stand. Rain streamed from her hair and cloak; the cold of it, and the shock, had driven the colour from her cheek—until it returned, all at once, in burning confusion, at finding herself observed.

The gentleman offered his services. Marianne’s modesty declined what her situation made necessary; she would have attempted, in spite of pain, to manage without him. But he, perceiving at once both her inability and her resistance, took her up in his arms without further delay and carried her down the hill.

It was done with a rapidity that left no room for ceremony—only for the quick, intimate awareness of another’s strength and nearness: the warmth of his coat against the chill of her wet clothes, the steady cadence of his steps, the sound of rain on his shoulders. Then, passing through the garden—the gate having been left open by Margaret in her haste—he bore her directly into the house.

Margaret had already reached the parlour, drenched and triumphant in her own rescue; and there, in the instant of astonishment before speech was possible, the gentleman entered behind her, still holding Marianne, and did not quit his burden until he had seated her carefully in a chair.

Elinor and her mother rose in amazement. The intrusion, the storm, Marianne’s disordered appearance, and the stranger’s presence—all struck at once. Yet as their eyes fixed on him, wonder mingled, in spite of themselves, with admiration. He apologized immediately, relating the cause of his entrance with a frankness and grace that made explanation itself a kind of charm. His person was uncommonly handsome, but it was the living addition of voice and expression—the ease of his manner, the warmth without presumption—that completed the impression.

Had he been old, ugly, and vulgar, Mrs. Dashwood’s gratitude would still have been secured by any act of attention to her child; she was not a woman to measure kindness by elegance. But youth and beauty and that air of breeding gave to the action an interest that came home more directly to her feelings. She thanked him again and again; and with that sweetness of address which never deserted her, invited him to be seated.

He declined, looking down at himself with a half-smile. He was, indeed, dirty and wet—the rain darkening the cloth, the mud staining the hem; the very scent of him brought the hillside into the room, a mingling of damp wool, cold air, and the sharp green of outdoors.

Mrs. Dashwood then begged to know to whom she was obliged.

His name, he replied, was Willoughby; and his present home was at Allenham, from whence he hoped she would allow him the honour of calling the next day to enquire after Miss Dashwood.

The honour was readily granted. He then departed—making himself, by leaving in the midst of a heavy rain, still more interesting. The door closed; the wind pressed at the window; the house seemed suddenly smaller for the absence of that unexpected energy.

His manly beauty and more than common gracefulness became instantly the theme of general admiration. Even the laugh which his gallantry raised against Marianne—who had, in truth, run too fast and trusted too much to luck—was warmed and enlivened by the memory of his exterior attractions. Marianne herself had seen less of his face than the rest; for the confusion which crimsoned over her cheeks when he lifted her had robbed her of the power of regarding him steadily after they entered the house. Yet she had seen enough to join in all their admiration, and with an energy which always adorned her praise.

His person and air were equal to what her fancy had ever drawn for the hero of a favourite story; and in his carrying her into the house with so little previous formality there was a rapidity of thought which particularly recommended the action to her. Every circumstance belonging to him became interesting. His name was good; his residence was in their favourite village; and she soon discovered—upon the evidence of her own heart—that of all manly dresses a shooting-jacket was the most becoming. Her imagination grew busy, her reflections pleasant; and the pain of a sprained ankle was treated as a trivial tax paid for a larger pleasure.

Sir John called on them as soon as the next interval of fair weather that morning allowed him to get out of doors. With the rain still ticking in the eaves and the air smelling of wet soil and cooling tea, Marianne’s accident was related to him; and he was eagerly asked whether he knew any gentleman of the name of Willoughby at Allenham.

“Willoughby!” cried Sir John. “What, is he in the country? That is good news however. I will ride over tomorrow, and ask him to dinner on Thursday.”

“You know him then?” said Mrs. Dashwood.

“Know him!—to be sure I do. Why, he is down here every year.”

“And what sort of a young man is he?” she asked, her tone soft but attentive; for she could never help being curious where her children’s feelings were engaged.

“As good a kind of fellow as ever lived, I assure you. A very decent shot, and there is not a bolder rider in England.”

“And is that all you can say for him?” cried Marianne, indignation flashing through her despite the bandaging of her ankle and the comfort of the chair. “But what are his manners on more intimate acquaintance? What his pursuits, his talents, and genius?”

Sir John, who liked a man for being agreeable and active and saw no need to inquire farther, looked rather puzzled.

“Upon my soul,” said he, “I do not know much about him as to all that. But he is a pleasant, good-humoured fellow, and has got the nicest little black bitch of a pointer I ever saw. Was she out with him today?”

But Marianne could no more satisfy him as to the colour of Mr. Willoughby’s pointer than he could describe to her the shades of Willoughby’s mind. What she carried away from the morning was not the dog’s coat but the cadence of a voice; not the facts of a family but the impression of a presence. And even in the room, the household’s ordinary sounds—the spoon against china, the low murmur of her mother and sister—seemed changed by the recollection of another sound layered upon them: the wind on the downs, and, against it, the clear human tone that had spoken her name with concern.

“But who is he?” said Elinor, whose curiosity moved by another road. “Where does he come from? Has he a house at Allenham?”

On this point Sir John could give more certain intelligence. Mr. Willoughby, he told them, had no property of his own in the country; he resided there only while visiting the old lady at Allenham Court, to whom he was related, and whose possessions he was to inherit; adding, with a broad significance meant to be both jest and counsel, “Yes, yes, he is very well worth catching, I can tell you, Miss Dashwood. He has a pretty little estate of his own in Somersetshire besides; and if I were you, I would not give him up to my younger sister, in spite of all this tumbling down hills. Miss Marianne must not expect to have all the men to herself. Brandon will be jealous, if she does not take care.”

“I do not believe,” said Mrs. Dashwood, with a good-humoured smile that yet held a quiet firmness, “that Mr. Willoughby will be incommoded by the attempts of either of my daughters towards what you call catching him. It is not an employment to which they have been brought up. Men are very safe with us, let them be ever so rich. I am glad to find, however, from what you say, that he is a respectable young man, and one whose acquaintance will not be ineligible.”

“He is as good a sort of fellow, I believe, as ever lived,” repeated Sir John, determined to be useful by repeating what he knew. “I remember last Christmas at a little hop at the park, he danced from eight o’clock till four, without once sitting down.”

“Did he indeed?” cried Marianne, her eyes sparkling as if the mere thought could dry the rain from her clothes and lift her from her chair. “And with elegance, with spirit?”

“Yes; and he was up again at eight to ride to covert.”

“That is what I like; that is what a young man ought to be. Whatever be his pursuits, his eagerness in them should know no moderation, and leave him no sense of fatigue.”

“Aye, aye, I see how it will be,” said Sir John, laughing with the satisfaction of a man who thinks he understands hearts because he enjoys teasing them. “I see how it will be. You will be setting your cap at him now, and never think of poor Brandon.”

“That is an expression, Sir John,” said Marianne warmly, the blood rising again—not with confusion now, but with principle—“which I particularly dislike. I abhor every common-place phrase by which wit is intended; and ‘setting one’s cap at a man,’ or ‘making a conquest,’ are the most odious of all. Their tendency is gross and illiberal; and if their construction could ever be deemed clever, time has long ago destroyed all its ingenuity.”

Sir John did not much understand the reproof—his ear was made for merriment, not for the fine distinctions of language—but he laughed as heartily as if he did, and replied, “Ay, you will make conquests enough, I dare say, one way or other. Poor Brandon!—he is quite smitten already, and he is very well worth setting your cap at, I can tell you, in spite of all this tumbling about and spraining of ankles.”

And while the parlour returned, as parlours must, to its ordinary order—the tea cooling, the rain lessening, the household voices settling into their accustomed tones—Marianne lay back and listened. The world outside was still ringing with wind and water; inside, a new name had been spoken into their domestic life. She could not have said, in any phrase Sir John would approve, what it meant to her; for what she felt had no neat translation into other people’s idioms. But it had entered her, as scent enters a room when a door is opened—uninvited, unmistakable, and impossible to forget.
