The Knights of the Temple

If you've ever wondered why the world seems to hold onto its dustiest traditions while the rest of us scrub the floors, look no further than the Inns of Court. While a modern workhouse or prison boasts plumbing and sanitation that would make a Victorian ghost weep with envy, the "respectable" colleges and legal halls remain stubbornly medieval. Men in these places pay a premium for the privilege of sleeping in closets that smell of centuries, while the poorest tailor in Spitalfields has running water at his elbow. The gentlemen of the Temple fetch their water in jugs carried by laundresses, living in stone boxes built before the concept of decency was invented. It's a funny thing, really; the ancestors of these current knights were likely the "Great Unwashed," yet somehow, they managed to survive.

Take Old Grump, for instance. A veteran of the Norfolk Circuit, he'd lived in the chambers beneath Warrington and Pendennis for thirty years, waking up daily to the roar of shower baths that occasionally leaked through the roof into his room. To Grump, these modern luxuries were nothing but absurd, dandified follies. He cursed the laundress who slopped the stairs and declared that he, and his fathers before him, had done just fine without them. One wonders, amidst all those painted escutcheons in the Upper Temple Hall, if there wasn't a single philanthropist good enough to invent a set of Hummums for the benefit of the lawyers. The Temple historian stays silent on such schemes, though one can't help but imagine how many a counsel of old might have benefited from a good pump.

Yet, there is a certain charm to these venerable Inns, with their Lamb and Flag and Winged Horse banners. There's a rough comfort and a freedom here that men always remember with a smile. Perhaps the law student indulges in poetical reminiscences as he walks past historical chambers, imagining Eldon, Coke, Chitty, and Barnewall in their prime. The man of letters loves this place even more, populated by the ghosts of Sir Roger de Coverley and Samuel Johnson rolling through the fog. It's a stage where Harry Fielding dashes off midnight articles with ink-stained ruffles, while the printer's boy sleeps in the passage.

Imagine the history of a single day in one of those dingy four-story houses where Pen and Warrington lived. On the ground floor, a parliamentary counsel drives off to Belgravia for lunch, leaving his clerk—who was once a boot-black—to take his place. The clerk, now a gentleman, entertains friends, unaware that six months ago he was starving in a garret, waiting for clients who never came. On the first floor, a venerable recluse named Doomsday spends fifty years amassing learning and a fortune, only to die alone, leaving his heirs to burn half his library. Over him lives Grump, playing whist with three seniors after a heavy dinner of Port. On the third landing, where Pen and Warrington dwell late into the night, sits Mr. Paley.

Paley is a different breed entirely. He takes the highest honors, reads cases until dawn, and works until an hour before dinner. He has brought a great intellect down to the level of a mean subject, fiercely excluding all higher thoughts, poetry, love, and beauty from his mind so he can master the enormous legend of the law. Once a competitor with Warrington, Paley is now praised for his industry while everyone whispers that Warrington is wasting his time. But who is using their time best? Paley has no time for kindness, friendship, or the sight of a beautiful sunset; he sees only law-books. When he turns off his lonely lamp, he feels thankless and remorseless, shuddering only when he meets Warrington on the stairs, avoiding him as if he were doomed.

It was likely this cadaverous ambition and self-complacent meanness in Paley's yellow face that deterred Pen from pursuing the Bench with the same ardor. Pen, however, enjoyed the Temple life with relish. His relatives thought he was studying hard, and his uncle wrote home saying the lad had sown his wild oats and was becoming steady. The truth was, the rough pleasures of a London bachelor were novel and agreeable to him. He walked in the Park instead of riding Rotten Row, content with the view. He was too young and lazy to succeed without a better name and fortune, so he let the Major believe he was reformed and buried in books.

In reality, Pen's day consisted of a morning's reading, a walk, a pull on the river, a stretch to Hampstead, and a modest tavern dinner. His nights were spent in joviality, not vice, for Pen admired women so heartily that he couldn't bear the company of anyone he didn't fancy as pure. Evenings were quiet, shared with a friend and a pipe, accompanied by a humble potation of British spirits that Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, invariably tasted for quality. This was not an unpleasant life.

During term-time, Pen showed praiseworthy regularity in eating his dinners in the Hall of the Upper Temple. It is a sight not uninteresting, a meal that feels like a throwback to the seventeenth century, save for some anachronisms. The bar has their messes, the students their tables, and the benchers sit at the high table surrounded by portraits of judges and royals. Pen, on his first introduction, found the scene amusing. The hall was a melting pot: grey-headed attorneys, dandies in rowing shirts, swarthy colonials, and Irish gentlemen making a sojourn before returning home. There were squads of law-talkers, rowing enthusiasts, and political orators. Pen gradually made acquaintances with most of them, finding points of sympathy.

The Inn provides an excellent, wholesome dinner for a moderate price: soup, meat, tarts, and port or sherry. Arranged in messes of four, each quartet shares a joint, apple-pie, and a bottle of wine. But the honest habitues, especially the lower-ranking students with a taste for good living, have many harmless arts to improve their banquet.

"Wait a bit," said Mr. Lowton, one of these Temple gourmands, tugging at Pen's gown. "The side-tables are very full, and there's only three benchers to eat ten dishes. If we wait, perhaps we shall get something from their table."

Pen looked with amusement toward the high table, where three old gentlemen stood before silver dish-covers while the clerk quavered out a grace. Lowton was a captain of the mess, aiming to be the first to the table and secure the thirteenth glass of port. His pleasures were harmless; an eager epicure whose desires did not go beyond eighteen pence.

Pen was older than many of his fellow-students, with a style that stamped him as a man of ton, unlike the pale law-talkers or ferocious dandies in astonishing waistcoats. Lowton, humble and good-natured, had been attracted by Pen's superior looks and presence.

"This is boiled-beef day, I believe, sir," Lowton said.

"Upon my word, sir, I'm not aware," Pen replied, barely containing his laughter. "I'm a stranger; this is my first term."

Lowton began pointing out the notabilities. "That's Boosey the bencher, the bald one under the picture. Next is Balls, the King's Counsel, and Swettenham. That's old Grump, the senior of the bar; they say he's dined here forty years. Do you see those four fellows opposite? Those are regular swells—Mr. Trail, the Bishop of Ealing's son; Honourable Fred Ringwood, Lord Cinqbar's brother; and Bob Suckling. They'll have good places, I bet."

"Why do you like to mess with them if they don't eat?" Pen asked, puzzled.

"How green you are," Lowton chuckled. "They don't drink any wine, don't you see? A fellow gets the bottle to himself if he messes with those three chaps. That's why Corkoran got in with 'em."

Pen delighted in Lowton's slyness. Lowton explained that these "swells" only pretend to dine, going to grand clubs or parties instead. Their names appear in the *Morning Post*, but they won't be in the Hall for two hours. "They don't drink wine," Lowton insisted. "That's why you get the bottle."

"There's a lot of Irish here," Lowton continued, cataloging the room. "Corkoran, Molloy Maloney of Ballymaloney, nephew to Major-General Sir Hector O'Dowd... He's always bragging about his uncle. And that one with the long black hair is a tremendous rebel. By Jove, sir, to hear him at the Forum it makes your blood freeze."

"It's your turn to fill your glass," Lowton said, offering Pen a toast. "What? You won't have any port? Don't like port with your dinner? Here's your health."

Pen found himself attached to Lowton precisely because he disliked port.

It was during one of these dinners that a gentleman in a barrister's gown joined them, unable to find a seat among his peers. Dressed in old, faded clothes and a ragged shirt, he strode over the table and took the bench where Pen sat. He smiled when he saw Pen's name on the slip.

"We ought to know each other," he said. "We're both Boniface men; my name's Warrington."

"Are you St—— Warrington?" Pen exclaimed, delighted.

"Warrington—yes," he laughed. "I recollect you in your freshman's term. But you appear to have quite cut me out."

"The college talks about you still," Pen said, admiring the talent. "The bargeman you thrashed, Bill Simes, wants you up again at Oxbridge. The Miss Notleys..."

"Hush!" Warrington interrupted. "Glad to make your acquaintance, Pendennis. Heard a good deal about you."

The young men became friends immediately, deep in college talk. Seeing Warrington take his share of the port with gusto, Pen didn't scruple to help himself, much to Lowton's disappointment. After dinner, Warrington asked where Pen was going.

"I thought of going home to dress and hear Grisi in *Norma*," Pen said.

"Are you going to meet anybody?"

"No—only to hear the music," Pen admitted.

"You had much better come home and smoke a pipe with me," Warrington suggested. "A very short one. I live close by in Lamb Court, and we'll talk over Boniface and old times."

They left; Lowton sighed after them, knowing Warrington was a baronet's son and looking up to the aristocracy with simple reverence. From that night, Pen and Warrington were sworn friends. Warrington's cheerfulness, good sense, rough welcome, and never-failing pipe charmed Pen, who found it more pleasant to dive into shilling taverns with him than to dine in solitary state among the silent frequenters of the Polyanthus.

Soon, Pen gave up his lodgings in St. James's, finding it more economical to move into Warrington's vacant room in Lamb Court. No man was more easily led than Pen to do a thing when it was a novelty or to his liking. Pidgeon, the youth, and Flanagan, the laundress, now divided their allegiance between the two friends.
