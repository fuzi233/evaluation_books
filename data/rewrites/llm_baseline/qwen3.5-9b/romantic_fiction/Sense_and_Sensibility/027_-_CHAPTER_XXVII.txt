"If this fine weather continues much longer," remarked Mrs. Jennings as they gathered for breakfast the following morning, "Sir John won't be pleased with the prospect of leaving Barton next week. It's truly pitiful for men of sport to lose a single day of pleasure. Poor fellows! I always feel sorry for them when they do; they seem to carry it so deeply in their hearts."

"That is quite true," exclaimed Marianne, her voice bright and cheerful. As she spoke, she moved toward the window to inspect the day outside. "I hadn't considered *that* aspect. This weather will surely keep many sportsmen right here in the countryside." It was a fortunate realization; her spirits, previously dampened, were instantly restored.

"It is simply ideal for them," she continued, settling into her chair at the breakfast table with a radiant expression. "One can only imagine how delightful it must be for them! However" (a flicker of anxiety returning to her tone), "one shouldn't expect it to endure forever. Given the season and the prolonged rainfall we've endured, we shall undoubtedly see very little more of it. Frosts will inevitably arrive soon, likely with considerable severity. Perhaps within a day or two; this extreme mildness can hardly persist—nay, perhaps tonight itself will bring freezing temperatures!"

"At any rate," interjected Elinor, eager to divert her mother's gaze from her sister's evident distress, "I daresay Sir John and Lady Middleton will be back in town by the end of next week."

"Ay, my dear, I'll wager you're right," Mrs. Jennings agreed. "Mary always gets her own way."

"And now," Elinor observed silently to herself, "she will dispatch a letter to Combe via today's post." Yet, if she did, the letter was composed and dispatched with such discretion that it evaded all of Elinor's attempts to verify the fact. Regardless of the truth, and despite Elinor's lack of total contentment regarding the matter, she felt no great discomfort so long as she witnessed Marianne's buoyant mood. Marianne was indeed spirited, delighted by the gentle warmth of the air and even more thrilled by the anticipation of an impending frost.

The morning was largely devoted to calling on acquaintances of Mrs. Jennings to announce their presence in London. Throughout these visits, Marianne remained engrossed in monitoring the wind's direction, scrutinizing shifts in the sky, and envisioning atmospheric changes.

"Do you not find it cooler than this morning, Elinor?" she asked. "There appears to be a distinct difference. I struggle to keep my hands warm even inside my muff. It wasn't so yesterday, I believe. The clouds seem to be breaking apart; the sun will emerge momentarily, and we shall have a clear afternoon."

Elinor felt alternating waves of amusement and pain, yet Marianne persisted. Whether gazing into the bright glow of the fireplace at night or observing the morning atmosphere, she interpreted every sign as a sure indication of coming frost.

The Miss Dashwoods possessed no greater cause for dissatisfaction with Mrs. Jennings' lifestyle or circle of friends than with her treatment of them, which was consistently kind. Everything in her household management followed the most generous principles. Aside from a handful of elderly city friends whom, to Lady Middleton's chagrin, she had never managed to drop, she visited no one whose acquaintance might disturb the sensibilities of her younger companions. Delighted to find herself more comfortably situated in this regard than anticipated, Elinor was quite willing to trade off the lack of genuine enjoyment found in their evening gatherings. These parties, held at home or elsewhere, consisted primarily of card games and offered little amusement for her.

Colonel Brandon, holding a general invitation to the house, visited almost daily. He came ostensibly to admire Marianne and converse with Elinor. While Elinor often derived more satisfaction from talking with him than from any other daily occurrence, she simultaneously harbored deep concern regarding his enduring attachment to her sister. She feared it was a growing sentiment. It grieved her to witness the earnestness with which he often watched Marianne, and his spirits were noticeably lower than they had been at Barton.

About a week after their arrival in London, it became confirmed that Willoughby had also arrived. His visiting card lay on the table when they returned from their morning drive.

"Good heavens!" cried Marianne. "He has been here while we were out!"

Elinor, relieved to confirm his presence in London, ventured to suggest, "Be assured, he will call again tomorrow." But Marianne seemed barely to register her words. Upon Mrs. Jennings' entry, Marianne snatched up the precious card and fled the room.

This event, while lifting Elinor's spirits, ignited in her sister a renewed and intensified agitation. From that moment onward, her mind found no peace; the hourly expectation of seeing him rendered her incapable of focusing on anything else. She insisted on staying behind the next morning while the others went out driving. Elinor's thoughts filled with speculation about what might be transpiring in Berkeley Street during their absence, but a fleeting glance at her sister upon their return sufficed to reveal that Willoughby had paid no second visit.

Just then, a note was delivered and placed on the table.

"For me!" cried Marianne, hastening forward.

"No, madam, for my mistress," the servant replied.

But unconvinced, Marianne seized the note immediately. "It is indeed for Mrs. Jennings! How vexatious!"

"You are expecting a letter, then?" Elinor asked, unable to remain silent any longer.

"Yes, a little—not much." After a brief pause, she added, "You have no confidence in me, Marianne."

"Nay, Elinor, this reproach from *you*—from one who claims to trust no one!"

"Me!" Elinor responded, somewhat flustered. "Indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to disclose."

"Nor I," Marianne answered with vigor. "Then our situations are identical. Neither of us has anything to tell; you, because you choose not to communicate, and I, because I conceal nothing."

Distressed by this accusation of reserve against herself—a trait she felt powerless to deny under the circumstances—Elinor did not know how to urge Marianne toward greater openness.

Mrs. Jennings soon appeared, and upon receiving the note, read it aloud. It was from Lady Middleton, informing them of her arrival in Conduit Street the previous night and inviting her mother and cousins to join her the following evening. Business obligations on Sir John's part and a severe cold afflicting Lady Middleton had prevented them from calling at Berkeley Street. The invitation was accepted.

However, as the appointed hour approached, Elinor encountered significant difficulty persuading her sister to accompany her. Though civil attendance was necessary, Marianne, having seen nothing of Willoughby, felt neither inclined for social diversion nor willing to risk his appearing again in her absence.

When the evening concluded, Elinor realized that changing one's residence does not necessarily alter one's nature. Although scarcely established in London, Sir John had managed to gather nearly twenty young people around him and entertain them with a ball. This was an affair, however, that Lady Middleton disapproved of. In the country, an unplanned dance was perfectly acceptable; but in London, where the reputation for elegance was paramount and difficult to maintain, it was a dangerous gamble to allow it to become known that Lady Middleton had hosted a modest dance of eight or nine couples, accompanied by only two violins and a scant refreshment spread.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer attended. Having not seen them since their arrival in town, and mindful of avoiding any appearance of undue attention to his mother-in-law, Mr. Palmer carefully kept his distance. Upon their entrance, he cast a slight, seemingly unaware glance at them, offering only a nod to Mrs. Jennings from across the room.

Marianne glanced around the room as she entered; it was sufficient—*he* was not there. Consequently, she sat down, equally disinclined to receive or share any pleasure.

After an hour of assembly, Mr. Palmer strolled over to the Miss Dashwoods to express his surprise at seeing them in town, noting that Colonel Brandon had been the first informed of their arrival and had made a witty remark upon hearing the news.

"I thought you were both still in Devonshire," he said.

"Did you?" replied Elinor.

"When do you intend to return?"

"I do not know." Thus ended their exchange.

Never in her life had Marianne been so reluctant to dance, nor so exhausted by the exertion. She complained bitterly as they returned to Berkeley Street.

"Aye, aye," said Mrs. Jennings. "We understand the reason for all that perfectly well. If a certain individual, whom I shall not name, had been present, you would not have felt a fraction of that fatigue. To tell the truth, it was not very becoming of him to fail to meet you when he was invited."

"Invited!" cried Marianne.

"So my daughter Middleton informed me, for it seems Sir John encountered him somewhere in the streets this morning."

Marianne said nothing further but looked profoundly hurt.

Impatient to do something that might alleviate her sister's suffering, Elinor resolved to write the following morning to her mother. Hoping to awaken maternal fears for Marianne's health and thereby secure the long-delayed inquiries, she was driven even more urgently to this course by observing, after breakfast the next morning, that Marianne was once again writing to Willoughby. She could not possibly suppose it was addressed to anyone else.
