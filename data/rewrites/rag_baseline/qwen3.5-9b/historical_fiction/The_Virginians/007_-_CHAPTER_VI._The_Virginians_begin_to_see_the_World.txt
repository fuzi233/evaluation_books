The silence in the house was heavier than the snow outside. George Esmond carried the weight of a quarrel he refused to speak of, a tension that settled in his chest until it manifested as a fever. For nights, the boy tossed on his bed, his mind fracturing under the strain, shrieking words that echoed in the dark: "Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!" His mother watched, helpless, as the illness defied her remedies, draining her strength and her patience.

Desperation drove her to the door of Mr. Dempster. She rode through the falling snow on a pony, seeking aid for her son. The old physician accepted her plea, burying his own resentment beneath the necessity of healing. With a lancet and a small bag of medicines, he retreated to his hut, leaving the sick boy behind. Under his care, the fever receded, the attacks softened, and George was restored to a fragile health. Doctors suggested a voyage to England, but the widow remembered her estranged relations there. Instead, they turned north and east.

George, Harry, and Mr. Dempster boarded a ship bound for New York, traveling up the Hudson to Albany, then moving into the French provinces. It was a circuitous education. Harry camped with the Indians, trading for furs and shooting bears. George, however, found no joy in the hunt. His delicacy made him a favorite among the French ladies, who admired his fluency in their tongue. He perfected his accent until he could pass for one of them, dancing the minuet with elegance and playing the violin with a technical precision that silenced the room. He learned the latest songs, though his voice had broken, shifting from treble to bass. In his absence, Harry earned an affair of honor with a young ensign, a duel that ended in a scarred shoulder and a sworn friendship. Madame de Mouchy wrote to the widow, praising George's behavior. The praise felt hollow to Mr. Whitfield, who would have seen the vanity in such a display.

Ten months later, the boys returned. Time had blurred their distinctions; George had grown tall enough to stand beside his younger brother. Their hair, usually powdered, was now natural—George's raven black, Harry's light locks tied with ribbons. Yet, the internal landscape remained unchanged. George returned with no goodwill toward the honorable gentleman who would become a legend, nor did his view of his mother's friend soften.

Washington looked older than his years, carrying a gravity that belied his age. He managed the family affairs with a simplicity and trustworthiness that commanded respect from peers twice his senior. But Mrs. Mountain, the widow's companion, saw only patterns of obsession. She was a matchmaker by nature, convinced everyone sought to marry everyone else. She believed Mr. Ward coveted the widow, that Washington desired a wealthy bride despite his youth compared to her. She calculated the odds: major generals marrying young mistresses was a tradition of the family. Her logic was circular and absolute.

She poured these theories onto George, begging him to listen. He pleaded for silence, invoking heaven itself. She promised to close her lips but claimed she could not close her eyes. She listed circumstantial evidence, the invisible threads connecting Washington and the widow. "Poor Harry," she sighed, "he cannot keep a secret. If I speak to him, he will tell your mother, and there will be a quarrel."

"Keep this quiet, Mountain," George said, his voice tight with dignity. "Neither to me nor anyone else must you mention such an absurd suspicion."

Why absurd? Because Washington was everywhere. His name hung in the air whenever the widow spoke. She consulted him on every decision regarding the estate, from buying horses to selling barrels of tobacco. There was a room at Castlewood designated solely for him, filled with his clothes and portmanteau even when he was gone. "One day he won't go away," Mountain groaned, returning to the forbidden topic.

George responded with a frigid courtesy that chafed the honest gentleman, or a sharp sarcasm that cut through the air like a rifle shot. While Harry rode with sporting friends, Washington visited Castlewood. He treated the mistress with a tenderness that nearly sparked a rupture in George's jealousy. But this visit was a farewell.

Washington was departing for the western frontier, a dangerous journey into the unknown. The French had begun to press inward, their aggression alarming the governments of Virginia and Pennsylvania. The British held a theoretical claim to the land stretching to the Pacific, a charter that defined boundaries north and south but ignored the vast expanse to the west. The French, conversely, sought to connect their northern and southern colonies via the Mississippi and the great lakes, ignoring the indigenous owners of the soil entirely. The logicians of St. James and Versailles viewed the conflict as a European dispute, using the tomahawk merely as a tool for their litigation.

The Ohio Company, granted lands by Virginia, found their settlements invaded by French detachments. They appealed to Governor Dinwiddie, who decided to send an ambassador to demand the cessation of hostilities. Young Washington seized the opportunity for distinction. In the autumn of 1753, accompanied by a guide and interpreters, he followed Indian tracks from Williamsburg to Lake Erie, finding the French commander at Fort Le Boeuf. The reply was brief and final: hold the place, drive out the English. The French intended to possess the Ohio.

Washington returned through forests and frozen rivers, navigating by compass and camping by firelight. Harry, absent on a cockfight, cursed his bad luck, missing the noble sport of diplomacy. Upon his return, Washington was hailed as a hero. The widow pointed to him as a model, contrasting his wilderness vigilance with Harry's trivial amusements. "Learning is good," she told George, "but I wish my eldest son were serving his country."

"I desire no better," George replied, eager to join the ranks. "Will you not have me serve under Mr. Washington?"

"An Esmond serves only with the King's commission," the widow stated firmly. "As for asking favors from Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie, I would rather beg my bread."

Washington was raising a regiment, a small force of provincials attempting to halt the French advance. Another officer, Colonel Trent, had been sent ahead to fortify positions near what would become Pittsburgh. The French, vastly outnumbering the English, attacked the outpost. A Virginian officer with forty men faced twenty times that number of Canadians. He withdrew without resistance; the French fortified his position, renaming it Fort Duquesne.

No shots had been fired yet. The armies stood in presence, guns loaded, but the word "fire" had not been spoken. It was strange that in a savage forest, a young Virginian officer should eventually strike the first blow, igniting a war that would last sixty years, reshaping continents, and costing empires. It would create a great Western republic and tear apart the Old World. Of all the myriads involved, the greatest fame would belong to the man who pulled the trigger.

He knew none of this. A simple gentleman, anxious to do his duty, he volunteered for the first service. In the following year, he took command of the provincial troops marching to repel the French. He encountered their vanguard and fired, killing their leader. But the momentum was irreversible. Compelled to retreat before superior forces, he was forced to capitulate. On July 4, 1754, Colonel Washington marched out from the hastily built Fort Necessity, surrendered the place to the conqueror, and walked home, the first shot of a global conflict already fired.
