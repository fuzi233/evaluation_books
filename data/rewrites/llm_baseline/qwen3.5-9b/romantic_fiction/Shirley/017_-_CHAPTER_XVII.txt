There was no war in sight, nor any enemy to hunt, yet the band led by priests and directed by women played marches that sounded strangely military. Some in the crowd, particularly Miss Keeldar, heard in those notes not a call to arms, but a stirring of the heroic spirit. Old Helstone glanced at her by accident; he laughed, and she laughed back.

"There is no battle ahead," he remarked. "Our nation does not require us to fight for it. No tyrant threatens our freedom. We are merely taking a stroll. Keep your hand on the reins, Captain, and dampen that fiery spirit. It serves no purpose."

"Follow your own counsel, Doctor," Shirley replied. Turning to Caroline, she murmured, "I shall borrow from imagination what reality refuses to give. We are not soldiers—bloodshed holds no allure for me—or if we are, we are soldiers of the Cross. Time seems to have rolled back centuries, binding us on a pilgrimage to Palestine. Yet, that is too fanciful. I crave a sterner dream. Imagine ourselves as Lowland Scots, following a Covenanting captain into the hills to hold a gathering safe from persecuting troops. We know battle may follow prayer; convinced that even in defeat Heaven claims the victory, we are ready to stain the peat-moss with our blood. That music awakes my soul; it quickens my entire being, beating my heart not with its usual rhythm, but with a thrilling, new vigor. I almost long for danger—for a faith, a land, or simply a lover worth defending."

"Look, Shirley!" Caroline interrupted. "What is that red speck above Stilbro' Brow? Your sight is sharper than mine. Turn your eagle eye toward it."

Miss Keeldar peered. "I see," she said, adding shortly afterward, "it is a line of red. Soldiers—cavalry, specifically. They ride swiftly. There are six of them. They will pass us... no, they have veered right. They spotted our procession and are circling to avoid us. Whence do they go?"

"Perhaps they are merely exercising their horses."

"Perhaps so. We lose sight of them now."

Mr. Helstone then spoke up. "We shall proceed through Royd Lane to reach Nunnely Common via a shortcut," he announced.

Accordingly, the procession filed into the straits of Royd Lane. It was exceedingly narrow, barely wide enough for two to walk abreast without slipping into the ditches lining either side. Halfway through, agitation rippled among the clerical leaders. Boultby's spectacles trembled, Helstone's hat was askew, the curates nudged one another nervously, and Mr. Hall turned to the ladies with a smile.

"What is the matter?" someone demanded.

He pointed his staff toward the end of the lane. There, emerging from the opposite direction, was another procession, equally headed by men in black and accompanied by music.

"Is it our double?" Shirley asked, her voice tinged with irony. "Our manifold wraith? Here comes a card turned up."

"If you desired a battle, you are likely to get one—at least of glances," Caroline whispered, laughing.

"They shall not pass us!" the curates shouted in unison. "We yield not!"

"Yield?" Helstone retorted sternly, spinning around. "Who speaks of yielding? Boys, consider your actions. Ladies, I know you will stand firm. Not a churchwoman here lacks the resolve to hold her ground against these folk for the honor of the Establishment. What says Miss Keeldar?"

"She asks what this is."

"It is a unholy alliance of Dissenting and Methodist schools—the Baptists, Independents, and Wesleyans—who have deliberately entered this lane intending to block our march and drive us back."

"Rude behavior!" Shirley declared. "And I detest rudeness. They certainly deserve a lesson."

"A lesson in civility," Mr. Hall suggested gently, ever the peacemaker. "Not an example of incivility."

Old Helstone stepped forward, quickening his pace until he marched several yards ahead of his company. He had nearly reached the opposing black-clad leaders when the hostile commander-in-chief—a large, greasy man with black hair combed flat against his forehead—ordered a halt. The procession stopped. He produced a hymn-book, recited a verse, set a tune, and they launched into the most mournful of chants.

Helstone signaled his band. They brayed out with all the force of brass. He ordered "Rule, Britannia!" and commanded the children to sing along, which they did with enthusiastic fervor. The enemy was sung down, their psalm drowned out. In terms of noise, they were conquered.

"Now, follow me!" Helstone exclaimed. "Not at a run, but at a firm, smart pace. Steady, every child and woman. Stay together. If necessary, hold onto each other's skirts."

He strode onward with such determination and deliberation, perfectly supported by his scholars and teachers who obeyed instantly—neither running nor faltering, but marching with cool, solid momentum—that even the curates, caught between the vigilant gazes of Helstone and Miss Keeldar, were forced to comply. Both watched for deviations with lynx-like intensity, ready to rebuke the slightest breach of order with a cane or a parasol. Amazed, then alarmed, the body of Dissenters was pressed back, eventually forced to turn tail and clear the exit of Royd Lane. Boultby suffered greatly in the onslaught, but Helstone and Malone managed to keep him upright, guiding him through safely, though winded and sorely tried.

The fat Dissenter who had led the hymn remained sitting in the ditch. A spirit merchant by trade and a leader of the Nonconformists, it was rumored he had drunk more water that single afternoon than he had consumed in the preceding year. Mr. Hall ensured Caroline's safety, and Caroline attended to him. Later, he and Miss Ainley exchanged quiet comments on the incident. Once the entire party had cleared the lane, Miss Keeldar and Mr. Helstone shook hands warmly. The curates began to exult, but Helstone soon checked their innocent spirits, reminding them they lacked the sense to know what to say and advising silence, noting that the affair was not their responsibility.

Around half-past three, the procession turned back, regaining the starting point by four o'clock. Long rows of benches were arranged in the closely shorn fields surrounding the school. Children were seated, and huge baskets covered with white cloths, alongside great smoking tin vessels, were brought out. Before the distribution of treats began, a brief grace was pronounced by Mr. Hall and sung by the children. Their youthful voices sounded melodious and touching in the open air. Large currant buns and hot, well-sweetened tea were then served with generous liberality. On this day, there was no stinting; the rule was that each child receive roughly twice as much as they could possibly eat, leaving a surplus for those unable to attend due to age, sickness, or other impediments. Meanwhile, buns and beer circulated among the musicians and choir members. Afterward, the benches were removed, allowing everyone to relax with licensed games.

A bell summoned the teachers, patrons, and patronesses to the schoolroom. Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone, and many other ladies were already there, arranging their separate trays and tables. Most of the neighborhood's female servants, along with the wives of clerks, singers, and musicians, had been enlisted as waitresses. Each competed in the smartness and daintiness of her attire, and many handsome figures were visible among the younger women. About half a dozen sliced bread and butter, while another half-dozen supplied hot water drawn from the rector's kitchen coppers. The profusion of flowers and evergreens adorning the white walls, the display of silver teapots and bright porcelain on the tables, and the active, blithe figures in gay dresses created a refreshing and lively spectacle. Everyone talked, not loudly, but cheerfully, while canaries sang shrilly in their high-hung cages.

As the rector's niece, Caroline took her place at one of the three principal tables; Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall officiated at the others. These tables were reserved for the *élite*, as strict rules of equality were not fashionable at Briarfield. Miss Helstone removed her bonnet and scarf to escape the oppressive heat. Her long curls fell over her neck, serving almost as a veil, and her modest muslin dress, fashioned like a nun's robe, allowed her to dispense with a shawl.

The room filled. Mr. Hall stationed himself beside Caroline, who, as she rearranged cups and spoons, whispered remarks on the day's events. He looked somewhat grave regarding the confrontation in Royd Lane, and she tried to smile him out of his seriousness. Miss Keeldar sat nearby—unusually silent and still, gazing vigilantly around. She seemed fearful that an intruder might claim the seat she clearly intended to reserve next to her. Periodically, she spread her satin dress over an excessive portion of the bench or placed her gloves and embroidered handkerchief upon it. Caroline finally noticed this maneuver and asked who she expected.
