We must now, before continuing with Miss Lydia’s story, clarify that passage in Mr. Warrington’s letter to his brother concerning Lady Maria Esmond—a sentence that may still puzzle some gentle readers. How, indeed, could anyone of decent sensibility imagine such a turn of events? How might an innocent young person suppose that a woman of noble lineage, ancient descent, and mature years—whom we ourselves saw deeply in love only a few months past—could so abandon herself as (oh, my fingers tremble at the thought!) not only to fall for a man of humble birth and many years her junior, but actually to marry him, openly, or rather, secretly, beneath the very eyes of society? For it was not exactly public; no grand ceremony marked the union. No, Parson Sampson quietly tied the knot in his chapel in Mayfair, away from prying gazes.

But pause before you condemn her entirely. Because Lady Maria once entertained a foolish fondness for her cousin, does that mean she should never again feel love? Are men alone permitted to change their hearts, while women are condemned for seeking comfort in new affections? What greater rudeness, what more unfeeling judgment, than Hamlet’s tirade against his mother’s remarriage? The truth is likely simpler: that tender, clinging creature needed something to hold onto—and when Hamlet Senior was gone, she wrapped herself around Claudius. Indeed, we have known women so eager to attach themselves that they’ve managed to entwine round two gentlemen at once. Why, then, should there not be marriage tables after funeral feasts? If you gave thanks yesterday for your meal, does that mean you cannot hunger today? Your appetite remains keen, your relish strong—surely tomorrow evening at eight, you’ll need dinner again. I, for one, when Flirtilla or Jiltissa took a fancy to me (let us imagine them both of dazzling beauty and high rank), always reminded myself that time would come when their hearts would turn elsewhere. We are served à la Russe, devouring one dish at a time, like those poor souls in Polyphemus’s cave. *Hodie mihi, cras tibi*—there are anthropophagi who consume dozens of us: the old, the young, the delicate, the tough, the plump, the lean, the plain, the fair. There is no escape. One by one, we vanish down their vast maws.

Look at Lady Ogresham! Last year we all watched how she seized upon poor Tom Kydd—devoured him, picked his bones clean, and cast him aside. Now it is Ned Suckling who lies within her grasp. He quivers beneath her great eyes, helpless, entranced. See the small, trembling creature panting under her gaze! She draws nearer, he moves closer. Soon there will be a faint cry for mercy—and *hobblegobble*—he will disappear! Ah, how sad it is. I knew, for instance, that Maria Esmond had lost her heart many times before Harry Warrington found it; yet I liked to believe he would keep it—though mischance and troubled times might have kept her love safe, cherished in quiet celibacy. If I were to fall madly in love tomorrow, I would still pretend I’d secured the full ownership of my beloved’s heart—not merely leased a battered furnished apartment, where the wine glasses are clouded by countless lips, and the sofas stained by the boots of previous tenants. Dear, dear nymph! To be loved and beautiful! Suppose I once felt a fleeting passion for Glycera (and truly, her complexion was as pure as polished Parian marble); suppose you once admired Telephus, with his low collars and absurd neck—those infatuations are long gone, aren’t they? We love each other now, don’t we? Yes, forever. Let Glycera go to Bath, and let Telephus take his *cervicem roseam* to Jack Ketch, *n’est-ce pas*?

No, we never think of changing, my dear. However the winds blow, however time flies, however spoons stir our potage—now piping hot—it shall never grow cold. We may have allowed ourselves passing fancies in the past; and truly, your attachment to Telephus (do not frown so, my darling, and deepen those lovely lines on your brow)—I say, it was the talk of the town. And Glycera? She behaved abominably toward me. Well, well—now that we understand each other, our hearts are united forever, and we can look at Sir Cresswell Cresswell and snap our fingers at his wig. But this Maria of the last century was a woman of disordered mind. You, my love, who know the world, understand that in her life much must have transpired unfit for light, unsuitable for telling. You know (not that I mean you’ve experienced such things, but you’ve heard—heard your mother speak) that an old flirt, having played the fool with one passion, will soon play it again with another. Flirting, like drinking—once the brandy is gone, Glycera, who has taken to drinking, will turn to gin. So if Maria Esmond has found a successor to Harry Warrington, and set up a new sultan in the empire of her heart, what, after all, could we expect from her? That realm was like the Low Countries—long accustomed to conquest, perpetually open to invasion.

And her present captor was none other than Mr. Geoghegan—or Hagan—the young actor who had performed in George’s tragedy. His voice thrilled, his eyes sparkled, his bearing was noble; clad in gilt leather armor and a large buckled wig, delivering the poet’s fiery lines, he stirred the lady’s heart as Ariadne’s was stirred by Bacchus when her affair with Theseus ended. The young Irishman was touched and uplifted by the noblewoman’s affection. He might have preferred a Lady Maria Hagan younger in years, but one more tender-hearted he could scarcely find. She clung to him closely. When their marriage became known and her furious relatives disowned her, she withdrew with him to his modest lodgings in Westminster.

General Lambert brought the news home from Whitehall one day, sharing it with laughter and jests. In those simpler times, humor was broad, and even fine ladies laughed at Fielding’s bawdy pages and wept over Clarissa’s letters—letters that would make your modern ladyship recoil in horror. The General made all manner of witticisms about the marriage—about George’s role in bringing it about, about Barry’s jealousy when he learned of it. He swore it was cruel that cousin Hagan hadn’t chosen George as groomsman; that the first child should be named Carpezan or Sybilla after the play. They wouldn’t quite afford a coach, but perhaps they could borrow a chariot and pasteboard dragons from Mr. Rich’s theatre. The baby might be christened in Macbeth’s caldron, and Harry and Harlequin ought certainly to be godfathers.

“Why shouldn’t she marry him if she likes him?” asked little Hetty. “Why should he not love her because she is a little old? Mamma is a little old, and you love her none the worse. When you married my mamma, sir, I’ve heard you say you were very poor; and yet you were very happy, and nobody laughed at you!”

Thus spoke the impudent child, unaware of Lady Maria’s past indiscretions.

“So her family has deserted her?” George recounted the fury they had shown—how Lady Castlewood had gone into mourning, how Mr. Will had sworn he’d have the rascal’s ears, how furious Madame de Bernstein was, above all. “It is an insult to the family,” said haughty little Miss Hett, “and I can imagine how noble ladies must be outraged by their relative marrying someone of Mr. Hagan’s condition. But to abandon her is a different matter.”

“Indeed, my dear,” replied mamma, “you speak of things you do not understand. After Lady Maria’s conduct, no respectable person can visit her.”

“What conduct, mamma?”

“Never mind,” said mamma. “Little girls should not inquire too closely into such matters. Suffice it to say, I am shocked she ever came here, and I repeat: no honest person should associate with her!”

“Then, Aunt Lambert, I must be whipped and sent to bed,” said George, with mock solemnity. “I confess (though I did not sooner, since it was not my affair) that I have visited my cousin the player, and his lady. I found them in very shabby lodgings in Westminster, where the wretch keeps not only his wife, but his old mother and a younger brother whom he sends to school. I met Mr. Hagan, and left with a liking, even a respect for him—though I admit he has made a most imprudent marriage. But how imprudent some people are about marriage, aren’t they, Theo?”

“Improvident, if they marry spendthrifts like you,” said the General. “Master George found his relations, and I’ll wager he left his purse behind.”

“No, not the purse, sir,” said George, smiling gently. “Theo made that. But I must admit it came empty. Mr. Rich is furious. He says he hardly dares put Hagan on stage, fearing a riot—like the one Garrick faced over foreign dancers. This will be a gentleman’s riot. The macaronis are furious, swearing they’ll pelt Hagan and cudgel him afterward. My cousin Will at Arthur’s has vowed he’ll have the actor’s ears. Meanwhile, since the poor man doesn’t perform, they’ve cut his salary. Without it, these unfortunate lovers have no means to buy bread and cheese.”

“And you brought them food, sir? It was like you, George!” said Theo, adoring him.

“It was your purse that carried it, dear Theo,” replied George.

“Mamma, I hope you’ll go see them tomorrow!” pleaded Theo.

“If she doesn’t, I’ll get a divorce, my dear!” joked papa. “Come and kiss me, you little wench—that is, *avec la bonne permission de monsieur mon beau-fils*.”

“Monsieur mon beau fiddlestick, papa!” said Miss Lambert, and I suspect she obeyed without hesitation. And thus, for the first time, George Esmond Warrington, Esquire, was called a fiddlestick.

Any man—even today—knows the price of an imprudent marriage: the scorn, the wrath, the pity of his family. If your respectable kin cry out when you marry the curate’s daughter—one in ten of whose children are charming—or engage yourself to a young barrister whose only income comes from court reports and who must pay his Oxford bills from your slender fortune—if your friends protest such matches, imagine the outcry when Lady Maria Hagan’s family learned of this union.

First, there is old Mrs. Hagan. Her son had supported her diligently and comfortably since leaving Trinity College after his father’s death, appearing as Romeo at Crow Street Theatre. His salary had lately sustained his brother at school, helped his sister working as a companion, and provided fire, clothing, tea, dinner, and warmth for the widow of a clergyman. And now, a fine lady with extravagant habits arrives, claiming the humble home, dividing the scant loaf and mutton! Had Hagan not been proud, and the old mother afraid of him, I doubt Lady Maria’s life in those Westminster lodgings would have been peaceful. It was selfish, perhaps, to take a seat at that small table, to claim the cream of the milk, the wings of the chickens, the best half of everything when there was barely enough. No wonder the poor old mother-in-law grumbled. But what was her complaint compared to the uproar in Kensington among Lady Maria’s noble kin? Think of the gossip across the city! The whispers at the Princess’s court, where Lady Fanny held a position; the jokes of Mr. Will’s fellow officers at the usher’s table; the witticisms in newspapers and magazines; the outrage of moralists; the laughter of clubs and the sneers of the irreverent!

At the news, Madame Bernstein fell into fits and fled to the solitude of her beloved rocks at Tunbridge Wells, where she saw no more than forty people a night at cards. My lord refused to see his sister; and the Countess, in mourning as we’ve said, paid a visit to a gracious Princess, who offered her condolences upon the disgrace and calamity that had struck her house. For three whole days, the town buzzed with scandal—then came other news: a victory in Germany, uncertain reports from America, a general returning to face trial, a new soprano from Italy—and the public forgot Lady Maria in her garret, eating the hard-earned meal of the actor’s family.

This is an extract from Mr. George Warrington’s letter to his brother, describing personal matters and his visit to the newlyweds:

“My dearest little Theo,” he writes, “was eager to accompany her mother on this charitable errand; but I thought Aunt Lambert’s visit best suited the circumstances, without the presence of her little spinster aide-de-camp. Cousin Hagan was out when we arrived; we found her ladyship in loose dress, hair loosely pinned, playing cribbage with a neighbor from the second floor, while good Mrs. Hagan sat opposite the fire with a glass of punch and *The Whole Duty of Man*.

“Maria—your Maria once—cried a little when she saw us; and Aunt Lambert, of course, offered her sympathy. While she comforted Lady Maria, I paid the best compliments I could to the elderly lady. When conversation between Aunt L. and the bride flagged, I turned to the latter, and together we did our best to make the dreary visit pleasant. We spoke of you, of Wolfe, of war—you must be facing the French by now, and God send my dearest brother safe and victorious from battle! We follow your steps anxiously—we imagine you at Cape Breton. We have plans of Quebec and charts of the St. Lawrence. Shall I ever forget your joy when you saw me return safely from that little skirmish with the little Frenchman? So will my Harry return from his battle. I feel certain of it, elated by the prospect of your success and safety. And I’ve shared this cheerfulness with everyone here. We speak of the campaign as over, Captain Warrington’s promotion assured. Pray Heaven, all our hopes may be fulfilled soon.

“How strange it is that you, the bold one (you know you are), have escaped quarrels thus far, while I, a peaceful youth wishing no harm to anyone, am thrust into battles! What do you think of my having another affair on my hands—this time with whom, pray? With none other than your old enemy, our kinsman, Mr. Will.

“I cannot guess what provoked him. Spencer (who acted as my second—don’t be alarmed, it’s all over, and no one is hurt) thinks someone incited Will, but who? His behavior has been outrageous. We’ve met often lately at good Mr. Van den Bosch’s house, where his pretty granddaughter was entrusted to both of us by our dear mother. Oh, dear mother! Did you know the little thing would become a *causa belli*, sparking duels and endangering lives? Yet so it has. To show his spirit, or doubt mine, whenever Will and I meet at Mynheer’s—he’s always there—he treats me with open rudeness, requiring extraordinary patience to endure. He contradicted me once, twice, thrice in front of the family, plainly out of spite. Is he courting Miss Lydia and her father’s ships, slaves, and forty thousand pounds? I suspect so. The old gentleman talks endlessly of money, adores his granddaughter, and since she’s beautiful, many here adore her too. Was Will vile enough to think I’d trade Theo for a million guineas, slaves, and Venus herself? Could such baseness enter his mind? I don’t know if he accused me of stealing Van den Bosch’s silver or robbing on the highway. But for one reason or another, he’s jealous of me. Though I’ve answered his insults with dry sarcasm (always civil in company), I may have angered him. Miss Lydia, unknowingly, fueled the fire—especially yesterday, when we spoke of you.

“‘Ah!’ says the careless little thing, ‘it’s lucky for you, Mr. Esmond, that Captain Harry isn’t here.’

“‘Why, miss?’ asks he, using one of his usual conversational ornaments. He must have offended a fairy in youth, cursed to drop oaths like the girl who spat toads and serpents. (I know someone whose gentle lips only yield pearls and diamonds.) ‘Why?’ says Will, roaring with curses.

“‘O fie!’ says she, covering her prettiest little ears with her prettiest fingers. ‘O fie, sir! Such naughty words. It’s lucky the Captain isn accountability, or he might quarrel with you. Mr. George is so peaceful, he won’t. Have you heard from the Captain, Mr. George?’

“‘From Cape Breton,’ I reply. ‘He is well, thank you—that is—’ I couldn’t finish, so furious was I.

“‘From the Captain, as you call him, Miss Lyddy,’ says Will. ‘He’ll distinguish himself as he did at Saint Cas! Ho, ho!’

“‘So I apprehend he did, sir,’ says Will’s brother.

“‘Did he?’ asks our dear cousin. ‘Always thought he ran away; took to his heels; got ducked, and ran off as if a bailiff chased him.’

“‘La!’ says Miss, ‘did the Captain ever have a bailiff after him?’

“‘Didn’t he? Ho, ho!’ laughs Mr. Will.

“I suppose I looked fierce, for Spencer, dining with us, trod on my foot. ‘Don’t laugh so loud, cousin,’ I said gently. ‘You might wake good old Mr. Van den Bosch.’ The old gentleman slept in his armchair, as he often does after dinner.

“‘Oh, indeed, cousin,’ says Will, turning to wink at a friend, Captain Deuceace—whose own and whose wife’s reputation you may have heard of in the clubs, and whom Will has introduced into this simple household as a man of the highest fashion. ‘Don’t be afraid, miss,’ says Mr. Will, ‘nor my cousin needn’t be.’

“‘Oh, what a comfort!’ cries Miss Lyddy. ‘Keep quiet, gentlemen, don’t quarrel, and come up when I send for tea.’ And with a sweet curtsey, she vanishes.

“‘Hang it, Jack, pass the bottle, and don’t wake the old gentleman!’ continues Mr. Will. ‘Won’t you help yourself, cousin?’ he adds, particularly facetious in the word *cousin*.

“‘I’m going to help myself,’ I said, ‘but I won’t drink it. And I’ll tell you what I’ll do with it, if you’re quiet, cousin.’ (Desperate kicks from Spencer.)

“‘And what the devil do I care what you do with it?’ asks Will, looking pale.

“‘I’m going to fling it into your face, cousin,’ I said—and did so.

“‘By Jove, and no mistake!’ cries Mr. Deuceace. As he and William shouted curses, old Van den Bosch woke, removed his handkerchief, and asked what was wrong.

“I said it was just a glass of wine spilled. The old man replied, ‘Well, well, there’s more where that came from! Let the butler bring what you want, young gentlemen!’ and sank back to sleep.

“‘From the back of Montagu House Gardens, there’s a fine view of Hampstead at six in the morning; and the statue of the King on St. George’s Church is elegant, cousin!’ I resumed.

“‘D---- the statue!’ begins Will.

“‘Don’t, cousin!’ I said. ‘Or you’ll wake the old gentleman. Shouldn’t we go upstairs to Miss Lyddy’s tea-table?’

“We arranged a meeting for the next morning—our bodies might have lain under a coroner’s verdict by now—but just as we were to duel, three of Sir John Fielding’s men intervened, carried us to Bow Street, and bound us over to keep the peace.”
