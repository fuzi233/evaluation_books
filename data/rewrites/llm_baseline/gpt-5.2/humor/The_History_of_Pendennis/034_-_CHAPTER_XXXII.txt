Pen, in the very middle of his small, noisy pleasures—his chop from the cook-shop, his pint of porter that hissed as it was drawn, his nightly laughter that rang along Lamb Court and was answered by the thin clack of boots on Temple flags—felt all the while as if a knife hung by a thread above his plate.

It was not a poetic knife either, hung in some antique legend over a king’s banquet. It was a plain, London knife: blunt from too much carving of other people’s joints, and certain, sooner or later, to come down upon his own.

His money was going. Not in a grand catastrophe, but in the most humiliating manner—by subscriptions, by tips he could not help giving, by little civilities to beggars whose children looked as if they had never heard a wholesome kitchen sound in their lives, and by those fatal cabs that rattle you into extravagance as surely as a drum encourages marching. He had furnished his little bedroom with the grave air of a young nobleman establishing himself—bed, table, a few proud conveniences. He had still his gold chain and his dressing-case that smelt faintly of past gentilities; and now, at last, he had come down to one solitary five-pound note, lying in his pocket-book like the last slice of cold beef at the bottom of a dish: respectable, melancholy, and already imagined as gone.

Warrington, for his part, had never asked, and could not guess. An only son, a mother somewhere in the country, an uncle who dined daily with a great man and had the air of knowing the whole bill of fare of the kingdom—why should not Pen have a private larder of bank-notes, and a cellar of sovereigns, for anything the other knew? Pen had the look of a man whose boots might be brushed by fate itself. Not that he was an epicure in the vulgar meaning: he could attack a plate of beef from the cook’s shop with a gratitude as honest as a ploughman’s, and set to with the relish of a healthy youth. But he had never been trained to those penny-wise domestic arts by which men keep a dinner safe for to-morrow. He could not do the delicate arithmetic of refusing twopence to a waiter; he could not walk when it rained; and when he did take a cab, the jingling, splashing, wheel-grinding thing had him at its mercy, and he overpaid it as naturally as he breathed. He had a kind of disdain for cleaned gloves—those little economies which smell of soap and self-denial. And if a poor devil with a sorrowful tale came whining at him, or two children with faces as pale as flour offered for sale in a cellar, Pen’s hand went into his pocket as if his fingers had been born to do it.

Perhaps it was generosity; perhaps vanity; perhaps merely that he could not bear to be thought stingy by cabmen and waiters, those harsh critics who can praise you with a “Thank ye, sir” more intoxicating than claret. Who knows? We are all cooks of our own motives, and the dish we boast of most may be found, on examination, flavoured with the cheapest spice.

At length Pen, seated with Warrington, fell into a stare at that last note’s change upon the public-house tray—those small coins clinking like a warning bell among the pewter and stale beer-scent.

“It is the last rose of summer,” Pen said, with a tragical countenance, as if he were about to be buried with military honours; and then, in a rush—because there is a relief in getting the confession over, like gulping a bitter draught—he told Warrington the whole story: his mother’s narrow means, his own old follies, Laura’s generosity.

Warrington smoked and listened without interrupting, the pipe making a steady domestic sound in the pauses—puff, draw, tap—like a kitchen clock that will not stop for anybody’s romance.

When Pen had done, Warrington knocked the ashes out with the composure of a man who has seen many dinners end.

“Impecuniosity will do you good,” he said. “For an honest man—mind you. For rogues it’s like water to a duck. But for a man who means to keep his hands clean, there is nothing so wholesome as being in a state of tick. It’s an alterative and a tonic. It braces you, as when you’re riding at a fence and must take it, or when a man’s single-stick is up before you and you must meet it. Necessity brings out the pluck, if there’s any in you. You’ll discover how many things you can do without when you’ve no money to buy them. New gloves, varnished boots, eau de Cologne, cabs—poof! You’ve been nursed like a molly-coddle, Pen; spoilt by the women. A single man with health and brains who can’t find a livelihood doesn’t deserve to stay in the world. Let him pay his last halfpenny and jump over Waterloo Bridge. Or let him steal a leg of mutton, get transported, and at least have the decency to quit the country. Dixi. I have spoken. Give us another pull at the pale ale.”

“You have spoken enough for three pamphlets,” Pen said. “But how is one to live? Beef and bread are plentiful in England, but you must pay for them with work or money. Who will take my work? What work can I do?”

Warrington laughed. “We’ll advertise in the Times for an usher’s place at a classical and commercial academy. ‘A gentleman, B.A. of St. Boniface College, and plucked for his degree—’”

“Confound you,” cried Pen.

“‘—wishes to give lessons in classics and mathematics, and the rudiments of the French language; he can cut hair, attend to the younger pupils, and play a second on the piano with the daughters of the principal. Address A. P., Lamb Court, Temple.’”

“Go on,” Pen said, growling, though his mouth twitched.

“Men take to all sorts of trades. Bloundell-Bloundell is a professional blackleg, and lives on young gentlemen as other men live on mutton. Bob O’Toole drives the Ballynafad mail, and carries honest Jack Finucane’s letters. I knew a doctor’s son—don’t look savage, I meant nothing—who came to his last five-pound note, let his moustachios grow, went down to a provincial town, and set up as Professor Spineto, chiropodist to the Emperor of All the Russians. By a happy operation on the editor of the local paper he established himself in practice, lived three years, and is now reconciled, and has succeeded to his father’s gallypots.”

“Hang gallypots!” Pen cried. “I can’t drive a coach, cut corns, or cheat at cards. Is that all your wisdom?”

“No,” Warrington said. “There’s our own correspondent. Every man has his secrets, look you. Before this confession, with your airs and look, any one would suppose you had a bank behind you as long as the Strand. But with what you tell me of your mother’s income, you must not touch it. You can’t go on spunging on the women. You must pay off that trump of a girl—Laura, is it? Here’s your health, Laura!—and carry a hod sooner than ask for a shilling from home.”

“But how earn one?” Pen persisted.

“How do I live?” Warrington asked, and for a moment the jest fell away from him like smoke blown aside by a wind. “On my younger brother’s allowance, do you think? I made away with that five years ago. If I had made away with myself a little sooner, it might have been better.” He said it lightly, but his face did not consent to the lightness. “I’ve played off my own bat ever since. I don’t want much. When my purse is empty, I work and fill it; and then I lie idle like a serpent or an Indian till I’ve digested the mass.” He held out a long, lean purse with a few sovereigns huddled at one end like last potatoes in a sack. “Look. I begin to feel empty.”

“But how do you fill it?” Pen asked.

“I write,” Warrington said; and, absurdly, blushed as if he had confessed to stealing. “I don’t proclaim it. Perhaps I’m an ass and don’t want it said that George Warrington writes for bread. But I do. I write in the Law Reviews—here, these are mine.” He turned over some sheets. The pages had that sober look which suggests ink that smells of midnight, and thoughts boiled down as carefully as stock. “And now and then I do a bit for a newspaper—an editor’s friend. Come to the club.”

They went; and Warrington called for a file of the Dawn, and, with no speech, touched two articles with his finger. Pen read, and his spirits rose as if he had tasted something strong after thin fare: the sense, the satire, the scholarship, the curt, clean strokes—like a knife that knows its trade.

“I’m not up to this,” Pen said honestly. “I know very little of politics or history. I’ve only a smattering. I can’t fly on such a wing as yours.”

“But you can on your own,” Warrington said, good-humouredly. “Lighter wings sometimes go higher. Those little scraps and verses you’ve shown me—there’s a natural gift in them. Rare enough. Don’t blush, you conceited young jackanapes. You’ve thought so yourself any time these ten years. You’ve got the sacred flame—a little real poetical fire. All our oil-lamps are nothing to it, though ever so well trimmed. You are a poet, Pen, my boy.” And he clapped him on the shoulder with a hand that might have flattened a joint of meat.

Arthur was so pleased that his eyes smarted. “How kind you are,” he said.

“I like you,” Warrington answered. “I was devilish lonely in chambers. Wanted somebody. The sight of your honest face pleased me somehow. I liked the way you laughed at Lowton—that poor good little snob. And—well, I don’t know why—so it is. I’m alone in the world, sir; and I wanted some one to keep me company.” There was a quick kindness and a melancholy in his dark eyes that would have been understood by any man who had ever listened to an empty room after a door shuts.

Pen, however, was too full of his own delicious vanity to notice. “Thank you,” he said; “and—what you say about me. I have often thought I was a poet. I will be one—if the world won’t, I will. Is it the Ariadne in Naxos you liked? I was only eighteen. Or the Prize Poem?”

Warrington broke into a roar. “Why, you young goose! Of all the weak rubbish I ever tried, Ariadne in Naxos is the most mawkish and disgusting. And the Prize Poem is so pompous and feeble that I’m surprised it didn’t get the medal. You don’t suppose you’re going to cut out Milton and Æschylus? Setting up for Pindar, you absurd little tom-tit, and fancying you’ve the Theban eagle’s pinion? No. I think you can write a magazine article and turn a pretty copy of verses. That’s what I think.”

“By Jove!” Pen cried, bouncing up, his pride as quick as a match. “I’ll show you I’m a better man than you think!”

Warrington only laughed the more, and answered with twenty-four puffs out of his pipe—rapid, dry, provoking as a boy drumming on a table.

An occasion for Pen’s trial came sooner than he expected.

Mr. Bacon, that eminent publisher of Paternoster Row—formerly Bacon and Bungay—proprietor of the legal Review where Warrington laboured, and of other grave periodicals that smelt of paste and authority, had also a yearly gaudy blossom: a gilt volume called the Spring Annual, edited by the Lady Violet Lebas, and stuffed with fashionable verse. In it young lords first lisped their rhymes; honourable gentlemen practised chivalry on paper; Eastern “Ghazuls” blossomed like forced fruit; and the whole book, like many vernal blossoms, has since perished out of the world, though it was born in gold.

It was illustrated with prints of reigning beauties and tender subjects, prepared long in advance—because engraving, like pickling, takes its own time; so it was not the painter who illustrated the poet, but the poet who was ordered to garnish the plate, as a cook is handed some absurd mould and told to make a dinner look handsome in it.

Just when the Annual was on the eve of publication, Warrington went to Paternoster Row to see Mr. Hack—Bacon’s reader and manager, a professional gentleman employed because Mr. Bacon had the good sense to know he had no taste. Warrington found Hack with proof-plates and sheets spread before him, and glanced at them.

One plate was called The Church Porch. A Spanish damsel, prayer-book in hand, hurried to church; a youth in a cloak lurked in a niche, watching her. The picture had a pretty sentiment. Unfortunately, the verses supplied by the Honourable Percy Popjoy were of that peculiar kind which can only be written by a young nobleman who has never been refused anything—not even praise.

Warrington laughed aloud as he read. Hack laughed too, but with the face of a man hearing the cook announce that the joint is burnt and the company already seated.

“It won’t do,” Hack said. “The public won’t stand it. Bungay’s people are bringing out a very good book and have set up Miss Bunyan against Lady Violet. We’ve the names, to be sure—but the stuff is too bad. Lady Violet herself owns it. She’s busy with her own poem. What’s to be done? We can’t lose the plate. The governor gave sixty pounds for it.”

“I know a fellow who’ll do the verses,” Warrington said. “Give me the plate; I’ll take it home in my pocket. Send to my chambers in the morning.”

“You’ll pay well, of course,” he added.

“Of course,” said Hack, with a sigh of relief; and Warrington, pocketing the Church Porch as coolly as if it were a handkerchief, went back to the Temple.

He flung the plate before Pen.

“Now, boy. Here’s a chance. Turn me off a copy of verses to this.”

Pen looked. “A Church Porch. A lady going in, and a youth out of—what?—a wine-shop window ogling her. What the deuce am I to do with it?”

“Try,” said Warrington. “Earn your livelihood for once, you who long so to do it.”

Pen set his jaw, as if he had been given a tough bit of meat and was resolved to make something of it. “Well. I will try.”

“And I’ll go out to dinner,” Warrington said, and left him.

Pen sat alone. The Temple had its own soundscape: distant wheels grinding on Fleet Street; clerks’ voices thinning away; a bell somewhere counting the hour; then quieter, the whisper of paper, the scratch of a pen, and the private little groan a man makes when he cannot find the right word. Outside, London never stopped humming—an iron, commercial hum—yet in that room Pen tried to listen for another music: the remembered toll of a minster bell above a city’s rout, the hush of a porch, the soft footfall of a girl with downcast eyes. He had never been to Spain; he had, however, been to supper, which is sometimes more instructive.

Warrington came home late. The air he brought in with him was full of tavern warmth and street damp; the candles smelt a little of grease; and Pen, pale and triumphant, pushed the pages over as if offering a dish that had taken him hours to reduce and season.

“There they are,” he said. “I’ve screwed ’em out at last. I think they’ll do.”

Warrington read; and as he went on, the mockery in his face subsided into a professional approval, the way a stern man nods at a well-made loaf. When he finished, he said, simply, “I think they will.”

Pen’s verses ran thus:

Although I enter not,  
Yet round about the spot  
Sometimes I hover,  
And at the sacred gate,  
With longing eyes I wait,  
Expectant of her.

The Minster bell tolls out  
Above the city’s rout  
And noise and humming  
They’ve stopp’d the chiming bell,  
I hear the organ’s swell  
She’s coming, she’s coming!

My lady comes at last,  
Timid and stepping fast,  
And hastening hither,  
With modest eyes downcast.  
She comes—she’s here—she’s past.  
May Heaven go with her!

Kneel undisturb’d, fair saint,  
Pour out your praise or plaint  
Meekly and duly.  
I will not enter there,  
To sully your pure prayer  
With thoughts unruly.

But suffer me to pace  
Round the forbidden place,  
Lingering a minute,  
Like outcast spirits, who wait  
And see through Heaven’s gate  
Angels within it.

“Have you any more?” Warrington asked. “We must make them give you a couple of guineas a page. If the stuff’s liked, you’ll get an entrée into Bacon’s magazines, and may turn a decent penny.”

Pen rummaged in his portfolio and found another ballad—one of those pieces that had been lying like a dried herb, waiting for boiling water. He thought it might do for the Annual too. Then, with Warrington as ambassador and agent, they walked from the Temple to Paternoster Row.

Bacon’s shop stood there, ancient and low-browed, with a few books displayed under a bust of my Lord of Verulam; the brass name on the private door had the polished air of prosperity. Opposite, Bungay’s house showed off in fresh paint and an elaborate seventeenth-century style, as if Mr. Pepys might step in to sniff at the bindings and note down the prices. The Row itself had its own peculiar chorus: the faint toll from Saint Paul’s overhead; the murmur of trade; the rustle of paper; the ding of a shop bell; the constant small shuffling of messengers—printer’s boys and men with parcels—each footstep part of the enormous machinery that grinds thought into print.

Warrington went in. Pen stayed out, pacing like a man waiting for a tooth to be pulled. He looked into the windows as if they were the consoling books on the dentist’s table. Here were black-letter volumes; there the pale neat types of Aldus and Elzevir. Next, the Penny Horrific Register, Halfpenny Annals of Crime, histories of celebrated murderers; Raff’s Magazine; The Larky Swell—ink boiled to a cheap, acrid broth for the multitude. In the next window stern portraits of dissenting divines stared out, with fac-similes of reverend signatures and sermons for mental pabulum. Hard by was a little casement hung with rosaries, medals, paltry prints of saints gilt and painted, and penny controversial books for faithful distribution; and close beside that, a sermon called Come out of Rome, preached at the opening of Shepherd’s Bush College by John Thomas, Lord Bishop of Ealing. Scarce an opinion but had its stall and its cry there, each with its own vocabulary—dominant words elbowing lesser dialects, each trying to drown the other out in print, while the bells of Saint Paul tolled on, indifferent and ancient.

Pen walked, waited, listened to the iron city-noise, and fancied he could hear, through it all, the tender hush he had written—minster bell, organ swell—like a remembered flavour in a room that smells of coal.

Warrington was long inside; he was pleading, bargaining, playing upon the small vanities and large appetites that govern publishers. Pen did not hear the speech—worse luck for his modesty—but it was delivered.

It happened that Mr. Bacon himself came downstairs into Mr. Hack’s room while Warrington was there. Warrington, knowing Bacon’s weaknesses, served up his friend as deftly as a cook humours a capricious palate. He put on his hat to address Bacon, seated himself on the table with the comfortable insolence of a gentleman, and spoke as if doing Bacon a favour by permitting him to buy genius.

“What! not know Mr. Pendennis, Mr. Bacon?” Warrington said. “You can’t live much in the world, or you would know him. A man of property in the West, of one of the most ancient families in England, related to half the nobility in the empire—cousin to Lord Pontypool—one of the most distinguished men at Oxbridge; dines at Gaunt House every week.”

“Law bless me,” said Mr. Bacon, swelling like a man who feels he is entertaining quality without having paid for it. “You don’t say so, sir. Well—really—Law bless me now.”

“I’ve just shown Mr. Hack some verses he sat up last night to write at my request; and Hack talks of paying him with a copy of the—what-d’-you-call-’em.”

“Law bless me now, does he? The what-d’-you-call-’em. Indeed!”

“‘The Spring Annual,’” Warrington snapped. “As payment. You don’t suppose such a man as Mr. Arthur Pendennis gives up a dinner at Gaunt House for nothing? The men of fashion want to be paid.”

“That they do, Mr. Warrington, sir,” Bacon said, with an approving groan.

“He’s a star,” Warrington continued. “He’ll make a name. He’s a new man.”

“They’ve said that of so many of those young swells,” Bacon sighed. “Lord Viscount Dodo now—I gave his lordship a good bit of money, and only sold eighty copies. Popjoy’s Hadgincourt fell dead.”

“Well then,” Warrington said, rising as if the whole conversation were beneath him, “I’ll take my man over to Bungay.”

The threat was like a knife at Bacon’s throat. He gave in at once; asked Hack, in a hurry, what the terms were; and when he heard it was only two ballads for the Annual, he cried, “Law bless you, give him a check directly.”

With this precious paper Warrington emerged at last, and put it into Pen’s hand with a grin.

Pen stared at it as if it were a hot pie placed before a starving man. He was elated as though someone had left him a fortune. The street seemed brighter; the bells friendlier; even the carts’ rattling less insulting.

He offered Warrington a dinner at Richmond on the spot. “And what shall I buy for Laura? For my mother? I must buy something.”

“They’ll like the book better than any trinket,” Warrington said. “With the young one’s name among the swells.”

“Thank God! thank God!” Pen cried. “I needn’t be a charge on the old mother. I can pay off Laura now. I can get my own living. I can make my own way.”

“I can marry the Grand Vizier’s daughter,” Warrington said dryly. “I can purchase a house in Belgrave Square. I can build a fine castle in the air! Well, you may get bread and cheese, Pen; and I own it tastes well—the bread you earn yourself.”

At dinner in the club that day Pen insisted on a magnum of claret—an extravagance he had not seen for many a day. Warrington would not baulk him. The cork came out with a report like a small gun; the wine smelt of dark fruit and old wood, like a memory of country dinners in rooms that did not echo with borrowed laughter; and they drank to the health of the Spring Annual as if it were a living benefactor.

And, because Fortune likes to make a joke of her own—first starving you, then flinging loaves at your head—another chance came quickly.

One day Warrington tossed a letter across the table. It had been brought by a printer’s boy—one of those small emissaries who carry other men’s hopes under their arm, and lie down to sleep in passages as if they were part of the furniture. The boy announced it “from Captain Shandon, sir,” and then, having done his duty, curled himself on Warrington’s accustomed bench outside, where his subsequent visits would become as regular as the knocking of type.

The letter ran:

F. P. Tuesday Morning.

“MY DEAR SIR,—Bungay will be here to-day, about the Pall Mall Gazette. You would be the very man to help us with a genuine West-end article,—you understand—dashing, trenchant, and d—— aristocratic. Lady Hipshaw will write; but she’s not much you know, and we’ve two lords; but the less they do the better. We must have you. We’ll give you your own terms, and we’ll make a hit with the Gazette.

“Shall B. come and see you, or can you look in upon me here?—Ever yours,

“C. S.”
