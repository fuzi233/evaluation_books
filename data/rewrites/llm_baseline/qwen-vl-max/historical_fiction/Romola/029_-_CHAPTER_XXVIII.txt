Four days had passed since Romola last set foot in the bustling streets of Florence, and now she walked toward Piero di Cosimo’s house on the Via Gualfonda, her steps measured and purposeful beneath the weight of a quiet anxiety. The city was no longer the same place it had been when she first came to know it—its air thick with tension, its silence broken by whispers of foreign power. French soldiers lined certain thoroughfares, their eyes scanning the ancient stone buildings as if claiming them, while Florentines returned the gaze with narrowed brows and guarded glances. There was little warmth in either direction. The French, accustomed to being at the center of Europe’s admiration, carried themselves with an air of superiority that grated upon those who had long prided themselves on intellect, art, and independence. And Florence, though outwardly compliant, simmered with quiet defiance.

The royal guest—this new Charlemagne, they called him—had come not as a friend but as a conqueror, his entrance marked by the clang of steel and the pomp of empire. He spoke of leaving a viceroy behind, of restoring the Medici—those exiled lords whose very name stirred bitter memories among the people. It was a strange logic: that Florence, which had resisted tyranny under Piero de’ Medici, should now be punished for its own liberty. The citizens met in secret councils within the Old Palace, their voices low but resolute. And already, the defiant spirit showed itself in bold emblems painted across the walls—symbols meant to mock the arrogance of the Frenchman.

Yet Romola moved through this charged atmosphere without fear. Cloaked in black, her face veiled, she was unseen by most, her presence softened by the old servant Maso walking beside her like a shadow. Her thoughts were not on politics or war, but on something far more personal—a portrait.

She had come to see Piero di Cosimo. Not for idle conversation, nor merely out of affection, but because he had promised her a copy of her father’s likeness as Oedipus—the only image of him from the time he was blind. That portrait, painted years ago, was precious beyond words. It held the memory of a man whose strength had never faltered, even when sight failed him. Romola clung to such relics not just with love, but with a deep sense of duty—her devotion demanded that nothing be lost, not even the faintest echo of his face.

Piero was unpredictable. He would abandon a canvas for months, burying it in a chest or corner where it might vanish entirely. He worked only when inspired, and inspiration came irregularly. But Romola had earned his favor; he often bent to her wishes. Still, trust was fragile with him—his whims could override any promise.

He had said the week before that the painting might be done by now. And so, with a nervousness that stirred her breath, Romola hurried to his door.

She lifted the iron slide and called in a soft, clear voice—like a child summoning Tito once had—“Piero!”

He answered quickly, but when he opened the door, he gave a half-apologetic shrug. “Ah, Madonna Romola? I thought my eggs had arrived—I needed them.”

“I’ve brought you something better than hard eggs,” Romola said, smiling gently as she drew back her veil. “Maso has a basket full of cakes and *confetti*.”

She took the basket from Maso and stepped inside. “I know you enjoy these things when they come easily,” she added, teasingly. “Confess you do.”

“Yes,” Piero replied, folding his arms and watching the sweets as Romola uncovered them. “When they arrive as naturally as light.” Then, lifting his eyes to her face, he smiled—truly smiled—for a moment. “And today, they’ve come with the light.”

Her hood slipped back, revealing her hair, and he looked at her with the painter’s eye, lingering on the curve of her brow, the glow of her skin. But then he turned away, catching himself. “But I know what these treats mean,” he said. “They’re bribes to keep me quiet while you scold me.”

“You may go ahead,” Romola laughed. “But first, let me see the picture.”

He gestured toward the next room. “Come in. I’ve worked on it since you were last here—though it’s not finished. But I didn’t promise, you understand. I never promise.”

He quoted aloud, with a touch of irony:

> *“Chi promette e non mantiene
> L’anima sua non va mai bene.”*
> *(Who promises and does not keep,
> His soul will never be at ease.)*

The door to the garden stood shut now, and the room was dim, lit only by the afternoon sun slanting through the window. Piero was at work—but not on her father’s portrait. That stood propped against the wall, untouched. As he reached to move it into better light, he revealed another painting hidden behind it—a small oil sketch, newly completed.

Romola froze.

“That is Tito!” she breathed, her face flushing with surprise.

Piero turned slowly, his expression unreadable. He gave a silent shrug—regretful, perhaps, for having forgotten where he’d placed it. She stared at the sketch, her mind racing. The figure was unmistakable: Tito, tall and noble, yet caught in a moment of stillness, almost vulnerability. Beside him stood a gaunt, chain-armored man, his hands bound, his face twisted in terror. A rope encircled his neck, dragging him forward.

“What a strange picture,” Romola whispered. “When did you paint this? What does it mean?”

“A mere fancy,” Piero said, pulling off his skullcap and scratching his head with the habitual grimace that masked his emotions. “I wanted a handsome young face—your husband’s was perfect.”

He stepped forward, picked up the sketch, and turned it away from her, pretending to examine it critically. “Not worth showing,” he muttered. “Just a reckless dabbling.”

But Romola wouldn’t let it go. “Don’t put it away,” she insisted, stepping close. “Let me look again. That man—he wore armor. I saw him. I saw you come to him in the Duomo. What made you paint him with Tito?”

Piero hesitated. Then, seeing no way around it, he offered part of the truth. “It was chance. The man was fleeing—up the steps, stumbling. He grabbed your husband’s sleeve. I happened to be there. I thought the fellow looked fierce enough for a subject. But it’s nothing—just a rough sketch. Worthless.”

He moved it swiftly to a high shelf, out of sight, and turned back to her. “Now come and see the Oedipus.”

But he had done too much. His haste to hide the sketch had only deepened her unease. Something about it troubled her—not just the image, but the way he had reacted. And yet, she could not press further. To question Tito now, after everything, would feel like betrayal. Her pride, her love, refused to allow suspicion—even in thought.

She said only, quietly, “He looked pitiful—strange, that prisoner. Do you know anything more of him?”

“No,” Piero replied. “I showed him to the hospital. That’s all.”

Then he turned to the Oedipus, holding it up for her inspection.

Romola stepped forward, her attention drawn fully to the portrait. She studied it in silence, her breath slow, her eyes tracing every line—the furrowed brow, the solemn mouth, the distant gaze that seemed to listen beyond the world. At last, she spoke.

“Ah,” she said softly. “You have given it what I asked for. More of the listening look. My good Piero…” Her voice trembled slightly. “I am truly grateful.”

Piero turned sharply. “Now, that’s what I can’t stand about women—you always pour out gratitude where none is due. Why should you thank me for a painting I’m paid to make? Especially when I make you wait? If I paint well, it’s for my own satisfaction, not yours. Should I be thanked for not being a fool or a thief? Let the man thank God for giving him a decent mind. But women think walls are built with honey.”

“You crusty Piero,” Romola said, laughing through her tears. “I forgot how sharp you can be.” She reached into the basket and pulled out a crisp, sweet treat. “Here—put this in your mouth.”

He accepted it with the same awkward grace as a bear might accept a pear in a dream—pleased, but hiding it beneath a gruff exterior. “It’s good,” he said, reaching again for another. “Madonna Antigone—good.”

Romola watched him, her heart softening. For a moment, the troubling vision of the sketch faded. She stood opposite him, content in the simple warmth of the moment.

“Good-bye, Piero,” she said finally, setting down the basket. “And I promise—I won’t thank you if you finish the portrait soon and well. You were bound to do it for your own credit.”

“Good,” he replied curtly, helping her fold her mantle and veil with practiced care.

Once the door closed behind her, Piero leaned against it, exhaling. “I’m glad she asked no more,” he thought. “I’d hate for her to guess I thought her fine husband made a good model for a coward. But I played it cool—she’ll forget.”

But Piero was mistaken. As Romola walked home, the image of the sketch burned in her mind—the armored man, the rope, Tito’s stillness. And the memory of the morning when the chain-armour was adopted—when Tito had first worn it—came flooding back. Was it coincidence? Or had something darker happened?

“It means nothing,” she told herself. “Only chance.”

Yet her heart beat faster. “Shall I ask Tito?” she wondered. Her mind answered firmly: *No. I will not question him unless he speaks first. It would be a betrayal of trust.* But her heart whispered: *I dare not ask.*

There was a crack in her certainty—a flaw in the faith she had placed in him. And like one who holds a fragile treasure, she feared to move too quickly, afraid that the slightest pressure might shatter what she believed unbreakable.
