Major Pendennis had made up his mind to keep his garrison stationed at Fairoaks until the enemy had completely withdrawn. While he didn't explicitly micromanage Pen's behavior or impose strict rules, the lad remained under the constant surveillance of his uncle and his network of spies. Young Arthur's every move was meticulously tracked by his vigilant guardian.

Let us pause for a moment to consider the universal plight of romance. Scarcely anyone who reads novels—or lives in reality—hasn't been thwarted in love by fate, circumstance, deceitful women, or their own foolish choices. Recall your own heartbreaks and apply them to Mr. Pen's agony. Ah, the weary nights! The sickening fevers! Those mad desires crashing against rocks of indifference, only to be bounced back by unyielding granite! Imagine a list compiled tonight in London of the groans, thoughts, and curses of tossing lovers. What a catalog it would be! I wonder what percentage of the male population of the metropolis lies awake at 2:00 or 3:00 AM tomorrow, counting the hours, rolling restlessly from side to side, yearning and heart-sick. What a pang it is! I've never known a man literally die of love, but I have seen a twelve-stone man shrink to nine-and-a-half stone under the weight of a disappointed passion. Nearly a quarter of him perishes! Though he eventually regains his size, perhaps growing larger than before, thanks to a new affection wrapping around his heart and ribs like a comforting corset. Young Pen will surely console himself like the rest of us. We say this merely to prevent the ladies from prematurely lamenting him or worrying unduly about his "complaint." His mother worried, of course, but what won't maternal fondness fear or invent? "Depend on it, my dear creature," Major Pendennis would say gallantly to her, "the boy will recover. Once we get her out of the country, we'll take him somewhere lively. For now, don't fret. Half a fellow's pain over losing a woman stems from vanity rather than affection. Being left by a woman is terrible, to be sure; but look how easily *we* leave them."

Mrs. Pendennis, however, remained ignorant of the depth of her son's torment. Such complex emotions were beyond her simple understanding. Indeed, she disliked discussing the subject; her own heart had suffered a private misadventure which she had bravely endured and cured, leaving her with little patience for others' passions—except, naturally, Arthur's. She felt his sufferings as her own, experiencing many of his pains far more intensely than Pen did herself. She watched him through this grief with a jealous, silent sympathy, even though he refused to confide in her.

One must grant the Major considerable merit and forbearance, displaying a truly admirable degree of family affection. Life at Fairoaks was excruciatingly dull for a man accustomed to entering half the houses in London and making bows in three or four drawing rooms nightly. Occasional dinners with Doctor Portman or a neighboring squire, followed by a dreary game of backgammon with the Widow—who tried desperately to entertain him—were his chief pleasures. He longed for the mailbag bearing letters and devoured every word of the evening paper. He diligently prescribed himself a regimen of quiet living, believing it suited him better than London banquets. He dressed laboriously twice daily, taking regular exercise along the terrace walk. Armed with his cane, his meticulous toiletries, his medicine chest, his backgammon box, and his newspaper, this worldly philosopher constructed a fortress against boredom. If he couldn't improve each shining hour like bees buzzing around the widow's garden wall, Major Pendennis simply dragged one hour after another past as best he could, rendering his captivity merely tolerable. Later, it was noted that he grew fond of steering conversations toward the American War, the Massacre of Wyoming, and the Battle of Saint Lucia, largely because he possessed two volumes of the 'Annual Register' in his bedroom which he studiously reviewed. Thus, a well-regulated man accommodates himself to circumstances, showing himself calmly superior to fortune.

Pen occasionally joined the late-night backgammon games or listened to his mother's simple summer music, yet he remained restless and wretched despite everything. He was known to rise before dawn, descending to a carp-pond in Clavering Park—a dreary pool filled with whispering rushes and green alders, where a milkmaid had drowned in the Baronet's grandfather's time, and her ghost was rumored to still wander. Pen did not attempt suicide, as his mother feared. Instead, he loved to fish there, thinking and pondering as the float quivered in the eddies and fish flapped nearby. If he caught a bite, he was thrilled, occasionally returning with carps, tenches, and eels which the Major cooked à la Française.

Beneath his favorite tree by this pond, Pen composed numerous poems suited to his melancholy circumstances, verses he later blushed to remember, wondering how he could ever have invented such rubbish. As for the tree itself, it holds a hollow where he once stored his tin-box of ground-bait and fishing tackle—a spot significant for reasons we shall advance to later. Suffice it to say, he wrote poetry and found relief therein. When a man's grief reaches this stage, it may be loud, but it loses its severity. When a gentleman struggles to find rhymes for sorrow beyond "borrow" and "tomorrow," his woes are nearing their end sooner than he realizes. So it was with Pen. He experienced hot and cold fits, days of sullenness and peevishness, periods of blank resignation, and occasional wild paroxysms of rage and longing. During these episodes, Rebecca would be saddled and ridden fiercely through the countryside or into Chatteris, her rider gesticulating wildly atop her, startling carters and toll-gate keepers as he shouted the name of the false one.

Mr. Foker became a frequent and delightful visitor to Fairoaks during this time. His good spirits and eccentricities amused the Major and Pen, while astonishing the Widow and little Laura. His tandem carriage caused quite a sensation in Clavering marketplace, where he overturned a stall, sliced through Mrs. Pybus's poodle over its shaved quarters, and consumed a glass of raspberry bitters at the Clavering Arms. Everyone in the little town heard of him and eagerly checked his name in their outdated peerage books. Being so young, and their books so archaic, his name appeared in few volumes; his mother, now an elderly lady, was listed among the progeny of the Earl of Rosherville as Lady Agnes Milton. Yet, his name, wealth, and honorable lineage quickly became common knowledge in Clavering, where, naturally, poor Pen's little affair with the Chatteris actress was also widely discussed.

Viewing the quaint little town of Clavering St. Mary from the London road, passing the lodge at Fairoaks, one sees the swift, shimmering River Brawl winding down from the town and skirting the woods of Clavering Park. The ancient church tower and peaked roofs of the houses rise amidst trees and old walls, backed by a fair panorama of sun-drenched hills stretching westward toward the sea. The place appears so cheerful and comfortable that many a traveler's heart yearns toward it from the coach top, imagining it as a calm, friendly nook to shelter in at life's end. Tom Smith, who drove the Alacrity coach, often pointed to a tree near the river commanding a fine view of the church and town, telling his passenger on the box, "Artises come and take hoff the Church from that there tree—It was a Habby once, sir:" Indeed, it is a lovely view, recommended for Mr. Stanfield or Mr. Roberts' next tour.

Yet, like Constantinople seen from the Bosphorus, or Mrs. Rougemont viewed from across the room, or many objects we pursue and admire before attaining them, Clavering is prettier at a distance than upon close inspection. The town, so cheerful a few furlongs off, looks remarkably blank and dreary up close. Except on market days, the streets are empty. The clacking of pattens echoes through half the place, and you can hear the creaking of the rusty old ensign at the Clavering Arms without hearing any other sound. No balls have been held in the Assembly Rooms since the Clavering Volunteers hosted one for their Colonel, the old Sir Francis Clavering. The stables, once housing a brilliant but defunct regiment, are now cheerless and empty, save for Thursdays when farmers park their tilted carts and gigs, creating a feeble display of liveliness, or during Petty Sessions when magistrates gather in what used to be the old card-room.

On the south side of the market rises the church, with its great grey towers. Sunlight illuminates the delicate carving, deepening the shadows of the massive buttresses and gilding the glittering windows and flaming vanes. Centuries ago, the image of the Church's Patroness was torn from the porch; such statues of saints as were within reach of stones and hammers during that era of pious demolition are now maimed and headless. Of those spared from destruction, only Doctor Portman knows their names and histories, for his curate, Smirke, lacks antiquarian interest, and Mr. Simcoe (husband of the Honorable Mrs. Simcoe), incumbent and architect of the Chapel of Ease in the lower town, considers them the abomination of desolation.
