Deep within the labyrinthine folds of Holborn, London, where ancient gabled houses stand like weary sentinels overlooking a street that has long forgotten the Old Bourne, lies a secluded enclave known as Staple Inn. It is a place where the mere act of turning the corner offers the pedestrian a sudden, profound relief—a sensation akin to stuffing cotton into one's ears and velvet into one's boots. Here, smoky sparrows twitter in smoky trees, playing a game of "country" that requires only a few feet of garden soil and a yard of gravel to confuse their tiny minds. It is a legal nook, housing a small hall with a lantern in its roof, the purpose of which remains a mystery to history itself.

In the distant past, when the very existence of a railroad threatened to offend the sensitive constitution of Cloisterham, this district stood alone, unshadowed by towering architecture. The sun once bathed it in glory, and the south-west wind swept through unimpeded. But on a December afternoon, nearing six o'clock, neither sun nor wind favored Staple Inn. Instead, a thick fog rolled in, filling the air until candles inside the occupied chambers shed only murky, blurred rays. Among these, one set of rooms in a corner house of the inner quadrangle stood out, marked above its grim portal by the inscription:

1747

Inside these chambers, Mr. Grewgious sat by his fire, his mind rarely troubled by the inscription above him. Occasionally, he would glance up, wondering if it stood for *Perhaps John Thomas* or *Perhaps Joe Tyler*, but mostly he ignored it. Who could judge Mr. Grewgious by his appearance? Had he ever known ambition? Disappointment? He had been trained for the Bar, aiming for chamber practice and the conveyancing of deeds—"convey the wise it call," as Pistol might say. Yet, Conveyancing and he had parted by mutual consent; she was wooed but never won.

However, an unexpected wind of Arbitration blew him toward success, earning him credit for indefatigable justice. This was followed by a more traceable wind delivering a lucrative Receivership to his pocket. By chance, he had found his niche. Now a Receiver and Agent for two wealthy estates, delegating legal matters to a firm below, he had extinguished his ambition and settled down beneath the dry vine and fig-tree of P. J. T., planted in 1747.

His room was a testament to order rather than luxury. Countless accounts, files, and strongboxes lined the space, arranged with such conscientious precision that they did not clutter the room but defined it. The fear of dying suddenly, leaving a single fact or figure incomplete, would have driven Mr. Grewgious mad. Fidelity to trust was his lifeblood; while other life-bloods course more gaily, none circulated with greater integrity.

Comforts were sparse. The room was merely dry and warm, centered around a faded fireside. Its private life consisted of the hearth, an easy-chair, and an old-fashioned round table that emerged from a corner like a shield after business hours. Behind it lay a closet stocked with good drink. The clerk's room was adjacent; Mr. Grewgious slept across the common stair and held some cellarage below. For three hundred days a year, he crossed to Furnival's Inn for dinner and returned to maximize these simple pleasures until the broad daylight of business returned.

As Mr. Grewgious wrote by the fire, so did his clerk, Bazzard, write by his own. Bazzard was a pale, puffy-faced man of thirty, with dark, lustreless eyes and a doughy complexion that seemed perpetually in need of a baker's touch. He possessed a strange power over Mr. Grewgious, sticking to his master's stool like a familiar summoned by a spell that refused to break. Though his presence was a burden, Mr. Grewgious treated him with unaccountable consideration.

"Now, Bazzard," Mr. Grewgious said as the clerk entered, looking up from his papers. "What is in the wind besides fog?"

"Mr. Drood," replied Bazzard.

"What of him?"

"He has called," said Bazzard.

"You might have shown him in."

"I am doing it," Bazzard replied.

The visitor entered.

"Dear me!" Mr. Grewgious exclaimed, surveying the pair of office candles. "I thought you had merely left your name. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Dear me, you're choking!"

"It's this fog," Edwin returned. "It makes my eyes smart like Cayenne pepper."

"Is it really so bad? Pray undo your wrappers. It's fortunate I have such a good fire; Mr. Bazzard has taken care of me."

"No, I haven't," Bazzard interjected from the doorway.

"Ah! Then I must have taken care of myself without observing it," Mr. Grewgious said smoothly. "Please sit in my chair. No, I beg! Coming from such an atmosphere, you should not sit in *my* chair."

Edwin took the easy-chair in the corner. The fog he brought in, and the fog he shed with his greatcoat and neck-shawl, was quickly consumed by the eager fire.

"I look," Edwin smiled, "as if I had come to stay."

"—By the by," cried Mr. Grewgious, interrupting his own train of thought. "Stay. The fog may clear in an hour or two. We can have dinner brought in from just across Holborn. You had better take your Cayenne pepper here than outside; please stay and dine."

"You are very kind," Edwin said, glancing around as if attracted by the prospect of a new, relishing sort of gathering.

"Not at all," Mr. Grewgious replied. "*You* are very kind to join issue with a bachelor in chambers and take pot-luck. And I'll ask," he dropped his voice, his eyes twinkling with a sudden bright thought, "I'll ask Bazzard. He mightn't like it otherwise.—Bazzard!"

Bazzard reappeared.

"Dine presently with Mr. Drood and me."

"If I am ordered to dine, of course I will, sir," the gloomy clerk answered.

"Save the man!" cried Mr. Grewgious. "You're not ordered; you're invited."

"Thank you, sir," Bazzard said. "In that case, I don't care if I do."

"That's arranged. And perhaps you wouldn't mind," Mr. Grewgious continued, "stepping over to the hotel in Furnival's and asking them to send in materials for laying the cloth. For dinner, we'll have the hottest, strongest soup available, the best made-dish recommended, a joint—perhaps a haunch of mutton—and a goose or turkey, or any stuffed thing on the bill of fare. In short, we'll have whatever is on hand."

These liberal directions were issued with Mr. Grewgious's usual air of reading an inventory or reciting a lesson by rote. Bazzard, after pulling out the round table, withdrew to execute them.

"I was a little delicate," Mr. Grewgious whispered after the clerk's departure, "about employing him in the foraging or commissariat department. Because he mightn't like it."

"He seems to have his own way, sir," remarked Edwin.

"His own way?" Mr. Grewgious returned. "Oh, no! Poor fellow, you quite mistake him. If he had his own way, he wouldn't be here."

"I wonder where he would be!" Edwin thought silently. But he only thought it because Mr. Grewgious stood with his back to the other corner of the fire, his shoulder-blades against the chimneypiece, collecting his skirts for easy conversation.

"I take it, without the gift of prophecy, that you have done me the favor of calling to mention that you are going down yonder—where I can tell you, you are expected—and to offer to execute any little commission for my charming ward, and perhaps to sharpen me up on any proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?"

"I called, sir, before going down, as an act of attention."

"Of attention!" Mr. Grewgious said. "Ah! Of course, not of impatience?"

"Impatience, sir?"

Mr. Grewgious had intended to be arch, bringing himself into scarcely supportable proximity to the fire to burn the effect of his wit into himself, much like subtle impressions are burned into hard metals. But his archness fled before the composed face of his visitor, leaving only the fire. He started and rubbed himself.

"I have lately been down yonder," Mr. Grewgious said, rearranging his skirts. "That is what I referred to when I said you are expected."

"Indeed, sir! Yes; I knew that Pussy was looking out for me."

"Do you keep a cat down there?" asked Mr. Grewgious.

Edwin colored slightly as he explained, "I call Rosa Pussy."

"Oh, really," Mr. Grewgious said, smoothing down his head. "That's very affable."

Edwin glanced at his face, uncertain if he objected to the appellation. But Edwin might as well have glanced at a clock face.

"A pet name, sir," he explained again.

"Umps," Mr. Grewgious nodded. But it was a compromise between unqualified assent and qualified dissent that disconcerted his visitor.

"Did PRosa—" Edwin began to recover himself.

"PRosa?" repeated Mr. Grewgious.

"I was going to say Pussy, and changed my mind; did she tell you anything about the Landlesses?"

"No," said Mr. Grewgious. "What are the Landlesses? An estate? A villa? A farm?"

"A brother and sister. The sister is at the Nuns' House and has become a great friend of P—"

"PRosa's," Mr. Grewgious struck in, his face fixed.

"She is a strikingly handsome girl, sir, and I thought she might have been described to you, or presented to you perhaps?"

"Neither," said Mr. Grewgious. "But here is Bazzard."

Bazzard returned, accompanied by two waiters—an immovable waiter and a flying waiter. The three brought in so much fog that the fire roared anew. The flying waiter, carrying everything on his shoulders, laid the cloth with amazing rapidity, while the immovable waiter, carrying nothing, found fault with him. The flying waiter then polished all the glasses, and the immovable waiter inspected them. The flying waiter flew across Holborn for the soup, returned, flew again for the made-dish, returned, flew again for the joint and poultry, and returned, taking supplementary flights for various articles as the immovable waiter discovered he had forgotten them. Yet, no matter how the flying waiter cleaved the air, he was always reproached on his return by the immovable waiter for bringing fog and being out of breath.

At the conclusion of the repast, the flying waiter was severely winded. The immovable waiter gathered the tablecloth under his arm with a grand air, sternly looked on at the flying waiter while he set the clean glasses, directed a valedictory glance toward Mr. Grewgious conveying, "Let it be clearly understood that the reward is mine, and Nil is the claim of this slave," and pushed the flying waiter out of the room.

It was like a highly finished miniature of the Circumlocution Department, Commandership-in-Chief of any sort, Government. It was an edifying little picture suitable for the National Gallery.

Since the fog was the proximate cause of this sumptuous repast, it also served as its general sauce. Hearing the out-door clerks sneeze and beat their feet on the gravel was a zest surpassing Doctor Kitchener's. Bidding the unfortunate flying waiter shut the door before he had opened it was a condiment of profounder flavor than Harvey. Notably, the leg of this young man, in its application to the door, evinced the finest sense of touch: always preceding himself and tray by some seconds with an angling air, and always lingering after he and the tray had disappeared, like Macbeth's leg accompanying him off the stage with reluctance to the assassination of Duncan.

The host had gone below to the cellar and brought up bottles of ruby, straw-coloured, and golden drinks, which had ripened long ago in lands where no fogs exist and had since lain slumbering in the shade. Sparkling and tingling after so long a nap, they pushed at their corks to help the corkscrew, like prisoners helping rioters to force their gates, and danced out gaily. If P. J. T. in 1747, or any other year of his period, drank such wines, then certainly P. J. T. was Pretty Jolly Too.

Externally, Mr. Grewgious showed no signs of being mellowed by these glowing vintages. Instead of drinking them, they might have been poured over him in high-dried snuff form and run to waste, for any lights and shades they caused to flicker over his face. His manner was unchanged. But, in his wooden way, he had observant eyes for Edwin. When, at the end of dinner, he motioned Edwin back to his own easy-chair in the fireside corner, and Edwin sank luxuriously into it after brief remonstrance, Mr. Grewgious, turning his seat round toward the fire and smoothing his head and face, might have been seen looking at his visitor between his smoothing fingers.

"Bazzard!" Mr. Grewgious suddenly turned to him.

"I follow you, sir," returned Bazzard, who had consumed meat and drink in a workmanlike manner, though mostly in speechlessness.

"I drink to you, Bazzard; Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard!"
