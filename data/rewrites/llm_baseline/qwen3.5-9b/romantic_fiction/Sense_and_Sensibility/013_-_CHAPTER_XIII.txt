The planned outing to Whitwell unfolded far differently than Elinor had anticipated. She had braced herself for rain, exhaustion, and perhaps even fright, yet the outcome proved even less fortunate: they did not go at all.

By ten o'clock, the entire party gathered at the park for breakfast. Despite the previous night's rainfall, the morning showed promise; clouds were breaking apart, allowing the sun to shine intermittently. Spirits were high, tempers excellent, and everyone was eager to embrace joy, ready to endure any hardship rather than miss the opportunity. However, the arrival of letters shattered this mood. Among the correspondence was one addressed to Colonel Brandon. Upon receiving it, he glanced at the sender, his complexion darkened instantly, and he departed the room without a word.

"What is troubling Colonel Brandon?" Sir John asked. "Nobody knows," someone replied. "I trust he hasn't received bad news," Lady Middleton interjected. "Something extraordinary must have occurred to make him abandon my breakfast table so abruptly."

Five minutes later, he returned. "No bad news, I hope," Mrs. Jennings said warmly as he entered. "None at all, ma'am, thank you."

"Was it from Avignon? I pray it doesn't concern your sister's health."

"No, ma'am. It arrived from London and is purely a matter of business."

"But how could a mere business letter disturb you so greatly? Come, Colonel, we deserve the truth."

"My dear madam," Lady Middleton chided gently, "please consider your words."

"Perhaps it announces that your cousin Fanny is married?" Mrs. Jennings guessed, ignoring her daughter's reproach.

"Not at all."

"Well, then, I know exactly who sent it. And I hope she is well."

"Who do you mean, ma'am?" he asked, flushing slightly.

"Oh! You know precisely who I mean."

"I am deeply sorry, ma'am," he addressed Lady Middleton, "to receive this letter today, for it concerns business requiring my immediate attention in town."

"In town!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "What possible business could keep you in town during this season?"

"My personal loss is significant," he continued, "in leaving such a delightful gathering. Yet, I am even more distressed because I fear my presence is essential to secure admission to Whitwell."

The announcement struck the group like a blow.

"If you simply write a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon," Marianne suggested eagerly, "wouldn't that suffice?"

He shook his head firmly.

"We must proceed," Sir John declared. "It cannot be postponed now that we are so close. You cannot travel to town until tomorrow, Brandon—that is final."

"I wish it were so simple," he replied. "However, I lack the power to delay my journey by even a single day."

"If you would only tell us the nature of your business," Mrs. Jennings pressed, "perhaps we could determine if it might wait."

"You wouldn't be delayed by six hours," Willoughby remarked smoothly, "if you postponed your trip until our return."

"I cannot afford to lose *one* hour."

Elinor caught Willoughby whispering to Marianne, "Some individuals simply cannot tolerate a pleasure party. Brandon is one of them. He likely feared catching a chill and concocted this excuse to escape. I'd wager fifty guineas the letter was written by himself."

"I have no doubt of it," Marianne responded.

"There is no convincing you to change your mind, Brandon, as I've known you since childhood," Sir John noted. "Nevertheless, I trust you'll reconsider. Think of it: the two Miss Careys traveled from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods walked up from the cottage, and Mr. Willoughby rose two hours earlier than usual just to join us at Whitwell."

Colonel Brandon reiterated his sorrow at disappointing the group but insisted his departure was unavoidable.

"Well, when will you return?"

"We shall hopefully see you at Barton," her ladyship added, "as soon as you can conveniently leave town. Consequently, we must postpone the Whitwell excursion until your return."

"You are very kind. Yet, given the uncertainty of my return date, I dare not make any promises."

"Oh! He must and shall return," Sir John cried. "If he isn't here by the end of the week, I shall go fetch him myself."

"Ay, do that, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings chimed in. "Perhaps then you'll discover the true nature of his business."

"I have no desire to pry into another man's affairs. I suspect it involves something he feels ashamed of."

As Colonel Brandon's horses were announced, Sir John asked, "Are you traveling to town on horseback?"

"No. Only to Honiton. From there, I shall take the post coach."

"Well, since you are resolved, I wish you a safe journey. Though, truly, you ought to change your mind."

"I assure you, it is beyond my power."

With these words, he took his leave of the assembly. Turning to the Dashwood sisters, he asked, "Shall I ever have the pleasure of seeing you and your sisters in town this winter?"

"I fear not at all."

"Then I must bid you farewell for a duration longer than I care to accept." To Marianne, he offered only a bow and silence.

"Come, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings urged before he departed, "do tell us what errand drives you away."

He wished them a good morning and exited, accompanied by Sir John. Once alone, the polite restraint dissolved, and universal complaints erupted. Everyone lamented the disappointment repeatedly.

"I can guess the reason for his business," Mrs. Jennings declared triumphantly. "Do you?" nearly everyone asked.

"Yes; it concerns Miss Williams, I am certain."

"And who is Miss Williams?" Marianne queried.

"What! Don't you know Miss Williams? Surely you've heard of her before. She is a relative of the Colonel's, my dear—a very close one. We won't specify how close, lest we shock the young ladies." Lowering her voice, she leaned toward Elinor and whispered, "She is his illegitimate daughter."

"Indeed!"

"Oh, yes! And she resembles him as closely as anyone could stare. I daresay the Colonel will leave her his entire fortune."

When Sir John returned, he enthusiastically joined the collective regret over the ruined plans, though he concluded by suggesting that since they were all assembled, they must find some way to enjoy themselves. After brief deliberation, they agreed that while true happiness awaited them at Whitwell, they could achieve a reasonable state of cheerfulness by touring the countryside. Carriages were summoned; Willoughby's led the way, and Marianne appeared happier than ever as she stepped inside. He drove swiftly through the park, and they vanished from sight quickly. No further mention of them arose until their late return, occurring only after the rest of the party had come back. Both seemed thrilled with their excursion, mentioning vaguely that they had stayed within the lanes while others ventured onto the downs.

A dance was scheduled for the evening, and everyone vowed to remain merrily occupied throughout the day. Additional members of the Carey family joined for dinner, resulting in a table seating nearly twenty guests, a prospect Sir John viewed with great satisfaction. Willoughby took his customary seat between the two eldest Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings sat to Elinor's right, and shortly after they were seated, she leaned forward, speaking loudly enough for both Willoughby and Marianne to hear, "I have uncovered your secret despite all your cunning. I know exactly where you spent the morning."

Marianne flushed and replied hurriedly, "Where, pray?"

"Didn't you know," Willoughby interjected, "that we rode out in my curricle?"

"Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I knew that perfectly well. My determination was to discover *where* you had gone. I hope you approve of your residence, Miss Marianne. It is quite spacious, I understand; and when I visit next, I hope you have furnished it anew, for it desperately needed updating when I visited six years ago."

Marianne turned away, deeply embarrassed. Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily. Meanwhile, Elinor realized that in her resolve to ascertain their whereabouts, she had inadvertently instructed her maid to inquire with Mr. Willoughby's groom. Through this channel, she learned they had gone to Allenham, spending considerable time strolling the gardens and inspecting the entire house.

Elinor struggled to believe this account plausible; it seemed highly improbable that Willoughby would propose entering the house, or that Marianne would consent, while Mrs. Smith resided there—with whom Marianne shared no acquaintance whatsoever.

Immediately upon leaving the dining room, Elinor questioned her maid, only to be astonished when every detail Mrs. Jennings recounted proved entirely accurate. Marianne became furious at Elinor's skepticism.

"Why did you imagine, Elinor, that we didn't go there, or that we didn't view the house? Isn't that something you have often wished to do yourself?"

"Yes, Marianne, but I would not enter while Mrs. Smith was present, especially with no companion other than Mr. Willoughby."

"Mr. Willoughby, however, is the sole individual entitled to show that house; moreover, since he arrived in an open carriage, it was impossible for anyone else to accompany us. I never experienced a more delightful morning in my life."
