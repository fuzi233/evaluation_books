The central figure among the assembled guests at the dinner party was Mr. Murthwaite. Upon his return to England, following his extensive wanderings, society had treated him with profound fascination, viewing him as a survivor of perilous adventures who had escaped to recount the tale. He had recently declared his intent to revisit the theater of his exploits and delve into territories previously untouched by exploration. This audacious disregard for his own safety, undertaken for a second time, reignited the waning adoration of his admirers for the hero. Yet, the laws of probability stood firmly against his survival on this occasion; it is rare indeed to encounter an eminent personality at dinner and simultaneously harbor the reasonable expectation that the news of his assassination will be the final report concerning him.

Once the gentlemen were left alone in the dining room, I found myself seated beside Mr. Murthwaite. With the women departed, removing the wholesome restraint their presence imposed, the conversation inevitably drifted toward politics, a necessary diversion for men of leisure. Regarding this all-consuming national obsession, I confess to being one of the most distinctly un-English individuals alive. Political discourse generally strikes me as the most tedious and futile of all conversations. Observing Mr. Murthwaite as the bottles completed their initial circuit of the table, I noted that he shared my sentiment. He managed it with considerable dexterity, showing due regard for his host's feelings, yet it was undeniable that he was preparing himself for sleep. It occurred to me that a strategic allusion to the subject of the Moonstone might serve as an effective stimulant, keeping him alert long enough to gauge his reaction to the latest complications in the Indian conspiracy, as detailed in the mundane confines of my office.

"If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite," I began, "you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder, and you took a keen interest in the bizarre sequence of events culminating in the loss of the Moonstone?"

The distinguished traveler woke instantly, asking with genuine surprise who I was. I explained my professional connection to the Herncastle family, omitting nothing regarding the peculiar role I had played vis-à-vis the Colonel and his diamond in times past. Mr. Murthwaite shifted in his chair, positioning the remainder of the company—both Conservatives and Liberals alike—behind him, and focused his entire attention squarely on plain Mr. Bruff of Gray's Inn Square.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he asked.

"I have every reason to believe," I answered, "that one of them paid me a visit in my office yesterday."

Mr. Murthwaite was not easily astonished, but this response completely staggered him. I recounted precisely what had transpired with Mr. Luker and what had happened to myself. "It is evident," I added, "that the Indian's final inquiry served a specific purpose. Why should he be so eager to ascertain the hour at which a borrower is customarily privileged to repay his debt?"

"Do you truly fail to perceive his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I am ashamed of my obtuseness, Mr. Murthwaite—I certainly cannot see it."

The great traveler became intensely engaged in probing the depths of my intellectual vacancy. "Allow me to pose a single question," he said. "In what state does the conspiracy to seize the Moonstone currently stand?"

"I cannot say," I replied. "The Indian plot remains a mystery to me."

"The Indian plot, Mr. Bruff, is a mystery to you solely because you have never subjected it to serious examination. Shall we review it together, tracing the timeline from the moment you drafted Colonel Herncastle's Will to the instant the Indian visited your office? Given your position, it is of grave importance to the interests of Miss Verinder that you possess a lucid understanding of this matter should necessity arise. Tell me, considering this imperative, will you endeavor to decipher the Indian's motive independently, or do you wish me to spare you the trouble of conducting such inquiries?"

I immediately recognized the pragmatic objective underlying his proposal and selected the first alternative.

"Very good," said Mr. Murthwaite. "Let us commence with the question of the ages of the three Indians. I can attest that they appear remarkably similar in age, and you can determine for yourself whether the man you encountered was in the prime of life. Not forty, you surmise? That aligns with my assessment. Let us assume he is not forty. Now, recall the period when Colonel Herncastle arrived in England and implemented the scheme to preserve his life. I do not require you to calculate the years; suffice it to say that these current Indians, given their age, must be the successors of three other Indians—all high-caste Brahmins, Mr. Bruff, when they departed their homeland—who accompanied the Colonel to these shores. Thus, these present men have succeeded to those who preceded them. Had they merely replaced their predecessors, the matter would lack significance. However, they have accomplished more. They have inherited the organization established by their forebears in this country. Do not protest; the organization is, by our standards, rather trivial. I estimate it comprises the command of funds, the services of that shadowy class of Englishmen who inhabit the byways of foreign life in London, and, finally, the clandestine sympathies of a select few compatriots, and formerly adherents of their own faith, employed to attend to various needs of this great metropolis. Nothing formidable, as you observe! Yet, worthy of note at the outset, for we may find cause to reference this modest Indian apparatus as we proceed. Having clarified the background, I propose a question requiring your experiential insight. What event provided the Indians with their first opportunity to seize the Diamond?"

I grasped the allusion to my own history.

"The first opportunity afforded them," I replied, "was undeniably created by Colonel Herncastle's death. They would naturally be aware of his demise?"

"As a matter of course. And his death, as you stated, granted them that first chance. Until that moment, the Moonstone remained secure within the bank's strongroom. You executed the Colonel's Will, bequeathing his jewel to his niece, and the Will was probated in the standard manner. As a legal practitioner, you are well-acquainted with the course the Indians would pursue, acting under English counsel, thereafter."

"They would procure a copy of the Will from Doctors' Commons," I said.

"Precisely. One of those shadowy Englishmen I mentioned would facilitate obtaining the copy you describe. That document would inform them that the Moonstone was bequeathed to Lady Verinder's daughter and that Mr. Blake the Elder, or his appointee, was tasked with delivering it. You will concur that acquiring necessary intelligence regarding individuals like Lady Verinder and Mr. Blake would be effortlessly attainable. The sole difficulty for the Indians lay in deciding whether to attempt seizure during the Diamond's transit from the bank or to await its delivery to Yorkshire at Lady Verinder's residence. The latter path was manifestly the safer option—and therein lies the explanation for the Indians' appearance at Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers, biding their time. In London, needless to say, they utilized their local network to monitor events. Two agents sufficed: one to track anyone departing Mr. Blake's house for the bank, and another to bribe the lower servants with beer to glean household news. These routine precautions would readily reveal that Mr. Franklin Blake had visited the bank and that he was the sole individual intending to travel to Lady Verinder. The subsequent developments, you recall, no less accurately than I do."

I remembered that Franklin Blake had detected one of the spies in the street, consequently advancing his arrival in Yorkshire by several hours, and, thanks to the prudent counsel of old Betteredge, had deposited the Diamond in the bank at Frizinghall before the Indians even prepared to approach the neighborhood. Everything was clear up to this point. Yet, since the Indians were unaware of these precautions, how is it that they made no attempt on Lady Verinder's house—where they presumably believed the Diamond resided—throughout the entire interval preceding Rachel's birthday?

In posing this dilemma to Mr. Murthwaite, I deemed it appropriate to mention that I had heard accounts of the little boy, the drop of ink, and related matters, noting that any explanation relying on clairvoyance would hold no conviction whatsoever for my mind.

"Nor does it for mine either," Mr. Murthwaite responded. "The clairvoyance in this instance is merely a manifestation of the romantic aspect of the Indian character. It would serve as refreshment and encouragement for those men—qualities entirely inconceivable to the English mind—to envelop their weary and perilous mission in this country with a certain halo of the marvelous and the supernatural. Their boy is undoubtedly a sensitive subject to mesmeric influence, and under such influence, he has undoubtedly reflected thoughts already present in the mind of the mesmerizer. I have tested the theory of clairvoyance and never observed manifestations exceeding that limit. The Indians do not investigate via this method; they regard their boy as a Seer of things invisible to mortal eyes—and, I repeat, in that marvel they discover a renewed interest in their united purpose. I merely note this as offering a curious perspective on human nature, surely novel to you. We have nothing to do with clairvoyance, mesmerism, or any other phenomenon difficult for a practical man to accept in the inquiry we are now pursuing. My objective in tracing the Indian plot step-by-step is to deduce results back to natural causes through rational means. Have I succeeded in satisfying your expectations thus far?"
