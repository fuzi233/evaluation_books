On being idle, I speak with the quiet authority of a man who has never been interrupted by a work ethic. The gentleman who used to pour education over my head at nine guineas a term—no extras, no gravy—declared I was the only boy he had ever met who could do less work in more time. My grandmother, who believed the Prayer-book could cure anything except my character, once remarked—by the way, as if it were merely a note about punctuation—that it seemed highly unlikely I should ever do very much I ought not to do, but that she felt certain I should leave undone nearly everything I ought to do.

I have, I regret to announce, proved her only half right. Heaven help me, I have done a great many things I ought not to have done, and that in spite of my laziness, which shows that sin has, at times, a kind of energy that virtue lacks. But in the matter of not doing what I ought, I have remained devout. Idling has always been my strong point. I take no credit for it. It is a gift, like a perfect pie crust: many attempt it, few achieve it, and those who do cannot explain how.

Because a genuine idler is not merely a lazy person. The world is full of slow-coaches and loiterers—people who shuffle about like underbaked dough, damp and heavy and determined to stick to everything. But the true idler is a rarity. He is not the man with his hands in his pockets. On the contrary, his most startling feature is that he always looks intensely occupied. The sacred art demands it. It is impossible to enjoy idleness properly unless one has plenty to do. There is no flavor in doing nothing when you have nothing to do; it is like chewing with an empty mouth. Wasting time then becomes an occupation—an exhausting one—and you come to the end of the day as fatigued as a cook who has stirred a pot that contains only air.

Idleness, like kisses, to be sweet must be stolen.

Many years ago—when I was young enough to imagine that my body was a loyal tenant rather than a landlord with opinions—I was taken very ill. I never could see that much was the matter with me beyond a beastly cold, the kind that makes a man feel tragically poetic and yet also resentful that nobody is arranging lilies around him. But it must have been something serious, for the doctor said I ought to have come to him a month earlier, and that if it—whatever it was—had gone on another week he wouldn’t have answered for the consequences.

It is an extraordinary thing that no doctor has ever been called in yet without it turning out that one more day’s delay would have made recovery hopeless. Our medical guide, philosopher, and friend is like the hero in a melodrama: he always arrives precisely—and only just—in the nick of time. Providence has a stethoscope. That is the only explanation.

Well, I was ordered off to Buxton for a month with strict instructions to do nothing whatever the whole time I was there.

“Rest is what you require,” said the doctor. “Perfect rest.”

It sounded delicious. Here, I thought, is a man who understands my complaint. I pictured a glorious month: four weeks of _dolce far niente_ with a tasteful dash of illness—just enough suffering to give it a bouquet, not so much as to spoil the digestion. I should get up late, sip chocolate, and take breakfast in slippers and a dressing-gown, with the serene air of a monk who has made vows of comfort. I should lie in a hammock in the garden and read sentimental novels that end badly, until the book slid from my listless fingers; and then I would gaze into the deep blue sky, watching the clouds go by like white-sailed ships, listening to the birds and the soft rustle of trees—those old rural sounds that used to belong to England before the age began to grind and shriek.

Or, if I became too weak to go out, I should sit propped at an open ground-floor window, looking wasted and interesting, so that all the pretty girls would sigh as they passed. Twice a day I should be wheeled in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to drink the waters—“drinking the waters” sounded fashionable, antique, Queen-Anne-ish, like something done by people who had footmen and kept their illnesses in silver boxes.

Oh, those waters. I knew nothing about them then. After the first three or four mornings, romance surrendered. Sam Weller’s description of their tasting like “warm flat-irons” is only a timid bow in the direction of the truth. They had the peculiar flavor of something that had been boiled in its own disappointment and then served with a moral. If anything could make a sick man get well quickly, it would be the knowledge that he must swallow a glass of that stuff every day until he recovers.

For six consecutive days I drank them neat and they nearly finished what the cold had started. After that, I adopted the sensible plan of taking a stiff brandy-and-water immediately on top of them, like laying a thick slice of buttery bread over a bad memory. It brought immediate relief. Various eminent medical gentlemen have since told me that the alcohol must have entirely counteracted the chalybeate virtues of the spring. I am delighted to hear it. It is always pleasant to learn, after the event, that one has been right.

But the waters were only a small portion of the torture. That month at Buxton—gouty, consumptive Buxton, with its air of having been designed as a waiting-room for ailments—was, without exception, the most miserable month I have ever spent. I did, for the best part of it, exactly what I had been ordered: nothing. I mooned about the house and garden and went out for two hours a day in a Bath chair.

The Bath chair, at first, broke the monotony. There is more excitement in Bath-chairing than an outsider imagines. A continual sense of peril accompanies the occupant. He is convinced every minute the entire contraption is going to topple; and the conviction becomes vivid whenever a ditch appears or you approach a stretch of newly macadamized road—those loose stones that rattle like cheap applause. Every passing vehicle he expects will run into him; and he never goes up or down a hill without immediately calculating his chances, supposing—an assumption that seems dreadfully probable—that the weak-kneed controller of his destiny should let go.

Even that exhilaration grew stale. The ennui became unbearable; I felt my mind giving way under it. My mind is not a robust instrument, and I judged it unwise to strain it. Somewhere about the twentieth morning I got up early, ate a good breakfast—solid, honest work on toast and whatever else a hotel believes will keep a man quiet—and walked straight off to Hayfield at the foot of Kinder Scout.

It is a pleasant, busy little town reached through a lovely valley, the sort of valley where you can still hear water doing what water is meant to do instead of being forced through iron throats, and where the wind speaks without having to compete with machinery. And it contained two sweetly pretty women—at least they were sweetly pretty then. One passed me on the bridge and, I think, smiled; the other stood in an open doorway making an unremunerative investment of kisses on a red-faced baby. It is years ago, and I dare say they have both grown stout and snappish since—time has a way of turning smiles into opinions.

On the way back I saw an old man breaking stones. The sound of it—stone on stone, regular and sharp—did something to me. It wasn’t the kind of noise that sets the nerves on edge like the city’s metallic din; it had a human rhythm, like bread being chopped or herbs being pounded, labor that belongs to hands. It roused such a longing in me to use my arms that I offered him a drink if he would let me take his place.

He was a kindly old man and humored me. I went for those stones with the accumulated energy of three weeks and did more work in half an hour than he had done all day. It did not make him jealous. Old age has its own untranslatable dialect of patience; it does not envy the young their bursts of foolish strength any more than a baker envies a child his ability to eat sugar with a spoon.

Having taken the plunge, I went further into dissipation. I began going out for a long walk every morning, and every evening I listened to the band in the pavilion. The band played, and the sound wandered through the air, trying to make the town feel cheerful by sheer brass. But the days still passed slowly, as if time itself had been put on a restricted diet. And when at last the final day came and I was being whirled away from Buxton to London, I was heartily glad.

We rushed through Hendon in the evening. I looked out, and the lurid glare hanging over the great city seemed to warm my heart like the sight of a kitchen fire after too long among cold plates. Later, when my cab rattled out of St. Pancras, the old familiar roar swelling up around me—wheels, voices, hoofs, a thousand restless echoes—sounded the sweetest music I had heard for many a long day. It was industrial, it was brutal, it was the voice of work; and after Buxton’s commanded silence it felt like being allowed to breathe again.

I certainly did not enjoy that month’s idling. I like idling when I ought not to be idling; not when it is the only thing I have to do. That is my pig-headed nature. The moment I most enjoy standing with my back to the fire, calculating how much I owe, is when my desk is heaped highest with letters that must be answered by the next post. The moment I most enjoy lingering over dinner is precisely when I have a heavy evening’s work before me—then I can taste each mouthful as if it were stolen, each sip of wine as if it were a small rebellion. And if, for any urgent reason, I ought to be up particularly early, it is then—more than at any other time—that I love to lie an extra half-hour in bed.

How delicious it is to turn over and go to sleep again—“just for five minutes.”

Is there any human being, I wonder—besides the hero of a Sunday-school “tale for boys”—who ever gets up willingly? There are men to whom getting up at the proper time is an utter impossibility. If eight o’clock is when they must rise, then they lie till half-past. If circumstances change and half-past eight becomes early enough, then it is nine before they can move. They are like the statesman of whom it was said that he was always punctually half an hour late.

They try all schemes. They buy alarm-clocks—artful contrivances that go off at the wrong time and alarm the wrong people. They instruct Sarah Jane to knock and call them, and Sarah Jane knocks and calls, and they grunt back “Awri,” in a dialect known only to bedclothes, and then go comfortably to sleep again. I knew one man who would actually get out and have a cold bath; and even that was useless, for afterward he jumped into bed again to warm himself, like a lobster returning to the pot out of gratitude.

For my own part, I believe I could keep out of bed well enough if once I got out. It is the wrenching away of the head from the pillow that is hard. No amount of overnight determination makes it easier. I say to myself after wasting the whole evening, “Well, I won’t do any more work tonight; I’ll get up early tomorrow morning,” and I am thoroughly resolved—then. In the morning I am less enthusiastic and reflect that it would have been much better if I had sat up last night. And then there is the trouble of dressing, and the more one thinks about dressing the more one wants to put it off. Buttons are little tyrants. Boots are moralists.

It is a strange thing, this bed—this mimic grave—where we stretch tired limbs and sink away so quietly into silence and rest. “O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head,” as sang poor Hood—you are a kind old nurse to us fretful boys and girls. Clever and foolish, naughty and good, you take us all in your motherly lap and hush our wayward crying. The strong man full of care, the sick man full of pain, the little maiden sobbing for her faithless lover—like children we lay our aching heads on your white bosom and you soothe us off to by-by. Our trouble is sore indeed when you turn away and will not comfort us.

How long the dawn seems coming when we cannot sleep. Those hideous nights when we toss and turn in fever and pain, lying like living men among the dead, staring into the dark hours that drift so slowly between us and the light. And those still more hideous nights when we sit by another in pain, when the low fire startles us now and then with a falling cinder, and the tick of the clock seems a hammer beating out the life we are watching. In such moments you learn that sound is not merely sound: it is a climate. A gentle clock is tolerable; an obstinate one can become a form of tyranny. The world talks about silence as if it were always peace, but there is a silence that screams, and there are noises—honest, human noises—that soothe. One cannot translate these experiences into any neat vocabulary; the words are too blunt. The dominant language always tries to label everything—_rest_, _health_, _convalescence_—as though the same syllables will fit every throat. They do not. Each body speaks its own dialect, and illness has idioms no dictionary admits.

But enough of beds and bedrooms. I have kept to them too long, even for an idle fellow. Let us come out and have a smoke. That wastes time just as well and does not look so bad.

Tobacco has been a blessing to us idlers. What the civil-service clerk before Sir Walter’s time found to occupy his mind with is hard to imagine. I attribute the quarrelsome nature of young men in the Middle Ages entirely to the lack of the soothing weed. They had no work to do and could not smoke; consequently they were forever fighting and rowing. If, by some extraordinary chance, there was no war going, they got up a deadly family feud with the next-door neighbor. If, in spite of this, they still had a few spare moments, they occupied them with discussions as to whose sweetheart was the best-looking—the arguments employed being battle-axes, clubs, and similar aids to criticism. Questions of taste were soon settled in those days.

When a twelfth-century youth fell in love he did not take three paces backward, gaze into her eyes, and tell her she was too beautiful to live. He said he would step outside and see about it. And if, when he got out, he met a man and broke his head—the other man’s head, I mean—then that proved his girl was a pretty girl. But if the other fellow broke _his_ head—look here: if A broke B’s head, then A’s girl was pretty; but if B broke A’s head, then A’s girl wasn’t pretty but B’s girl was. That was their method of conducting art criticism.

Nowadays we light a pipe and let the girls fight it out among themselves. They do it very well. They are getting to do all our work. They are doctors, barristers, artists. They manage theatres, promote swindles, edit newspapers. I look forward to the time when we men shall have nothing to do but lie in bed till twelve, read two novels a day, have nice little five-o’clock teas all to ourselves, and tax our brains with nothing more trying than discussions upon the latest patterns in trousers, and arguments as to what Mr. Jones’s coat was made of and whether it fitted him.

It is a glorious prospect—for idle fellows.
