It was the tenth hour of our day when we pushed the raft off from shore, and the paddles moved light and balanced in the carved rests I’d rigged earlier, just as I’d hoped. The craft cut through the smooth dark water with barely any effort, the Maid taking the fore paddle and I the stern, our steady, synchronized strokes carrying us at a pace almost as fast as we could walk the rough, broken shoreline of that sunken, light-dappled land.

By the twelfth hour we stopped to eat and drink, then took up our easy labor again. When we settled into the rhythm of the paddles, we scarcely noticed we moved at all, only the faint gentle rock of the raft under our feet. The hours slipped by soft and unrushed, our voices low as we spoke of small, dear things, the Maid glancing back over her shoulder often to smile at me with warm love, her lips curved in a little teasing pout that made me nearly drop my paddle every time to go to her, till she’d shake her head, laughing soft and bidding me keep to my work.

When the eighteenth hour arrived, we pulled our paddles aboard. The Maid spread our thick cloak out as a bed, we ate our rations, and we fell into a sound, happy slumber almost instantly, as if the soft lull of the water had rocked us both straight to sleep.

Eight full hours later we woke at the exact same moment, disoriented for a breath, not remembering at first where we were, before the full weight of our safe, shared journey settled over us. We laughed face to face, giddy with the joy of waking to each other, kissed long and slow, washed the grime of the voyage off in the cool sea water, and settled to our breakfast before shoving off to paddle onward. For the rest of that day we drifted along the coast, peaceful, content, no shadow of trouble touching us.

The entire voyage stretched across four full twenty-four hour days. We made no great haste, moved slow and easy, so I might take all the time I needed to rebuild my lost strength. Nothing untoward befell us in all that time, save once when we spotted a huge, lumbering beast heave itself up out of the sea far off on the shore, where it browsed slow on the thick herbage as if it belonged there. We were far too distant to make out its features clearly, but it filled us with no dread, only quiet relief we were not close enough to cross its path. It had no trace of that wrong, monstrous aura that clung to the abominations of the Night Land, it felt entirely natural, as if we had stumbled back into the earliest dawn of the world. That old saying about extremes meeting ran through my head then: the most ancient of natural life coexisting with the twisted, cursed half-human horrors of the land beyond the gorge. I did not waste long on the thought, only noted that the core spark of humanity could never be unmade, no matter how much the flesh might be modified by circumstance or cruel, twisted breeding — a truth that filled me with a quiet, unshakable hope for the bright, uplifted, holy world we had caught a glimpse of once, when we had stood pure together.

The only other thing of note we passed was a great Fire-Hill that rose straight out of the sea, so close we skirted its very edge. The water boiled hot around parts of its base, and two dozen great jets of steam and superheated gas shot hundreds of feet up into the dark air, their roar loud enough to reach us across the intervening water. Low, guttural grunts rumbled up from the depths of the sea at its foot, the sound of gasses fighting to escape the crust of the world below. We stared back at that seething hill of raw geologic power long after it had shrunk to a faint glowing dot behind us, aware for the first time of the titanic, sleeping energy coiled under every inch of that sunken land.

Beyond that, our path lay clear. We threaded the narrow, winding water passages that split the great inland sea into smaller, sheltered pools, and I pointed out the two spots where I had camped alone, starving and desperate, on my first journey through that sunken country. The Maid leaned in rapt, pestering me for every small, trivial detail of those lonely nights, her eyes bright with wonder.

Four full days to the hour from when we had pushed off, we beached the raft on a flat stretch of shore, where the land sloped upward to the dark mouth of the First Gorge I had traveled so long before. We dragged the craft up out of the tide line, and stood quiet for a moment, wondering if any soul would ever stumble across it for all the rest of eternity. The Maid cut a small, smooth splinter of its frame free, tucking it into the pocket of her tunic as a memento.

We laid all our gear out on the sand, and the Maid helped me strap back into my heavy armor, settle the scrip and the storage pouch firm on my back, and heft the Diskos into my hand, its weight familiar and reassuring. She hefted her own now much smaller pack, loosened her hunting knife in her belt, ready to walk. She knelt then, and pressed a soft kiss to the planks of the raft, all her old memories of the little island where she had nursed me back from the edge of death crowding up in her chest, one last gentle goodbye to that first chapter of our new life. I stooped to lift her to her feet, tucking her close against my side, and we set off, climbing the slow upward slope toward the dark mouth of the great gorge.

Miles off to our right loomed that unimaginably vast mountain, its four great fire vents glowing red far up near its peak, endless slopes of ash piled up below them that had been spewed out into the dark night for uncounted eons. The Maid stared at it for ages, awestruck, and I did not rush her — no human that had ever lived had seen a sight so grand, so utterly huge, as that.

At last we reached the high rim of the gorge, and paused at the sharp left bend where the path vanished into deeper, unlit gloom. We turned once more to look back over the whole sunken country of seas, glowing soft red far below us, spread out under the endless night of the world. We knew in that quiet moment we might well be the very last two olden humans ever to look on that lost land. I wondered, idly, if the brutish Humpt Men might one day, in some unimaginably distant eternity, nurture that soft, unbreakable human spirit hidden under their rough, crude flesh. For long minutes we stood silent, listening to the distant, low mutter of the great fire mountains, the faint hum of life drifting across the sunken land, only a few short paces away from the dead, absolute silence of the gorge that would lead us back to the Night Land. The Maid clung tight to my arm, silent tears rolling down her face, her heart heavy with equal parts sorrow and joy, already imagining the stories she would one day tell our children of this strange, wondrous land that no other soul would ever see.

We turned at last, her hand slipping firm into mine, and rounded the great sharp corner of the gorge, stumbling slow into the thick gloom.

We walked steady for sixteen hours, no trouble touching us, nothing but the deep dark to make us cautious, until we realized we had been awake for twenty-six hours straight — a foolish mistake, when I still needed every scrap of my full strength for the dangers that waited ahead. We found a sheltered nook between two great boulders, ate our rations, and consulted the notes I had scrawled on the pages of my journal to map our old outward path. We settled on a strict, unbreakable routine: sixteen hours of travel a day, eight full hours of unbroken sleep, no exceptions. It took us five full days to traverse the entire length of the great gorge. We cheered up every time we stepped out of the dark into the faint, dancing red light of a burning gas vent, holding our breath through pockets of choking, foul fumes that lingered in the rock crevices. My strength built back steadily, until I felt almost whole again. The Maid never once let me carry her, stepping light and sure over the broken rocks, growing used to the endless, steady march. At every camp spot I had rested at on my first lonely journey, she would stop and stand for a quiet moment exactly where I had once laid, heartbroken and certain I would never see her again, too tender with her affection for words.

She asked every few hours how much farther we had to the Night Land, when we would see the Mighty Redoubt, her excitement bright and thrumming, and my own heart beat just as fast as hers, every step making me more desperate to get her behind those walls, safe from all the monsters that roamed the dark. The gorge was utterly dead save for the occasional low moan or high thin whistle of a burning gas jet, the sound echoing for miles across the empty rock, making the vast loneliness of the place settle heavy in our bones. I felt a quiet, warm pride in my chest when I saw the way she looked at me, knowing she understood how much I had suffered that lonely journey out to find her, and that her love for me grew deeper with every step.

On the fifth day, around the seventh hour, I began to catch faint, odd sounds that seemed to whisper off the rocks all around us, as if the stone itself was muttering soft secrets. I pulled the Maid instantly tight to my side, drew the Diskos, and we moved forward slow, cautious. We passed three dancing gas fires, their moans mixing with the odd, whispering sounds, before I realized what we were hearing: they were just echoes, bouncing off the close rock faces, drifting all the way from a far greater, louder noise. I recognized it at once: the monstrous piping roar of the Great Gas Fountain I had encountered on my old journey. The Maid leaned forward instantly, bright with excitement — that landmark meant we were nearly out of the gorge, the long stretch of our travels almost at an end.

At first we could not pick the fountain out ahead of us, the dozens of smaller gas fires dotted along the length of the gorge blurring together into a sea of shifting light. But after we had walked a little further, we spotted it: the dark night far ahead flickered in constant shuddering bursts of blue light, as if the very darkness itself was gasping. Slowly that vague shimmer resolved into a vast, leaping pillar of brilliant blue flame, and the piping noise grew, rising into a shifting, thunderous roar that shook the very rocks under our feet. We walked past the last of the small, scattered gas fires, and the whole length of the gorge was lit in wavering, eerie blue light cast off by that giant fountain, so huge it loomed half the height of the gorge walls.

We came to a stop not far from the base of it, half deafened by the roar, two tiny, lonesome figures standing in that vast empty gorge, staring up at that impossible tower of flame. I wrapped my arm around the Maid, tucking her against my chest, and we stood silent for long minutes, unable to speak over the noise. When we turned to look at each other at last, we kissed soft and solemn, lit all over by that strange blue glow. We stared again at the fountain, watching its light spill out for miles across the distant gorge walls, turning the far dark peaks into the silhouette of some lost, long-forgotten world of empty mountains.

We pushed on a mile further to get far enough away that the roar stopped ringing in our ears, and stopped for our rest in a secure hollow among the boulders, the hour already well past the seventeenth. When we woke, we sat quiet a little while longer, watching the great flame dance, ringed round by towering dark rocks that looked like silent, giant sentinels standing guard over it for all eternity. We shouldered our packs, turned toward the utter black of the final steep slope that led up out of the gorge and back into the Night Land, and began the long, endless climb.

Every few steps at first we turned to glance back down the long height of the gorge, at the faint far glimmer of the fountain shuddering far below in the dark, its quaking light spilling through the blackness. Slowly it shrank to nothing behind us, vanished entirely, swallowed by the absolute, unbroken dark of the slope. We stumbled upward for sixteen straight hours, the thick dark wrapping so tight around us we began to feel half unreal, as if we had walked right out of the world we knew into some empty, eternal void.

For eight full days after that we crawled upward on hands and knees, I leading the way, the Diskos loose and ready at my hip. I tore two spare straps from the scrip and storage pouch, tying one end to my belt and the other to the Maid’s, so I would always know exactly where she was, no risk of her slipping away and getting lost in the total dark. We stuck to our routine unflinchingly: sixteen hours of crawling, stops to eat at the sixth and twelfth hours of travel, another meal before we settled to sleep, and one more on waking, with eight full unbroken hours of rest every cycle. Sometimes the endless upward grind wore me thin, sick of groping blind for handholds around boulders and gaping crevices, feeling as if we would never see light, never feel anything but that cold thick dark, for the rest of time. Every time that despair crept over me, I would stop, call soft to the Maid, and pull her into my arms in the pitch black, and we would hold each other tight, drawing all the comfort we could from each other, wrapped in the waiting silence of that endless night.
