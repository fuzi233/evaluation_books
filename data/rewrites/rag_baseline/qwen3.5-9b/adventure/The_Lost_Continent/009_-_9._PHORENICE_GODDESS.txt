The tunnel breathed heat. Not the dry, sterile warmth of a tomb, but the wet, sulfurous exhale of a living lung. Years had passed since the magma had flowed here, turning rock to glass, but the memory of fire clung to the air, thick and choking. I lit a lamp from a niche, the flame trembling against the damp walls, and moved forward. My throat burned, a raw scratch demanding water and herbs I no longer possessed. The packet of simples I'd stolen from Yucatan—the only medicine I owned—had sunk with Dason's fleet. Since washing ashore in Atlantis, survival had demanded speed, not refinement.

The vein branched wildly, a chaotic root system underground. Any other of the Seven Priests, stumbling into this maze without a map, would have suffocated in the blind turns or plummeted into the boiling vents below. But I carried the chart. Twenty years ago, during my initiation as Viceroy, I had memorized the currents of the earth. I knew the angles, the depths, the safe paths.

The journey was arduous. The floor shifted underfoot, uneven and treacherous. Daylight was fading, the sun dipping below the horizon outside, yet I knew I wouldn't reach the city until night was deep. And there was the vow: the Seven Priests cannot emerge from the Under-Vein until the mortal eye sees the end of the line. I tested every exit. Each one was blocked by loiterers, shadows watching the dark. The city hummed with a feverish energy, a hive buzzing with anticipation. I tried the path leading to the royal pyramid, hoping for anonymity, but the area was packed. Five other outlets yielded the same result. The city was agitated, restless.

Finally, I found a temple of a minor deity. Empty. The dust on the floor and the benches testified to abandonment; it hadn't been used in ages. I moved quickly, sweeping the tracks of my boots with a stray broom, sealing my passage behind me. The door was locked—a massive slab of stone guarded by a mechanism known only to the temple's attendants. If everyone else was out celebrating, I was trapped.

Silence reigned within the temple grounds, broken only by the creak of decaying wood. Decay everywhere. Yet, ancient law dictated a watcher must remain in the priests' quarters, located in the roof. I descended the stairs, expecting emptiness. Instead, I found Ro.

He answered my knock, his eyes adjusting slowly to the gloom. He was a man of the old school, stubbornly loyal in a time of betrayal. We had studied together in the Sacred College, though he was always the dullard. Little talent for theology, less for governance, none for warfare. He had risen to Second Priest solely because his smarter colleagues had fled. It was bitter irony that a fool held fast while the wise abandoned the gods.

Ro performed the proper obeisance, his movements stiff. His beard was curled poorly, his robes ill-fitting and unwashed. Sloppy, as always.

"The temple door is shut," he said, his voice flat. "Only I hold the key. You arrive by the secret way, my lord, as one of the Seven. I am your servant."

"Tell me, Ro. What stirs the city?"

"That impious Phorenice has declared herself Goddess," he replied, spitting the word. "She claims she will light the sacrifice with her own divine fire. She intends to do it. She does everything. But I pray the flames consume her instead. This new Empress is the plague of our Clan, Deucalion. The people ignore us; offerings have dried up. And now, with the rebels battering the walls, I might have starved if I hadn't saved a few grains of corn. The cult of the True Gods is leaking out of the land, drying up like a cracked riverbed."

"My brother," I said softly, "it seems the Priests of our Clan grew limp in their duty, allowing this rot to take hold."

"We did our best," Ro muttered, looking away. "We followed the teachings. But if the people won't listen to your sermons, what grip do you have on their souls? Still, the High Gods test our faith severely. Come. Look at my bed."

I saw the shape of a man wrapped in reddened bandages, a grotesque sculpture of flesh and linen.

"This is all that remains of my predecessor," Ro said, his voice tightening. "Yesterday was his turn to lead the weekly sacrifice to the Sun, using the great stones. Faugh! You should have seen him when they dragged him back. Mangled."

"Did the people rise against him?"

"They stayed silent," Ro said, bitterness edging his tone. "Only a handful attended. But our Lord the Sun decided to test his minister harshly. The wood was piled; the offering placed; the procession formed around the altar. Drums beat, trumpets blared, announcing that the prayers would ascend to the heavens. Then, cutting through the ceremony, came the rush of wings. A great bird, featherless, man-eating, larger than any recorded in history, descended from the sky."

"A spear or an arrow would have ended it," I suggested.

"All men learn that as children," Ro snapped. "But priests are unarmed by the rubric. They must trust the High Gods completely during the sacrifice. The beast landed on the pyre, tearing at the offering with beak and claw. My superior, still believing in miracles, leaped onto the altar, armed only with his teeth and bare fists. He expected salvation. Instead, he met leather wings and talons that nearly disemboweled him. The bird brushed him aside like a fly. He lay bleeding on the pavement while the sacrifice was torn apart before the eyes of the crowd. Then the bird flew off to the mountains."

"And the people offered no aid?"

"They screamed that it was a portent," Ro continued. "That the Sun was no god if he couldn't protect his own. Others shouted there was no god left. But a few began to chant that Phorenice represented all the gods now. The cry caught, spreading like wildfire. The great stones rang with her name. Some believed; many joined simply because silence was dangerous. The Empress punishes neglectors severely."

"The Empress is Empress," I stated formally. "Her name commands respect. We do not question her actions."

"I am a priest," Ro insisted, defending his post. "I speak as taught. But whether faith exists anymore... I doubt it. It brings little reward. No offerings for five months. Without my stored grain, I would have starved. And this incident won't reignite the cold hearts of our votaries."

"When did it happen?"

"Twenty hours ago. Today, Phorenice performs the sacrifice herself. That is the source of the commotion. The city prepares for her pageant."

"Open the doors," I commanded. "I must see this."

"It is not for me to advise a member of the Seven," Ro hesitated.

"It is not."

"But they say the Empress dislikes your absence," he murmured, fear creeping into his voice. "Do not let harm come to you, Deucalion."

"The future rests with the Highest Gods, Ro. They will judge our fate in their infinite wisdom, even if you have lost yours. Now, repay my debt with passage through the doors. Plague on you, man, stop reaching for coins. I possess none in the world."

He muttered about "habit" as he moved to the levers, pushing the massive stone aside. I passed through, leaving him behind. I could never truly like Ro.

Outside, a motley throng surged down the street, all moving in the same direction. Given the rumors, I knew they sought the great stone circle of the Sun. It grieved me to witness such profanation of a sanctuary built over millennia, standing firm against earthquakes and storms, only to be desecrated today. A darker thought crossed my mind: Was the Great Lord above guiding this woman to her doom? Would He unleash final vengeance when she completed her sacrilege?

The crowd pressed on, oblivious to the deeper currents beneath the spectacle. From the city walls, the roar of the rebel assault echoed clearly—rams battering timber, fire-darts streaking the sky—but no one listened. The townsfolk were calloused to siege warfare. Hunger, desperation, and adrenaline had sharpened their nerves to a point where only a direct storm could break their trance.

Every person carried a weapon. The naked wielded scavenged tools hoping to rob someone stronger; the armed prepared to fight for their possessions. Trust had evaporated, washed away like mud from feet in a flood. Civilization had receded, leaving them in a primal state akin to the Europeans of old legends. It was a grim testament to Phorenice's rule.

The crowd absorbed me without question. We wound through streets once pristine, now choked with filth and mud. Bones littered the ground, the remains of those who had died of starvation in recent years, crushed under our boots. Rising from this squalor were pyramids and palaces of breathtaking splendor, monuments to a vanished golden age. A jarring contradiction.

Eventually, we reached the central plaza, the only space Phorenice had spared from her construction frenzy. We stood on the secular ground surrounding the oldest, grandest temple of all.
