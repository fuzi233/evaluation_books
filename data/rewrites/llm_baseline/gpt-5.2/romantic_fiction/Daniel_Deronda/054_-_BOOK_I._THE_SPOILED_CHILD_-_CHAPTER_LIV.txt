“The unwilling brain feigns often what it would not.” The line has the sound of oars in black water: a steady dip and lift that keeps time with thoughts one would rather not hear. And there are lives in which the mind, like a galley-slave, pulls at its own chain—pulls until the air is full of the creak of strained timber and the low slap of consequences against the hull.

There was once, in an older story, a woman carried away to the Maremma, to the flats where the very ground seems to breathe fever. Her removal was meant for punishment and became, by the strange justice of the elements, a kind of deliverance: the marsh that was to imprison her also took her husband’s triumph from him. That figure stands in an Italian poem asking only to be remembered kindly. But pity grows finer by comparison; and if one turns from that far-off legend to the young English wife now afloat on the Mediterranean, one feels how much crueller it is to be kept, not in a visible prison of stone and swamp, but in the invisible net of one’s own consent—knots tied by one’s own hand.

Grandcourt had brought Gwendolen yachting with no wish to cast her off. He wished, rather, to possess her more completely: to have her beyond the reach of drawing-rooms, beyond the reach of sudden visits and arranged encounters, beyond the reach of the one man whose name, once spoken in her mind, made her heart beat as if it had found its native measure. He liked the sea for himself—the dreamy absolutism of it, the way hours could be smoked away with nothing demanded but the turning of a glass and the giving of a brief order. There was no modern clatter of carriages, no fashionable music turned into a commodity, no thickened air of other people’s voices. The world fell silent except for wind and cordage and the obedient footfalls of men paid to be quiet. For a nature like his, that silence was not peace but power.

His reasons, stated plainly, did not sound like wickedness. He suspected opposition in her, a growing inward stiffening against his will; and what he would have named “jealousy” in another man he allowed himself only as a practical resolve—to end the “foolery” he believed had been planned with Deronda, and which he had interrupted in London as one snaps a thread between finger and thumb.

And, had he defended himself, he might have made the defense plausible. The marriage was a contract. All the visible advantages had been given to her: the rank, the wealth, the handsome provision for her mother—more, indeed, than she had dared to bargain for. He had paid; he expected the purchased thing.

Gwendolen knew it too. She could not sincerely take refuge in the notion that she had meant—without saying so—to rule. She had been indulged, spoilt, trained by the easy admiration of others into believing her whims were laws; yet she had, under all that, a root of conscience. It was bitter to feel it alive now, like a seed that has sprouted in darkness and hurts as it forces its way.

Set her, then, on that small moving island of planks, with the Mediterranean a blue division between her and the world she knew. Set her within the domain of the husband to whom she felt she had sold herself—and sold not only her youth and her smile, but her truthfulness, her sense of justice, the very inward voice that says No and means it. Those faculties seemed collared and dragged, compelled to look on, compelled to be silent.

And what, outwardly, had she to complain of? The yacht was a pretty toy, as if devised by those who think beauty can hush any pain. The cabin was fitted to perfection: cedar breathed its warm, clean resinous scent from panels and drawers; silk hangings held a faint sweetness like dried rose-leaves hidden among linen; cushions yielded with an inviting softness, and mirrors made the space seem larger, multiplying her own face back at her—her own eyes returned again and again, as if the glass had become an accomplice to her self-watching. The crew were picturesque, attentive, trained to make their presence part of the pleasure; one had ringlets, and bronze skin, and the show of fine teeth—an elegance even in service. Mr. Lush was gone back to England as soon as he had seen the yacht fairly launched into its private tyranny. The sea did not make Gwendolen ill, at least not often; and there were moments when the logic of rope and sail, the forecast of necessary adjustments, might have gratified her old relish for command—command without responsibility, imagination playing at rule.

The weather was fine. They coasted southward where cracked clay on the shore, seamed by heat and rain, can glow as if set with gems under purple shadows; where sky and water answer each other in blues so deep they seem to promise that sorrow has been outgrown, as childhood outgrows a fright. The air had salt in it, sharp and clean; it entered the lungs like a draught meant to revive.

But there are hungers no landscape feeds. There are paradises that, for a soul in moral revolt, become only perfumed prisons. The very ease oppresses; the sweet smell of cedar turns heavy, as if it had absorbed sighs. In the evening, when Gwendolen lay throned on cushions and watched the sea-sky soften into that tender glory which makes lovers believe the world was made only to hold their joy, she was not thinking of beauty. She was thinking—hoping—that Grandcourt would not pause near her; would not look at her; would not speak. And somewhere, under a smoky city sky, a woman counting the price of eggs for her dinner might be listening for a step that meant safety and happiness; somewhere, a couple might be bending cheek to cheek over work, counting their earnings with the quiet confidence of companionship—while Gwendolen, surrounded by luxuries, could have envied the homely clink of a pan, the familiar crackle of a hearth, the honest noise of a kitchen where speech is free and affection has a dialect.

Grandcourt did not conceive what was happening in her. He understood that she did not love him; but why should she? Love, to him, was not a necessary condition of possession. What lay entirely beyond him was the idea that she could feel repulsion toward him—personal repulsion, deeper than dislike, a moral nausea. He had been critical all his life; he knew what it was to be disgusted by other people—by their smirks, their vulgarities, their “odious familiarities,” their costumes and scents and foolish efforts to please. He understood her repulsion for Lush because it matched his own. But that his own person—Henleigh Grandcourt—could be the object of such shrinking loathing, he could not imagine. He had too long taken his own attractiveness for granted; too long set himself in flattering contrast to other men whom he assumed must be revolting. Moral repulsion—the resentment that makes even beauty hateful because it gives hateful things a kind of advantage—was as inconceivable to him as the existence of a language he could not buy.

Outwardly, nothing betrayed them. No observer could have found scandal—not the foreign maid who had escaped sea-sickness, not the valet with his seasoned eyes, not the deck-hands who saw in the pair a model of high-bred composure. Their companionship was chiefly silence—silence so well-bred it seemed an ornament. Grandcourt had no playful talk to invite refusal, no pleasantries to provoke quarrel. He could be punctiliously polite, arranging an extra wrap over her shoulders when the wind sharpened, handing her any object he noticed she wanted. She, with pride enough to hate vulgarity more than pain, could not accept or reject such politeness rudely.

He would put up his telescope and say, “There’s a plantation of sugar-canes at the foot of that rock; should you like to look?”

“Yes, please,” she would answer, forcing herself to be interested in sugar-canes—green spears rising by the shore, promise of sweetness made into commodity—anything outside the circle of her own misery.

Then he would pace and smoke, the soft scrape of his shoe and the faint click of his ring against the rail marking time like a metronome. Sometimes he would point at a sail on the horizon. At last he would sit and look at her with that narrow, immovable gaze, as if she were part of the yacht’s equipment—a chosen object, a possession that completed the elegance of his floating despotism. And she, feeling the look like a cold finger, would exert all her ingenuity not to meet his eyes.

At dinner he would remark that the fruit was getting stale and they must put in somewhere for more; or, seeing she did not drink wine, would ask if she preferred another kind. There was always something for a lady to answer suitably. And besides, quarrelling with him was impossible. One might as well argue with a coiled serpent displayed as an ornament: its stillness is not peace but readiness, and you cannot make it ashamed.

He took intense satisfaction in having her captive in this small world. It gave him a kind of royal publicity without society: everything familiar was swept away; every person, paid or bound, did what was expected, whatever private protest might be choking them. The protest, kept private, sharpened the flavor of command for him the way a bitter herb makes a rich dish more piquant.

To Gwendolen, who in her girlhood had scarcely glimpsed heroism except as a theatrical attitude, the medium thrust before her everywhere was this husband—this relation that turned the world into a distorted glass. Those closest to us are often our interpreters of life; and the dull, trivial sentences of some companion, their petty standards, their loveless ennui, can become for another a theory of existence—like looking through yellow, wavy panes that warp forms and make color an affliction. Grandcourt’s presence did that to her: it stained the far horizon, so that even the sea’s clean music—the wash against the planks, the sighing of rope, the brief cries of gulls—could not be heard as comfort, only as part of an enormous silence where her own thoughts sounded too loud.

Some wives are soothed by the hope of motherhood. For Gwendolen, the idea came not as deliverance but as a horror. To desire a child for herself would have been, she felt, consenting to the completion of her own wrong. She dreaded it, as one dreads an additional chain.

In those fluctuating stages of despair, hope came in strange forms—often, in the imagined benignity of accident. A sudden fall, a storm, a blow from the rigging: any event that would end this life without her choosing to end it. To dwell on accident was a refuge from darker temptation.

And temptation did not come as a neat thought she could dismiss. It came like an illness of the mind, a fevered hand reaching out. Hatred, especially hatred rooted in fear, grows in ways onlookers cannot measure by outward cause. It feeds inwardly, like a seed drawing nourishment from the very substance of the soul, until all currents run toward it. In Gwendolen’s secrecy there were “hidden rites” of vengeance—imagined annihilations, visions that rose unbidden and would not be spoken. Yet they did not soothe; they terrified her, because with the dread of Grandcourt had grown the dread of herself. She began to fear not only what he could do, but what she might do, impulsively, as if in a dream—waking at once to find effects real though the images had been false: a death under her hands; daylight instead of darkness; guilt instead of freedom; and forever after a white dead face that she fled and could not escape.

She could not separate any imagined act from the image of Deronda’s judgment. He had become, in her mind, the listener to whom she could not lie—not because he demanded confession, but because her better self had awakened under his regard. No flattery from him, no indulgence, no dependence she could seize on to soften his power: his very restraint had made his influence more binding. Whatever remedy existed seemed to lie in him, or in what she felt toward him; and yet that feeling itself was a torment, because it made her more conscious of what she had become. He stood in her imagination like an angel with terrible brows: not a comforter who would excuse, but one before whom she must be truthful or be nothing.

She remembered his words as one remembers a melody that haunts the ear even in sleep:

“Turn your fear into a safeguard. Keep your dread fixed on the idea of increasing your remorse… Take your fear as a safeguard. It is like quickness of hearing. It may make consequences passionately present to you.”

Yes—her fear was like quickness of hearing. In the great hush of the sea, every inward footstep sounded distinct. Temptation and dread met in her as two pale phantoms, staring, each seeing itself in the other and blocking the way. And behind them, her fuller self looked on and sobbed—sobbing without words, because there are feelings that lose their meaning when translated into the common coin of speech.

Often, in the night, inarticulate prayers—no more shaped than a cry—went out of her into the vastness. The silence around was broken only by Grandcourt’s breathing, the soft plash against the hull, the creaking of masts—sounds that in another life might have been lullaby, but now seemed to measure time in a sentence she must endure. And if she thought of definite help, it took the form of Deronda’s presence: his voice, his guidance, his sympathy that did not cheapen her by pretending her wrong was small. Sometimes, after a fierce, white-lipped temptation had visited her like a demon with murdering fingers, the better moment came: she would lie with wide-open eyes, tears rising—tears that felt like a blessing because they proved she was not all stone—and the thought would shape itself simply: I will not mind—if I can keep from getting wicked.

Days passed: light breezes about the Balearic Isles, then Sardinia, then a gentle persuasion northward toward Corsica. The routine of meals, the laying of the table, the opening of fruit, the tasting of wine offered and refused, the salt on the lips, the sun-warmed smell of deck-planks—these domestic details of floating life might have made a little idyll. But to Gwendolen the gentleness became nightmare, because it left her alone with herself. Even the sea’s music—so pure of the industrial roar that makes modern art a thin performance against machinery—could not cleanse her inward noise.

“How long are we to be yachting?” she ventured one day, after they had touched at Ajaccio and the simple act of going ashore—hearing other voices, other steps, other languages—had given her a moment’s relief, as if her mind had stepped into a different acoustical air.

“What else should we do?” Grandcourt answered. “I’m not tired of it. I don’t see why we shouldn’t stay out any length of time. There’s less to bore one in this way. And where would you go to? I’m sick of foreign places. And we shall have enough of Ryelands. Would you rather be at Ryelands?”

“Oh, no,” she said, indifferently—because every place, once she imagined herself in it with him, became the same prison, only with a different view.

“I only wondered how long you would like this.”

“I like yachting longer than anything else,” he said. “And I had none last year. I suppose you are beginning to tire of it. Women are so confoundedly whimsical. They expect everything to give way to them.”

“Oh, dear, no!” She let her scorn out in a flute-like tone—thin, high, controlled. “I never expect you to give way.”

“Why should I?” he said, in that inward voice of his, and looked at her while choosing an orange, as if even the fruit must be selected with the calmness of dominion.

She forced herself to imagine yachting stretched into an endless line she could not see the end of. But the next day a squall came in the night—“the devil’s own work,” Grandcourt called it—and for the first time the sea made her ill, shaking her as if to loosen her from herself.

In the morning he came down to her. She lay very white amid white drapery, her throat dry with that bitter taste which sickness leaves, as if the body itself had turned against all sweetness.

“There’s been the devil’s own work in the night,” he said. “The skipper says we shall have to stay at Genoa for a week while things are set right.”

“Do you mind that?” she asked. Even her languor could not prevent a faint lift of hope.

“I should think so. Who wants to be broiling at Genoa?”

“It will be a change,” she said, incautiously, because the word change sounded like water to a thirsty mouth.

“I don’t want any change. Besides, the place is intolerable; and one can’t move along the roads. I shall go out in a boat, as I used to do, and manage it myself. One can get a few hours every day in that way instead of striving in a damnable hotel.”

A few hours every day—alone. The phrase fell into her mind like the first smell of bread to a starving person: not because it was luxury, but because it was life. Instantly her imagination, wild and contradictory, began to play with freedom—running away, which she had so often argued down as a worse evil than endurance, now returning with new reasons; and, alongside it, that other visionary relief—the lawful wish that something outside her will might break the prison wall for her, as a night wind might blow down stones and save a captive from desperate deeds.

Expectation revived her. She moved and spoke with a cheerfulness that was almost alacrity; her senses, long dulled by inward dread, sharpened as if a door had opened. The evening air smelled of salt and distant land; the shore-breath carried hints—tar, citrus, smoke from kitchens—like fragments of other lives. She watched through the dimming lights to the moon’s sinking with less of her usual awe, even with a vague impression that the mighty frame of things might be preparing rescue for her. Hope, after long starvation, returned like the first honest hunger to a languishing patient.

She was waked next morning by the anchor dropping in Genoa’s harbor—a heavy chain running out with a harsh, metallic music that seemed to bind the yacht to earth. She woke from a dream strangely mixed: she was escaping over the Mont Cenis, and found it warmer even in moonlight on the snow—till suddenly she met Deronda, and he told her to go back.

Within an hour or two she met him.

Not on a mountain pass, but on the palatial staircase of the Italia, where she felt warm in her light woollen dress and straw hat, and Grandcourt stood by her side. The hotel air had its own mingled smells—polished stone holding coolness, flowers too lavishly arranged, coffee rising from below with that roasted bitterness that promises comfort but cannot give it to everyone. There were voices in the distance—Italian syllables running together like poured oil, English consonants striking sharper—each language carrying its own memory and power, each making its own kind of world in the ear.

Deronda started—surprise showing itself before he could recover the usual composure. He lifted his hat and passed on. The moment did not allow more; and the circumstances of their last parting made him doubtful whether Grandcourt would even permit a civil glance.

Grandcourt, on seeing Deronda there—Deronda, in Genoa of all places—felt at once the hot stir of suspicion. He tried immediately to conceive what arrangement could have brought this about: what message, what letter sent from Marseilles or Barcelona, what preconcerted flight of a man who seemed able to perch anywhere at will. Cool-headed as he was, he saw the improbabilities; he did not, even in anger, commit himself to inventions others might call preposterous. Yet Deronda’s presence remained—disgusting, insoluble. And that was enough.

A man out of temper does not wait for proofs before feeling the world in conspiracy. He kicks the dog because the rain falls. Grandcourt felt toward Gwendolen and Deronda as if he knew them to be leagued against him; and this meeting at Genoa seemed an event in league with them. What he took to be certain—and in this he touched truth—was that Gwendolen now counted on an interview whenever his back was turned.

When they were in their rooms, he sat over his coffee at an angle that allowed him to watch her. He could read her as far as his narrow alphabet allowed. He discerned something—some fresh ease in movement and speech, some peculiar meaning in her eyes as they fell on indifferent things—that he interpreted as secret delight. Her troubles had not marred her beauty. Mrs. Grandcourt was handsomer than Gwendolen Harleth: suffering had given variety to her expression, new play to features, new attitudes in repose and movement; she had gained that nameless something which sometimes makes a woman more interesting after marriage—less sure that the world will answer to her opinion, yet less shy, more deeply human.

This morning the voyage’s supposed benefits seemed suddenly to show themselves as elasticity. When she rose from the table and lifted her two heavily jewelled hands to either side of her neck—a gesture habitual with her—she could not conceal the look of expectation that makes the present bearable, as a man becomes more amiable when he knows he will soon go out. Grandcourt, with an instinct as narrowly correct as a dog’s, saw those signs and gave them one meaning: she was glad—glad because Deronda was near. He did not imagine what else might be in that glow: the desperate gratitude for a single sound in the world that spoke to her in a dialect of conscience, not in the cold dominant lexicon of possession.
