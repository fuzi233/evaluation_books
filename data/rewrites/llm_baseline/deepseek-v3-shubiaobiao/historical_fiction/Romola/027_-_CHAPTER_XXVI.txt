By evening, Florence exhaled a collective sigh of relief—the grand entrance of the new Charlemagne was finally over. For those perched on rooftops, the spectacle had been magnificent: the relentless march of drums, the blare of trumpets, the thunder of hooves along the Pisan road, all woven into the fevered pealing of church bells. No sun gilded the banners, the spears, the plumed helmets, or the silken surcoats, but neither did dust obscure them. The absence of glare made the procession clearer—the towering Scotch archers, the hulking Swiss halberdiers, the nimble Gascons, the cavalry gleaming like figures from a chivalric romance. It was a comfort to believe such force would only be turned against the enemies of God.

Yet for the Signoria, stationed at the gates to deliver their carefully prepared welcome, the grandeur had been marred by mishap. Rain had cut short their Latin oration, reducing them to fumbling French. In the chaos, Tito Melema, ever the poised servant of opportunity, stepped forward. With effortless grace, he offered the king a few flawless words, then melted back into the crowd, letting others claim the credit.

The rest of the day had unfolded with similar imperfection. The Florentine procession, meant to meet the king at the Cathedral, found its path blocked and detoured through back streets. The monarch himself, beneath his jeweled canopy, looked less like Charlemagne and more like a hastily carved grotesque—his reptilian mouth, his exaggerated nose, his spindly leg resting on pearls and cloth of gold. But magnificence, as the old diarists sighed, was in the eye of the beholder. The canopy was torn to shreds by the crowd (as tradition demanded), the king kissed the altar, and by nightfall, the soldiers camped in the Prato, the nobles dispersed to palazzi, and the streets blazed with lamps, turning night to day.

Tito moved through the illuminated thoroughfares, his mind churning. The day had been a gauntlet of hidden anxieties. Now, slipping away from the revelry, he sought clarity. If only he had recognized Baldassarre sooner—if only he had not frozen. But regret was useless. The path was set. Survival demanded cold precision.

As he turned onto the Via dei Benci, the clang of an anvil cut through the night. The workshop of Niccolò Caparra stood ahead, its loggia framed by stone columns, the smith’s massive silhouette pounding rhythmically against the forge’s glow. Tito paused, struck by an idea.

Niccolò noticed him only after shaping an axe-head to perfection. “Business, Messer Tito?” he grunted.

“Indeed. That fine coat of mail I saw here—I need it.”

Niccolò’s eyes narrowed. “For whom?”

“Myself.”

The smith snorted. “Twenty florins. And only because it’s second-hand.”

Tito counted the coins. Niccolò handed over the armor, its links whispering like a secret. “It’ll stop a blade,” he muttered. “But wearing it? That’s carrying fear with you.”

Tito smiled thinly. “Scholars are cowards, Niccolò. Our pens don’t thicken our arms like your hammer does.”

Folding the mail beneath his cloak, he vanished into the night.
