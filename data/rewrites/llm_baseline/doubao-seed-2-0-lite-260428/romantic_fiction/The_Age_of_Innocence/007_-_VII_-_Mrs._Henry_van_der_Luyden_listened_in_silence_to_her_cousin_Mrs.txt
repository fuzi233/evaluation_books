Mrs. Henry van der Luyden sat perfectly still, her fine pale hands folded in her black velvet lap, and listened in unbroken silence to her cousin Mrs. Archer’s breathless account of the insult done to Countess Olenska.
Newland had told himself a dozen times beforehand that silence was simply Mrs. van der Luyden’s default state: that she was slow to commit to any opinion, but unfailingly gentle to those she counted among her intimates. Even so, the chill that wrapped around visitors the moment they stepped over the threshold of her high-ceilinged, white-walled Madison Avenue drawing room still made his skin prickle. The pale brocade armchairs were still draped in their summer dust covers, fine gauze veiled every gilded ormolu trinket on the mantel, and even the rich carved frame of the Gainsborough portrait of Lady Angelica du Lac hung half-shrouded, as if the room was never quite meant to be lived in at all.
Huntington’s portrait of Mrs. van der Luyden herself hung across from her ancestress, painted in black velvet and Venetian point lace that still defined every edge of her social persona, twenty years after the canvas was finished. Any onlooker would have sworn the living Mrs. van der Luyden, sitting straight under her own painted likeness, was the twin of the fair, soft-faced young woman drooping against a gilt armchair in the frame. Her fair hair had faded to a milky ash without ever turning grey, parted in flat, sharp overlapping points across her forehead, and the straight nose dividing her pale blue eyes was only just faintly more pinched at the nostrils than it had been the day she sat for the portrait. Newland often thought she looked as if she had been perfectly preserved for decades in the airless, spotless atmosphere of a life without a single misstep, the same way bodies trapped in glacial ice keep a rosy, deathless hue for generations. He and all his family esteemed her, admired her, deferred to her—but her soft, sweet bend of the neck felt far more unapproachable than the gruff, unyielding severity of his mother’s old spinster aunts, who said “No” before you had even finished stating your request. Mrs. van der Luyden never said no outright. She would waver, her lips curving into the ghost of a smile, and always reply, “I shall first have to talk this over with my husband.”
The two were so perfectly matched, so identical in their quiet, unassailable reserve, that Newland had often wondered how after forty years of marriage their merged identities could ever pull apart long enough to hold a “talking over” on any controversial topic. But as neither of them ever made a single decision without that secret, sacred conclave, Mrs. Archer and her son settled back, resigned to wait for that familiar line.
Mrs. van der Luyden surprised them. She reached out her long, slim hand and pulled the bell-rope.
“I think,” she said, “I should like Henry to hear what you have told me.”
A footman appeared, and she said gravely, “If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come.” She spoke the words “reading the newspaper” with the reverence a prime minister’s wife would use to reference a cabinet meeting, not out of arrogance, but out of a lifetime of habit that had cast every small, mundane action of her husband as something nearly sacred.
Her speed made it clear she considered the matter every bit as urgent as Mrs. Archer did, but she softened the implication that she had already made up her mind with a sweet, warm smile. “Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline. And he will wish to congratulate Newland on his engagement.”
The heavy double doors swung open, and Mr. Henry van der Luyden stepped through: tall, spare, in a crisp frock coat, with the same faded fair hair, the same straight nose, the same look of frozen, unassailable gentleness in pale grey eyes where his wife’s were pale blue. He greeted Mrs. Archer with cousinly warmth, murmured his congratulations to Newland in the same soft, measured tone, and sank into one of the brocade armchairs with the easy, unapologetic dignity of a reigning sovereign.
“I had just finished reading *The Times*,” he said, folding his long fingertips together. “In town my mornings are so occupied, I find it far more convenient to read the papers after luncheon.”
“Ah, my uncle Egmont used to say exactly the same thing,” Mrs. Archer replied eagerly. “He said he found the news far less agitating if he waited till after dinner to read it.”
“Yes, my father abhorred hurry,” Mr. van der Luyden said, his tone slow and deliberate as he glanced around the shrouded, silent room that was the perfect mirror of its owners. “But nowadays the whole world seems to rush headlong.”
“But you had quite finished your reading, Henry?” his wife put in gently.
“Quite, quite,” he assured her.
“Then I should like Adeline to tell you—”
“Oh, it’s really Newland’s story to tell,” his mother laughed, and proceeded to recount the entire outrageous tale of Lawrence Lefferts snubbing Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s dinner party to protest Countess Olenska’s invitation. “Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both agreed, of course—especially now that Newland is engaged to May—that you and Henry absolutely had to know what was going on.”
“Ah—” Mr. van der Luyden said, and drew a long, slow breath.
The monumental ormolu clock on the white marble mantel ticked so loud in the silence it sounded like the boom of a minute gun out at sea. Newland stared at the two faded, elegant figures sitting side by side, rigid as viceroys, the living voices of some old ancestral authority they had never asked to wield, who would far rather be hidden away in their country estate at Skuytercliff, pulling invisible weeds from their perfect lawns and playing patience together after dinner.
Mr. van der Luyden turned to Newland. “You are quite certain this is Lawrence Lefferts doing this on purpose?”
“Absolutely certain, sir,” Newland said. “Larry’s been more reckless than usual lately—cousin Louisa will forgive my saying it—he’s been carrying on a very obvious affair with the postmaster’s wife in their village, and whenever poor Gertrude starts to suspect something, he cooks up a big moral fuss like this to prove how impossibly upstanding he is, yelling about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet people he doesn’t approve of. He’s only using Madame Olenska as a lightning rod to draw attention away from his own scandal. I’ve seen him pull this exact trick half a dozen times before.”
“The Leffertses!” Mrs. van der Luyden breathed.
“The Leffertses!” Mrs. Archer echoed, horrified. “What would Uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts pronouncing judgement on anyone’s social position? It shows exactly what our society has come to.”
“We must hope it has not quite come to that,” Mr. van der Luyden said firmly.
“Ah, if only you and Louisa went out more!” Mrs. Archer sighed, then froze, horrified at her own misstep. The van der Luydens were notoriously sensitive to any criticism of their secluded lives. They were the final court of appeal for all New York society fashion and manners, they knew it, and they accepted the weight of that duty—but they were shy, quiet people who would far rather have hidden from public life entirely, and when they did come to town they turned down every invitation, citing Mrs. van der Luyden’s delicate health.
Newland hurried to his mother’s rescue. “Everyone in New York knows exactly what you and cousin Louisa stand for. That’s why Mrs. Mingott wouldn’t let this petty slight against Countess Olenska pass without consulting you first.”
Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband, and he glanced back.
“It is the principle of the thing I dislike,” Mr. van der Luyden said. “As long as a member of a well-known family is backed by their kin, their place in society should be considered final. No one else has the right to question it.”
“It does seem that way,” his wife said, as if the thought had only just occurred to her.
“I had no idea things had deteriorated this far,” Mr. van der Luyden continued. He paused, looked at his wife again. “It occurs to me, my dear, that Countess Olenska is already a distant relation of ours—through Medora Manson’s first husband. And of course, she will be far more than that once Newland marries May.” He turned to Newland. “Have you read this morning’s *Times*, Newland?”
“Why, yes, sir,” Newland said, who usually skimmed half a dozen papers over his morning coffee.
The husband and wife looked at each other again, their pale eyes locked in a long, silent consultation no words were needed for. Then a faint, secret smile flickered across Mrs. van der Luyden’s face. She had guessed his plan, and approved.
Mr. van der Luyden turned back to Mrs. Archer. “If Louisa’s health allowed her to dine out,” he said, “please tell Mrs. Lovell Mingott that the two of us would have been happy to step in and fill the empty seats left by the Lawence Leffertses at her dinner. But as you well know, that is impossible.” Mrs. Archer murmured her understanding. “But Newland tells me he has read this morning’s *Times*, so he will already have seen that Louisa’s cousin, the Duke of St. Austrey, arrives next week on the *Russia*. He is coming to enter his new sloop, the *Guinevere*, in next summer’s International Cup Race, and to shoot canvasback ducks at our place Trevenna, in Maryland. Before we take him down there, we are hosting a small dinner for a few close friends to meet him, followed by a small reception. I am quite sure Louisa will be as delighted as I am if Countess Olenska will allow us to include her among our guests.”
He stood up, bent his long frame in a stiff, friendly gesture toward his cousin. “I think I speak for Louisa when I say she will drop the invitation off herself with our cards, on her drive this afternoon.”
Mrs. Archer knew this was the polite hint that the van der Luydens’ famous seventeen-hand chestnut horses were waiting at the door, never kept waiting a second, and she stood up, stammering her thanks. Mrs. van der Luyden beamed at her, the smile of Esther stepping forward to intercede with King Ahasuerus for her people. But Mr. van der Luyden held up a gentle, sovereign hand.
“There is nothing to thank me for, dear Adeline. Nothing at all. This sort of petty cruelty has no place in New York society. It never will, as long as I can help it,” he said, as he steered his cousins toward the front door.
Two hours later, half of old New York had already heard that the famous C-spring barouche Mrs. van der Luyden rode in all year round had been seen pulling up to old Mrs. Mingott’s door, where a large, square cream envelope was handed in to the servants. That evening at the opera, Sillerton Jackson could announce to the entire club box that the envelope contained a personal, hand-delivered invitation for Countess Olenska to attend the van der Luydens’ exclusive dinner the following week, in honor of the Duke of St. Austrey.
A group of young men leaning against the edge of the box exchanged quiet grins, glancing sideways at Lawrence Lefferts, who was slouched carelessly in the very front of the box, twisting his long fair moustache. As the soprano paused between arias, he spoke up in his most authoritative tone: “No one but Patti should ever dare attempt the lead in *La Sonnambula*.”
