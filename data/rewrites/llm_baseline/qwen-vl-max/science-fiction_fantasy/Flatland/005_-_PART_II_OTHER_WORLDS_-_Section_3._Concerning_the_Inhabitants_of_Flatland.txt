In our world, the largest adult being rarely exceeds eleven of your inches in length or breadth—twelve inches being the absolute maximum. We are a society shaped by geometry, where form determines rank, destiny, and even social worth.

Our women are straight lines—simple, unadorned, and without angles. They are the most basic figures in our realm, yet their very simplicity grants them a unique place in our rigid hierarchy.

The soldiers and lowest laborers among us are isosceles triangles—figures with two equal sides, each about eleven inches long, and a third side so short it often measures no more than half an inch. This tiny base creates a sharp, dangerous vertex—an angle so acute that these men appear nearly indistinguishable from straight lines when viewed from afar. The more degraded among them, whose bases shrink to less than an eighth of an inch, are almost impossible to tell apart from women. These figures are known as Isosceles Triangles, and though they are not entirely devoid of reason, they are considered barely human due to their irregularity.

Above them stands the middle class: equilateral triangles—figures with all three sides equal. Their symmetry marks them as balanced, orderly, and fit for civic life. They serve as tradesmen, clerks, and minor officials.

Next come the professional classes: squares and pentagons. I myself am a square, and thus belong to this respected group. Squares are seen as wise, capable, and honorable—men of thought and action. Pentagons, with five equal sides, are even higher in status, representing greater intellectual and moral refinement.

Beyond them lies the nobility—a tier defined not by birth alone, but by increasing numbers of sides. It begins with hexagons (six-sided figures), then heptagons, octagons, and so on, ascending through ever more complex forms. As the number of sides grows and the length of each diminishes, the figure becomes smoother, more circular. When the sides become so numerous and so small that the shape appears perfectly round, the being is elevated to the highest order: the Circles—or Priests.

This progression is not merely symbolic; it reflects a natural law. A male child inherits one additional side compared to his father. Thus, the son of a square is a pentagon; the son of a pentagon, a hexagon—and so forth, climbing steadily toward the divine circle. This rule ensures that each generation advances in wisdom, virtue, and social standing.

But this law does not apply universally. Among the lower classes—especially soldiers and artisans—their offspring often remain unchanged. Since their sides are unequal, they cannot follow the path of upward evolution. An isosceles triangle will typically produce another isosceles triangle. Yet hope persists—even for the lowliest among us.

Through prolonged effort, discipline, and careful breeding, some families within the lower ranks gradually improve. Diligent work, military valor, or intellectual cultivation may lead to subtle changes: a slight broadening of the base, a softening of the apex. Over generations, these improvements accumulate. With the guidance of the Priestly class, intermarriages between such promising individuals can yield offspring closer to the ideal equilateral triangle.

Rarely—so rarely that it stirs national celebration—does a truly regular equilateral triangle emerge from isosceles parents. Such a birth is cause for joy across many furlongs. After rigorous inspection by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant is formally certified. If deemed Regular, he is ceremonially welcomed into the equilateral class.

Then comes a solemn act: separation. The child is taken from his parents—proud yet grieving—and adopted by a childless equilateral. By oath, this adoptive parent must never allow the child to return home or see his kin again. For fear that the influence of his original environment might weaken his new form, corrupting his development through unconscious imitation.

Such rare ascents are cherished not only by the poor, who see in them a flicker of hope amid their drudgery, but also by the elite. The aristocracy understands that while these exceptions do not threaten their power, they serve as a powerful safeguard against revolution. Had every isosceles been utterly hopeless, the masses might have risen with fury, led by bold and cunning minds. But nature has devised a clever balance.

As the lower classes grow wiser, more skilled, and more virtuous, their acute angles—once weapons of terror—begin to soften. The sharper the mind, the duller the point. The most brutal soldiers, though fierce and deadly, lose their penetrating edge as they gain intelligence. Their physical threat diminishes just as their mental capacity grows. This is the Law of Compensation—a perfect harmony, proof of the natural and divine order of our society.

The ruling Polygons and Circles exploit this law wisely. When unrest stirs, they act swiftly. Some rebellious leaders are subtly reshaped—compressed or expanded by state physicians—until they become regular enough to be admitted into the privileged classes. Others, still flawed but hopeful, are lured into State Hospitals, where they live in comfort under lifelong supervision. Only the most stubborn, irregular, or foolish face execution.

Then, the rest—the rabble—are left leaderless and disoriented. Either they are swiftly subdued by a loyal force of paid isosceles troops maintained by the Chief Circle, or—more commonly—they are turned against one another. Through carefully sown distrust and jealousy, the Circular party incites civil conflict. And so, they destroy themselves, cut down by their own angles.

History records no fewer than one hundred twenty major uprisings, plus two hundred thirty-five smaller revolts. Not one has succeeded. Each ended in silence, blood, and the quiet triumph of order.

Thus, our world endures—not by force alone, but by design, by law, by the gentle yet unyielding logic of geometry itself.
