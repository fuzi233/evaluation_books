Tito Melema’s sandals tapped light as a sparrow’s step across Florence’s sun-warmed cobblestones, the sharp, cold fear that had coiled in his gut after his recent brush with ruin dissolving like mist off the Arno. His natural easy joy had reasserted itself, and he was hurrying toward the de’ Bardi house on the hill, to see Romola. It was true that Romola’s whole life was stitched through with that tender, self-sacrificing devotion to her blind father, that endless patience with tedious, unglamorous scholarly labor he had always instinctively shrunk from. But he was not yet tired of goodness, not ready to fling himself headlong into vice: he was still young, his blood thrumming with soft, open fondness for all that was beautiful and worthy of admiration, his appetite for ordinary human joy still unspoiled. The poison of his recent secret betrayal of the man who had raised him could only work slowly, after all. He had bound himself to evil, but for now life felt almost exactly as it had before the bargain he had struck, so much so he barely noticed the chain around his neck. He intended everything to go back to how it was: he would win universal praise through diligent work, clever erudition, his own gentle, obliging charm. He had no intention of doing anything that would break the harmony he shared with the people he cared for most in the world. And no one mattered more to him than Romola. There might be wealthier wives he could win one day, if his talents carried him far enough, but there was no other woman in all of Florence like her. When she was near, her sincere hazel eyes fixed on him, he fell under a spell as strong and unshakable as a melody that seizes your whole body, leaving you restless the moment it fades until you hear it again.

By the time he was climbing the worn stone steps to their door, the thrill of just anticipating her nearness had already wrapped around him.
“Welcome, Tito mio,” Bardo’s voice called out before Tito could even knock. There was new fire in the old blind man’s tone, new cheer softening his lined face, ever since that first fateful meeting more than two months prior. “You’ve brought fresh manuscript, no doubt—but last night, after we parted, new ideas came to me. We must broaden our scope. We must go back over the work we’ve already laid out.”

Tito bowed to Romola as he stepped across the threshold, moving straight to Bardo’s chair as was his custom, resting his hand in the old man’s waiting palm before settling onto the low cross-legged leather seat with scrolled carvings pulled up right next to the blind man’s elbow.
“I have the new manuscript,” he said gently, “but it can wait as long as you please. My legs are young, you know—I can walk back up the hill easily whenever you’re ready to see it.”
He didn’t glance at Romola as he spoke, but he knew perfectly well her eyes were fixed on him, bright with unhidden delight.
“That is well said, my son.” Bardo had started calling Tito that, unprompted, only a few weeks prior. “I am glad to see you do not shrink from labor—for the poet is right, when he says life has granted mortals nothing without it. Too many young men chase the palma sine pulvere, the glory of the prize without the dust of the race. But the Greek proverb says it best: in the morning of life, work; in midday, give counsel; in the evening, pray. I might look to all the world as if I have reached that helpless evening, but I have not, not while I carry unspoken counsel in my chest. My mind was once dammed up, you see: all those years of learning, all that thought, lay dark and still behind the wall of my blindness. But you, Tito, you opened a channel for it, and now the waters rush forward with a force that surprises even me. I want us to revisit all our early preliminary ground, build out a far wider scheme of commentary and illustration, else I will lose opportunities I can now see in hindsight that may never come again. You follow my meaning?”

Tito had just stooped to pick up a roll of manuscript that had slipped off Bardo’s lap, and the old man’s sharp, jealous ear caught the tiny movement. Even if he had been allowed to let out a sigh of exasperation at the extra months of tedious work ahead, Tito was not naturally impatient. He had been raised steeped in that painstaking, detailed, copious erudition that was the greatest intellectual labor of his age, and with Romola so near, every little discomfort felt light, buoyed up by waves of soft, pleasant sensation.
“Absolutely,” he said. “You wish to expand your commentary on certain passages we’ve already cited.”
“More than that. I want to add occasional excursuses, deep dives on every author I have spent years studying, for I may die before I can write any separate work of my own. This is no time for careful, well-sifted learning to sit idle: we face not just rash ignorance, but men like Calderino, whom Poliziano rightly exposed for spouting brazen fake citations just to feed his own vanity and win arguments for his wrong ideas. So we cannot let this opportunity slip through our fingers. We will go back to the Thucydides passage we marked earlier, and I want you to walk through all my notes on Lorenzo Valla’s Latin translation—you know, the one the incomparable Pope Nicholas V, whom I met when I was young, back when he was still plain Thomas of Sarzana, paid Valla five hundred gold scudi for, and not a scudi too much. Even with Valla’s other questionable reputation, his scholarship is held in such high regard that the epigrammatist joked that after he descended to the underworld, Pluto himself dared not speak any ancient language for fear of being corrected. That is exactly why we cannot let his name be a rubber stamp for shoddy, false work: this excursus on Thucydides will be the perfect place for my corrections to his text. Romola, my dear—go fetch the volumes we need, you know where they sit, fifth shelf of the cabinet.”

Tito stood up at the exact same moment Romola did. “Let me get them, just point the way,” he said, hurrying after her into the small adjoining room lined floor to ceiling with perfectly ordered books, the air smelling of old vellum and dried lavender. Every book sat exactly where Bardo had placed it, years before he lost his sight. The two of them had never been alone in that little room before.
“I hope,” Romola said, turning her full, grave, trusting gaze on him, “that this extra work won’t weary you. It makes him so happy, you see.”
“And it makes me happy too, Romola—if you will only let me say that I love you. If you will only think me worth loving just a little.”
His voice dropped to the softest murmur, his dark beautiful face closer to hers than it had ever been, his eyes shining with tender pleading.
“I do love you,” Romola whispered back. She carried all her usual quiet, noble dignity, but her voice had never sunk so soft before. For a long, breathless moment they only looked at each other, before she added, “I know now what it is to be truly happy.”
Their faces tilted toward each other, their dark curls tangled for a heartbeat with the rippling gold of hers. Quick as lightning, Tito stepped up onto a projecting shelf edge to reach down the two volumes they needed, and both fell quiet, content to walk back to Bardo without any more words: that first blissful, shared secret was far more precious unbroken by chatter. The moment had passed as irreversibly as two streams flowing into one, and Bardo, blind and jealous of their attention, had not even had time to grow impatient.
“You have the volumes, Romola?” the old man called as they drew near. “Now get your pen ready: Tito will mark out the scholia we decide to extract, and you will copy them straight away, minding the numbers carefully to match the markers Tito will write in the text.”
Romola always had her own part to play in their shared work. Tito took his place at the tall writing desk where he read and transcribed, and she sat at a table just in front of him, ready to pass Bardo anything he reached for, or take a finished book out of his hands. They had sat in those exact positions every day since their work began, but today it felt brand new: every small movement, every time Tito took a book from her hands as she lifted it off Bardo’s knee, carried a new, quiet weight of joy. Romola’s deep, calm happiness wrapped around them like the soft, fading evening light that drives all restlessness away.

They had worked for two full hours, and were just stopping as the last of the sunlight faded through the leaded windows, when the door swung open to admit a figure that could not have been more out of place in their quiet, vellum-scented scholarly bubble. She was a short, stout, black-eyed woman of about fifty, wearing a black velvet pearl-embroidered close cap over massive braids of jet-black hair that bulged over her small forehead, her plump cheeks dusted with a startling bright carmine rouge above a sallow undertone. Three rows of pearls and a lower gold necklace rested on the soft wide cushion of her neck; her trailing black velvet gown and long drooping rose damask sleeves were embroidered around the edges, slightly faded, but any Florentine would recognize instantly that they were six months’ fine work from a master embroiderer. Her rose-colored petticoat, trimmed with dimmed white fringe and seed-pearl arabesques, was on full proud display, and a handsome coral rosary hung from one side of her niello-work silver clasp belt, a large crimson velvet pearl-stitched purse hanging on the other. Her plump little right hand, soft as paste left too long in the oven, held a richly decorated devotional book bound in velvet, silver, and pearls.

Monna Brigida was no stranger to Tito. She was Bardo’s late wife’s cousin, allowed full run of the house even though Bardo ordinarily banned all frivolous feminine visitors, for she had helped raise Romola when she was small. Romola smiled affectionately at her, rising to pull a spare seat far enough from her father’s chair that the gush of incoming gossip would not disturb him too much.
“Cugina?” Bardo asked, catching the short, heavy steps and the swish of heavy velvet.
“Yes, it’s your troublesome cousin,” Monna Brigida said, lifting her fingers playfully at Tito before leaning up to let Romola kiss her cheek. “Always bursting in on all your deep wisdom. Well, if I didn’t bring you bits of news from the outside world now and then, you’d forget there was any life at all beyond these mouldy old ancients of yours, who’d probably need a sprinkling of holy water if half the stories I hear about them are true. Not that the world’s any good these days, of course—scandals pop up everywhere you step, and you can’t go about with your head in a sack. I was just at Dianora Acciajoli’s wedding to that young Albizzi boy everyone’s been talking about, the one everyone expected to happen yesterday. Cieli! That wedding was so grim it might as well have been put off until Lent as a penance. The bride looked like a white nun, not a single pearl on her, and the groom was as solemn as Saint Joseph. Half the guests were Piagnoni, those new saints of Fra Girolamo’s making! Can you imagine two families as grand as the Albizzi and Acciajoli going in for that nonsense, when they can afford the finest clothes in Florence? I couldn’t even stand still in my best gown without giving offence—there was Monna Berta, who’s hidden far worse secrets in her time than I ever told, glowering at me from under her hood like a pinzochera, telling me I ought to read Fra Girolamo’s book for widows for guidance. Holy Madonna! According to them, widows ought to buy their coffins the second their husbands die and wait to rot, instead of enjoying themselves for the first time in their lives. And what did they serve us for entertainment at dinner? A long, rambling sermon from Fra Domenico of San Marco, listing off Fra Girolamo’s three great doctrines, marking them off on his fingers until my flesh crept. The first one says Florence—or the Church, he mixed them up—will be scourged, and he talked so much about plague I half thought the swellings would pop up under my arms right there at the table. Then he said Florence would be regenerated—well, what good will that do if we’re all dead of the plague? The third one, the one he said over and over, is that all of this will happen in our lifetimes. He tapped that one hard on his thumb until I was shaking like the jelly on my plate! The jellies still had both families’ coats of arms molded in colored sugar, so they haven’t turned the whole world upside down quite yet, but all they talked about was going back to the old ways: up stands Francesco Valori, who I used to dance with in the Via Larga when he was a bachelor and loved the Medici as much as anyone, making a whole speech about how we ought to ban fine clothes and fancy feasts and bad books, as if any of that belongs at a wedding!”

“Stop, cousin!” Bardo cut her off in his imperious, sharp tone, desperate to stop her endless rambling long enough to speak. Monna Brigida jumped, pursed her mouth, and stared at him with wide round eyes.
“Francesco Valori is not entirely wrong,” Bardo continued. “Bernardo thinks he only calls private grudges public zeal, but I say the old noble frugality of the ancients, the very mother of true greatness, is slipping away from us. Men now spend more on one transient joust than would be enough to found a whole library, to leave a lasting gift to the world for centuries. But for all the Neapolitan and Milanese courtiers laugh at us Florentines for having so little silver, for borrowing table furniture from each other at feasts, we still carry more of that great sober generosity, that hates trivial waste so it can be grandly lavish on things that matter, than any other city in Italy. The laughter of fools is half applause to wisdom, after all.”
Monna Brigida burst back into speech the second Bardo paused. “Laughter? No one laughed at that wedding! Young Niccolò Machiavelli tried to tell jokes from Franco Sacchetti’s book, about how no sumptuary laws for women ever worked, because we’re cleverer than painters and architects and all the logic doctors, we can make black look white and yellow look pink and give any forbidden thing a new name—but the Piagnoni all looked even more glum, and someone said Sacchetti’s book was wicked! I’ve never been so uncomfortable at a party, we non-Piagnoni all sat there as soggy as wet chickens. The only other time I felt that bad was last Lent, when I went to hear Fra Girolamo preach in San Lorenzo. He called us poor women cows, lumps of flesh, wantons, mischief-makers—and then he described a vision of a pale dead young woman lying in her bier, and all us light-minded widows rushing up to her bareheaded like vultures, cutting off her young dead hair to plait into our own old heads! I had nightmares for weeks! I came home and took off all my jewels, this very clasp, everything, tied them up in a parcel ready to give to the Good Men of Saint Martin for the poor, and then thank God I went first to my confessor Fra Cristoforo at Santa Croce, and he told me it was all the devil’s work, the Dominicans were trying to turn the whole world upside down, I never had to go hear Girolamo preach again. I just wish that poor Dino, Bardo’s son, hadn’t joined the Dominicans all those years ago—then I would have been happy when I heard everyone say he’s come back to Florence now, after all this time—”

“Silenzio!” Bardo roared, his whole frame shaking with passion. Romola half rose out of her chair, clasping her hands and turning to Tito, as if she instinctively wanted his support. Monna Brigida yelped a little, and bit down hard on her lip.
“Woman,” Bardo said, his voice trembling, “hear my will once and for all. Bring no reports of that name to this house. Romola, I forbid you to ever ask after him. My son is dead.”

No one dared break the heavy silence that followed. Monna Brigida lifted her shoulders and her hands in silent dismay, then got up as quietly as she could, waving frantically at Tito and Romola to stay seated, and slipped out of the room like a fat, guilty spaniel who had barked at the wrong time.

Meanwhile, Tito’s mind was racing at lightning speed. So Bardo’s son was not dead, as he had assumed this whole time. He was a Dominican monk. He was “come back.” That Fra Luca he had run into outside the Duomo, the one whose face had seemed half-familiar? Of course he looked like Bardo and Romola. What were the odds that man dropped dead back in Fiesole this very moment? The selfish, desperate thought pushed all other feelings aside. It was true that Bardo’s iron will was strong enough to keep Romola from ever seeking out her brother, but it could not stop Fra Luca from telling other people what he knew—especially if anyone ever connected the monk who was hunting for Baldassarre to Tito Melema, the exact name the description the old man had sewn into his tunic named as the man who had robbed and abandoned him. Nothing but Fra Luca’s death could wipe that threat away. But it was very possible the monk was already dying, after all the stories they had told of his fragile health. Tito breathed slow, and let himself sink back into that quiet, hopeful confidence.

The three of them sat in the deepening twilight for many long minutes, before Romola dared speak.
“Shall I light the lamp, father, and we can go back to work?”
“No, Romola. We will do no more work tonight. Tito, come sit here by me.”
Tito left the writing desk, and settled on the other side of Bardo, close to his left elbow.
