Yersted's intelligence confirmed that there was no time to lose. I had to reach the Temple of Issus in secret before Tars Tarkas launched his assault at dawn. Once inside those hated walls, I was certain I could overpower the guards of Issus and carry off my princess, for I would arrive backed by a force ample for the occasion.

No sooner had Carthoris and the others joined me than we began transporting our men through the submerged passage to the mouth of the gangways leading from the submarine pool at the temple end of the watery tunnel to the pits of Issus. Many trips were required, but finally, all five thousand of us stood safely together again at the beginning of the end of our quest. These were seasoned fighting-men of the most warlike race among the Red Men of Barsoom. Since Carthoris alone knew the hidden ways of the tunnels, we could not divide the party to attack the temple from multiple points simultaneously—a tactic that would have been ideal—but it was decided he would lead us all as quickly as possible to a point near the temple's center.

As we prepared to leave the pool and enter the corridor, an officer drew my attention to the waters upon which our submarine floated. Initially, they seemed merely agitated, as if disturbed by some great body moving beneath the surface. I immediately conjectured that another submarine was surfacing in pursuit. However, it soon became clear that the water level was rising—not with extreme rapidity, but with terrifying certainty. Soon, it would overflow the sides of the pool and submerge the chamber floor.

For a moment, I failed to grasp the terrible import of the slowly rising tide. It was Carthoris who realized the full meaning of the situation—its cause and its deadly logic.

"Haste!" he cried. "If we delay, we are all lost. The pumps of Omean have been cut. They intend to drown us like rats in a trap. We must reach the upper levels of the pits before the flood arrives, or we shall never make it. Come!"

"Lead the way, Carthoris," I commanded. "We will follow."

At my signal, the youth leaped into one of the corridors, and in a column of twos, the soldiers followed in good order, each company entering only at the command of its dwarf-captain. Before the last unit filed from the chamber, the water was ankle-deep, and the men's nervousness was evident. Unaccustomed to anything but shallow pools for drinking or bathing, the Red Martians instinctively shrank from such formidable depths and menacing activity. Yet, their lack of fear while the water swirled and eddied about their ankles testified to their bravery and discipline.

I was the last to leave the submarine chamber. Moving through water that reached my knees, I followed the rear of the column toward the corridor. The passage itself was flooded to the same depth, its floor level with the chamber below, showing no perceptible rise for many yards. Our march was as rapid as the narrow passage allowed, but it was insufficient to gain significant ground on the pursuing tide. As the water level rose, so did the threat; soon, it became obvious to me, bringing up the rear, that the flood was gaining on us rapidly.

I understood the physics of the disaster: as the waters rose toward the apex of the dome-shaped pit Omean, the rate of ascent would increase inversely proportional to the diminishing space available to be filled. Long before the last of the column could hope to reach the upper pits above the danger line, I was convinced the waters would surge after us in overwhelming volume, drowning fully half the expedition.

Desperate to save as many as possible, I spotted a diverging corridor to my right, rising at a steep angle. The water now swirled about my waist. Those directly ahead were already panic-stricken. Something had to be done instantly, or they would rush forward in a mad stampede, trampling hundreds beneath the flood and clogging the passage beyond any hope of retreat for those in front.

Raising my voice to its utmost, I shouted my command to the dwarves ahead:

"Call back the last twenty-five utans! Here seems a way of escape. Turn back and follow me!"

My orders were obeyed by nearly thirty utans, turning some three thousand men around to hasten into the teeth of the flood toward the corridor I indicated. As the first dwarf passed with his utans, I cautioned him to listen closely for my commands and under no circumstances to venture into the open or leave the pits for the temple proper until I arrived.

*"Or you know that I died before I could reach you,"* I added grimly.

The officer saluted and departed. The men filed rapidly past me, entering the diverging corridor I hoped would lead to safety. The water rose to breast height. Men stumbled, floundered, and sank. Many I grasped and set upright, but the task was greater than I could cope with alone. Soldiers were being swept beneath the boiling torrent, never to rise again.

Finally, the dwarf of the tenth utan took a stand beside me. He was a valiant soldier named Gur Tus. Together, we kept the thoroughly frightened troops in semblance of order and rescued many who would otherwise have perished. Djor Kantos, son of Kantos Kan, a padwar of the fifth utan, joined us when his unit reached the opening through which the men were fleeing. Afterward, not a single man was lost among the hundreds passing from the main corridor to the branch.

As the last utan filed past, the waters surged about our necks. We clasped hands and held our ground until the final soul had passed to the comparative safety of the new passageway. Here, we found an immediate and steep ascent; within a hundred yards, we had reached a point above the waters.

For a few minutes, we raced up the steep grade, hoping to reach the upper pits leading into the Temple of Issus. But cruel disappointment awaited. Suddenly, a cry of "Fire!" echoed far ahead, followed swiftly by screams of terror and the loud commands of dwarves and padwars directing men away from grave danger.

"They have fired the pits ahead!" the report came back.

"We are hemmed in by flames in front and flood behind!"

"Help, John Carter! We are suffocating!"

Then, a wave of dense smoke swept backward upon us, sending us stumbling and blinded into a choking retreat. There was nothing to do but find a new avenue of escape. Fire and smoke were a thousand times more fearsome than water. Seizing the first gallery leading out and upward from the suffocating haze, I waited to the side while the soldiers hurried through on the new route. About two thousand must have passed at a rapid run before the stream ceased, though I was not certain all had escaped the zone of origin.

To ensure no poor soul was left behind to die a horrible, unsuccored death, I ran quickly up the gallery toward the flames, which I could now see glowing with a dull, malevolent heat. It was hot and stifling work, but I reached a point where the fire illuminated the corridor enough to confirm that no soldier of Helium lay between me and the conflagration. What burned on the far side, or what lay within that seething hell of chemicals, I could not know, nor could anyone have passed through it alive to tell the tale. Satisfied in my sense of duty, I turned and ran rapidly back to the corridor my men had used.

To my horror, my retreat was blocked. Across the mouth of the corridor stood a massive steel grating, evidently lowered from above to effectively seal my escape.

Our principal movements were clearly known to the First Born, given the attack on our fleet the previous day. The stopping of Omean's pumps at this critical moment, the ignition of chemical combustion in our specific corridor, and now the dropping of the steel gate to pin me between fire and flood—all pointed to invisible eyes watching us at every moment. What chance did I have to rescue Dejah Thoris if forced to fight foes who never revealed themselves? A thousand times I berated myself for walking into such a trap, knowing these pits could easily be ambushed. Perhaps it would have been wiser to keep our force intact and launch a concerted attack from the valley side, trusting in our fighting prowess to overwhelm the First Born.

The smoke drove me further back down the corridor toward the surging waters I could hear roaring through the darkness. The last torch had gone out with my men, and unlike the lower levels, this corridor lacked the radiance of phosphorescent rock. This absence assured me I was nearing the upper pits directly beneath the temple.

Finally, I felt the lapping waters about my feet. The smoke was thick behind me. My suffering was intense. There seemed but one option: to choose the easier death confronting me. I moved on down the corridor until the cold waters of Omean closed about me, and I swam on through utter blackness toward... what?

Even when unafraid and possessing full reasoning faculties, the instinct of self-preservation is strong when one knows that positive, unalterable death lies just ahead. Thus, I swam slowly, waiting for my head to touch the top of the corridor—the point where my flight ended and I would sink forever into an unmarked grave.

But to my surprise, I ran against a blank wall before reaching the point where the waters met the roof. Could I be mistaken? Feeling around, I confirmed I had reached the main corridor, and still, there was a breathing space between the water's surface and the rocky ceiling.
