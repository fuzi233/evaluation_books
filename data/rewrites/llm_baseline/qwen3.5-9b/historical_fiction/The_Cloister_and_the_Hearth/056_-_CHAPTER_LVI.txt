Gerard secured a modest lodging on the west bank of the Tiber and set out daily in pursuit of employment, carrying samples to every workshop known to execute such commissions. The reception he met was uniformly frosty. "Our letters are cut somewhat thinner than this," remarked one merchant. "Your ink is far too dark," observed another. Yet the prevailing complaint echoed through the shops: "Of what avail is this? Latin script is scarce in Rome nowadays. Cannot you write Greek?" "Yes, but not nearly as well as Latin." "Then you shall never earn your bread in Rome."

Desperate, Gerard borrowed a beautiful Greek manuscript at a steep price, returning home with a hollow purse but unbroken spirit. Within a fortnight, he had mastered the Greek characters with remarkable speed. Eager to capitalize on this skill, he worked until noon and spent the afternoon hunting clients. When he finally presented a Greek specimen surpassing anything they owned, the traders merely shrugged, informing him that both Greek and Latin were unsellable; the city was flooded with works from across Europe. He should have arrived a year prior.

In his frustration, Gerard purchased a psaltery. His landlady, charmed by his appearance and demeanor, frequently offered him kind words. One day, she invited him to dine with her and, to his surprise, asked what had dampened his spirits. He confessed his struggles. She offered her interpretation: "Those sly traders," she declared, "have writers in their pay for whom they pay nobly, paying you poorly. No wonder they blow cold on you. It seems you write too well. How do I know? Perhaps because you do not lock your door like the churl Pietro, and women are naturally curious. Yes, yes, you write too well for them."

When Gerard sought clarification, she explained, "Your fine work might blind those trying to sell theirs." He sighed. "Alas, dame, you read people on the wrong side, yet you are so kind and frank yourself." "My dear little heart," she replied, "these Romans are a subtle race. As for me, I am a Siennese, thanks to the Virgin."

"My mistake was leaving Augsburg," Gerard admitted. "Augsburg?" she retorted haughtily. "Is that a place comparable to Rome? I have never heard of it." She then assured him he would make his fortune despite the booksellers. "Seeing you as a stranger, they lie without sense or discretion. All the world knows our great families are bitten by the writing spider; they pour out their money like water, turning good land and houses into sheets of parchment to store in chests. God help them through this frenzy, sending them safely as He has through countless others. Since scribbling became popular, there has indeed been less cutting and stabbing among rival factions, less vindictive devouring of opponents' livers, minced and fried. I can name two: His Eminence Cardinal Bassarion and His Holiness the Pope himself. These are a pair who could keep a scribe working day and night. I shall speak to Teresa; she hears the court gossip."

The following day, she returned with news that Teresa had spoken of five additional signors afflicted by the writing spider. Gerard recorded their names, purchased parchment, and spent several days preparing new specimens. He left copies with his name and address at each of their doors, awaiting results in hopeful anticipation. None came. Days turned into weeks, leaving him heartsick. Strangely, this was the same period when Margaret fought desperately against overwhelming odds to feed her male dependents in Rotterdam, only to be arrested for practicing medicine without a license rather than killing with it. Gerard felt ruin staring him in the face.

He spent his afternoons learning canzonets and laying in playing cards to color, managing to strike off a meal a day. This final stroke of genius brought fresh trouble. In these *camere locande*, the landlady prepared all meals, though the lodgers supplied the provisions. Her hostess quickly detected his deception. With a brusque opening that made him blush and look frightened, she pacified herself instantly and appealed to his common sense: Was adversity something to be faced on an empty stomach?

"Patienza, my lad! Times will mend; meanwhile, I will feed you out of love for heaven." (Italian for "gratis.")

"Nay, hostess," Gerard protested, "my purse is not yet entirely void, and it would add to my trouble if honest folk suffered loss through me."

"Why, you are as mad as your neighbor Pietro with his single bad picture."

"Why, how do you know it is a bad picture?"

"Because nobody buys it. There is one who lacks talent; he must don a helmet and carry his panel as a shield."

Gerard pricked up his ears. She continued, explaining that Pietro had arrived from Florence with money and an unfinished painting, renting an unfurnished room opposite hers and furnishing it neatly. Upon completion, Pietro received visitors and offers—offers she deemed generous—which he refused so disdainfully that he made enemies. Since then, he had often taken the painting out to sell but always returned it. Over the past month, she had watched furniture disappear from his room; now he wore only one suit and slept on a large chest. She had discovered this only by peering through the keyhole, noting how vigilantly he locked his door whenever he left.

"Is he afraid we shall steal his chest or his picture, that no soul in all Rome is foolish enough to buy?"

"Nay, sweet hostess; do you not see it is his poverty he wishes to hide?"

"And the greater fool he! Should we not all give him a trial?"

"How you speak of him! His case is mine; besides, he is your countryman."

"Oh, we Siennese love strangers. Is his case yours? Nay, quite the contrary. You are the comeliest youth ever lodged in this house, with hair like gold; he is a dark, sour-visaged lunatic. Moreover, you know how to win over a woman, whereas he does not. Nevertheless, I wish he would not starve to death in my house, giving me a bad reputation. Anyway, one starving guest is enough in any household. You are far from home, and it falls to me, as the mistress here, to count your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, who is far away. We two women shall settle that matter. Mind your own business, being a man, and leave cooking and such tasks to us, who are in the world for little else but roasting fowl, suckling men at birth, and sweeping up the cobwebs of grown-ups."

"Dear kind dame, truly you often remind me of my mother, who is far away."

"All the better; I shall remind you more before I am done with you." The honest soul beamed with delight.

Gerard, neither an egotist nor blinded by female affection, recognized his own sorrow in the plight of poor, proud Pietro. The more he pondered it, the more resolved he became to share his humble means with the unfortunate artist; Pietro's sympathy would surely repay him. He attempted to intercept Pietro but failed. One day, he heard a groan inside the room. Knocking yielded no answer, but a second knock elicited a gruff command to enter. Obeying somewhat timidly, Gerard stepped into a garret furnished simply with a chair, a picture facing the wall, an iron basin, an easel, and a long chest where a haggard young man sat coiled. Nothing resembled a cobra ready to strike the first intruder more vividly.

"Good Signor Pietro," Gerard began, "forgive me that, weary of my own solitude, I intrude on yours. I am your nearest neighbor in this house and think your brother in fortune. I am an artist too."

"You are a painter? Welcome, signer. Sit down on my bed." Pietro leaped up and gestured grandly to the vacant space. Gerard bowed and smiled, though hesitating slightly.

"I may not call myself a painter. I am a writer, a calligrapher. I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts when I can find patrons."

"And you call that an artist?"

"Without offense to your superior merit, Signor Pietro."

"No offense, stranger, none. Only, it seems to me an artist is one who thinks and paints his thought. Now a calligrapher merely traces in black and white the thoughts of another."

"'Tis well distinguished, signor. But then, a writer can record the thoughts of the great ancients and matters of pure reason, such as no man may paint: yea, and the thoughts of God, which angels could not paint. But let that pass. I am a painter as well, albeit a sorry one."

"The better thy luck. 'They will buy thy work in Rome.'"

"But seeking to commend myself to one of thy eminence, I thought it wise to call myself a capable writer rather than a scurvy painter."

At that moment, footsteps sounded on the stairs. "Ah! 'Tis the good dame!" cried Gerard. "Hostess, I am conversing with Signor Pietro. I dare say he will allow me to have my humble dinner here."

The Italian bowed gravely. The landlady brought in Gerard's dinner, steaming and savory. She placed the dish on the bed with a face devoid of expression and departed. Gerard began to eat but stopped midway.
