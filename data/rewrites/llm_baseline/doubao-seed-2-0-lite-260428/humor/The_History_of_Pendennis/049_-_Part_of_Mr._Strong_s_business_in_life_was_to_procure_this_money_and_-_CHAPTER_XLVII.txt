That all-nighter rager at Gaunt House nearly turned Major Pendennis into a heap of gouty old bones on the drawing room rug. The second he could hobble without groaning loud enough to rattle the chandeliers, he tottered off to Buxton, determined to soak his aches out of his ancient carcass in the local healing springs. Parliament dissolved, Sir Francis Clavering and his entire clan fled town, and all the little schemes we’ve been yammering about over the last few chapters ground to a complete halt over the lull of lazy summer weeks. London was all but empty, a ghost town of fancy addresses and abandoned club armchairs.

The social season was well and truly dead and buried. Pen’s lawyer neighbours had bolted for the country circuit, all his flashy fashionable mates had booked their cross-Channel ferries, or legged it up to the Scottish moors chasing grouse and a spot of non-toxic air. You couldn’t spot a single dandy lingering by the club bow windows, or loitering on Pall Mall’s empty pavement. The red-coated foot guards had vanished from outside Buckingham Palace, the St James’s tradesmen were all off on their own holidays, the tailors had sprouted ridiculous mustaches and run off to cruise the Rhine, and the bootmakers were loitering at Ems or Baden-Baden, turning bright red when they spotted their wealthy customers punting at the gambling tables right next to them, too embarrassed to say hello and ask for their overdue bills. The St James’s clergymen were preaching to half-empty pews with not a single notable sinner in the congregation, the Kensington Gardens band had packed up their brass horns and silver trumpets, and only two or three rickety old carriages were creeping along the banks of the Serpentine. Poor Clarence Bulbul, trapped in town by his oh-so-arduous Treasury clerk duties, took his afternoon ride in Rotten Row and sighed so hard his horse almost stopped, swearing the park was as vast and empty as the Arabian desert and he was the only Bedouin dumb enough to be wandering through its dusty, lonely wastes. Warrington stuffed an entire carpet-bag full of Cavendish tobacco and trundled off to his brother’s house in Norfolk, as he always did for the holidays. Pen got left alone in his chambers for a stretch, since this self-proclaimed man of the world couldn’t just up and leave town whenever he pleased: he was stuck running operations for his newspaper, *The Pall Mall Gazette*, while chief editor Captain Shandon was off lolling in Boulogne-sur-Mer with his family, claiming the salty sea air was a health necessity and not just a convenient excuse to never write a single leading article unless someone threatened to dock his pay.

Pen had spent years bragging to anyone who’d listen that he was completely blasé, burnt out on life, and far too sophisticated to get excited about anything. The truth was he was a strapping, healthy young buck with an appetite so ferocious he could polish off even the worst tavern mutton chop with utter glee, and a craving for company that proved he was the farthest thing from a misanthrope alive. If he couldn’t get a fancy dinner at a gentleman’s club, he’d happily chow down on a terrible meal at the nearest public house. If he couldn’t hang out with witty nobles or pretty society girls, he’d pal around with whoever wandered by, perfectly content larking up the Thames on a Greenwich steamboat, trundling out to Hampstead with his newspaper colleague Finucane, or popping over the river to the scruffy summer theatres or Vauxhall Royal Gardens. He was such good mates with the famous comic singer Simpson that he’d shake hands with the top equestrian performers between acts, snickering at their silly gags and softening up when he spotted the rest of the crowd: the roistering lads hell-bent on having a good time no matter the cost, the honest working parents with their kids screaming and clapping at the acrobats, even the ragged street girls cackling loud and rough, bringing their youth and their secrets out to dance and drink till dawn so they could forget their troubles for a few hours. Pen bragged all the time about this little quirk of his, saying anthropology was his favourite hobby, the same way other men obsessed over art or botany. He’d gape with equal delight at a wrinkled old dowager preening in a ballroom, a blushing young aristocrat making her debut, a hulking guardsman flirting with a servant girl in the park, or a tiny toddler feeding ducks while his nurse gossiped. A man with a mostly clean heart can enjoy that kind of people-watching forever, after all, even if it’s a little secret, a little sad, because he’s just far enough removed from the mess to care without getting dragged into it.

He’d ramble about that nonsense at Warrington all the time, all fire and bravado. “I was so madly in love when I was a kid I burned that whole flame right out for good. If I ever get married it’ll be a cold, sensible business deal, some well-bred, good-tempered pretty girl with a little bit of money to cushion our carriage through the rest of life. Romance is dead for me. I spent every last penny of it years ago. I’m old before my time, and I’m proud of it.”

“Piffle!” Warrington would growl around his pipe. “You thought you were going bald last month, bragged about it like it was some fancy medal, and the second the hairdresser told you to use bear’s grease you dove for the pot. You smell like a barber’s shop every time you walk in the door now.”

“You’re just Diogenes living in a tobacco-stinking tub,” Pen huffed, blushing bright pink. “Violets smell better than stale shag, you grizzly old cynic.” For a man who claimed he didn’t care what the world thought of him, Pen spent an absurd amount of time primping, wearing boots so tight his feet went numb halfway through the day, and polishing his boots until they were so shiny you could see your reflection in them.

It was in that dead, boring stretch of autumn, on a sparkly Friday night, that Pen finished writing a brilliant leading article at the newspaper office—so good even Shandon in a good mood would have nodded and stolen all the credit for it, if he’d bothered to show up to work. He read over the damp proof sheet and patted himself on the back, then decided he deserved a treat: he’d cross the Thames and go watch the fireworks at Vauxhall. He stuffed his press pass that read *Editor of the Pall Mall Gazette + 1 Friend* in his pocket, paid his penny to cross Waterloo Bridge, and set off for the gardens. The walk was lovely, stars twinkling overhead, none of the Vauxhall rockets and Roman candles up yet to outshine them.

Everyone who’s ever been to Vauxhall knows you have to trudge through a dark, miserable little entry tunnel first, hiding all the glittering lamps and fun from passersby. The walls of the tunnel have two little illuminated windows, where two men sit at desks ready to take your cash, or wave you through if you’ve got an admission ticket. Pen got to the window ready to show his pass, only to find a man and two ladies already arguing with the ticket taker ahead of him.

The man had his hat perched lopsided on his head and a shabby short cloak he was carrying off like he was the most elegant dandy alive, yelling in a brogue Pen recognised instantly.

“Begorrah, sir, if ye doubt my honour, will ye do me the favour of stepping out of that little box there and—”
“Law, Capting!” the older lady yelped, tugging at his sleeve.
“Stop bothering me,” the ticket man sighed.
“—and go find Mr. Hodgen himself, who’s inside the gardens, and tell him I sent you! I’ve no intention of fighting ye, sir, not in front of ladies anyway. But go ask him, will ye? I had an admission order from him, he’s me dearest friend, he’s singing the *Body Snatcher* for the crowd tonight! I’ve seen Vauxhall a hundred times, I don’t care if I don’t get in, but one of these ladies has never been here in her life. You wouldn’t be so cruel as to take advantage of me misfortune losing the ticket, and rob the poor girl of her fun, would ye?”

“Sorry, Captain, I can’t leave my post to run errands for you,” the ticket man grumbled. The Captain swore a fine colourful Irish oath, the older lady groaned “Law, how provoking!” and the younger girl looked up at the Captain and said “Never mind, sir, I don’t want to go at all. Come on, mamma.” No sooner were the words out of her mouth than she burst into tears, the poor little thing.

“Me poor child!” the Captain wailed at the ticket taker. “Can ye see that and still turn us away?”

Arthur wandered up right then, recognised Costigan immediately, and called out “Don’t you know me, Captain? Pendennis!” He tipped his hat and bowed to the two ladies, and Costigan threw his arms around him like he was his long-lost brother, yammering on about their terrible unlucky situation. He’d had an order from Hodgen for two admissions, he’d thought it would cover three, he’d lost the scrap of paper on the walk over, and now the poor young girl was heartbroken.

Pen was a soft touch for anyone’s misfortunes, and he couldn’t very well turn his back here. He’d seen the girl’s sweet innocent face, the little tremor of her lip before she started crying. Even if it had been his last guinea, he’d have spent it to make her happy. She hid her teary eyes behind her handkerchief the second she spotted a strange gentleman looking at her. Pen stood there, all handsome, kind, blushing a little, hat in hand, the perfect courteous gentleman, and asked who they were. The older lady looked familiar, he realised—he’d seen her around the block before.

“If I can help you out here, Captain Costigan, please let me,” he said. “No need for any more fuss. I’ve got a ticket that admits two people—ma’am, would you do me the honour of letting me walk you all in?” He didn’t want to make a big show of flaunting his money like some grand aristocrat, so he just handed his purse to Costigan and pulled out his ticket, offering his arm to the older lady first.

“Fanny, take the gentleman’s arm,” the older lady said. “You’re far too kind, sir. I’ve seen you walking in and out of our gate a hundred times, you going up to Captain Strong’s rooms at No. 3.”

Fanny curtsied, and slipped her hand under Pen’s arm. She wore a shabby little glove, but her hand was small and pretty, warm against his sleeve. She was barely more than a girl, not quite a full grown woman yet, the tears already drying on her cheeks, her face flushing bright pink with gratitude and delight as she looked up at Pen. Pen rested his free hand gently over hers on his arm. “Fanny’s a lovely name,” he said. “You know who I am, then?”

“We run the porter’s lodge at Shepherd’s Inn, sir,” Fanny said, curtsying again. “I’ve never been to Vauxhall before, papa wouldn’t let me come, and—oh law! Look at all the lights!” She gasped and shrank back, eyes wide as saucers, as the full glittering wonder of Vauxhall lit up in front of her: millions of twinkling lamps, more beautiful than any fairy tale or pantomime she’d ever seen at the theatre. Pen was tickled pink by how giddy she was, pressing her hand a little closer to his side. The self-proclaimed burnt-out blasé young man thought to himself, he’d give half his worldly goods to feel that kind of innocent, uncomplicated joy again.

“Your purse, Pendennis me dear boy,” Costigan’s voice boomed behind them. “You can count it, it’s all there! Ye know ye can trust old Jack Costigan. You’re a saint, Pen, I’ve known ye since ye were a nipper, Mrs Bolton, this gentleman owns Fairoaks Castle, I’ve drunk many a cask of claret up there with the finest gentry in the whole county! Mr Pendennis, ye’ve saved us all tonight! Mr Simpson, your most obedient servant, sir!” Pen was being all polite and grand to the ladies, but he had nothing on Costigan, who was bowing so deep to every stranger he passed you’d think he was meeting royalty, yelling “Bravo!” at every singer and acrobat within earshot.

Costigan, being a descendant of a long line of proud Irish chieftains and county magistrates, was far too dignified to walk arm in arm with a lady who sometimes swept his rooms and cooked his mutton chops, after all. He trundled along behind Pen and Fanny with Mrs Bolton, pointing out all the fancy buildings on the walk over and rambling about all the famous noblemen he claimed to have dined with across Europe. No one expected him to be seen giving an arm to a part-time charwoman in front of 20 thousand glittering Vauxhall lamps, least of all Mrs Bolton herself.

Pen had no such silly hangups. Fanny didn’t make his bed or sweep his chambers, so he was perfectly happy to keep his pretty little companion hanging off his arm. Fanny’s cheeks got pinker and pinker, her eyes sparkling, as she leaned on Pen’s arm, thinking he was far handsomer and grander than any other gentleman in the whole gardens. There was every sort of person there that night: rakish young surgeons, fast clerks from the City, the odd dandy from the guards regiments, and old Lord Colchicum lumbering around leaning on the arm of Mademoiselle Caracoline, the famous trick rider, who was yelling loud French swears that made every nearby prude turn red. Colchicum followed her around, and little Tom Tufthunt followed Colchicum around, perfectly happy to act as the old viscount’s errand boy, carve the fowl at supper, hold his brandy flask, and do all the little menial jobs a rich old roué needs done. When their group passed Pen and Fanny, Colchicum only gave Pen a lazy little leer, but Tom Tufthunt wagged his head and called out “How are you, old boy?” looking like he knew every single secret in the world.

“That’s the famous rider from Astley’s, I’ve seen her perform there,” Fanny said, staring after Mademoiselle Caracoline. “Who’s that old man hanging off her arm?”

“That’s Viscount Colchicum, Miss Fanny,” Pen said, all lordly and protective, not minding a bit that Fanny was hanging off his arm and the old roué had spotted them together. Fanny was very pretty, he noticed: dark bright eyes, teeth like tiny little pearls, her lips almost as red as the rider’s vermilion-painted ones, and her laugh was a thousand times sweeter and more innocent than the older woman’s rough cackle. It was only recently, while dusting the mantelpiece over at Bows and Costigan’s rooms, that Fanny had stared in the little mirror and realised she was pretty. A year before she’d been a gawky clumsy girl, all the other girls at her day school had called her a dowdy nobody, and she’d grown up blooming unnoticed in the dark little porter’s lodge of Shepherd’s Inn, a tiny flower no one had bothered to look at.

They wandered the gardens together, and even though half of London had fled the city, there were still two million people lingering around, plenty of whom recognised Arthur Pendennis. One of them was Henry Foker, wandering around alone, pale, hands stuffed in his pockets, giving Pen a rueful nod when they passed. Poor Harry was trying every distraction he could think of to forget Blanche, but no matter where he wandered in the dark walkways, no matter what pretty light display he stared at, he was still thinking of her. He’d paid the fortune teller to read his palm, and got furious when she told him he was in love with a dark-haired lady who’d make him happy—he wanted names, specifics, guarantees. He didn’t hear a single one of the famous Mr Momus’s comic songs at the concert, his face was so glum.

Fanny noticed his woebegone face and asked Pen what was wrong with the poor odd little man. “I think he’s been crossed in love,” Pen said, grinning down at her. “Isn’t that enough to make any man miserable, Fanny?” Fanny sighed, her dark eyes soft with pity for the lovesick stranger, though Harry never noticed her looking at him.

Then a loud voice boomed out “How dy do, Mr Pendennis!” It was Sam Huxter, the medical student from Clavering, dressed in a huge white coat, red neckcloth, dingy shirt collar, a gaudy cheap brass pin stuck in it, a ridiculous waistcoat with fanciful glass buttons, and trousers so loud they practically screamed “Look how tawdry and cheap I am!” He had one hand stuck in his pocket holding a walking stick, the other looped through the arm of a girl in a pink satin dress. “Forget me, I dare say? Huxter, from Clavering! Remember that row we had at Wapshot’s? Great famous author now, aren’t you? Saw your name in the Morning Post. Too much of a swell to come hang out with your old mates, right? Why don’t you come round Charterhouse Lane tomorrow night? We’ve got a crowd of lads from Bartholomew’s, and some devilish good gin punch. Here’s my card!” He pulled the card out of his case by biting the top off, and handed it to Pen.

“Very kind of you, I’m sure,” Pen said in his snootiest nobleman tone, “but I’m afraid I have an out of town engagement tomorrow night.” He slipped the card into his waistcoat pocket, wondering how this oaf had the audacity to hand him a visiting card. Neither of them seemed to remember that Huxter’s dad was a surgeon-apothecary back in Clavering, and Pen’s own late dad had been a surgeon-apothecary in Bath, once upon a time.

“No worries, old man!” Huxter yelled, already half drunk. “Pop round any time you’re passing, I’m home every Saturday, and we always have a full cheese cupboard. Ta ta—there’s the firework bell! Come on, Mary!”
