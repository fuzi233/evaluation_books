The winter rain had penned us within our rock like bees sealed in a hive, and yet the evenings were not bleak. In our rosy study—where the lamplight warmed the stone and made the woven mats shine like new straw—we worked with our hands while our tongues travelled. The boys and Jenny took turns reading and telling tales; their voices rose and fell with the crackle of the fire, and outside the rain wrote its steady, soft percussion on leaf and ledge. Even now, when I shut my eyes, I can hear that season as a kind of household music: the low rasp of knife on reed, the pliant whisper of plaited fibres, the distant breathing of the sea muffled by storm. It was a gentler soundscape than any city’s—no iron wheels, no hammers, no shrill calls sold for coin—only weather, work, and the human voice carrying memory.

Then one morning the rain stopped as if a hand had lifted it away. The air came in clean and young; the whole island seemed to exhale. We stepped out and almost laughed at the thought that we had been “prisoners,” for the very stones were bright with spring, and the sun on wet earth smelled like opened grain.

At once the old rhythms returned: field, garden, orchard. The lads sprang to them with the hunger of bodies long restrained; their mother and Jenny found their own empire again among the poultry and the house. There is a dignity in such labour when it is not forced by any master’s whistle but taken up as one takes up a family dialect—because it is the language of home. Each station, each settlement, was visited and set in order: Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, Shark and Whale Islands. The care of the battery on Shark Island belonged to Jack and Franz, and they set to it as earnestly as if the island’s safety depended on the shine of brass.

That day they repaired the flagstaff, rehoisted the flag, and cleaned the two guns till the metal caught the light. Evening drew down, and the rest of us paced the beach, letting the sea-breeze cool our faces. We watched the boys load and run out the guns; then they paddled the cajack out with an empty tub and left it bobbing as a mark. Back they came, shouted to one another, and fired. The barrel sprang apart in flying staves. Their triumph rang across the water, a boyish cheer tossed into the wind; then, with the careful motions drilled by habit and respect, they cleaned the guns and ran them in.

Scarcely had they finished when, as if the sea itself had answered, three heavy reports boomed from the westward.

We stopped as one body, speechless. For a moment the world was nothing but listening: the late gulls, the hush between waves, and then that distant, unmistakable thunder. Was it fancy? An echo? Had Jack and Franz fired again? No—there they were already leaping into their canoe, faces changed, paddling towards us with strokes that threw little bright knives of water.

“Did you hear them? Did you hear them?” they gasped as they grounded the canoe. “What shall we do? Where shall we go?”

Jack, with his quick heart on his sleeve, seized Fritz’s hands. “It must be a European ship! We shall find her. We shall see our Fatherland once more!”

Till that instant I had not fully understood what a longing had grown in the two young men since Jenny’s arrival—how the sound of her English, the turn of her songs, the very names she used for bread and tea and home, had made “civilized life” less an idea than an ache. Language has that power: it can be a rope thrown across years, and if one catches it, the whole body of memory is pulled.

All eyes turned to me. What would I advise?

“At present,” I said, “we can do nothing. Night is drawing on. We must make what preparation we can, and pray for guidance.”

We returned to the house in a fever of talk, questions crossing questions, hopes tripping over fears. It was not only joy that stirred us, but dread as well: friend or enemy? Help or harm? A European vessel—and with it news, letters, law, the pull of old duties—or a pirate craft, bringing violence into our carefully tended peace. We could not know.

Few slept. The boys and I took turns keeping watch from the verandah. Even there, we measured the night by sound: the sea’s ceaseless heaving, the wind’s sigh among palms, the occasional sharp click of bamboo. We listened for another signal shot, or the stealthy scrape of a boat. But toward midnight the air shifted; the wind rose like a beast wakening. Soon a fearful storm was raging. The surf hammered the reef with a roar that swallowed everything; had a broadside been fired at the bay’s mouth, we should not have heard it through the howling.

For two days and two nights the hurricane held us. When the third morning broke clear and the sea began to lay down its anger, the island looked rinsed and raw-edged, as if newly made. Anxiety drove us as much as hope. If a ship had been near, what had become of her? Driven on the rocks? Forced away to weather the gale at sea? If she left the coast, she might never return.

I could not deny the boys their desire to fire our guns again. I went with Jack and Franz to the fort. The air on Shark Island was sharp with salt and wet stone; the flag tugged and snapped, sounding like cloth applauding. We ran out the guns.

One—two—boom.

We waited, breath held, eyes fixed on empty water. For a few minutes there was only the sea’s own voice and the faint cry of birds. Then, far off, an answering report rolled back over the waves.

There was no longer room for doubt. Strangers were still near, and they knew we were here.

We waved the flag and returned quickly to Rockburg. The whole family met us in a tumult. I tried to calm them, for I could not answer all they asked, nor could I honestly promise that this visit would be a blessing. Sometimes Providence comes with a sharp edge.

Fritz and I prepared at once to reconnoitre. We armed ourselves—guns, pistols, cutlasses—took the spy-glass, and seated ourselves in the cajack. My wife’s parting words were not theatrical; they were the plain, steady caution of one who knows what a life costs to build. We paddled out of Safety Bay and rounded the high cliffs to the left, keeping our strokes quiet, letting the water take the sound of them.

For nearly an hour we advanced towards where the guns had spoken. Nothing appeared but frowning rock, the restless waves beating at its base, and the long moan of sea-caves that made the coast seem to breathe. At last we reached Cape Pug-Nose and began to round its blunt old face.

In a moment our doubts were dispelled. Joy—and gratitude to the Great Giver of all good—filled us so suddenly that it almost hurt. Beyond the cape, in a little sheltered cove, lay a brig-of-war with sails furled and anchor down. At her masthead flew the English colours.

Through the glass I could make out figures on deck; on shore beyond, several tents were pitched under trees, and smoke rose in pale ribbons. Yet even in that gladness, a misgiving pricked me. What was an English vessel doing so far from the usual track? I remembered tales—told in ports with lowered voices—of mutinous crews, of ships disguised, of disciplined names used to cover lawless hands.

Fritz, peering, burst out, “I can see the captain, father—he’s speaking to an officer. I can see his face. He is English; I am certain. The flag speaks the truth!”

He passed me the glass again. I kept under the cliff’s shelter and examined the vessel slowly, like a man tasting before he swallows. The decks were spotless, the steel and brass burnished, the whole air of ship and camp full of order. Authority and discipline showed themselves in small things: the line of a hammock, the spacing of men, the clean geometry of ropes. My fears eased.

We watched longer, then decided to return without betraying our presence. I would not appear before strangers until we could do so with some form—so that we would not be mistaken for beggars clinging to a wreck, but be seen as what we were: a family that had made a life, a household with laws and plenty, lords and servants of our own land.

At Rockburg they waited like a jury for our report. We told all we had seen. They approved our caution. Even Jenny—whose eyes had brightened at “English colours,” whose longing for her father and her native speech rose in every breath—agreed to wait until the next day, when our yacht could be set in order and we could present ourselves with dignity.

The rest of the day became its own kind of feast, though not of eating: the feast of preparation. Our dainty little craft was made to look her best. Decks scrubbed till the wood smelled sweet and clean; brass guns burnished until they caught the sun; all lumber removed and carried ashore; the flag of England hoisted to her peak, snapping crisply in the wind like a clear syllable spoken out loud.

My wife overhauled our wardrobes with the same care she gave to sorting seeds for planting—choosing what would thrive in the eyes of others. Neatest uniforms were laid out for the boys and me. Daily work had dressed us in what comfort required: wide-brimmed hats, linen coats and trousers, broad leathern belts, hairy buskins—clothes made for soil and sweat. But we should have astonished a man-o’-war’s crew if we had appeared in that rough guise, and in truth it was right to show respect in the language of dress, as one respects a guest by setting out clean linen and a well-cooked dish.

At the break of that eventful morning—the morning destined to return human voices other than our own to our ears—we assembled in the little breakfast-room. The meal was hurried and almost silent. Food has always been more than fuel to us; it is the island’s conversation made edible, the season translated into the mouth. Yet now even the fragrance of warm bread and fruit could not slow our thoughts. The island outside seemed to listen with us, the birds quieted, the surf keeping time like a distant drum.

Fritz and Jack slipped to the garden and returned with baskets of the choicest fruits—fresh, fragrant profusion: skins still cool from dawn, leaves beaded, perfume rising as the baskets shifted. We meant them as presents, a first offering of our island’s generosity—sustenance as greeting, not gold as bribe.

We went on board the yacht. Anchor weighed, sails set. The canoe followed in tow. Our little vessel, as if sharing our hopes, bounded over the waters of Safety Bay, gave the Reef a wide berth—those frowning rocks where the sea still lashed to foam—and kept away for the cove beyond Pug-Nose. When we rounded the point and came within hail, the strangers’ surprise was almost visible, like a sudden lift of heads.

Every eye on ship and shore turned to us; every glass was produced and fixed upon our motions. A pleasure yacht, manned by such a party, cruising this inhospitable coast—such a sight had no place in their expectations.

Fritz and I stepped into our boat and pulled for the brig. In another minute we were on her deck. The captain, with the plain frankness of a British seaman, welcomed us cordially. He led us into his cabin and begged to know what fortune had brought residents to a coast generally thought uninhabited or the haunt of fierce savages.

I gave him an outline of the wreck and our years upon these shores. I spoke also of Miss Montrose, and of the providential manner in which we had rescued her from loneliness.

At that name the captain rose and grasped Fritz by the hand. “Then let me thank you,” he said, “in my own name and in that of Colonel Montrose. It was the hope of finding some trace of that brave girl that brought me to these shores. The disappearance of the Dorcas has been a terrible blow to the Colonel; and yet, though for three years no word has reached England of her or any who sailed in her, he has never wholly abandoned hope.

“A few weeks ago, when I was about to leave Sydney for the Cape, I found three men who declared themselves survivors of the Dorcas. They said their boat—one of four that left the wreck—was the only one, to their knowledge, that reached land in safety. From them I learned particulars. I applied for permission to cruise in these latitudes, and sailed in hopes of finding further traces of the unfortunate crew. My efforts have been rewarded beyond expectation.”

Fritz answered modestly, colour rising in his cheeks at praise that felt too large for him. The captain then asked to be introduced to my wife and Miss Montrose.

“And,” he added, “if it be not contrary to your rules of discipline for the whole ship’s company to be absent at once, I will send a boat for the remainder of your party.”

An officer was dispatched to the yacht. Soon my wife, Jenny, and the three younger boys were aboard the brig. The captain and his officers vied in doing us honour; their manners were easy, cheerful, and curious without being rude. Time passed rapidly, stitched together by questions and answers, by laughter, and by that peculiar comfort of hearing one’s own language echoed back after long solitude—though even within “English” and “German” there were differences of phrase and accent, reminding me that no speech carries experience whole. A word may point, but it cannot pour the entire taste of a life into another person’s mouth.

At luncheon the captain told us that an invalid gentleman, Mr. Wolston, had sailed with him from Sydney, with his wife and two daughters. The sea voyage had been recommended for his health; yet the storm that drove the Unicorn into the bay for repairs had shaken him cruelly, and he had begged to rest on land.

We were eager to meet them, and in the afternoon we went ashore to the tents pitched under shady trees. Mr. Wolston sat by one, enjoying the cool sea-breeze. His face showed that peculiar thinness of long illness, but his eyes were bright with interest. He and his family were delighted to see us; conversation came readily, and the hours slipped away so fast that evening found us still there. It was too late to return to Rockburg. The captain offered tents for those who could not find room in the yacht. The boys, delighted with the novelty of the camp and the ship’s quiet night-sounds—ropes creaking, waves brushing the hull like soft hands—spent the night on land.

That night my wife and I spoke long and seriously. The question lay between us like a chart spread out: Did we truly wish to return to Europe? Was it anything more than the sudden pull of opportunity, like a bright trinket dangled before the eye?

We had both feared to speak first, lest we press the other against a secret desire. But slowly, gently, as one coaxes a careful truth into daylight, we found we were of one mind. Neither of us felt any strong wish to leave the island’s peace. The real longing beneath our hearts was to adopt New Switzerland as our home henceforward.

There is little more delightful than discovering such harmony where a momentous decision is to be made. My dear wife said she desired nothing more than to spend the rest of her days in the place to which she had grown so attached—provided I, and at least two of her sons, also wished to remain.

From the other two she would part, if they chose to return to Europe, on one condition: that they would endeavour to send out emigrants of good class to join us and form a prosperous colony. She added that she thought the island ought still to bear the name of our native country, even if in future it sheltered colonists from England as well as from Switzerland. Names, too, are a kind of memory; to change them lightly is to let the past be mislaid.

I approved warmly, and we agreed to consult Captain Littlestone about placing the island under Britain’s protection. Then came the question of which sons were best suited to stay, and which to go. We left it undecided, trusting that in a few days their own natures would declare themselves.

They did so sooner than we expected.

After breakfast it was proposed that Captain Littlestone should bring his ship round to Safety Bay, that he and his party might visit us at Rockburg. We also invited Mr. Wolston and his family, hoping that a comfortable residence ashore would benefit his health.

No sooner was the plan adopted than Fritz and Jack hurried off in the canoe to prepare for our guests’ reception. The brig and our yacht followed more leisurely.

What words can express our visitors’ amazement as, rounding the rocky cape at the entrance, Safety Bay opened before them, and beyond it the ordered beauty of Rockburg—our lawns, our avenues, our shrubberies, the marks of long, patient cultivation where they had expected wilderness?

Greater still was their astonishment when eleven guns boomed from the battery on Shark Island and the royal standard of England floated majestically on the morning breeze. The reports rolled across the water and came back from the cliffs with a measured dignity that felt, to my ear, like a ceremony restoring us to the human world.

Surprise and pleasure lit every face. Poor Wolston’s spirits revived at the sight, as if the very idea of peace and happiness here acted as medicine.

He was carried ashore with great care and established in my room. A camp-bed for Mrs. Wolston was added so she could attend him conveniently. Meanwhile the harbour and all round Rockburg became a living scene—merriment, calling voices, quick footsteps, exclamations at every discovery. Our quiet domain filled with foreign laughter and the shuffle of boots; it was not unpleasant, but it altered the island’s acoustics, as if a new instrument had been added to the ensemble.

A summons to dinner scarcely caught attention, so eager were they to explore. But a visit to Falconhurst being projected, the company at last consented to be seated and partake of our good cheer. The young people soon grew restless again, roaming our lawns and avenues until I was half persuaded their number had tripled.

Towards evening the excitement ebbed, and we assembled for supper with tolerable composure. Mr. Wolston joined us—rested, and brightened by hope. He expressed plainly a wish, in his own name and his wife’s, to make a long stay among us; and he proposed that they, with their eldest daughter (also delicate), should remain, while the younger daughter went for the present to her brother at the Cape of Good Hope. If he ultimately decided to settle altogether, he would have his son leave the Cape and join our colony.

With sincere satisfaction I welcomed the proposal, saying it was my wish and my wife’s to remain in New Switzerland for the rest of our days.

“Hurrah for New Switzerland! New Switzerland for ever!” shouted the company, raising their glasses. The touch of glass upon glass rang out—clear, bright, almost like small bells—one of those sounds that carry unanimity better than any speech.

“Prosperity to New Switzerland; long may she flourish,” echoed on all sides.

“Long life and happiness to those who make New Switzerland their home!” added Ernest, to my great surprise, leaning forward to ring his glass with mine, his mother’s, and Mr. Wolston’s.

“Won’t somebody wish long life and prosperity to those who go away?” Jenny asked with a pretty, arch look. “Much as I long to return to England and my father, my inclination will waver if all the cheers are for New Switzerland!”

“Three cheers for England and Colonel Montrose,” cried Fritz, “success and happiness to us who return to Europe!” The vaulted roofs rang with the cheer; and Jenny’s glance thanked him for understanding that her longing was not a rejection of us, but the pull of a name and a voice that had been her first home.

When silence returned, I said, “Since Fritz resolves to go to England, he must undertake for me the duty of bringing happiness to a mourning father by restoring to him this dear daughter—whom I have been ready to regard as my own, by right of finding her cast on the shores of my island.

“Ernest chooses to remain with me. His mother and I rejoice in this decision, and promise him all the highest scientific appointments in our power to bestow.

“And now—what is Jack’s choice? The only talent I can say he possesses is that of a comic actor, and to shine on the stage he must needs go to Europe.”

Jack laughed outright. “Jack is not going to Europe, however. He means to stay here; and when Fritz is gone, he will be the best rider and the best shot in New Switzerland—that is the summit of his ambition. The fact is,” he added, grinning, “I stand in awe of their European schools, and I should expect to find myself caught and clapped into one if I ventured too near them.”

“A good school is exactly what I want,” said Franz. “Among many students there is emulation and enthusiasm, and I shall have a chance of rising in the world. Fritz will probably return here some day; but it might be well for one of us to go home with the intention of remaining there altogether. As I am the youngest, I could more easily adapt myself to a different life. My father, however, will decide for me.”

“You may go, my dear son,” I replied. “God bless all our plans and resolutions. The whole earth is the Lord’s, and where, in His sight, you lead good and useful lives—there is your home.

“And now that I know your wishes, the only question is whether Captain Littlestone will enable you to carry them out.”

All eyes turned to him. After a brief pause the gallant officer spoke.

“I think my way is perfectly clear. I consider I have been providentially guided to place this family again in communication with their friends and the civilized world. My orders were to search for a shipwrecked crew. Survivors from two wrecks have been discovered. Three passengers wish to leave my ship here instead of at the Cape, while at the same time I am requested to give three persons a passage to England. Could anything suit better? I am willing to undertake the charge of those committed to my care.

“Every circumstance has been wonderfully linked by Divine Providence; and if England gains a prosperous and happy colony, it will be a fitting clasp to this fortunate chain of events. Three cheers for New Switzerland.”

Deep emotion moved through us as we separated for the night. Many felt themselves on the threshold of a new life. For me, a weight rolled away; I thanked God that a difficulty which had oppressed me for years was solved.

After that, nothing was thought of but preparations for the departure of the dear ones bound for England. Captain Littlestone gave as much time as he could, but it was necessarily short; so for several days our settlement filled with ceaseless movement and industry. We packed everything that could add to our children’s comfort on the voyage or help them upon arrival. A large share of my possessions—pearls, corals, furs, spices, and other valuables—would enable them to take a good place in commerce. We also stored the Unicorn with fresh provisions: fish, vegetables, fruit—gifts that carried the island’s flavour and the labour of our hands.

To their care I committed private papers, money, and jewels that had belonged to the captain of our ill-fated ship, begging them to deliver these, if possible, to his heirs. I gave Captain Littlestone a short account of the wreck, with the names of the crew from a list I had found.

And in a long conversation with my sons—spoken not for show but as one passes on a precious, fragile thing—I charged them solemnly with the responsibilities of their future lives: duty toward God, toward their fellow men, and toward themselves. I named the temptations likely to beset their differing characters, and exhorted them affectionately to hold fast to the faith in which they had been brought up, even when surrounded by voices and words that might try, by their very dominance, to rename what they were. For a man may survive shipwreck and hunger, and still be undone by forgetting the language of his own conscience.
