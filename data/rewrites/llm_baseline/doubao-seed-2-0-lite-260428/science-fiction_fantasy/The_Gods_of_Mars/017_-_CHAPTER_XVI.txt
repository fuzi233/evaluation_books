The four of us—Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I—stood rooted to the red dust, staring up at the glimmering Helium battleship that hung low over the hills, the very symbol of the home we had all thought lost forever, when a second, then a third massive hull crested the ridgeline and glided gracefully down to join its sister. Before we could speak, a score of one-man air scouts launched from the nearest vessel’s upper decks, streaking down in long, screaming dives to fan out and form a ring of sharp, cold steel around us.

An officer stepped forward to bark the order for our capture, then his gaze locked on Carthoris. He gasped, surprise bursting over his face, and he bounded forward to clap both hands to the prince’s shoulders. “Carthoris! My Prince, Kaor! Kaor!” he cried, grinning so wide his fangs showed. “Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris and John Carter. Where have you been? All Helium has wept for you these months. Calamity after calamity has crushed our great nation from the day you vanished.”

“Grieve no more, good Hor Vastus,” Carthoris laughed, turning to gesture at me. “I do not come home alone. I bring back the saviour of Barsoom, John Carter, Prince of Helium.”

Hor Vastus spun to face me, and went pale as a Thern’s robes, swaying where he stood like he might collapse from shock. “John Carter—” he started, then the words died on his lips. I knew the unspoken question hanging in the air: How are you here? You walked the banks of the Lost Sea of Korus, you stepped foot in the Valley Dor, you drank from the waters of Iss. No man returns from that. The loyal man would never force me to say it out loud. He shook off his stunned pause and bowed low. “That you are here is all that matters. Let Hor Vastus’ sword be the first to do you honour.”

He unbuckled his scabbard and laid his blade at my feet. To any red Martian, the act held the weight of a sacred, unbreakable oath: *My sword, my body, my life, my soul are yours, now and after death. Your word is my only truth. Whoever raises a hand to you answers to me*. No man save the most legendary, chivalrous Jeddaks of old had ever received that tribute from a warrior, and I knew what my response had to be. I bent to lift his blade, pressed the hilt to my lips in respect, then buckled it back at his hip with my own hands. “Hor Vastus, I will never ask you to draw this sword for anything but truth, justice, and the good of Barsoom,” I told him, and he nodded, fierce and proud, like he had never doubted it for a heartbeat.

As we spoke, shuttles darted between the ground and the battleship hull overhead, and a ten-person flier drifted down to land light as a feather beside us. Its captain saluted Hor Vastus. “Kantos Kan orders your party brought immediately to the deck of the *Xavarian*,” he said. That was when I noticed Thuvia was not among us. No one had seen her since Carthoris had sent her galloping away on her madly spurring thoat toward the hills to get her out of harm’s way. Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen scouts to comb every ridge and ravine for her at once, but a cold little knot of dread settled in my gut.

We stepped aboard the shuttle, and minutes later were greeted on the *Xavarian*’s deck by my old friend Kantos Kan himself. He had risen to command all of Helium’s aerial forces, but he was the same wild, loyal comrade who had starved beside me in a Warhoon dungeon, fought beside me through the bloody excesses of the Great Games, and run the gauntlet of Zodanga’s defences to rescue Dejah Thoris so many years before. He did not ask me where I had been. He did not want to hear the answer, not yet. He hugged Carthoris like he was his own son, clapped Tars Tarkas on the back, and told us that the whole of Helium had wept ten million tears the day word came the young prince was lost.

“Wait,” I said, his last line niggling at me. “What did you mean, ‘his poor mother’?”

He drew me aside, his face grim. “Ever since you did not return from the atmosphere plant, Dejah Thoris has carried that grief. The only thing that softened the blow was her new son—Carthoris hatched from his shell the very night you vanished. But when the boy disappeared, that last spark of hope went out. Expedition after expedition came back empty handed, no clue where he’d gone, and she started fading. All of us thought she would waste away in weeks. As a last resort, Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak took two full fleets to comb every inch of Barsoom’s northern hemisphere a month ago. We haven’t heard a word from them in two weeks. Rumour says every last ship was lost. Zat Arras, the Jed of Zodanga, has been pressuring Dejah Thoris to marry her ever since. Six days ago he met with her in secret, and the next day she was gone. She took a dozen of her guard, her whole household staff, and Sola—Tars Tarkas’ daughter—with her. No note, no message. Everyone knows what that means. She took the voluntary pilgrimage down the Iss, to join you in the Valley Dor. Zat Arras has been leading this whole fleet on a useless search for her ever since.”

My blood turned to ice. I knew exactly what horrors waited for any woman fool enough to reach the banks of the Iss, the plant men, the white apes, the lying, cruel Therns. I was just about to demand Kantos Kan send every ship he had to hunt for her when a dispatch flier dropped from the fleet flagship, carrying an order directly to Kantos Kan from Zat Arras.

“Zat Arras commands us to bring you all before him as prisoners,” Kantos Kan spat, furious. “He has no right. He should be down here kneeling to honour the Prince of Helium, not summoning us like common thieves.”

“Zat Arras has hated me since the day I beat him in the pits of Zodanga,” I said, grinning. “Nothing would make him happier than to humiliate me before he kills me. Let’s go give him the chance to try.”

We boarded the small flier, and moments later stepped onto the deck of Zat Arras’ flagship. He did not smile, he did not bow, he did not even greet his missing prince Carthoris, his face cold and haughty. “Why were these prisoners not disarmed?” he snapped at Kantos Kan.

“They are not prisoners,” Kantos Kan shot back. “Two of them are of Helium’s royal blood. Tars Tarkas is our oldest, most valued ally. The fourth is John Carter. That is all the justification I need.”

“Not enough for me,” Zat Arras snarled. “Speak, John Carter. Where have you been?”

“I came from the Valley Dor, from the land of the false gods the Therns call the Land of the First Born,” I said plainly.

He lit up, like I had handed him the rope to hang myself. “So you do not deny it? You returned from the bosom of Iss, you blasphemer! Do not think you will fool us with your lies to save your cowardly carcass—”

No man calls John Carter a coward and a liar and lives to boast of it. I was across the deck before any of his guards could move, one hand locked tight around his throat, hauling him half over my knee. “I don’t care if I came from heaven or hell, Zat Arras,” I growled in his ear. “No man speaks that way to me and walks away without begging forgiveness.”

His officers lunged to pull me off, but Kantos Kan was at my side in an instant, hissing in my ear. “Stop, John Carter! My whole crew will turn on them to defend you. We’ll have mutiny, civil war, the whole of Helium tearing itself apart while Dejah Thoris is lost. For the sake of the Jeddaks, for the sake of Barsoom, stop.”

I released him, turned my back on the shaking, sputtering Jed, and walked for the rail. “Come, Kantos Kan. The Prince of Helium is returning to the *Xavarian*.” No one dared lay a hand on us. One old officer who had served Tardos Mors for forty years leaned in to murmur as I passed, “My blade is yours, John Carter.” I nodded my thanks, and we boarded the flier back.

Fifteen minutes later, we received our orders: the whole fleet was sailing for Helium. The trip was quiet, heavy with dread. Carthoris and I were both sick with fear for Dejah Thoris. Kantos Kan knew Zat Arras was bound to invoke the old, terrible law that condemned anyone who returned from the Valley Dor to a hideous public death. Tars Tarkas grieved for his lost daughter Sola. Only Xodar, the fugitive First Born, was unconcerned. “What do I have to lose?” he shrugged. “At least we’ll all die with good red Martian blood on our blades.”

The crew of the *Xavarian* was already splitting into factions long before we sighted Helium’s mile-high crimson spires. Half the men clustered around us whenever they could, loyal to their prince out of years of love and shared memory. The other half kept their distance, polite but distant, their eyes full of superstitious horror. I could not blame them. The old religion of Iss and Korus had held Barsoom in its grip for ten thousand years, and I could not ask them to throw that all away overnight for the word of a man who came back from the dead.

We descended in wide, slow circles over Helium’s naval docks, and the streets below were packed shoulder to shoulder with millions of people, notified by radio-aerogram of our arrival. We were transferred to a tiny, unmarked flier, not to be paraded down the broad Avenue of Ancestors like heroes or condemned criminals, but flown directly to the roof landing pad of the Temple of Reward—where Martian justice is meted out, to decorate heroes and condemn felons alike. Zat Arras dared not let the crowd see us, afraid their love for Carthoris and me would override their superstitious fear and tear his whole scheme apart.

They locked us in a south-facing chamber, looking out over the five mile stretch of the Avenue of Ancestors all the way to the far Gate of Jeddaks. The crowds below stood silent, no jeers, no cheers, many of them covering their faces and weeping when they spotted us at the window. Late that afternoon, a messenger arrived from Zat Arras, bearing our formal notice: we would be tried by a council of noble judges in the temple’s great hall at the first zode of the following morning, roughly 8:40 A.M. Earth time.
