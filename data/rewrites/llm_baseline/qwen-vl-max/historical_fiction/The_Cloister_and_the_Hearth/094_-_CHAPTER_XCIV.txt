Her manner was one that stirred compassion rather than fear in any heart not hardened by prejudice. Her chest rose and fell like a bird’s, her cheeks flushed with shifting hues of red and pale white, and she pressed her hand against the wall instinctively, as timid creatures do when trembling. The look in her eyes—deep purple and full of love, reverence, sorrow, and tender remembrance—was fixed upon the thin, gaunt figure of the hermit, and it was a moment rare in nature.

“Aha!” he exclaimed. “At last thou comest—flesh and blood! As thou camest to holy Anthony. But I know thee. I suspected thy schemes were not yet spent. I am prepared.”

With these words, he snatched up his small crucifix and held it out before her, his hand shaking violently, while in the other he clutched a candle. “I exorcise thee!”

“Oh, no!” she pleaded, holding out both hands gently. “Do not harm me! It is Margaret.”

“Liar!” he shouted. “Margaret was fair, but not so surpassingly fair as thou. Thou flinched at that sacred name—thou cunning deceiver! In the name of God, I exorcise thee!”

“Oh, Jesus!” she gasped, overwhelmed with dread. “Do not curse me! I will go back. I thought I might come. For mercy’s sake, do not turn me away! Oh, Gerard—is this how we meet after all? After all?”

She sank almost to her knees, sobbing from deep distress and wounded affection. Though steeped in fear of evil spirits, he might still have doubted whether this gentle creature, so clearly human and real, was not a mere illusion or a trick of the dark. Yet her heartfelt cry to the divine name broke through his delusion, letting him see and hear with clear senses. He gave a cry of regret and reached for her—but weakened by fasting and emotion, he could not lift her.

“What?” he panted, bending over her. “Is it truly you? And did I greet you with harsh words? Alas!”

They stood silent for a time, choked by feeling, unable to speak.

“I care little,” said Margaret bravely, struggling against tears. “You took me for another—for something terrible. Oh, oh, oh!”

“Forgive me, sweet soul,” he whispered. “Since I came here, I have been sorely tempted by darkness.”

She looked around with a shudder. “Then take my hand, and let me lead you from this place.”

He stared at her in astonishment. “Leave my cell? Return to the world? Is that why you came?”

“Yes, Gerard,” she said softly. “I have come to bring you home—to your vicarage in Gouda. You are now the priest there, thanks to Heaven and your brother Giles. Your mother and I have made it warm and welcoming. It is comfortable enough in winter, I promise. But wait till spring comes—the hawthorn blooms, and the walls grow bright with roses and sweet woodbine. Have we forgotten you? Or the things you loved? And dear Gerard, your mother waits—she should not be out of bed so late. Please, please, come with me. And once we leave this place, I’ll show you a treasure you’ve gained without knowing it. Oh, what have I said to cause such pain?”

He had drawn himself into a tight, fearful posture, watching her with a gaze half filled with suspicion, half with dread.

“Poor child,” he said solemnly, though deeply troubled. “Would you have me risk both our souls for a quiet house and its flowers? This is not your doing—some cruel force sends you, poor innocent, with this lure. But I shall unmask it even through you, and you shall see it and loathe it as I do. Margaret, my lost love—why am I here? Because I love you.”

“No, Gerard,” she replied, “you do not love me—or you would not have hidden from me. There was no need.”

“Let there be no falsehood between us,” he said, “who once loved so faithfully—and who may never meet again on earth.”

“God forbid!” she answered. “I love you. And if you had forgotten me, you would have married long ago. You are the one who found me—buried here, unseen by men. You are a priest, a monk. What good can come from our living near each other, nurturing a passion once pure, but now—by Heaven’s will—unholy? No, though my heart breaks, I must stand firm. I am the man now; I am the priest. We must not meet again—until I am taught by solitude, and you are joined to another.”

“I accept my fate, but not yours,” she said. “I would rather die ten times than let you stay in this lonely place, when a kind home awaits you.”

Clement groaned. With every word she spoke, two truths grew clearer: his duty, and the agony it would cost.

“My beloved,” he said, voice thick with tenderness and resolve, “I bless you for giving me one more glimpse of your face. May God forgive you and bless you for breaking in a moment the peace I built over six months of silence. Still, a year of penance shall restore me to calm. My poor Margaret, I seem cruel—but I am kind. It is best we part—now.”

“Part, Gerard? Never. We have seen what parting brings. Part? You haven’t heard half my tale—not even a tenth. I do not bring you here merely for comfort. Listen!”

“I cannot,” he said. “Even your voice is a temptation—its music, its memories.”

“But I say you must listen,” she insisted. “For I leave this place unheard.”

“Then we must part by other means,” he said sadly.

“What other way? Would you shut me out, being stronger?”

“No, Margaret,” he said, “I know well I would endure many deaths before laying a hand upon you. Your body is dearer to me than my own. But a thousand times dearer are our immortal souls—both yours and mine. I have resisted this trial long enough. Now I must flee it. Farewell. Farewell.”

He moved toward the door, opened it, and stepped halfway out—when she darted after him and seized his arm.

“No,” she said. “Another must speak for me. I meant to reward you for yielding to me—but since you are unkind, I need help. Turn this way for a moment.”

“No,” he said.

“But I say yes. Then turn your back on us—if you can.”

She loosened her grip slightly, thinking he would not refuse such a small request. But at that moment, he saw his chance and grasped it.

“Run, Clement, run!” he cried, and in a burst of strength born of devotion, he tore himself from her grasp and fled. A few steps later, he leapt across the narrow stream and ran beside it. When he realized she did not follow, he stopped and turned back.

She lay face down, arms stretched out. He had knocked her down, perhaps hurt her.

When he saw this, he groaned and took a step forward—then suddenly threw himself into the icy water.

“There,” he cried, “destroy my body—but save my soul!”

Standing waist-deep in cold water, he watched as Margaret rose slowly to her knees. She was pale, her eyes glistening in the night. Then, in a voice broken by grief, she called:

“Oh, God! You who know all—You see how I am treated. Forgive me. For I will not live another day.”

She sprang to her feet and ran past him, close to where he hid, screaming:

In one cry, so much anguish poured forth—wounded love, wounded pride, despair, and the edge of madness.

Clement heard it—and his heart froze with terror and remorse, worse than the cold water numbing his bones. He knew he had driven her away forever. In the midst of his grim triumph, a fierce impulse rose within him—to curse the Church, to curse faith itself, for demanding such cruelty from a mortal soul.

At last, half-drowned, he crawled from the water and staggered back to his cave.

“I am safe here,” he groaned. “She will never come near me again. Unworthy, ungrateful wretch that I am.”

He collapsed onto the floor, not without a secret hope that he might never rise again.

But soon he noticed the hourglass—midnight had passed. Slowly, he removed his wet clothes, moaning at the pain he had caused the one he loved. Then he put on the old hermit’s cilice—a rough garment of bristles—and over it, a breastplate. He had never worn either before, doubting himself worthy of such armor. But now he needed all aid. The bristles might distract his sorrow with bodily pain; the breastplate might carry some holy virtue.

He knelt and prayed humbly, asking God to free him that very night from the burden of flesh. Then he lit all his candles and recited his psalter, each word falling like lead from a heavy heart. Through it all, he moaned quietly.

Suddenly, he noticed a small bundle in the corner, unnoticed before. At one end, something glimmered—like gold woven into silk. He approached, and as he looked closer, he raised his hands in joy.

“It is a seraph,” he whispered. “A lovely seraph. Heaven has witnessed my trial and approved my sacrifice. This heavenly flower is sent to comfort me, fainting under my burden.”

He knelt, gazing at the golden hair, the soft skin, the cheeks like peach blossoms.

“Let me feast my eyes on you before you leave for your blessed home—and my cave grows dark once more, as it did when she left.”

But the hermit startled the child. The boy opened wide eyes—blue as the sky—and seeing a strange figure kneeling over him, cried:

Tears streamed down his tiny cheeks.

Perhaps, after months without seeing a human face, Clement judged childhood beauty more truly than others could. This northern child, with his long golden hair, seemed far more angelic than any imagined figure of heaven.

Yet the spell was broken—not unhappily. Clement, who had always loved children, found his heart touched by the child’s innocence, his sorrow, his tearful plea for his mother.

He used all his gentleness to soothe him. The boy, though young, was remarkably perceptive. Soon, the cries ceased, replaced by wonder. Quietly, he studied the wild-looking figure before him—strange, yet kind; armored, yet harmless.

Clement wondered how the child had come to lie there, glowing in his dark cave. Then he remembered—he had left the door open. He concluded that someone, perhaps in desperation, had placed the child there, seeking to rid themselves of him.

At this, his heart ached for the abandoned child. Tears welled in his eyes—pure tenderness. Even beneath the mother’s fault, he saw divine mercy guiding her act to ease his own sorrow.

“Now bless you, bless you, bless you, sweet innocent,” he said. “I would not trade you for an angel in heaven.”

“'Tis pooty,” the child replied, dismissing the praise with childish indifference.

“What is pretty here, besides you?” Clement asked.

“Ookum-gars,” the boy said, pointing to the breastplate.

“Quot liberi, tot sententiunculae!” Hector’s child once screamed at his father’s shining helmet. Here, a medieval boy was enchanted by polished armor, and his sadness eased.

“There are prettier things,” Clement said. “There are little birds. Do you love birds?”

“Nay. Ay. En um ittle, ery ittle? Not ike torks. Hate torks um bigger an baby.”

He confessed, in broken speech, that storks frightened him—great wings flapping, darkening his days.

“Ay, but my birds are small and good—oh, so pretty!”

“Den I ikes 'm,” the child declared. “I ont my mammy.”

“Oh, sweet dove,” Clement sighed. “I fear I must take your place. Do you have a daddy as well as a mommy?”

The conversation, considering their ages and circumstances, was unlike any ever held between mortals. And just as he asked, an unseen listener heard every word—with tenfold intensity.

So now, you must see through her eyes, hear through her ears.

Margaret, running past him, was nearly undone. She was in the state where mothers have killed their children—sometimes themselves too. She rushed to Reicht Heynes, pale and trembling, and clung to her neck.

“Oh, Reicht! Oh, Reicht!” she gasped, unable to speak further.

Reicht kissed her and began to weep. Even the great mastiff whined—a sign of sorrow.

“Oh, Reicht!” Margaret moaned. “See how he has treated me!” She showed her bleeding hands from falling on the stones.

“He threw me down—so eager to escape. He took me for a devil. Said I came to tempt him. Am I the woman to tempt a man? You know me, Reicht.”

“Nay, indeed, sweet Mistress Margaret—the last person in the world.”

“And he would not look at my child. I’ll throw myself and him into the river tonight.”

“Oh, fie! Fie! Sweet woman, don’t speak so. Is any man worth your child’s life?”

“My child! Where is he? Why, Reicht—I left him behind. Oh, shame! Can I love him so much as to forget my own child? Ah, I am rightly punished.”

She sat down, and Reicht joined her. They wept together.

After a while, Margaret stopped crying and said firmly, “Let us go home.”

“But the bairn?” Reicht asked.

“Oh, he is well where he is. My heart turns against my own child. He cares nothing for him—wouldn’t see him, wouldnere speak of him. I brought him proudly, dressed him nicely, combed his hair. Nay, turn about—it’s his child as well. Let him keep it awhile. Perhaps that will teach him to care for its mother—and for himself.”

“High words on an empty stomach,” Reicht said. “Time will tell. Come home.”

They started off, but at the second step, Margaret stopped—unable to move.

“Reicht,” she whispered, “what else have I left? I cannot.”

“Who said you could? Think you I paid attention? Words are breath. Go back for him. Time to rest.”

Reicht led the way. Margaret followed—then stopped again as they neared the cave.

“Reicht,” she said, “go ask him—will he give me back my boy? I cannot bear to see him.”

“Alas, mistress,” Reicht sighed, “this seems a sorry end to all that has passed between you. Does your heart whisper no excuse for him? Do you truly hate him, whom you waited so long for? Oh, weary world!”

“Hate him, Reicht? I would not harm a hair of his head. But I cannot look at him. I have grown afraid of him. Oh, when I think of all I suffered for him, and what I came here to do—bringing my darling to call him father. Ah, Luke, my poor chap—your wound shows mine. I thought too little of your pain, whose true love I scorned. And now mine is scorned. If the poor lad were here now, he’d have a fair chance.”

“Well, he is not far off,” Reicht said—though not with cheer.

“Speak not of men to me,” Margaret said bitterly. “I hate them all.”

“For the sake of one?”

“Flout me not. Prithee, go forward and get me what is mine—my only joy.”

Reicht went ahead. Margaret sat by the roadside, covering her face with her apron, rocking herself as women of her land did, her soul full of bitterness.

So great was her inner struggle, she did not hear Reicht return—nor feel her hand on her shoulder.

“Mistress Margaret!” Reicht said quietly. “Take a fool’s advice—go softly to that cave, with all the ears and eyes your mother gave you.”

“Why, Reicht?” she stammered. “I thought the cave was burning—it was so bright inside. And I heard voices.”

“Voices?”

“Ay, not one, but two—unlike—one man’s, one child’s—talking as pleasant as you and I.”

“I’m no expert at peeping,” Reicht admitted. “But if voices are speaking in that cave, and they’re near as those are to you, I’d crawl on my belly like a serpent before I missed a word.”

“Whisht, Reicht! Bless thee! Stay here. Kiss me! Pray for me!”

And before the words faded, Margaret was flying toward the hermitage, silent as a lapwing.

Near the cave, she crouched—her movements smooth, quiet, almost snake-like. She reached the door and found a crack. She listened.

She fought hard not to rush in. Warned by failure, she was cautious.

After a while, she slowly lifted her head—like a serpent raising its crest—and looked through the widest gap.

The boy, asked if he had a father, first shook his head. But when pressed, he remembered:

“Dad-da ill man; run away and left poor mum-ma.”

She who heard winced. It was news to both.

Some careless woman had told the child this—and now it came out, right before Gerard.

His reply surprised her. He cried:

“The villain! The monster! To abandon you, sweet one. He knows not the joy he turned his back on. Well, my little dove, I must be father and mother to you—since one ran away, and the other left you to me. Tomorrow, I shall ask those who bring my food to bring milk and eggs. Bread is good for old me—but not for you. And I shall teach you to read.”

“I can yead, I can yead.”

“Ay, so young? All the better. We shall read good books. I shall show you the way to heaven. Heaven is beautiful—far fairer than earth. There are little cherubs like you, in white, glad to welcome you. Would you like to go to heaven one day?”

“Ay, along wi'-my-mammy.”

“Not without her?”

“Nay. I ont my mammy. Where is my mammy?”

(Oh, what it cost poor Margaret not to rush in and hold him!)

“Well, fret not, sweetheart. Perhaps she will come when you sleep. Will you be good and sleep now?”

“I not eepy. Ikes to talk.”

“Then let us talk. Tell me your name.”

“Baby.”

He blinked at the giant’s ignorance.

“Hast none other?”

“Nay.”

“What shall I do to please you, baby? Shall I tell you a story?”

“I ikes tories,” he clapped.

“Or sing you a song?”

“I ikes tongs,” he said, growing excited.

“Choose—song or story.”

“Ting I a tong. Nay, tell I a tory. Nay, ting I a tong. Nay—”

His mouth drooped—he couldn’t choose.

Then his face cleared.

“Ting I a tory.”

“Sing you a story, baby? Well, why not? Sit on my knee?”

“Yea.”

“Then I must remove this breastplate—it’s too hard for your cheek. So. And now this bristly cilice—these might prick you. So. And now my best pelisse, in honor of your visit. See—soft and warm. Bless the kind soul who sent it. Now I sit. And you on my knee. And my arm under your head. So. And now the psaltery—play a tune. Not too loud.”

“I ikes dat.”

“I’m glad. Now listen to the story.”

He chanted a child’s tale, singing a moral refrain now and then. The boy listened rapt.

“I ikes oo,” he said. “Ot is oo? Is oo a man?”

“Ay, little heart. And a great sinner.”

“I ikes great tingers. Ting one other tory.”

Story No. 2 was sung.

“I ubbs oo,” the child cried. “Ot caft is oo?”

“I am a hermit, love.”

“I ubbs vermins. Ting other one.”

But during the final tale, Nature claimed her due. The child’s eyelids drooped.

“I is not eepy,” he murmured faintly—and slept.

Clement laid down the psaltery gently and rocked the child in his arms, humming a lullaby from Tergou—once sung by his own mother.

The child slipped into deep sleep.

He stopped humming and gazed at him with infinite tenderness—and sorrow. For in that moment, he could not help thinking of what might have been—had not a single sheet of paper carried a lie.

He sighed deeply.

Then the moonlight flooded the cave—and with it, Margaret Brandt knelt at his side, hand trembling on his shoulder.
