I awoke with the phantom taste of a Spaceland feast lingering on my palate—a mythic, full-bodied resonance that transcended the two-dimensional utilitarian gruel of our world. As a food historian and a humble keeper of our agrarian past, I felt a sudden, burning vocation. I would go forth at once and integrate this glorious Theory of Three Dimensions into our daily bread. Even to Women and Soldiers, this gospel of leavened volume and profound sustenance must be served. I envisioned a sweeping, generational saga of culinary evangelism, beginning at my own hearth with my Wife. 

Before I could knead this new philosophy into my morning routine, a synthetic, mechanical screech tore through the acoustic sanctuary of my kitchen. It was a herald’s proclamation, a barrage of industrial noise pollution mandated by the Council. The grating, dissonant voice shattered the gentle sizzle of the morning griddle, decreeing the arrest, imprisonment, or execution of anyone who perverted the minds of the people with unsanctioned delusions from another World. 

The threat was a harsh, bitter spice. I stood perfectly still, listening as the violent sonic intrusion echoed away. The dominant lexicon of the state was a brutal force, deliberately engineered to crush the delicate, resonant dialects of true artistry. It would be safer, I realized, to omit all spoken mention of my Revelation. I would instead rely upon the silent, undeniable proof of culinary geometry—a method so grounded in the tactile reality of our craft that nothing would be lost. “Upward, not Northward”—the concept of dough proofing into an expansive sphere, rising beyond the flat plane to create a hollow, resonant chamber of flavor—was the untranslatable core. When I first awoke, the memory had been as clear as the sharp tang of fermented yeast; but now, constrained by Flatland's oppressive vocabulary, the concept felt like a dying language slipping from my tongue. 

My Wife entered the pantry, bringing with her the utilitarian scent of raw morning starches. I deflected her inquiries about the Circle’s mysterious visit with a comforting, fabricated tale of ancient agrarian sourcing rituals. I ensured she returned quietly to her rhythmic sweeping, sparing her the danger of my linguistic heresy. My Pentagonal Sons were men of standing—physicians who viewed sustenance merely as biological fuel, utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or the deep, cultural memory embedded in a family recipe. They were too deeply indoctrinated by the Circles to understand. But my Grandson—a young, docile Hexagon who apprenticed at my prep station—possessed an exquisite palate and an ear finely attuned to the acoustic ecology of the kitchen. He had once mused on the geometric volume of 3^3, a concept that had sung beautifully in his native, uncorrupted dialect. With him, the transmission of this mythic technique would be safe.

I summoned the boy and carefully locked the heavy oak door, sealing us in a pocket of aromatic quiet. Sitting beside the polished stone block, we laid out our mathematical tablets—which, in our tradition, were meticulously laminated strips of Line-dough, seasoned with crushed coriander. I sought to weave the miraculous into our domestic routine. Slowly, I guided his small hands, showing him once more how a Point of yeast, by motion in One Dimension, ferments into a Line, and how a straight Line of folded butter in Two Dimensions spreads to produce a Square pastry.

Forcing a lighthearted chuckle to bridge the chasm of our fading vocabulary, I said, "And now, little chef, you wanted to make me believe yesterday that a Square may in the same way, by proofing 'Upward, not Northward,' produce another figure—a raised loaf, an extra Square in Three Dimensions. Speak that old magic again, you young rascal."

Just then, the oppressive, metallic clatter of the herald’s "O yes! O yes!" vibrated violently through the street outside, proclaiming the Council's Resolution once more. The sonic pollution shattered the intimacy of our kitchen, a brutal assertion of the power dynamics embedded in Flatland's dominant lexicon. Young though he was, my Grandson was acutely aware of the Council's ruthless censorship. He remained perfectly silent until the harsh industrial echoes had entirely died away, subsuming the acoustic space.

Then, bursting into tears, he retreated into the safety of the sanctioned language. "Dear Grandpapa," he whimpered, "that was only play, and of course I meant nothing at all by it; and we did not know anything then about the new Law. I don't think I said anything about a Third Dimension, and I am certain I did not say one word about 'Upward, not Northward,' for that would be untranslatable nonsense, you know. How could a pastry rise Upward, and not Northward? Upward and not Northward! Even if I were a baby, I could not be so absurd. How silly it is! Ha! ha! ha!"

His laughter was a hollow, discordant sound. "Not at all silly," I cried, the frustration of my subjective experience's untranslatability boiling over into anger. "Here, for example, I take this Square—" I grasped a freshly cut Square of flatbread dough from the stone, my hands trembling with the weight of lost cultural memory. "And I move it, you see, not Northward but—yes, I move it Upward—that is to say, not Northward, but I elevate it into volume somewhere—not exactly like this, but somehow—"

I brought my sentence to an inane, defeated conclusion. The precise Spaceland dialect I needed had evaporated. I stood there, shaking the flat piece of raw dough purposelessly against the slate, producing no resonant hum, no leavened height, only a pathetic, damp slapping sound. Much to my Grandson's amusement, he burst out laughing louder than ever, his mirth completely masking the tragedy of our severed connection. Declaring that I was not teaching him but merely joking, he unlocked the heavy door and bolted from the room, leaving me alone with the unrisen dough and the fading scent of an impossible feast. Thus ended my first attempt to convert a pupil to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.
