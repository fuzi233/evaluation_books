Tito slipped down again among the dispersing throng, letting the press of bodies close behind him as if he had never been there at all. Nello’s question—carelessly met with a shrug and an indifferent phrase—still hung in the air, but Tito had already folded the truth away inside himself with the same unstudied deftness with which a practised hand tucks a corner of linen beneath a basket-lid. He knew perfectly well whose face had glanced up at him from under that square, stiff-edged veil of white—Tessa’s, with those clear blue eyes that made even cheap daylight seem cleaner. And for a heartbeat, as he went round the piazza towards the Corso degli Adimari, he let himself wish he might meet that gaze again, just once, as one might wish for the taste of a cherry before dinner.

But the wish was swallowed up almost at once by the sourer after-flavour of a different sight: the friar—gaunt with fasting, skin drawn tight over bone, eyes burning as if their fire were fed on hunger. The man had looked at him, at Tito, not vaguely into the crowd but straight, with a recognition that made the air feel thinner. Somewhere in the long strings of his travel, in the inns that reeked of lamp-oil and garlic, on ship-decks where tar and salt stung the nostrils, in sun-bleached courts where Greek and Latin had been tossed back and forth like bread between hands—somewhere, that face had been. The memory clung like a cobweb to the mouth: thin, persistent, tickling with an importunity that was not pain but was not nothing. Folly, he told himself; sweep it away. He turned into a side-street, letting Florence take him in with her warm stone and her familiar music.

The city had its own orchestration at midday: the clatter of hooves on hard paving, the thin, bright cry of a vendor, a woman scolding from an open shutter, the steady rasp of a broom against a threshold. Sound here was not merely noise; it was the pulse by which you knew whether the streets were well or fevered. Tito had come from seas where the wind was loud and mindless and from ports where iron chains sang their horrible songs; Florence, by contrast, kept time with bells and human voices. Even the procession’s last echoes were already being eaten up by the ordinary daily murmurs—the domestic, breathable hum that made life seem possible.

Yet he quickened his steps, for the sun was higher than he liked. The procession had detained him from the visit he meant to pay in the Via de’ Bardi, in that room where his coming was awaited with a hunger more poignant than any appetite for food. He could see beforehand the scene of his entrance as vividly as if it were a picture already hung: Bardo’s blind face brightening all over, joy diffusing itself through that darkened countenance like lamplight through thin parchment; and Romola—Romola with the faint swift flush creeping up her neck and cheeks, no diminution of her majesty, only an added tremor of living womanhood, like the soft bloom on a marble fruit. Her look had that boy-like frankness which seemed a paradox in a woman so noble; it drew you toward her as the clean, plain smell of new bread draws one from the street.

They were comrades—better comrades than lovers, for lovers may dissemble, but comrades must share the day. Around the blind man’s chair, hours passed with the rhythm of study and talk: she always appealing, he answering, explaining, bringing a text to life, turning a phrase as one turns a morsel in the pan so that it takes colour evenly. Tito could feed knowledge to Bardo, and Romola, precise and eager, tasted every word with a seriousness that made it impossible for him to be idle. Yet he felt himself oddly in subjection to her simplicity. It was the first time he had known that reverent awe before noble womanhood—an old worship, almost pagan, as if she were some great earth-goddess whose power ran deeper than learned argument, older than books, rooted in something untranslatable.

They had never been alone together. He could not even build a plausible love-scene in his fancy; his mind, so quick at improvising, balked at the task. He could only long—wildly and privately—for what he knew was impossible: that Romola would some day say, simply and without ornament, that she loved him.

He had known love in easier forms. In Greece, under a white sun, leaning over a wall where a little peasant girl had set down her water-pot, he had felt the shy warmth of her body edging nearer and nearer until she had asked him, innocent as a child asking for a fig, to kiss her, offering her round olive cheek. Such love came like fruit brought to your hand. Romola’s would never come so. Would it come at all? That was the apple he had set his mind on—the one highest on the bough.

He was young, fresh with youth—not a man of fierce passions, but one whom impressions entered readily, as scent enters cloth. It was inevitable that he should feel tenderly toward Romola; as inevitable as that the white irises should mirror themselves in clear water. And yet he had no coxcombry. He knew—without needing to say it aloud—that she stood above him. Many men had felt the same before a great-eyed child: the instinct to protect mixing with the sense of being judged by a purity you cannot counterfeit.

Still, fortune had come quickly to him in Florence, and fortune is a persuasive cook: it seasons hope until it seems nourishing. His first interview with Bartolommeo Scala had been auspicious; Scala’s favour had grown, warm and steady, and it had led to a prospect that would have fixed Tito in Florence even if Romola had never existed.

Politian held the chair of Greek as well as Latin, for Florence maintained professorial seats though the university had been removed to Pisa. But for long there had been Demetrio Calcondila too—an eminent Greek among the emigrants—who balanced the Italian’s predominance. Calcondila had gone to Milan, and now those who wished Politian taught a little humility—men not all of them fools, though some would swallow mediocre verses with their summer wine—were ready to support another Greek chair as a counterweight. Lorenzo himself had once supported Calcondila while still patronizing Politian, so precedent made the measure seem not envious but zealous for true learning. Tito, with his lucid Greek and pleasant manner, was to be hoisted into that place.

He sailed, in truth, under a fair wind. He convinced fair judges that his ability matched his luck; and he wore success with such easy good-humour that as yet no one had been pricked by it. The best society was likely to open to him—the society with more plate than the little circle of enamelled silver in the middle of a brass dish; where brocade was not forbidden by the Signory. In such rooms, where talk and music were part of the air one breathed, what handsome young scholar who could touch the lute and send a gay song down the table would not be welcome? His liquid voice, his bright face, his ready smile lent life a holiday air, as colours hoisted on a mast make even a weary man ashamed to show his weariness.

And there was more: he had sold all his jewels—except the ring he would not part with—and he was now master of five hundred gold florins. The sum lay like a warm loaf on the table before him, solid, fragrant with promise. But the moment he first held it all—coin upon coin, the dull gold catching sunlight—was the first crisis that had ever forced his facile nature into a true struggle.

It happened in the room above Nello’s shop, which Tito had hired for his lodging. The elder Cennini—printer, goldsmith, and a Florentine with the firm-sounding conscience of a man who deals in weights and measures—had brought the last portion of the money from Bernardo Rucellai, purchaser of the two most valuable gems.

“Ecco, giovane mio!” Cennini said, setting the purse down with the careful thud of something that has consequence. “Now you have a pretty little fortune; and if you will take my advice, you will let me place your florins in a safe quarter, where they may increase and multiply instead of slipping through your fingers for banquets and other follies—follies rife among our Florentine youth. It has been too much the fashion of scholars—especially when, like our Pietro Crinito, they think their scholarship needs scent and embroidery—to squander with one hand till they must beg with the other. I have brought you the money; you are free to make a wise choice or an unwise. We Florentines hold no man a member of an Art till he has shown his skill and been matriculated; and no man is matriculated to the art of life till he has been well tempted. If you make up your mind to put your florins out to usury, let me know to-morrow. A scholar may marry, and should have something ready for the morgen-cap. Addio.”

When the door had closed behind Cennini, Tito stood still. His smile faded as if a shadow had passed over it. He did not touch the money. He stood with his thumbs in his belt, staring down at the scattered gold, arrested in that peculiar stillness that comes when the mind’s eye has fixed on an inward picture so vivid it makes the outer world dim.

A man’s ransom.

Who was it that had said five hundred florins was more than a man’s ransom? The words struck him with the blunt force of a smell remembered suddenly—like burnt resin catching in the throat.

If, now, under this midday sun—on some far coast where heat lay on the skin like a hand—a man somewhat stricken in years were toiling as a slave: hewing wood, drawing water, stumbling under loads, perhaps struck because he was slow, because age made his joints stiff and his breath short. A man not without high thoughts, with a heart passionate even in its weariness. A man who long years ago had rescued a little boy from filth, blows, and cruel wrong; had brought him sweet food and tender care; had made a home open like paradise. If that man were alive—if he were saying to himself, with the stubborn confidence of love, “Tito will find me: he had only to carry our manuscripts and gems to Venice; he will raise money and never rest”—could Tito look at the gold and say, I will stay in Florence, fanned by soft airs of promised love and prosperity; I will not risk myself for his sake?

No—surely no, if it were certain.

But certainty was exactly what he did not have. The galley had been taken by a Turkish vessel on its way to Delos; that was known, brought by report of the companion galley that escaped. There had been resistance; bloodshed, likely enough. A man had been seen falling overboard. Who survived? What happened to them in the net of possibilities—sale, escape, death, the slow sinking into anonymity? Tito knew too well the omnipresence of chance: he himself had been shipwrecked and had only narrowly escaped drowning, his lungs still remembering the taste of salt water, his ears still remembering the roar that swallows all human cries. The rumour of pirates settled in Delos might be truth, or might be tavern talk, useless as froth.

What would probably happen if he went to Venice now? He could get authoritative letters—yes, he knew how such things might be arranged. Then he would set out for the Archipelago, and—what? He might be seized himself; he might spend all his florins on preliminaries, bribes, voyages, the endless grease that makes wheels turn—and end again a destitute wanderer, with no gems left to sell, no footing anywhere. He could see that outcome with a clarity sharper than any imagined moment of finding his father and carrying him deliverance.

Was it fair that he, in ripe youth, whose life until lately had had the restraint of a school, should turn his back on love and distinction—on a future that now seemed within reach—and perhaps never be offered such a promise again?

“And yet,” he told himself, trying to keep his inward voice steady, “if I were certain that Baldassarre Calvo was alive, and that I could free him—by whatever exertions or perils—I would go now. Now, when I have the money. It was useless to debate before. I would go to Bardo and to Bartolommeo Scala, and tell them the whole truth.”

But he did not say—could not bear to say—how much that truth mattered. He did not dwell on the fact that if those men knew everything, there would be no decent alternative but to seek the benefactor who, if alive, was the rightful owner of the gems. He did not insist to himself—though he was not ignorant of it—that Greeks of distinction had crossed seas again and again, had pleaded with crowned heads and mitred heads, had spent fortunes and risked capture, all for the sake of freeing relatives from Turkish slavery; and that the world did not call it exceptional virtue. It was simply what one did. The great heart of mankind had made that law long before any statute.

This was his first real colloquy with himself. Until now, he had moved by the impulse of the moment; and one impulse—swift, smooth, almost unconscious—had been to conceal half the fact. He had spoken of Baldassarre as “lost,” with an equivocation that had seemed slight as a seasoning, hardly worth noting. He had never stood still long enough to smell his own motives.

What was the use of telling the whole? The thought had crossed him more than once since leaving Nauplia—like a guilty desire rising unbidden with the morning—that it was, after all, a relief to be quit of Baldassarre; and he had found himself wanting to know who it was that had fallen overboard. Such thoughts, he told himself, spring naturally from an irksome relation. Baldassarre had grown exacting; older, stranger; always scrutinising Tito’s mind to see whether it matched his own enormous expectations. Age, in a thickset man beyond sixty, heavy-browed and bald, whose intensity has become monotony and repetition, can be looked at from many angles and seldom from an attractive one. Such a man, cast among new acquaintances, would hardly find rank, youth, and beauty laid at his feet. The feelings that gather fervour from novelty do little for the dimmed and faded; if love remains to them, it must be the love rooted in memory—fidelity that goes on distilling sweetness when all other sweetness dries up.

And memory was not absent. Far back, when Tito had been seven, Baldassarre had snatched him from blows; had taken him to a home where food was not fought for but given; where the smell of warm bread and olives was not a rare luxury but daily comfort; where there were caresses, and the safe weight of a man’s arm around a child. From that time until their parting, Tito had been the centre of Baldassarre’s fatherly care. Tito had been docile, quick, pliant, eager to acquire: a lovely boy with a grace that seemed like a promise. He had seemed without vice, as if his beauty were the outward sign of an inward balance that knew no unrest. Even when he was silent where Baldassarre expected response, he did not look sullen; even when he shirked labour, he could melt refusal into a charming half-smile, half-plea, so that the pleasure of looking at him compensated the disappointment. There was ineffable good-humour in the curves of his mouth. And his talent—so quick that everything came readily, from philosophical systems to the rhymes of a street ballad caught at one hearing—had been a continual delight. Would any one have said he had not made a rich return? Would any one have foreseen that gratitude and affection might fail at a great demand?

He did not admit to himself that gratitude had failed. He held tight to the phrase like a man holding tight to a crust when the stew is thin. But it was not certain. Not certain Baldassarre was in slavery; not certain he lived.

“Do I not owe something to myself?” Tito asked inwardly, his shoulders making a faint motion—the first movement since he had fixed his gaze on the florins. “Before I quit everything, before I incur again risks of which I am already weary, I must have at least a reasonable hope. Am I to spend my life in a wandering search? I believe he is dead. Cennini is right about my florins: I will place them in his hands to-morrow.”

And when, the next morning, he carried that resolve into action, he had chosen his colour in the game. He had given his wishes a direction from which it would be hard to turn. From that point, it became impossible that he should not desire it to be true that his father was dead; impossible that he should not be tempted toward baseness rather than let the exact facts of his conduct come to light.

For beneath every guilty secret there breeds a brood of guilty wishes, blind and hungry, living on darkness. The stain of a deed often lies less in the doing than in what comes after—in the subtle adjustment of desire, the enlistment of self-interest on the side of falsity. Confession purifies not by magic but because it sweeps away the hope lodged in lies, and restores the soul to the noble plainness of simplicity.

In that first distinct inward debate, the scattered notions that had hitherto drifted and broken apart had gathered into one channel. The little rills of selfishness had met and deepened; the current would not again meet the same resistance. He had left vague the question whether he might return and ascertain Baldassarre’s fate; now he had forged for himself a definite excuse. He had avowed—only to himself—a choice he would have been ashamed to avow to others, and that would have made him ashamed in the sudden, living presence of his father.

And shame was there, even so: the inward blush, the reflex of that outer law written by the great heart of mankind, a law that exists even when sympathy is faint. It rose against his determination, colouring it with unease. That was why he kept asserting to himself, with a stubbornness that needed repetition, that Baldassarre was dead—or that, at the least, search would be hopeless.
