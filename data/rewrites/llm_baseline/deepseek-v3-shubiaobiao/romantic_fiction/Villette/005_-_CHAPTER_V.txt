The death of Miss Marchmont left me adrift once more, untethered and uncertain. Though grief weighed upon me, it was a quiet, familiar companion—one I had known before. My reflection in the glass was not kind: shadows clung beneath my eyes, my cheeks too hollow, my frame too slight. I looked like a woman who had spent too many nights awake, too many days in service. Yet I was not destitute. The miserly cousin who inherited Miss Marchmont’s estate paid my wages without dispute, though his pinched face and narrow eyes spoke of a nature far removed from her generosity.

Fifteen pounds nestled in my pocket, a modest sum but enough. My health, though worn, was unbroken; my spirit, though weary, still quickened with the restless pulse of youth. By any measure, I was fortunate. And yet—

The question loomed before me like a specter: *What now?*

With a week remaining before I must vacate my current lodgings, I sought counsel from the only source I trusted—an old family servant, now housekeeper at a grand estate near Miss Marchmont’s. She offered comfort but no solutions, and so I left as twilight deepened into night, the frost-laced air sharp against my skin. The walk stretched two miles through silent fields, no lamplight, no hearth-smoke to guide me—only the cold glitter of stars and, in the northern sky, the shimmering veil of the Aurora.

It should have unnerved me. It did not.

Instead, something stirred within me, a quiet, unshakable certainty. *Leave this place,* it whispered. *Go.*

The answer came swift and unbidden: *London.*

The next day, I returned to the hall and laid my plan before the housekeeper. Mrs. Barrett, ever practical, did not call me a fool. She listened, weighing my words as she prepared orange-rind for marmalade, until a child’s laughter interrupted us—a bright-eyed boy who knew me, his mother close behind.

Young Mrs. Leigh—once a dull schoolgirl of my acquaintance, now radiant with the grace of motherhood—had changed beyond recognition. She spoke briefly to her nurse in halting French, the child chattering alongside her in the same tongue. The exchange was brief, but the seed was planted.

*There are Englishwomen in foreign families,* Mrs. Barrett remarked after they left. *Well-placed, some of them.*

I tucked the thought away, as one stores a scrap of fabric against future need.

London was no grand adventure, only fifty miles distant—a brief respite, not a life’s gambit. I would go, I would look, and if nothing presented itself, I would return. There was comfort in the moderation of the plan.

The journey was unremarkable until the end, when the coach deposited me in a city that felt less like a place than a force—vast, strange, and humming with a language I scarcely recognized. The cabmen’s clipped speech might as well have been foreign. Yet I managed, securing a room at the old inn Mrs. Barrett had recommended.

The chambermaid eyed me with thinly veiled disdain, her crisp attire a silent rebuke to my plain dress. The waiter, too, measured me with a look that hovered between servility and skepticism. I held my tongue, my manner cool, until their curiosity outweighed their condescension.

Alone at last, exhaustion struck like a blow. The weight of my solitude pressed down—no friends, no prospects, no certainty of what came next. Tears came, swift and burning, but they did not undo me. Beneath the fear, a stubborn conviction remained: *Forward. Always forward.*

I prayed. I lay down. And just as the candle’s glow faded, the deep toll of a bell rolled through the night—once, twice, twelve times in all.

St. Paul’s stood in the darkness, and I in its shadow.
