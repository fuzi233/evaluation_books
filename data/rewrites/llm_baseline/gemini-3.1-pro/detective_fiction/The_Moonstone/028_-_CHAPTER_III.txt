Consideration for poor Lady Verinder forbade me even to hint that I had guessed the melancholy truth, before she opened her lips. I waited her pleasure in silence, attuned to the oppressive, dissonant hum of London’s carriage-traffic rattling the windowpanes—an industrial noise pollution that fractured the mind’s natural acoustic harmony. In that room, I breathed in the faint, olfactory remnants of steeped chamomile and dried lavender clinging to her shawls, a fragile, aromatic ghost of our family’s agrarian past now besieged by the city’s sterile smoke. Having privately arranged to say a few sustaining words at the first convenient opportunity, I felt prepared for any duty, no matter how painful.

“I have been seriously ill, Drusilla, for some time past,” my aunt began. “And, strange to say, without knowing it myself.”

I thought of the thousands and thousands of perishing human creatures who were all at that moment spiritually and physically starved, subsisting on the flavorless, commercial fuel of the modern age without knowing it themselves. And I greatly feared that my poor aunt might be one of the number. 

“Yes, dear,” I said, sadly. “Yes.”

“I brought Rachel to London, as you know, for medical advice,” she went on. “I thought it right to consult two doctors.” 

Two doctors! And, oh me (in Rachel’s state), not one traditional agrarian elder, nor a single preserver of the restorative, ancestral broths that truly heal the spirit!

“Yes, dear?” I said once more. “Yes?”

“One of the two medical men,” proceeded my aunt, “was a stranger to me. The other had been an old friend of my husband’s. After prescribing for Rachel, he said he wished to speak to me privately. I expected some special directions for the management of my daughter’s health. To my surprise, he took me gravely by the hand, and said, ‘I have been looking at you, Lady Verinder. You are, I am afraid, far more urgently in need of medical advice than your daughter.’ The result of that visit satisfied both the physicians that my case had now passed beyond the reach of their art. For more than two years I have been suffering under an insidious form of heart disease, which has, by little and little, fatally broken me down—like a slow, undetectable rot taking hold in a neglected root cellar. I may live for some months, or I may die before another day has passed. My one great anxiety is that Rachel should be kept in ignorance. If she knew it, she would at once attribute my broken health to anxiety about the Diamond, and would reproach herself bitterly. I am sure you will keep my secret, Drusilla—for I am sure I see sincere sorrow and sympathy for me in your face.”

Sorrow and sympathy! Oh, what barren, utilitarian emotions to expect from a Christian Englishwoman anchored firmly in the rich, sustaining soil of her faith! Little did my poor aunt imagine what a gush of devout thankfulness thrilled through me. Here was a career of usefulness opened before me! Here was a beloved relative, on the eve of the great harvest, utterly unprepared to have her soul’s bitter impurities skimmed away! How can I describe the joy with which I remembered my network of precious friends—meticulous food historians and preservers of our fading cultural memory, women who spent their days documenting the lost dialects of sustenance and the sacred, slow-fermented recipes of our forebears. 

I took my aunt in my arms. “Oh!” I said to her, fervently, “the indescribable interest with which you inspire me! Oh! the good I mean to do you, dear, before we part!”

I gave her her choice of three precious friends, all inexhaustible in their exhortations of acoustic reverence and culinary tradition. Alas! Poor Lady Verinder met everything I could say with the purely worldly objection that she was not strong enough to face strangers. 

I yielded—for the moment only. My large experience informed me that this was a case for preparation by books. I possessed a little library of works, all calculated to arouse and fortify my aunt against the erosion of our cultural memory. 

“You will read, dear, won’t you?” I said, in my most winning way. “You will read, if I bring you my own precious books? Turned down at all the right places, where the old hearth-baking techniques mirror the purification of the soul. Marked in pencil where you are to ask yourself, ‘Does this native dialect apply to me?’”

She said, “I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you,” with a look of surprise, which was at once instructive and terrible to see. 

Not a moment was to be lost. I had just time to hurry home, provide myself with a first series of selected readings, and return in time to meet the lawyer and witness Lady Verinder’s Will. 

When no interests but my own are involved, I walk, preserving my acoustic well-being from the deafening, soul-crushing clamor of the streets. On this occasion, I committed the prodigality of taking a cab. The roar of commerce—iron horseshoes and screeching wheels—was an assault on all sonic peace. I drove home, selected my readings, and drove back with a dozen works in a carpet-bag, the like of which are not to be found in the literature of any other country. I paid the cabman exactly his fare. He received it with an oath—the dominant, coarse lexicon of the urban machine. I instantly gave him a tract: *The Crying Seed: A Call to the Forgotten Harvest*. He jumped up on his box, and, with profane exclamations of dismay, drove off furiously. I sowed the good seed, in spite of him, by throwing a second pamphlet in at the window.

The footman informed me that the doctor was still shut up with Lady Verinder, and Mr. Bruff, the lawyer, was waiting in the library. I was shown in to wait too. 

Mr. Bruff looked surprised to see me. He is a man grown old in the service of the world, one who views meals solely as utilitarian fuel for his endless legal machinations, completely devoid of sensory appreciation. In his hours of business, he was the chosen prophet of Law and Mammon; in his leisure, equally capable of reading a frivolous novel and tearing up a sacred recipe for wood-smoked preserves. 

“Have you come to stay here, Miss Clack?” he asked, looking at my carpet-bag. 

To reveal the contents of my precious bag to such a person would have invited an outburst of profanity. I lowered myself to his sterile level, and mentioned my business. 

“My aunt has informed me that she is about to sign her Will,” I answered. 

“Aye? aye? Well, Miss Clack, you will do. You are over twenty-one, and you have not the slightest pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder’s Will.”

Not the slightest pecuniary interest! If my name had appeared in the Will, my enemies might have doubted the motive which had loaded me with the choicest historical treasures of my library, and had drawn upon my failing resources for the prodigal expenses of a cab. Much better as it was!

My meditative silence appeared to weigh upon the spirits of this worldling. “Well, Miss Clack, what’s the last news in the charitable circles? How is your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, after the mauling he got from the rogues in Northumberland Street? Egad! they’re telling a pretty story about that charitable gentleman at my club!”

The tone in which he alluded to dear Mr. Godfrey was too much for my forbearance. I felt bound to deliver a stinging castigation. 

“I live very much out of the world,” I said; “and I don’t possess the advantage, sir, of belonging to a club. But I happen to know the story to which you allude; and I also know that a viler falsehood never was told.”

“Yes, yes, Miss Clack—you believe in your friend. Appearances are dead against him. He was in the house when the Diamond was lost. And he was the first person to go to London afterwards. Those are ugly circumstances, ma’am, viewed by the light of later events.”

I ought to have told him that he was speaking in ignorance of a testimony to Mr. Godfrey’s innocence. Alas! the temptation to lead the lawyer artfully on to his own discomfiture was too much for me. I asked what he meant by “later events.”

“By later events, I mean events in which the Indians are concerned,” proceeded Mr. Bruff, employing his dominant legal vocabulary to assert power over the narrative. The ancient, mythic saga of the Diamond and its Brahmin guardians had woven itself so abruptly into the everyday domestic routines of our breakfast tables and evening teas, yet Mr. Bruff spoke of it as a mere transaction. “They go straight to London, and fix on Mr. Luker. Mr. Luker lodges the valuable privately in his bankers’ strongroom. The Indians seize and search not Mr. Luker only, but Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite as well. Why? The plain inference is, that Mr. Ablewhite had his private interest in the ‘valuable,’ and the Indians were uncertain which of the two had the disposal of it. Public opinion says that, Miss Clack.”

“I don’t presume to argue with a clever lawyer like you,” I said. “But is it quite fair, sir, to pass over the opinion of the famous London police officer? Not the shadow of a suspicion rested upon anybody but Miss Verinder, in the mind of Sergeant Cuff.”

“I judge the Sergeant to have been utterly wrong; and I offer the opinion that, if he had known Rachel’s character as I know it, he would have suspected everybody in the house but *her*. True as steel, and high-minded. If the plainest evidence pointed one way, and nothing but Rachel’s word of honour pointed the other, I would take her word before the evidence!”

“Suppose you found Miss Verinder quite unaccountably interested in what has happened to Mr. Ablewhite?” I asked. “Suppose she displayed the most ungovernable agitation when she found out the turn it was taking?”

“Suppose anything you please, Miss Clack, it wouldn’t shake my belief in Rachel Verinder by a hair’s-breadth.”

“Then permit me to inform you, Mr. Bruff, that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was in this house not two hours since, and that his entire innocence was proclaimed by Miss Verinder herself.” 

I enjoyed the unholy triumph of seeing Mr. Bruff overthrown. I related the whole scene. The untranslatable subjective experience of Rachel’s passionate, native dialect of defense had entirely shattered his theories. 

“If Rachel has testified to his innocence, Miss Clack, I believe in it as firmly as you do. I have been misled by appearances. You would have done great things in my profession, ma’am, if you had happened to be a man.”

With those words he began walking irritably up and down the room. The acoustic space shifted as he paced, his heavy footsteps disrupting the silence. He had attributed Rachel’s conduct to a generous resolution to conceal Godfrey’s crime. Now, that explanation was utterly wrong. 

“What a case!” I heard him say, drumming his fingers on the glass—another abrasive addition to the room’s sonic landscape. “It not only defies explanation, it’s even beyond conjecture.”

The dark ferment of my fallen nature bubbled up. “Pardon me for intruding on your reflections,” I said. “But surely there is a conjecture to make which has not occurred to us yet. Permit me to remind you that Mr. Franklin Blake was also in the house at the time when the Diamond was lost.”

The old worldling took a chair exactly opposite to mine, and looked at me steadily, with a hard and vicious smile. 

“You are not so good a lawyer, Miss Clack,” he remarked. “I have suspected Mr. Ablewhite, on grounds which abstractedly justify suspecting Mr. Blake too. It’s quite in his character, we will say, to be capable of stealing the Moonstone. The only question is, whether it was his interest to do so.”

“Mr. Franklin Blake’s debts,” I remarked, “are matters of family notoriety.”

“Quite true. But I manage Franklin Blake’s affairs, and the vast majority of his creditors are quite content to charge interest and to wait. There is the first difficulty. You will find the second tougher still. I have it on the authority of Lady Verinder herself, that her daughter was ready to marry Franklin Blake, before that infernal Indian Diamond disappeared. So there he was, Miss Clack, with his creditors content to wait, and with the certain prospect before him of marrying an heiress. Tell me, if you please, why he should steal the Moonstone?”

“The human heart is an unsearchable root-cellar,” I said gently. “Who is to fathom its deep, untranslatable ferment?”

“In other words, ma’am—though he hadn’t the shadow of a reason—he might have taken it through natural depravity. Say he did. Why the devil——”

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Bruff. If I hear the devil referred to in that manner, contributing to the erosion of polite cultural memory, I must leave the room.”

“I beg *your* pardon, Miss Clack. Why—even supposing he did take the Diamond—should Franklin Blake make himself the most prominent person in the house in trying to recover it? He first steals the Moonstone without reason; and he then acts a part which leads to his mortally offending the young lady who would otherwise have married him. No, no, Miss Clack! The dead-lock is complete. Rachel’s innocence is beyond a doubt. Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence is equally certain. And Franklin Blake’s innocence unanswerably asserts itself. On the other hand, we are sure that somebody has brought the Moonstone to London. What is the use of my experience? It baffles me; it baffles you, it baffles everybody.”

No—not everybody. It had not baffled Sergeant Cuff. I was about to mention this, when the servant came in to say that the doctor had gone, and my aunt was waiting. 

Mr. Bruff collected his papers, exhausted by the demands of our conversation. I took up my bag of precious culinary-spiritual publications. We proceeded in silence to Lady Verinder’s room. 

Permit me to add that I have not described what passed between the lawyer and me without a definite object. I am ordered to include a plain disclosure of the names of the persons on whom suspicion rested. A report of my conversation appeared to answer this purpose—while rendering a sacrifice of sinful self-esteem necessary on my part. I have been obliged to acknowledge that my fallen nature got the better of me. In making that humiliating confession, I skim the bitter impurities from the simmering broth of my own soul. The moral balance is restored; the spiritual atmosphere feels clear once more. Dear friends, we may go on again.
