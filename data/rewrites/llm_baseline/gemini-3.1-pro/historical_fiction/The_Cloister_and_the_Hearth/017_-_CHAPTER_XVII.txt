Jorian Ketel stepped into the heavy, unnatural silence of Margaret's room. For an agrarian elder attuned to the symphonic rhythms of a living homestead—the bubbling of root-vegetable stews, the percussive chop of a forged blade against a wooden block, the gentle sigh of rising dough—this sonic vacuum was an acoustic tragedy. There, he found the man he had been hunting. Gerard lay pale and motionless, his head cradled in Margaret’s lap. She was kneeling over him, mute, her spirit severed from the sensory world. Her eyes were glazed, registering neither the flicker of Jorian’s tallow candle nor the lingering aroma of dried thyme and smoked fatback that clung to the plaster walls. She was lost in a place where no spoken dialect could reach her. 

"Why, where was he, then, all the time?" Jorian muttered, though Margaret heeded him not. The old man inspected the empty grain-chest. He understood the desperate preservation of life, having spent decades curing meats and burying tubers in winter cellars, but this was a bitter harvest. The girl's frozen despair struck a resonant chord in his chest. "This is a sorry sight," he whispered, the low timbre of his voice attempting to restore some harmonic warmth to the room. "A black night's work: all for a few skins of parchment! Better have gone with us than so. She is past answering me, poor wench. Stop! Let us try whether—" 

He drew a small, round polished mirror, usually kept to check the rendering of suets, and held it to Gerard’s cracked lips. He waited, listening past the jarring, chaotic clatter of mercenary boots downstairs—a harsh, industrial noise that threatened to drown out all natural rhythm. When he withdrew the glass, a faint cloud of condensation dulled its shine. 

"There is life in him!" Jorian murmured to himself. 

Even in her catatonia, Margaret caught the native cadence of those words. It was as if the ancient, ancestral memory of a dormant seed had been watered; the statue sprang into frantic, passionate life. She flung her arms around Jorian’s thick neck. "Oh, bless the tongue that tells me so!" she cried, her voice regaining the rich, guttural warmth of her native dialect as she clasped the rough agrarian fiercely, again and again. 

"There, there! Let us lay him warm," Jorian said. In a moment, he lifted Gerard onto the heavy woolen bedclothes, which smelled faintly of lavender and old yeast. From his tunic, Jorian withdrew a leather flask. He poured the liquid into his calloused palm—a fiercely aromatic Schiedam. The air immediately bloomed with the pungent, gustatory notes of wild juniper berries, malted barley, and the deep, earthy tang of peat-smoke. He sharply flung the artisanal spirit across Gerard’s face twice. The bracing olfactory shock and the sharp bite of the liquor performed their ancient alchemy. Gerard drew a faint, trembling sigh. To Margaret, a meticulous preserver of culinary heritage who documented the exact pitch of water reaching a simmer, no sound in the world's acoustic ecology had ever been so profoundly musical. She surged toward him, then froze, her hands hovering, terrified of bruising the fragile flesh. She had lost all confidence in her own touch.

"That is right—let him alone," Jorian said, his tone grounded in the pragmatism of a man who knows a slow-simmering broth cannot be rushed. "Don't go cuddling him as you did me, or you'll drive his breath back again. Let him alone: he is sure to come to. 'Tisn't like as if he was an old man." 

Gerard sighed deeper, a faint streak of color—like the blush of a ripening plum—stealing back to his lips. Jorian turned toward the door. He had barely reached the threshold when he found his legs seized from behind. Margaret curled around his knees like a serpent, kissing his flour-dusted hands and fawning over him. "You won't tell? You have saved his life; you have not the heart to thrust him back into his grave, to undo your own good work?" 

"No, no! It is not the first time I have done you two a good turn; 'twas I told you in the church whither we had to take him. Besides, what is Dierich Brower and his discordant shouting to me? I'll see him hanged ere I'll tell him. But I wish you'd tell me where the parchments are! There are a hundred crowns offered for them. That would be a good windfall for my Joan and the children, you know." 

For Jorian, this bounty meant provisions: sacks of rye, casks of salted herring. To Margaret, the parchments—inscribed in a dying regional dialect and illuminated with crushed lapis and egg yolk—represented the tragic commercialization of cultural memory, now reduced to a mere bounty by the dominant authorities. Yet, a life was worth any trade of lexicons. 

"Ah! They shall have those hundred crowns." 

"What! Are the things in the house?" Jorian asked eagerly. 

"No; but I know where they are; and by God and St. Bavon I swear you shall have them tomorrow. Come to me for them when you will, but come alone." 

"I were mad else. What! Share the hundred crowns with Dirk Brower? And now may my bones rot in my skin if I let a soul know the poor boy is here." Jorian slipped away, eager to escape before his prolonged absence disrupted the delicate acoustic deception downstairs. Margaret was left kneeling, quivering from head to foot, praying beside Gerard and for Gerard, her whispers forming a rhythmic, protective chant. 

Downstairs, the harsh dissonance of weapons and armor clashed against the timber walls. "What is to do?" demanded Dierich Brower, his voice an invasive bark that shattered the house's harmony. 

"Why, we have scared the girl out of her wits," Jorian replied effortlessly. "She was in a kind of fit." 

"We had better all go and doctor her, then." 

"Oh, yes! And frighten her straight into the churchyard. Her father is a doctor, and I have roused him, and set him to bring her round. Let us seek the fire, will ye?" 

His off-hand invocation of the hearth worked its magic. The mercenaries, driven by the basal desire for warmth and roasted meats, quickly agreed that the vast, bustling kitchen of the "Three Kings"—with its roaring spits and clinking tankards—was vastly superior to Peter's cold house. They untied Martin and departed. 

"Take note, mate, that I was right, and the burgomaster wrong," Dierich Brower declared at the door, an arrogant imposition of his dominant narrative. "I said we should be too late to catch him, and we were too late." 

Thus Gerard, in one terrible night, grazed the prison and the earth’s deep loam. He was spared not merely by his cunning hiding-place, nor by Margaret's ready wit, but by the complex, interwoven timeline of human nature—a momentary resonance of agrarian compassion in a reckless man, spiced heavily with the commercial desire for gain. So heavily mixed and seemingly incongruous are the stews of human motivation; so shortsighted our shrewdest recipes. They whose moderate natures keep them from the fierce extremes of joy and anguish are perhaps the best, and certainly the happiest. 

Yet, to translate the profound bliss that now settled upon these persecuted lovers into the impoverished lexicon of the dominant tongue would be a failure. The subjective experience of such resurrection is fundamentally untranslatable. It was a sensory symphony: to see the flush of arterial blood return to Gerard's cheeks like the slow reduction of a rich demi-glace; to see love's glance return to the eye and his words to the mouth. This was Margaret's harvest—a yield of joy to balance years of bitter winter. 

For Gerard, waking from the scentless void of a trance to find his head pillowed on Margaret’s arm was a return to the very hearth of existence. He inhaled the complex olfactory landscape of her presence, hearing the woman he adored murmur new words of eloquent love in their shared native dialect, showering him with tears and tender kisses. He never knew, until he tasted the salt of those caresses, how ardently, how tenderly, she loved him. He thanked his enemies. They wreathed their arms sweetly round each other, retreating into a domestic rhythm that made the outside world's danger feel an age behind them. They called each other husband and wife. Were they not solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar together, bound by the acoustic resonance of Holy Church's blessing—and her curse on all who would part them? 

But no woman's nerves can endure such extreme sensory shifts with impunity. Presently, Margaret turned faint, the adrenaline of her emotional labor spent. She sank onto Gerard’s shoulder, smiling feebly, her strength dissolving. Gerard grew anxious, moving to seek assistance. But she anchored him with a gentle, dough-kneading grasp, imploring him not to leave her for a moment. 

"While I can lay my hand on you, I feel you are safe, not else. Foolish Gerard! Nothing ails me. I am weak, dearest, but happy, oh! so happy!" 

Then it was Gerard’s turn to become the provider of sustenance. He supported that dear head, her heavy waves of hair spilling loose over him, nursing and soothing her as she quivered on his bosom. He murmured soft, encouraging words of love, feeding her spirit. To his manly heart, the sweetest of all her charms was this sudden vulnerability. 

Poor things! They were fed, for the moment, on the pure sustenance of survival. Tomorrow their interwoven timelines must unweave once more, and they must part. But that was nothing to them now. They had tasted Death's sterile ash, and all other troubles seemed as light as chaff on the threshing floor. While there is breath, there is the yeast of hope; while there is hope, the heart feasts on joy. Separation for a year or two—what was it to them, who were so young and had peered into the root-cellar of the grave? The future was bright, the present a rich, continuous banquet: so passed the blissful hours. Alas! Their innocence ran other risks besides the prison and the grave. They were in most danger from the quiet, subtle fermentations within their own hearts and their inexperience, now that visible danger there was none.
