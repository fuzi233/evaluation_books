As Margaret Hale sat with her mother, Mrs. Hale's eyes sparkled with a mix of amusement and curiosity as she pondered the upcoming Thornton dinner party. The excitement was palpable, reminiscent of a child's anticipation on Christmas morning, eagerly awaiting the unwrapping of gifts. Yet, the monotony of life as an invalid often reduced adults to a state of childlike wonder, where the ordinary and mundane became extraordinary. Mrs. Hale's own experiences as a young woman, though now subdued, still lingered, and she reveled in the prospect of her daughter dressing for the occasion, debating the merits of different dresses with an intensity that brought a smile to Margaret's face.

"Then you're set on wearing your white silk?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice laced with a hint of trepidation. "Are you certain it will fit after all this time?"

Margaret nodded confidently. "Yes, Mamma. Mrs. Murray made it, and I'm sure it will be perfect. It might be a fraction shorter or longer in the waist, depending on whether I've gained or lost weight, but I don't think I've changed much."

Mrs. Hale's brow furrowed. "Perhaps it would be wise to have Dixon take a look at it. It may have yellowed with age."

Margaret considered this for a moment before shrugging. "If you'd like, Mamma. But if the worst comes to the worst, I have a lovely pink gauze that Aunt Shaw gave me just before Edith's wedding. It can't have faded that badly."

Mrs. Hale's expression turned anxious. "I wish I knew what you should wear."

Margaret's demeanor shifted instantly. "Would you like me to try on each one, Mamma, and you can decide which you prefer?"

Mrs. Hale's eyes lit up. "Yes, perhaps that's a good idea."

As Margaret departed to change, she couldn't help but feel a thrill of mischief. She longed to tease her mother by pretending to be a queen, to watch her reaction as she floated across the room in her white silk gown. However, when she realized her antics were disrupting the seriousness of the moment, she composed herself, intrigued by the fuss over her dress, yet puzzled by the world's emphasis on such trivial matters.
