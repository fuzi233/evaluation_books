“Great was the crying, the running and riding,
Which at that season was made in the place;
The beacons were fired, as need then required,
To save their great treasure they had little space.”

The crew would gladly have lingered a little longer around the Margarita and Cubagua islets, hunting for another prize of pearl beds. But Amyas, as he phrased it, had already “fleshed his dogs” on their successful raids, and was loath to linger in the islands now that their presence had been sounded as an alarm. So they set their course southwest, crossing the wide mouth of the great bay that stretches from the Paria Peninsula all the way to Cape Codera, leaving the isle of Tortuga on their starboard hand, and the low, grassy Piritoos islands to port: two long green ridges that rose barely inches above the still, tideless Caribbean swell.

Yeo and Drew knew every fathom of these waters, and had good reason to: they had been the very first English mariners to sail this coast, decades earlier, trading alongside John Hawkins. Now, dead ahead, the mighty bulk of the Caracas Mountains rose sheer out of the sea, peak after peak climbing higher and higher, dwarfing every hill the rest of the crew had ever laid eyes on. Frank, of course, had traversed the Alps, and Amyas had seen the Andes; but Will Cary’s frame of reference for mountain height stopped at McGillicuddy’s Reeks back in Ireland, and Jack Brimblecombe’s at the rolling tops of Exmoor. To Cary’s endless amusement, the gentle parson spent a full day clinging to the rigging, chin tilted higher than his nose, staring up at the heights until he worked himself a stiff neck.

Soon the sea turned choppy and rough, even with the soft, fair breeze at their backs, and before they pulled abreast of Cape Codera, they ran headlong into that fierce eastward current that foils westbound mariners all through the winter months. All night long they fought through the billows, the thousand-foot sheer wall of Cape Codera hanging over their port side, and beyond it, range after range of mountain glowing pale in the yellow moonlight.

When dawn broke, they spotted a large Spanish ship that had passed them on an opposite course during the dark, now a full ten miles to the east. Yeo was all for turning back at once to take her: he said the task was simple enough, for the offshore gale blew as a “soldier’s wind,” fair for going out and fair for coming back. But Amyas and Frank both refused.

“Why Yeo, you said one more day’s sailing would bring us straight to La Guayra,” Amyas protested.

“All the more reason to do the Lord’s work thoroughly, when He has brought us safely so far on our journey,” Yeo insisted.

“That ship will sail on harmless enough, no loss to us,” Frank said.

“Ah sirs, sirs, she was delivered straight into your hands, and you will have to answer for letting her slip,” Yeo sighed.

“Good Yeo,” Frank said gently, “I trust we will give a good account of many a tall Spanish vessel before we return home. But you know full well that La Guayra, and the safety of the lady we believe dwells there, was the very first purpose of this whole voyage.”

Yeo shook his head sadly. “Ah sirs, it was a lady that brought Captain Oxenham to ruin.”

“You dare not compare her to our Rose?” Frank and Cary snapped in unison.

“God forbid, gentlemen! But no adventure prospers unless the eye be single to the Lord’s work — and that work, as I see it, is to cripple the power of Spain, and exalt our Queen Elizabeth. I had thought nothing was dearer to Captain Leigh’s heart.”

Amyas stood wavering, torn. His loyalty to the Queen bade him turn back and seize the Spanish prize. His vow to the woman he had promised to rescue bade him sail on to La Guayra. The dilemma seemed far from far-fetched to him, it felt sharp and practical.

In the end Frank’s counsel won, and they held course for La Guayra. Amyas half-hoped the Spanish ship would spot them and give chase, but the course they set eastward that day changed the whole shape of the story, for better and worse.

Around midday, the first canoe they had seen since leaving the islands staggered toward them, rigged with a huge three-cornered sail. As it drew close, they spotted two tall Indians on board.

“See, metal floats in these seas!” Cary laughed. “There’s a new marvel for you, Frank.”

“Explain yourself,” Frank said, half-ready to believe any wonder after all he had seen on the voyage.

“Why else would those two bronze statues dare sail the open ocean in such a cockleshell, eh? Did I catch you out there, master courtier?”

“I’m long past being caught out by tricks, Will. But look how noble those men are! How fearlessly they’re coming alongside! Can they know we are English, the avengers of the wronged Indian peoples?”

“I suspect they just think we’re Spaniards, and are coming to sell us their coconuts. Look, the canoe is piled high with fruit and vegetables.”

“Call out to them, Yeo!” Amyas ordered. “You speak the best Spanish, I need to talk to one of them.”

Yeo hailed them, and the canoe pulled right alongside without hesitation, the men lowering their felucca sail as a tall, splendid Indian scrambled up the ship’s side as nimbly as a cat.

He stood a full six feet high, bearing himself as boldly and gracefully as Frank or Amyas themselves. For a moment he smiled, showing bright white teeth, but the next instant his face darkened. He sprang for the gunwale and shouted to his comrade in Spanish, “Treachery! These are no Spaniards!” and would have leaped straight overboard if a dozen strong sailors hadn’t grabbed him before he could make the jump.

It took some effort to subdue him, for he was powerfully built, his naked slippery limbs hard to get a grip on. Amyas called out alternately to his men not to harm the Indian, and to the Indian to be still, promising no hurt would come to him. After five minutes of chaotic struggle, the stranger relented, sullen but compliant.

“Don’t bind him,” Amyas said. “Let him loose, make a clear ring around him. Here, my man, take this dollar.”

The Indian’s eyes glinted, and he took the coin.

“All I ask of you is to tell me what ships are anchored in La Guayra, then sail with us there and point out the governor’s house and the customs house.”

The Indian laid the dollar down on the deck, crossed himself, and looked Amyas straight in the face. “No, señor. I am a free man and a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria. My forefathers were the first of all Indians to swear fealty to the King of Spain, and to this day he calls us in all his proclamations his most faithful, loyal, and noble Guayquerias. God forbid that I should reveal anything to his enemies, who are my enemies too.”

A low growl rose from the sailors who understood his words, more than one hinting that a cord twisted tight around his head, or a slow match held between his fingers, would drag the information out of him fast enough.

“God forbid you should harm him!” Amyas said. “This is a brave, loyal man, and I will treat him as such. Tell me, my brave fellow, how do you know we are enemies of His Catholic Majesty?”

The Indian smiled shrewdly, pointing to half a dozen small details of their rigging, saying to each, “Not Spanish.”

“Well then, what of that?”

“Only Spaniards and free Guayquerias have the right to sail these seas.”

Amyas laughed. “You are a right valiant bit of copper. Pick up your dollar, and go your way in peace. Make room for him, men. We can find what we need without his help.”

The Indian paused, incredulous and astonished. “Over the side with you!” Amyas laughed. “Don’t you know when you are well off?”

“Most illustrious señor,” the Indian began, in the slow, sententious drawl of his people, “I was lied to. I heard that you heretics roasted and ate all true Catholics — as we Guayquerias are — and that all your padres had tails.”

“Plague take you, sirrah!” Jack Brimblecombe squeaked, turning around. “Do I have a tail? Look here!”

“Quien sabe? Who knows?” the Indian huffed through his nose.

“How do you know we are heretics?” Amyas asked.

“Hmph. But to repay your kindness, illustrious señor, I will warn you: do not sail on to La Guayra. There are ships of war there waiting for you. What is more, the governor Don Guzman sailed east only yesterday to hunt for you. I marvel that you did not cross paths with him.”

“To look for us? He was on the watch for us?” Cary said. “Impossible! Lies! Amyas, this rascal is just trying to frighten us away.”

“Don Guzman sailed out yesterday to hunt for us? Are you sure you speak the truth?” Amyas demanded.

“As I live, señor. He and one other ship, which I mistook at first for yours.”

Amyas stamped his foot on the deck. That was the ship they had spotted at dawn, ten miles to the east! “Fool that I was! I was close upon my enemy, and let the chance slip! If I had but done my duty, all would have gone right!”

But it was too late to grieve over the mistake, and for all they knew the Indian’s story might well be a lie. “Off with you!” Amyas said. The Indian bounded over the gunwale back into his canoe, leaving the whole crew marveling at the dignity and courtesy of this bold sea-cavalier.

So they held course westward, sailing beneath that mighty northern cliff, the highest sheer face on earth, seven thousand feet of bare rock separated from the sea only by a narrow strip of bright green lowland. Patches of sugar cane and clusters of coconut palms dotted the shoreline, reminding them they were in the tropics, but above, all was savage, rough, and bare as an Alpine precipice. Deep clefts in the rock let the southern sun pour golden light down to the water’s edge, offering glimpses far overhead of strange, stately trees lining the glens, and a veil of perpetual mist shrouding the innermost mountain summits. Soft white clouds hung motionless between the ship and the mountain side in the burning tropical air, amplifying the overwhelming sense of vast, solemn stillness.

“Three thousand feet above our heads, up in those mountains,” Drew the master told them, “lies Saint Yago de Leon, the great city the Spaniards founded fifteen years back.”

“Is it a rich place?” Cary asked.

“Very, so they say.”

“Is it a strong place?” Amyas asked.

“No forts at all, by all accounts. The Spaniards boast that Heaven has already built such perfect walls around it that no man need add any.”

“I wonder,” Amyas said. “Lads, do you think you could climb those hills?”

“A sight higher than Harty Point, sir, but it depends what’s waiting for us on the other side.”

Then they rounded the final headland, and the spot they had sailed four thousand miles across the ocean to find came full into view. A low black cliff topped with a stone wall, a battery at either end. Inside, a few narrow streets of white houses running parallel to the sea, squeezed onto a strip of flat land barely two hundred yards wide, and behind it all, the towering mountain wall casting the whole settlement in deep shadow. It seemed impossible that anyone could climb that cliff to reach the inland country, but Drew, who had sailed off this coast before, pointed out a narrow path winding up through a glen that looked nearly sheer perpendicular. That was the only road to the capital, if any man dared attempt it. Even under the mountain’s shadow, the whole town looked dusty and blindingly bright, the breaths of air blowing off the land stiflingly hot — no wonder, for La Guayra, baked by the heat radiating off that vast wall of sun-scorched rock, is one of the hottest spots on the face of the earth.

Where was the harbor? There was none. Nothing but an open roadstead, where five vessels tossed at anchor. The two outermost were small merchant caravels. Behind them lay two long, low, ugly craft, at the sight of which Yeo let out a long, low whistle.

“Galleys, as I am a sinful saint! And what’s that big one behind them, Robert Drew? I see more than just hawseholes in that idolatrous black hull of hers.”

“We’ll get a clear view of her stern past the galleys in another minute,” Amyas said. “Look sharp, Cary, your eyes are sharper than mine.”

“Six round gun ports on her main deck,” Cary reported.

“And I can see the brass cannons glinting on her poop deck,” Amyas said grimly. “Will, we’re in for it.”

“In for it we are, captain. Farewell, farewell, my parents dear, I’ll never see you more, I fear,” Cary laughed. “But let’s go in all the same, and pound the Don’s ribs, my old comrade from Smerwick. Eh? Three to one is very fair odds.”

“Not under those fort’s guns, if you please sir,” Yeo protested. “If the Philistines will only come out to meet us on the open sea, we will make an end of them like Zeba and Zalmunna.”

“True enough,” Amyas agreed. “Game cocks are game cocks, but reason is still reason.”

“The Philistines aren’t coming out, but they’re sending a messenger instead,” Cary yelled. “Watch out, all you thick skulls!”

As he spoke, a puff of white smoke rolled from the eastern fort, and a heavy cannon ball plunged into the water between the battery and their ship.

“I don’t like that at all,” Amyas frowned. “What do they mean by firing on us without warning? And what are these warships doing here? Drew, you told me the Spanish armadas never anchored in this roadstead.”

“Nor do they, as far as I know, sir, the anchorage is far too rough, as you can see. I’m mortal afraid that rascal Indian’s story was true, and the Dons have got wind of our coming.”

“Run up a white flag, at any rate. If they were expecting us, they must have known long enough ago, otherwise how would they have gotten all these ships here?”

“True, sir. They must have sailed all the way from Santa Marta at the very least, maybe even from Cartagena. That would take a full month, going and coming.”

Amyas suddenly remembered Eustace’s threat back in that country inn on their way to the Devon coast. Could he have betrayed their whole purpose? Impossible!

“Let us call a council of war, Frank,” he said.

But Frank was paying no attention, completely absorbed in a different sight. Half a mile east of the town, two or three hundred feet up the steep mountain side, stood a large, low white house half-buried in trees and gardens. There was no other building of similar size anywhere nearby, no space for one. And wasn’t that the royal flag of Spain flapping in the breeze before it? That must be the governor’s house! That must be the home of the Rose of Torridge! Frank stared at it wild-eyed, until he had convinced himself he could make out the figure of a woman walking on the front terrace, a figure that could be none other than the one he had traveled so far to find. Amyas had to practically drag him away to join the war council, a gloomy, barely civil gathering on the ship’s poop deck.

The three leaders, with Brimblecombe, Yeo, and Drew, drew apart, each staring the other in the face. Their months of careful planning and bright hopes had been dashed to nothing in a single hour.

“It’s impossible, you see,” Amyas said at last, “to surprise the town from the landward side while these warships are here. If we land our men, we leave our ship completely undefended.”

“And just as impossible to challenge Don Guzman to a fight when he’s not even here,” Cary added.

“I wonder why the warships haven’t already opened fire on us,” Drew said.

“Maybe they respect our flag of truce,” Cary said. “Why not send a small boat in to parley with them, and ask after —”

“After her?” Frank cut him off. “If we let them see we know she is here, her name will be forever tarnished in the eyes of those jealous, suspicious Spaniards.”

“And as for them respecting our flag of truce, gentlemen,” Yeo said, “take the advice of an old man: do not trust them. They will keep the same faith with us as they kept with Captain Hawkins at San Juan d’Ulloa, that accursed business that was the start of all these wars. We could have seized the entire plate fleet, two hundred thousand pounds worth of gold on board, and we did no such thing. We only asked leave to trade like honest men. And after they granted us that license, tricked us with fair speeches into landing ourselves and all our ordnance, the governor and the whole fleet turned on us, five to one, and gave no quarter to a single soul they captured. No sirs, the only reason they aren’t attacking us now is because their crews haven’t gotten back on board their ships.”

“They will soon enough, then,” Amyas said. “I can see soldiers pouring down the town’s landing stairs right now.”

Sure enough, boats piled high with armed Spanish men-at-arms were pushing off the shore, heading straight for the anchored warships.

“Thank the Lord we didn’t sail here two hours earlier,” Drew said. “The sun will set before they can get their ships ready for sea, and those fellows won’t have the stomach to chase us out on the dark ocean.”
