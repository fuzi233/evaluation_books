My days were now fully occupied—filled with purpose and steady effort. Between teaching others and studying myself, I had little time to spare. It was satisfying, this sense of progress: no longer idle or stagnant, but actively shaping my mind, sharpening it through constant use. The world around me offered a broad experience—one not confined by narrow limits. Villette is a city of many peoples, and in Madame Beck’s school, girls from nearly every European nation mingled freely, each bearing the marks of her own station in life. Equality was deeply rooted here; though not formally republican, Labassecour lived almost as if it were. At the desks of Madame Beck’s institution, noble daughters sat beside those of humble birth, indistinguishable by outward signs—except that often, the commoner girl carried herself with greater openness and kindness, while the aristocratic one wore an air of polished insolence, masked beneath a delicate veneer of deceit.

In the blood of these young ladies, there was sometimes a quick French spark mixed with sluggish northern temperament. And sadly, this vitality seemed to manifest itself not in noble impulses, but in a slick ease with which flattery flowed—and fiction, too. Their manner was light, lively, even charming—but empty of sincerity, devoid of heart.

Yet let fairness be said: the native girls of Labassecour had their own kind of hypocrisy—though coarser, more blatant, less likely to fool anyone. When a lie was needed, they delivered it without hesitation, untroubled by conscience. No soul in Madame Beck’s house—from the kitchen maid to the headmistress—would blush at deception. To invent was not seen as virtue, but it was considered among the least serious of sins. “I have lied several times,” became a routine confession every month. The priest listened without surprise, absolved without reluctance. But miss mass? Read a novel instead of scripture? That was another matter—those were true transgressions, met with penance and rebuke.

Still unaccustomed to this way of life, still learning its hidden currents, I managed well enough in my new role. After the first few tense lessons—delivered amid danger, like standing on the edge of a volcano rumbling beneath my feet—I found the storm seemed to quieten, at least as far as I was concerned. My desire for success was strong. I could not bear the thought of failing, not because of my own shortcomings, but due to the willful disobedience and indifference of others. Many nights I lay awake, plotting how best to gain control, how to win influence over these restless spirits. I saw clearly that I could expect no help from Madame Beck. Her principle was to remain beloved by the pupils, no matter what cost to justice or comfort for the teachers. To seek her support during any crisis was to invite dismissal. She took only what pleased her—what was sweet, agreeable, flattering—while demanding that her subordinates endure the harsh duties that brought disfavor.

So I must rely on myself alone.

First, it was evident that force would never work. These girls could not be driven. They required patience, gentle handling. A calm, respectful demeanor won their regard; a rare, sharp jest could even delight them. But long mental labor, heavy demands on memory or attention—they rejected outright. While an English girl of average ability might quietly accept a difficult task, a Labassecourienne would laugh, throw it back, and declare: “Heavens, how hard! I don’t want it. It bores me too much.”

A wise teacher would take the assignment back without argument, carefully simplify it, return it with clear explanation—and then apply sarcasm with boldness. They would wince, perhaps, but they did not resent such mockery—provided it was not sour, but hearty, spoken plainly so all could read it: a reflection of their own ignorance, laziness, weakness. They’d protest over extra lines of homework, but never over a wound to their pride—little as they possessed, it had been trained to submit, and even welcomed the firm pressure of a strong hand.

As I grew more fluent in their language, able to wield its sharper idioms with skill, the older, brighter girls began to respond to me—not with affection, exactly, but with a certain respect. I noticed that whenever one of them felt a stir of honest shame or worthy ambition, she was already half-conquered. If I could make her ears burn under her thick hair, all was well. Soon, bouquets began appearing on my desk each morning. In return, I sometimes walked with a few favored students during breaks.

During these conversations, I once ventured—without planning—to correct some of their twisted notions of morality. Especially, I spoke about the wrong of lying. In a moment of candor, I added that between falsehood and missing church occasionally, I saw the lie as worse. The girls were taught to report everything I said to their priests. An unexpected consequence followed.

Something invisible, unnamed, slipped between me and my most attentive pupils. The flowers kept coming, but conversation ceased. Whenever I walked the paths or sat in the garden bench, a student would approach my right side—only for a teacher to appear instantly at my left. And Madame Beck, silent as a shadow, would materialize behind me, swift and sudden as a breeze.

Once, a pupil who had received small kindnesss from me leaned close and said: “Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!”

“Why?” I asked.

“Because when you die—you’ll burn straight into Hell.”

“Do you believe that?”

“Certainly. Everyone knows it. Besides, the priest told me.”

Isabelle was blunt, odd, childlike. Then, in a whisper: “To save your soul up there, it would be better to burn alive down here.”

I laughed—it was impossible not to.

Have you forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? If so, allow me to reintroduce her—now a thriving pupil at Madame Beck’s school. She arrived in the Rue Fossette just days after my own arrival, meeting me with no great surprise. There was something remarkable in her—she moved through life with the effortless grace of a duchess, utterly unconcerned, completely nonchalant. Her emotions were thin, fleeting—her likes and dislikes spun from gossamer. Yet one thing was solid, enduring: her selfishness.

She was not proud, and—being as good-natured as I was—she quickly made me her confidante. She teased me endlessly with complaints: the salt fish on Fridays, the hard eggs, the soup, the bread, the coffee—all beneath her taste. She called the staff despicable, simply because they were foreigners. For a while, I endured her criticisms with patience, but eventually, tired of repetition, I snapped back—something I should have done earlier. A firm reprimand always suited her.

Even longer I suffered her demands for help with sewing. Her wardrobe was elegant, well-stocked with fine clothes—but not with undergarments or stockings, which she neglected. She hated needlework, so she dumped heaps of hose and linen onto my desk, expecting me to mend them. When it became clear this would become unbearable, I finally told her plainly: she must mend her own things.

She wept, accused me of abandoning our friendship. But I stood firm, letting her cry out her tantrum.

Despite her flaws—many, and none particularly noble—how lovely she was! On a sunny Sunday morning, dressed in pale lilac silk, her golden curls falling over her white shoulders, she looked radiant. Sundays were holidays spent with friends in town. Among these friends, she soon hinted, was one who wished to be more than friend.

By glances and whispers, by the glow in her eyes and the lift of her step, it became clear: she was being courted—perhaps loved—with deep passion. She called him “Isidore.” Not his real name, she said—just a title she chose for him, since his actual name was “not very pretty.” Once, when boasting of his devotion, I asked if she returned his love.

“Comme cela,” she replied. “He’s handsome, and he loves me madly—so I’m amused. That’s enough.”

Seeing she persisted longer than I expected—given her fickle nature—I decided to inquire further. Was this man someone her parents, especially her uncle—who controlled her fortune—would approve?

She admitted it was doubtful. “Isidore” had little money.

“Do you encourage him?” I asked.

“Furiously, sometimes,” she answered.

“Without knowing whether you’ll ever be allowed to marry him?”

“Oh, how dull you are! I don’t want to marry. I’m too young.”

“But if he loves you as much as you say, and nothing comes of it—he’ll suffer.”

“Of course he’ll break his heart. I’d be shocked if he didn’t.”

“I wonder if M. Isidore is a fool?” I said.

“He is, about me. But he’s clever otherwise—everyone says so. Mrs. Cholmondeley thinks he’ll rise through talent. All I know is, he sighs near me, and I can twist him round my finger.”

Wanting a clearer picture of this devoted M. Isidore—whose position seemed fragile—I asked her to describe him. But she couldn’t. She lacked words, or the skill to shape them into vivid images. She hadn’t truly observed him—nothing of his face, his expression, stayed with her. All she could say was: “He’s handsome, but more a gentleman than a boy.”

My patience often failed, my interest flagged—yet one thing held me: every hint, every detail she dropped, revealed, unconsciously, that M. Isidore treated her with care, dignity, restraint. I told her plainly that I believed him far too good for her—and that she was merely a vain flirt. She laughed, tossed her curls, and danced away as if I’d paid her a compliment.

Miss Ginevra’s studies were superficial. She took music, singing, dancing seriously—also embroidering fine cambric handkerchiefs she couldn’t afford to buy ready-made. History, geography, grammar, arithmetic? Left undone—or done by others. Much of her time was spent visiting. Madame Beck, aware her stay was limited and would not be extended regardless of progress, allowed her great freedom.

Mrs. Cholmondeley—her chaperone—a fashionable, lively woman—invited her frequently, and sometimes took her to evening parties. Ginevra approved this arrangement—except for one flaw: she had to dress well, yet lacked funds for varied attire. This problem consumed her thoughts. Her usually idle mind became fiercely active, driven by necessity and vanity.

She begged boldly of Mrs. Cholmondeley—not with shame, but cheerfully:
“My darling Mrs. C., I have nothing fit to wear for your party next week. You _must_ give me a book-muslin dress and a sky-blue sash. Do—there’s an angel! Will you?”

At first, Mrs. Cholmondeley gave in. But as requests multiplied, she resisted—like all of Ginevra’s friends. Soon, no more gifts came. Yet visits continued, and dresses were still supplied—along with gloves, bouquets, even jewelry. These, unlike her usual behavior—since she was not secretive—were hidden for a time.

Then, one evening, before a grand ball, she burst into my room, eager to show off her splendor.

She looked stunning—so young, so fresh, with a delicacy of skin and grace of form distinctly English, rare among continental women. Her dress was new, expensive, perfect. Every detail was flawless—costly finishes that elevated the whole effect.

I studied her from head to toe. She turned playfully, showing me every angle. Confident in her beauty, she was in high spirits—her small blue eyes sparkling.

She reached for a kiss, in her girlish way. But I stepped back.
“Steady,” I said. “Let us be steady. Let’s understand what this means.”

“Shall I do?” she asked.

“Do?” I repeated. “There are many ways to ‘do.’ And frankly, I don’t understand yours.”

“But how do I look?”

“You look well-dressed.”

She thought that praise too cold. She pointed to her jewels.
“Look at this _parure_—the brooch, earrings, bracelets. No one in the school has such a set—not even Madame.”

“I see them,” I said. (Pause.) “Did M. de Bassompierre give you these?”

“My uncle knows nothing about them.”

“Mrs. Cholmondeley?”

“Not a chance. She’s stingy. She gives me nothing now.”

I turned away.

“Now, old Crusty—old Diogenes,” she said, using her pet names for me when we clashed. “What’s wrong now?”

“Go away. I don’t enjoy looking at you or your trinkets.”

She hesitated.

“What now, Mother Wisdom? I haven’t gone into debt. Not for the jewels, nor the gloves, nor the bouquet. My dress isn’t paid for—but Uncle de Bassompierre will cover it. He never checks the items, just the total. And he’s rich. Who cares about a few guineas?”

“Will you leave? I want to shut the door… Ginevra, people may tell you you’re beautiful in that gown—but to me, you’ve never looked lovelier than in the plain gingham dress and straw bonnet I first saw you in.”

“Others don’t have your Puritan tastes,” she snapped. “And why do you preach to me?”

“Because I have little right—but you have even less to come strutting into my room like a jay in borrowed feathers. I respect neither your plumes, nor especially those peacock eyes you call a _parure_. Beautiful, yes—if you bought them with your own money. But not now.”

“On est là pour Mademoiselle Fanshawe!” announced the porter. And she skipped off.

The mystery of the _parure_ remained until a few days later, when she returned to confess.

“You needn’t be angry with me,” she began. “I’m not running Papa or Uncle deeply into debt. Everything is paid—except the recent dresses. The rest is settled.”

Ah, there was the clue—since Mrs. Cholmondeley hadn’t given them, and Ginevra’s own funds were slight.

“Ecoutez!” she whispered, drawing closer. “I’ll tell you everything. You’ll see it’s not only right, but clever. First, I _must_ go out. Papa said I should see the world. He told Mrs. Cholmondeley I had a bit of a schoolgirl air—‘bread-and-butter’ he called it—and wanted me to lose it before my debut in England. So I must go out. I must dress. Mrs. Cholmondeley is stingy. It would be unfair to burden Uncle with all my needs. That’s fair, isn’t it? That agrees with your sermons.”

“Well, then,” I said, “someone heard me complaining to Mrs. Cholmondeley about needing ornaments—someone who, instead of refusing, was delighted to offer a gift. You should have seen him—how shy, how nervous, how trembling with fear of rejection!”

“That’s enough, Miss Fanshawe. I suppose I’m to understand that M. Isidore is your benefactor—that he gave you this costly _parure_, the gloves, the bouquets?”

“You speak so unpleasantly,” she said. “But yes—I allow Isidore the pleasure of expressing his admiration by giving me a small token.”

“It’s the same thing. Now, Ginevra, I don’t understand these matters well—but I believe you’re doing something very wrong. Perhaps you think you’ll marry him? Have your parents agreed? Do you love him truly?”

“Mais pas du tout!” (She always switched to French when speaking something cruel or defiant.) “Je suis sa reine, mais il n’est pas mon roi.”

“Excuse me,” I said, “but I think this is mere coquetry. You’re not great—but you’re above taking advantage of a man’s generosity when you feel nothing for him. You love M. Isidore more than you admit.”

“No,” she said. “I danced with a young officer the other night whom I love a thousand times more. I often wonder why I feel so cold toward Isidore. Everyone says he’s handsome. Other women admire him. But somehow—he bores me. Let me think…”

I encouraged her.

“Yes,” I said. “Try to understand yourself. To me, it seems chaotic—like a rag-bag.”

“Like this,” she said suddenly: “he’s too romantic, too devoted. He expects more from me than I’m willing to give. He thinks I’m perfect—full of virtues I’ve never had, nor intend to have. And in his presence, I find myself trying to live up to his idea of me. It tires me to pretend to be sensible, to be good. He really believes I am sensible. But with you, dear crosspatch—you take me as I am, flawed, silly, selfish, frivolous. You know me, and that makes it easier.”

“This is all well,” I said, struggling to keep my tone grave, “but it doesn’t change the fact that you’re accepting gifts. Pack them up, Ginevra, and send them back.”

“No,” she said firmly.

“Then you’re deceiving him. By accepting his presents, you imply he’ll one day receive something in return—your love.”

“But he won’t,” she interrupted. “He has his reward now—the joy of seeing me wear them. That’s enough for him. He’s only bourgeois.”

That phrase—arrogant, meaningless—shook me from my momentary softness. I hardened again.

She went on:
“Besides, he doesn’t expect marriage. He knows I’m not serious. He’s content with adoration. And I’m content with admiration. It’s a fair exchange.”
