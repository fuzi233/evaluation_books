The morning air hit Archer like a physical weight, thick and humid, pressing down on the platform of the Fall River train. Stepping onto the ground, he emerged not into a city, but into a steaming cauldron of midsummer Boston. The scent was immediate and overwhelming—a cloying mixture of sour beer, burnt coffee, rotting fruit, and the damp wool of a thousand bodies. Around him, a shirt-sleeved populace shuffled through the station grounds with the intimate, resigned abandon of boarders heading for the communal bathhouse, moving as if the very atmosphere demanded their submission.

Seeking refuge from the sensory assault, Archer hailed a cab and instructed the driver for the Somerset Club. Even in these fashionable quarters, the city retained an air of untidy domesticity, a chaotic warmth that no amount of European refinement could degrade. Caretakers in coarse calico lounged on the doorsteps of the wealthy, watching the world drift by, while the Common beyond looked less like a public park and more like a pleasure ground abandoned on the morning after a drunken picnic. If Archer had attempted to conjure images of Ellen Olenska in exotic, improbable settings, he would have failed; nowhere was she more difficult to place than here, amidst this heat-prostrated, deserted sprawl.

Inside the club, he ate with a renewed vigor and method. A slice of melon, followed by toast and scrambled eggs, fueled a new sense of kinetic energy. Since announcing to May the previous night that he had business in Boston and intended to catch the Fall River boat that evening before continuing to New York, he had felt a strange liberation. It was understood he would return early in the week, yet a letter from the office, conveniently placed on the hall table, had justified his sudden pivot. He felt a flicker of shame at the ease with which he had manipulated the situation, a reminder of Lawrence Lefferts' masterful contrivances for securing his own freedom, but the feeling dissipated quickly. He was not in an analytical mood today.

After breakfast, he smoked a cigarette and scanned the *Commercial Advertiser*. Two or three acquaintances entered, exchanging the perfunctory greetings of a world that remained stubbornly intact despite his internal dislocation. He checked his watch; it was half-past nine. Rising, he retreated to the writing room, penned a brief note, and dispatched a messenger to the Parker House with instructions to await a reply. Sitting behind another newspaper, he calculated the travel time to the hotel, trying to impose order on the chaos outside.

"The lady was out, sir," a waiter's voice cut through his thoughts.

"Out?" Archer stammered, the word sounding alien in his mouth.

He rose and stepped into the hall. It must be a mistake. She could not be gone at this hour. Anger flushed his cheeks at his own stupidity; why had he not sent the note immediately upon arrival? Grabbing his hat and stick, he stepped out into the street. The city had transformed, becoming vast, empty, and strangely hostile, as if he were a traveler lost in a foreign land. Hesitating on the doorstep, he resolved to proceed to the Parker House anyway. Perhaps the messenger had been wrong; perhaps she was still there.

He crossed the Common, stopping on the first bench beneath a sprawling tree. There she was. She wore a grey silk sunshade over her head—a detail that shattered his previous mental image of her with a pink one. Her posture was listless, slumped as if gravity had doubled its pull. He noted the drooping profile, the knot of hair fastened low in the neck beneath her dark hat, and the long, wrinkled glove holding the shade. As he approached, she turned.

"Oh," she said.

For the first time, he registered a startled look in her eyes, which quickly softened into a slow smile of wonder and contentment.

"Oh," she murmured again, on a different note, as he stood gazing down at her. Without rising, she gestured for him to join her on the bench.

"I'm here on business—just got here," Archer explained. Without understanding why, he began to feign astonishment at the sight of her. "But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?" He felt as though he were shouting across an ocean, fearing she might vanish before he could close the distance.

"I? Oh, I'm here on business too," she answered, turning her head until they faced one another. The words barely reached him; he was consumed by the startling clarity of her voice, devoid of any echo in his memory. He realized then that it was low-pitched, carrying a faint roughness on the consonants.

"You do your hair differently," he said, his heart hammering as if he had spoken an irreversible sentence.

"Differently? No—it's only that I do it as best I can when I'm without Nastasia."

"Nastasia; but isn't she with you?"

"No; I'm alone. For two days it was not worth while to bring her."

"You're alone—at the Parker House?"

She looked at him with a flash of her old malice. "Does it strike you as dangerous?"

"No; not dangerous—"

"But unconventional? I see; I suppose it is." She considered the statement for a moment. "I hadn't thought of it, because I've just done something so much more unconventional." A faint tinge of irony lingered in her eyes. "I've just refused to take back a sum of money—that belonged to me."

Archer sprang up, stepping back. She had furled her parasol and sat absently tracing patterns in the gravel. He returned to stand before her.

"Some one... has come here to meet you?"

"Yes."

"With this offer?"

She nodded.

"And you refused—because of the conditions?"

"I refused," she said after a pause.

He sat down beside her. "What were the conditions?"

"Oh, they were not onerous: just to sit at the head of his table now and then."

Silence stretched between them. Archer's heart had slammed shut in a peculiar way, and he sat vainly searching for words.

"He wants you back—at any price?"

"Well—a considerable price. At least the sum is considerable for me."

He paused, beating around the question he felt compelled to ask.

"It was to meet him here that you came?"

She stared, then burst into laughter. "Meet him—my husband? *Here*? At this season he's always at Cowes or Baden."

"He sent someone?"

"Yes."

"With a letter?"

She shook her head. "No; just a message. He never writes. I don't think I've had more than one letter from him." The allusion brought color to her cheek, reflecting in Archer's own vivid blush.

"Why does he never write?"

"Why should he? What does one have secretaries for?"

The young man's blush deepened. She had pronounced the word as if it held no more significance than any other in her vocabulary. For a moment, the question "Did he send his secretary?" hovered on his tongue, but the memory of Count Olenski's single letter to his wife silenced him. He paused, then plunged forward again.

"And the person?"

"The emissary? The emissary," Madame Olenska rejoined, still smiling, "might, for all I care, have left already; but he has insisted on waiting till this evening... in case... on the chance..."

"And you came out here to think the chance over?"

"I came out to get a breath of air. The hotel's too stifling. I'm taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth."

They sat in silence, not looking at each other but staring straight ahead at the passersby. Finally, she turned her eyes to his face. "You're not changed."

He felt the urge to answer, "I was, till I saw you again," but instead, he stood up abruptly and glanced around at the untidy, sweltering park.

"This is horrible. Why shouldn't we go out a little on the bay? There's a breeze, and it will be cooler. We might take the steamboat down to Point Arley." She glanced up hesitantly, and he pressed on. "On a Monday morning there won't be anybody on the boat. My train doesn't leave till evening: I'm going back to New York. Why shouldn't we?" he insisted, looking down at her. Suddenly, he broke out, "Haven't we done all we could?"

"Oh"—she murmured again. She stood up, reopening her sunshade and scanning the scene as if consulting the landscape for permission to remain. Then her eyes returned to his face. "You mustn't say things like that to me," she said.

"I'll say anything you like; or nothing. I won't open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm can it do to anybody? All I want is to listen to you," he stammered.

She produced a small gold watch on an enamelled chain. "Oh, don't calculate," he broke out, "give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. At what time was he coming?"

Her color rose again. "At eleven."

"Then you must come at once."

"You needn't be afraid—if I don't come."

"Nor you either—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you, to know what you've been doing. It's a hundred years since we've met—it may be another hundred before we meet again."
