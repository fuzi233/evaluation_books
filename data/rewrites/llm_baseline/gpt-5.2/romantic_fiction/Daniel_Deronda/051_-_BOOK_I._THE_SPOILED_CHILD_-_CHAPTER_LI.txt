When Deronda presented himself at the door of his mother’s apartment in the Italia, something long-buried in him stirred awake—not tenderness exactly, but that earlier boyhood tremor, like the first thin cry of a kettle before water boils. Even the antechamber made him newly aware of sound: the hush was not natural quiet, but the manufactured stillness of thick curtains and trained servants, with the sea outside held back as if by a door’s consent. The two attendants looked at him with a marked surprise, as if expecting a physician with old hands and a leather case of tinctures, not this young man whose evening dress sat on him as easily as a well-cut jacket on a chef who knows his own worth.

But he scarcely saw them. The second door opened, and the room beyond—large, dimly mellowed, and arranged like a stage awaiting a single entrance—offered him at its far end a figure standing to receive him.

She was covered, save her face and part of her arms, in black lace that fell loosely from the summit of whitening hair and lengthened behind her in a train. The lace looked like sea-foam turned to mourning, light and intricate, yet weighted with intention. Her arms, bare to the elbow except for rich bracelets, were folded, and her head had that poised habit which makes a face seem handsomer than it is by giving it authority. Deronda had no interval for examining; he was already near, taking the hand she held out—cool, fine-boned, with a firmness that suggested not domestic caress but command—and lifting it to his lips.

She did not withdraw. She kept his hand in hers and looked at him as if measuring a story she had once rehearsed and now found altered by time. Her eyes were piercing; her face so mobile that even while she watched him, the shift of brow and nostril spoke like a dialect he could feel without knowing its words. Deronda dared no movement. His chief consciousness was his own strange helplessness: the long private imaginings, those ideal meetings with a mother made tender by absence, had been more vivid than this. He felt his color rise and fall with the quick shame of youth, while emotion itself—warmth, joy, grief—hung back, as if waiting for a cue. He could not even guess what language she would choose. It seemed impossible that she should speak plain English.

Suddenly she dropped his hand, placed both hers on his shoulders, and her face gave out a flash of admiration in which every worn line vanished, as if light had poured through her from some earlier hour.

“You are a beautiful creature!” she said, in a low melodious voice, the syllables edged with a foreign outline that made English itself seem like an adopted tongue. “I knew you would be.” Then she kissed him on each cheek, and he returned the kisses; yet it was like a ceremonial greeting between royalties, not the meeting of flesh that has remembered flesh.

She paused—lines returning to her face as if they belonged there and could not be kept away—and her tone cooled.

“I am your mother. But you can have no love for me.”

“I have thought of you more than of any other being in the world,” Deronda answered, his voice trembling with nervousness rather than tears.

“I am not like what you thought I was,” she said decisively, withdrawing her hands and folding her arms again, inviting him—almost challenging him—to look. He had often pictured a face akin to his own. He saw the likeness now, but set within differences more striking. Her beauty was worn, yet not softened by time; it had a strangeness in it, as if she were not wholly a human mother but a Melusina—half-bound to some world that keeps its own laws and does not ask ours.

“I used to think you might be suffering,” he said, anxious above all not to wound. “I used to wish I could be a comfort to you.”

“I am suffering. But with a suffering that you can’t comfort,” she replied, harder than before, moving to a sofa where cushions had been placed with the careful solicitude of servants accustomed to guarding a precious, temperamental instrument. “Sit down.” She pointed to a seat near. And then, noticing his distress, she added more gently, “I am not suffering at this moment. I am at ease now. I am able to talk.”

He sat and waited. Already she had thrown him to a distance—not by space, but by the air of inevitability around her, as if he had come not to a mother but to a Fate that had decided long ago and was only now explaining the decree.

“No,” she began. “I did not send for you to comfort me. I could not know beforehand—I don’t know now—what you will feel toward me. I have not the foolish notion that you can love me merely because I am your mother, when you have never seen or heard of me in all your life. But I thought I chose something better for you than being with me. I did not think I deprived you of anything worth having.”

“You cannot wish me to believe that your affection would not have been worth having,” Deronda said, because her pause seemed to demand answer.

“I don’t mean to speak ill of myself,” she flung out with proud impetuosity. “But I had not much affection to give you. I did not want affection. I had been stifled with it. I wanted to live out the life that was in me, and not to be hampered with other lives. You wonder what I was. I was no princess then.” She rose suddenly and stood again, and Deronda rose too without thinking; he felt as if the air itself had altered.

“No princess in this tame life that I live in now. I was a great singer, and I acted as well as I sang. All the rest were poor beside me. Men followed me from one country to another. I was living a myriad lives in one. I did not want a child.”

The words had the vehement edge of self-defence, spoken as if she were hurling them against a tribunal that had waited years for her confession. Deronda stood listening—his hand clutching his coat-collar as if he were keeping himself from sinking—while his blood moved in him with the bewildered commotion one might feel at witnessing a rite that sanctifies what one has been taught to call crime. What else was she going to tell him? Her face had that pale illumination of a performer in tragic passage, and she went on.

“I did not want to marry. I was forced into marrying your father—forced, I mean, by my father’s wishes and commands; and besides, it was my best way of getting some freedom. I could rule my husband, but not my father. I had a right to be free. I had a right to seek my freedom from a bondage that I hated.”

She sat again; her eyes and closed lips made that subtle movement which is like speech held back only because breath must be gathered. Deronda remained standing. After a moment she looked up with less defiance and more pleading.

“And the bondage I hated for myself I wanted to keep you from. What better could the most loving mother have done? I relieved you from the bondage of having been born a Jew.”

“Then I am a Jew?” The question burst from him with a deep-voiced energy that made her shrink back against her cushions. “My father was a Jew, and you are a Jewess?”

“Yes—your father was my cousin,” she said, watching him with a change as if she had seen something she might need to fear.

“I am glad of it,” he said, impetuously, in the veiled voice of passion.

He could not have imagined beforehand that the admission would come like that—hot and sudden, not as the quiet acceptance of a truth, but as opposition to her. Anger shook through him—anger at a mother who had borne him unwillingly, made herself a stranger by choice, and who now seemed to reveal herself under compulsion. That last suspicion flashed a light over her speech.

Her anger rose too, visibly physical. Her frame trembled; her pallor made her eyes look larger.

“Why do you say you are glad? You are an English gentleman. I secured you that.”

“You did not know what you secured me. How could you choose my birthright for me?” Deronda answered, throwing himself sideways into his chair as if the posture could help him hold down the surge inside him. He leaned his arm over the back and looked away.

The intolerance in him felt foreign, like a voice borrowed from someone harsher; and already he struggled to master it. With the anger came horror—horror lest he speak too hard in a moment that could never be called back. A pause fell. When she spoke again, her tone had hardened into a composed resistance, finely varied, like a singer controlling breath.

“I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself. How could I know that you would have the spirit of my father in you? How could I know that you would love what I hated?—if you really love to be a Jew.”

The bitterness made her words sound like enmity. Yet Deronda’s fuller self returned; his mind reached out, as it habitually did, to the life within the other person. He remembered the signs of suffering in her frame, the best years gone, the effort of speaking. His face regained its penetrative calm; and that calm, instead of soothing her, seemed to disturb her like an unwelcome mirror.

“Forgive me, if I speak hastily,” he said with diffident gravity. “Why have you resolved now on disclosing to me what you took care to have me brought up in ignorance of? Why—since you seem angry that I should be glad?”

“Oh—the reasons of our actions!” She gave a ring of sarcastic scorn. “When you are as old as I am, it will not seem so simple a question—‘Why did you do this?’ People talk of their motives in a cut and dried way. Every woman is supposed to have the same set of motives, or else to be a monster. I am not a monster, but I have not felt exactly what other women feel—or say they feel, for fear of being thought unlike others. When you reproach me in your heart for sending you away from me, you mean that I ought to say I felt about you as other women say they feel about their children. I did not feel that. I was glad to be freed from you. But I did well for you, and I gave you your father’s fortune. Do I seem now to be revoking everything?—Well, there are reasons. I feel many things that I cannot understand. A fatal illness has been growing in me for a year. I shall very likely not live another year. I will not deny anything I have done. I will not pretend to love where I have no love. But shadows are rising round me. Sickness makes them. If I have wronged the dead—I have but little time to do what I left undone.”

Every change of tone was as perfectly delivered as any trained actress could manage; and yet it was sincere—her nature turned feeling into representation at once, so that truth and performance were not enemies but twin threads, braided tightly. Deronda did not reflect on that. He heard only the purport: illness, approaching death, and an obligation to the dead. Compassion rose and forbade him to press her with questions while she sat silent, brow knit, head turned slightly away, eyes fixed as if on something in the air that only she could see.

She broke the silence abruptly, turning her eyes on him, speaking more quickly.

“Sir Hugo has written much about you. He tells me you have a wonderful mind—you comprehend everything—you are wiser than he is with all his sixty years. You say you are glad to know that you were born a Jew. I am not going to tell you that I have changed my mind about that. Your feelings are against mine. You don’t thank me for what I did. Shall you comprehend your mother, or only blame her?”

“There is not a fibre within me but makes me wish to comprehend her,” Deronda said, meeting her sharp gaze solemnly. “It is a bitter reversal of my longing to think of blaming her. What I have been most trying to do for fifteen years is to have some understanding of those who differ from myself.”

“Then you have become unlike your grandfather in that,” she returned, “though you are a young copy of him in your face. He never comprehended me—or if he did, he only thought of fettering me into obedience. I was to be what he called ‘the Jewish woman’ under pain of his curse. I was to feel everything I did not feel, and believe everything I did not believe.”

She spoke of it as of a house whose smells still clung to her hair and skin—old oil and wax and shut-up cloth, the sour breath of long fasts, the heavy sweetness of festival dishes that came to her not as comfort but as coercion. Her voice sharpened with remembered disgust.

“I was to feel awe for the bit of parchment in the mezuza over the door; to dread lest a bit of butter should touch a bit of meat; to think it beautiful that men should bind the tephillin on them, and women not,—to adore the wisdom of such laws, however silly they might seem to me. I was to love the long prayers in the ugly synagogue, and the howling, and the gabbling, and the dreadful fasts, and the tiresome feasts, and my father’s endless discoursing about our people, which was a thunder without meaning in my ears. I was to care forever about what Israel had been; and I did not care at all. I cared for the wide world, and all that I could represent in it.”

As she said “howling” and “gabbling,” Deronda’s mind—trained to attend—could almost hear the soundscape she described: a crowded room of male voices swelling and breaking, the clatter of benches, the insistent rhythm of words repeated until sense dissolves into sound. It came to him how such noise could become an enclosure, how a life might feel driven by it as surely as by walls. And yet in him, the same rituals were gathering another meaning, not from habit but from hunger: the desire to know the tongue of origins, to taste what had been withheld, to name himself with the old names rather than borrowed ones.

She continued, the words pressing forward as if they had waited years behind her teeth.

“I hated living under the shadow of my father’s strictness. Teaching, teaching for everlasting—‘this you must be,’ ‘that you must not be’—pressed on me like a frame that got tighter and tighter as I grew. I wanted to live a large life, with freedom to do what every one else did, and be carried along in a great current, not obliged to care. Ah!” Her tone cut more bitterly. “You are glad to have been born a Jew. You say so. That is because you have not been brought up as a Jew. That separateness seems sweet to you because I saved you from it.”

“When you resolved on that, you meant that I should never know my origin?” Deronda asked, the question coming out with the urgency of someone who has been fed on substitutes and has suddenly smelled the real broth.

“Yes, that was what I meant. That is what I persevered in. And it is not true to say that I have changed. Things have changed in spite of me. I am still the same Leonora”—she pointed with her forefinger to her breast—“here within me is the same desire, the same will, the same choice, but—” She spread her hands, palms upward, on each side as if showing emptiness. Her voice fell into muffled rapid utterance. “Events come upon us like evil enchantments: and thoughts, feelings, apparitions in the darkness are events—are they not? I don’t consent. We only consent to what we love. I obey something tyrannic.” Again the spread hands. “I am forced to be withered, to feel pain, to be dying slowly. Do I love that? Well, I have been forced to obey my dead father. I have been forced to tell you that you are a Jew, and deliver to you what he commanded me to deliver.”

“I beseech you to tell me what moved you—when you were young, I mean—to take the course you did,” Deronda said, trying to draw her away from the present’s heart-rending mixture of suffering and defiance. “I gather that my grandfather opposed your bent to be an artist. Though my own experience has been quite different, I enter into the painfulness of your struggle. I can imagine the hardship of an enforced renunciation.”

“No,” she said, shaking her head, folding her arms again with decision. “You are not a woman. You may try—but you can never imagine what it is to have a man’s force of genius in you, and yet to suffer the slavery of being a girl. To have a pattern cut out—‘this is the Jewish woman; this is what you must be; this is what you are wanted for; a woman’s heart must be of such a size and no larger, else it must be pressed small, like Chinese feet; her happiness is to be made as cakes are, by a fixed receipt.’”

The metaphor startled him with its domestic cruelty—happiness measured like ingredients, womanhood baked to a prescribed shape, any appetite beyond the recipe treated as sin. And behind it he felt another theme beating, older than their quarrel: how language itself, the naming and defining, can be a cage—how a dominant lexicon writes a person’s possibilities as if writing a menu that forbids hunger.

“That was what my father wanted,” she went on. “He wished I had been a son; he cared for me as a make-shift link. His heart was set on his Judaism. He hated that Jewish women should be thought of by the Christian world as a sort of ware to make public singers and actresses of. As if we were not the more enviable for that! That is a chance of escaping from bondage.”

“Was my grandfather a learned man?” Deronda asked, eager for particulars, fearing her fierce generalities would pass over the details that might anchor him.

She answered impatiently, lifting her hand. “Oh, yes,—and a clever physician—and good: I don’t deny that he was good. A man to be admired in a play—grand, with an iron will. Like the old Foscari before he pardons. But such men turn their wives and daughters into slaves. They would rule the world if they could; but not ruling the world, they throw all the weight of their will on the necks and souls of women. But nature sometimes thwarts them. My father had no other child than his daughter, and she was like himself.”

Her folded arms and set gaze were like armor, as if she expected mastery to strike again from the past.

“Your father was different. Unlike me—all lovingness and affection. I knew I could rule him; and I made him secretly promise me, before I married him, that he would put no hindrance in the way of my being an artist. My father was on his deathbed when we were married: from the first he had fixed his mind on my marrying my cousin Ephraim. And when a woman’s will is as strong as the man’s who wants to govern her, half her strength must be concealment. I meant to have my will in the end, but I could only have it by seeming to obey.”

Her voice changed; something like an involuntary tremor entered it.

“I had an awe of my father—always I had had an awe of him: it was impossible to help it. I hated to feel awed—I wished I could have defied him openly; but I never could. It was what I could not imagine: I could not act it to myself that I should begin to defy my father openly and succeed. And I never would risk failure.”

The last sentence fell with abrupt emphasis, like a knife set down on a board. She paused, and Deronda, listening, felt his own emotions re-arranging. The first repulsion at her frank coldness, the first indignation at what jarred his deepest instincts—these began to subside. In their place came his accustomed effort toward just allowance, and with it an unwilling admiration for a nature violently alive, whose errors lay along high pathways rather than low. Yet he could not be dispassionate. He trembled at what she might say next, feared the coercion that seemed to drive her to lay bare her mind, and still felt that terrible fascination which makes one listen even when every word burns.

He tried again to recall her to concrete things, as if particulars could be a rope thrown across a chasm.

“Where was my grandfather’s home?”

“Here in Genoa, where I was married; and his family had lived here generations ago. But my father had been in various countries.”

“You must surely have lived in England?”

“My mother was English—a Jewess of Portuguese descent. My father married her in England. Certain circumstances of that marriage made all the difference in my life: through that marriage my father thwarted his own plans. My mother’s sister was a singer, and afterward she married the English partner of a merchant’s house here in Genoa, and they came and lived here eleven years.”

As she spoke, the room seemed to fill with an older Genoa: merchants’ footsteps on stone, the salt-and-tar breath of the port rising into narrow streets, voices in shifting languages—Italian, English, Hebrew—each carrying its own music, each insisting on its own meanings that did not translate cleanly into the others. Deronda felt, almost physically, how a life might be torn between tongues, how memory itself can be eroded when the words that held it are forbidden or mocked.

“My mother died when I was eight years old,” she continued, “and my father allowed me to be continually with my Aunt Leonora and be taught under her eyes, as if he had not minded the danger of her encouraging my wish to be a singer, as she had been. But this was it—I saw it again and again in my father:—he did not guard against consequences, because he felt sure he could hinder them if he liked. Before my aunt left Genoa, I had had enough teaching to bring out the born singer and actress within me: my father did not know everything that was done; but he knew that I was taught music and singing—he knew my inclination. That was nothing to him: he meant that I should obey his will.”

Her voice had the old heat again.

“And he was resolved that I should marry my cousin Ephraim, the only one left of my father’s family that he knew. I wanted not to marry. I thought of all plans to resist it, but at last I found that I could rule my cousin, and I consented. My father died three weeks after we were married, and then I had my way!”

She uttered those words with a brief exultation, like someone remembering the first mouthful of forbidden fruit. Then her face changed; bitterness tightened her mouth.

“It has not lasted, though. My father is getting his way now.”

She looked at Deronda with renewed contemplation, as if he were both evidence and consequence.

“You are like him—but milder—there is something of your own father in you; and he made it the labor of his life to devote himself to me: wound up his money-changing and banking, and lived to wait upon me—he went against his conscience for me. As I loved the life of my art, so he loved me. Let me look at your hand again: the hand with the ring on. It was your father’s ring.”

Deronda drew his chair nearer and gave her his hand. Her own hand—much smaller—was of the same type: the same long, firm fingers, the same shape as if the family had carried its identity not only in faces and names but in the tools by which they might have worked, blessed, broken bread. Her fingers closed around his with a grip that felt less like a mother’s possession than like a musician’s recognition of an instrument.

As he felt her hand holding his, and saw the face that bore a likeness to his own—aged not by time’s gentle wearing but by intensity—something in him that was deeper than indignation asserted itself: that reverential tenderness which made him long to heal even what he could not approve. In his most fervent tone he said,

“Mother! take us all into your heart—the living and the dead. Forgive everything that hurts you in the past. Take my affection.”

She looked at him admiringly rather than lovingly; then she kissed him on the brow. The kiss was light, like a sign made for meaning, not for warmth.

“I reject nothing,” she said sadly, “but I have nothing to give.”

She released his hand and sank back on her cushions. Deronda turned pale with that pain which is less emotion than sensation—the sharp ache of tenderness repulsed, as if one had offered bread and found the other could not taste it.
