CHAPTER XIX. “I pity the man who can travel from Dan to Beersheba, and say, ‘’Tis all barren’: and so it is: and so is all the world to him who will not cultivate the fruits it offers.”—STERNE: _Sentimental Journey_.

To call Deronda romantic would be a misjudgment of his nature. He was not given to flights of fancy or theatrical emotion; yet beneath his quiet demeanor, beneath the stillness that often masked his thoughts, there burned a deep sensitivity—a capacity to find wonder in the ordinary, beauty in the fleeting moment. Poetry, for him, was not confined to verse or legend; it lived in the ripple of water on a riverbank, in the glance of a stranger’s eyes, in the quiet ache of recognition. And romance? It wasn’t something he sought—it found him, unbidden, in the turning of a leaf, the fall of light, the sudden clarity of a soul revealed.

He sat awake long into the night, replaying the moments since he had first seen Mirah by the river’s edge. The memory was alive, vivid as if it were unfolding again—the way she stood, trembling but resolute, her voice like a thread of music carried on the wind. He tried to read, hoping the words might dull the intensity of what he felt, but the printed page blurred before him. Instead, he saw her clearly—her face, her hands, the look in her eyes when she spoke of her mother and brother. And beyond the facts, he saw possibilities: hopes that stirred like embers, fears that flickered into shadowed visions.

Mirah’s search for her family stirred something deep within him—something personal, almost painful. He too had longed to know his mother, though that longing had always been laced with dread. What if she were cruel? What if she were lost to him forever? And now, seeing Mirah’s yearning, he could not help but imagine the same tragedy for her. She said her mother and brother were good—kind, loving people—but how could she know? Ten years had passed since she last saw them. Time changes everything. The woman she remembered might no longer exist. Or worse—she might have become someone Mirah could never recognize, someone whose presence would wound rather than heal.

Deronda had never truly known Jews—not as people, not as individuals. His knowledge of them came through stereotypes, through stories told in whispers or sneers. He had assumed they were either assimilated, eager to shed their past, or hidden in poverty, clinging to ancient customs. He had no sympathy for prejudice, and yet—he had never cared deeply enough to learn otherwise. Now, however, Mirah’s innocence made him question everything. Her grace, her purity, the gentle strength in her voice—they seemed to belong to a world apart, untouched by the harshness he feared might await her.

And so he imagined what he might find. A narrow street, dimly lit. A door opening onto a cramped room where a woman, sharp-eyed and weary, bartered away the last remnants of a daughter’s dignity. Or perhaps a more polished quarter, where a young man, dressed in borrowed elegance, spoke with easy familiarity, offering favors to gentlemen while hiding a life built on secrets and shame. These images were not born of malice, but of fear—fear that Mirah’s reunion might bring not comfort, but sorrow.

He did not wish to think this way. He despised the kind of prejudice that reduced entire peoples to caricatures. Yet here he was, caught in the web of assumptions, shaped by history and hearsay. But he refused to act without knowing. He would wait—wait to hear from Mirah herself, perhaps through Mrs. Meyrick, to gather clues from her speech, her manner, her memories. Only then could he judge.

Yet even as he hesitated, he knew what must be done. Mirah needed shelter, protection—someone to stand beside her, not just in thought, but in action. He felt a chivalric duty rise within him, stronger than reason, fiercer than caution. He wanted to shield her, to make sure no one could ever claim she owed him anything. Not out of pride, but because he believed she deserved freedom—freedom to choose her own path, to live without being bound by the kindness of others.

And so he wrestled with another truth: secrecy. He hated it. Not only because it bred lies, but because it twisted relationships, turned love into obligation, trust into suspicion. He had vowed to himself that truth should never be used as a weapon—to never hide the facts of his life in a way that would shame another. He had learned, painfully, that some truths are not ours to control. They come from the past, from forces beyond our will. And to conceal them was to betray the very soul of honesty.

At one point, he decided he would tell Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger everything at breakfast the next morning. Let them know the story, the whole of it. But then he paused. What if something new emerged? What if Mirah revealed more about her past, or if Mrs. Meyrick offered insight he hadn’t anticipated? Better to wait. To listen. To see.

And so, finally, he closed his eyes. The night was still heavy with thought, but the decision was clear: he would wait. He would go to Mrs. Meyrick, speak with Mirah, learn what he could—and then act. Not out of impulse, but from understanding. For Mirah, and for himself, the truth mattered. And he would not let it be buried.
