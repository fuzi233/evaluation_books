From our vantage point just below the crest of the Sacred Mountain, the city sprawled beneath us like a forgotten map etched in stone and shadow. The harbor shimmered at its edge, the great estuary glinting under the dark sky. Beyond, the hills exhaled smoke and steam as they always did—ancient breaths from the earth’s deep lungs. At the heart of the city stood the massive stone circle dedicated to our Lord the Sun, stark and silent. Closer still rose the royal pyramid, its golden sides catching fire from the setting sun. And there, in the square before it, stood the granite throne—small now from distance, no larger than a molehill—but within that hollow, for nine long years, my love had lain asleep.

Old Zaemon followed my gaze, his face grave. “Yes,” he said softly, “I know where your thoughts lie. Deucalion, when he first landed from Yucatan, was a man of iron will. The king we’ve chosen—though the best among us—is not so strong.”

“I can be stronger,” I replied. “Give me Nais, alive and warm, and I’ll be more than any cold soldier or distant ruler could ever be.”

He nodded slowly. “I have already sworn it. You shall have her. But it will cost you—your new kingdom, perhaps, and possibly Phorenice and her followers. Though I doubt she’ll fall so easily. She is too cunning. Once we begin, she may sense the hand behind the storm—and flee before harm reaches her. The High Gods have seen fit to grant her some knowledge of how these earth tremors may be stirred.”

“I’ve seen her play with them,” I said. “But tell me your plan.”

“It will unfold in time. For now, sleep. Tonight, you must go into the city, take your woman—my daughter—and bring her back here. You’ll need every ounce of strength you have.” He stepped back, studying me. “You’re no longer the clean-cut Deucalion I knew. Your beard is wild, your skin burned, your body stripped bare and grimy. You look like some savage cast ashore from Europe. That might serve you well tonight—when the city shakes and screams. No nobleman will mistake you for a prince. They may think you a beast, and leave you be.”

“I pity the fool who tries to claim you as servant,” I said.

Zaemon laughed—a rare sound, sharp and sudden. Then silence fell between us again, heavier than before.

“Go rest,” he said. “I must enter the Ark of Mysteries and prepare with another of the Three. What we are about to do is no small matter. We stir powers older than mountains. There is danger for us all. If we do not meet again until we stand before the stars to answer the Gods, then farewell, Deucalion.”

That night, I slept like a warrior—deep, unbroken, without thought. Only the weak let fear disturb their rest. When darkness settled fully, I woke and called those who would tend the rope. The Moon was absent this cycle, and the world wore a cloak of absolute blackness. As I descended, I vanished from sight.

I reached the shattered rocks at the cliff’s base and moved with caution. Phorenice’s soldiers huddled around their fires, restless, uneasy—as if sensing the storm approaching. I did not wish to awaken them further. So I slipped through the shadows, swift and silent, until I came to the hidden passage I once used to slip beneath the city walls.

The lamp waited, burning low. I moved quickly through the winding tunnels. The air tasted thicker, fouler than before—charged with vapors. I sensed Zaemon had already begun to stir the deep forces beneath the land. Yet again, I struggled to find an exit. Not because guards blocked my way, but because the city’s masonry had changed. In ancient times, the Priests’ Clan oversaw every design, ensuring no structure disrupted their secret paths. Now, the priests were powerless, and architects built without care, sealing off old passages by accident.

So I emerged where I could, not where I wished. I found myself on the far side of the city, near where the outer wall met the harbor. I slipped out unseen, closed the trace behind me, and felt the ground beneath me shift—like a ship’s deck in calm seas. No visible movement yet, but the earth no longer felt solid. It hummed with tension, waiting to break.

Would I be too late? Would she be uncovered, exposed to the crowd when the tremors broke loose? I saw in my mind their crude hands reaching for her, helpless and still. My chest tightened. I ran—not with reason, but with rage and hope—down the narrow, twisting streets, driven by one thought: reach the square before the world fell apart.

At first, only beggars shouted, “Thief! Stop him!” But none pursued. My speed carried me beyond them. Soon, the earth began to groan. Buildings creaked, dust rained down. People fled their homes, screaming, clutching children. But none paid attention to the ragged, naked figure rushing past, kicking up mud.

And then—there—I reached the great square before the pyramid. I arrived just in time.

It was chaos. Dust choked the air. Shadows swarmed. The ground rippled like water, growing more violent with each passing moment. Houses collapsed into silence, swallowed by the dark. The pyramid stood firm—built to endure. Its stones interlocked like teeth, held together by metal bands. It was said half its foundation could be removed and it would still stand.

Torches flickered at its doors. Frightened souls darted out, vanishing into the gloom. But I cared nothing for the pyramid. I didn’t even care if Phorenice was trapped inside. I crouched beside the granite throne, feeling its stones grind against each other like broken bones.

In that darkness, vision was impossible. Yet somehow—perhaps by grace—I could see. I watched as the capstone of the throne shifted slightly with each tremor. A tiny jerk. Then another. Always in the same direction.

Zaemon’s work had purpose. Even in my blind haste, I now understood. I climbed the steps on the side where the stone moved, clinging tight, heart pounding.

The shaking grew worse. The crash of collapsing walls echoed louder. Dust filled the air so thickly I coughed and choked. The night turned blacker as stars vanished beneath the storm. I clung to the throne’s edge, barely holding my place.

Still, the capstone held Nais fast. I wanted to scream, to tear the stone apart with my bare hands. But I could not. The people poured into the square, terrified, mad with fear. Some cried, “Phorenice, Goddess, save us!” When she did not answer, they fell into frantic prayer, reciting childhood chants to gods they’d long abandoned. A bitter irony.

Then, thunder roared above. Lightning split the sky, illuminating the scene.

And there—she stood.

Phorenice.

Safe. Unmoved. Standing on a flagstone in the middle of the square, swaying with the tremors like a sailor on a rolling sea. Her face was calm, untouched by time. Her red hair curled about her neck, dust brushed away with delicate fingers. No fear. Only weariness, disdain—perhaps amusement.

She looked upon the ruin and smiled.

My blood boiled. I would have killed her then, if I could have reached her. But she remained, serene, while beside her, crumpled on the stone, lay Ylga—her fan-girl once more—pale and sick. I remembered Ylga was Nais’s twin. I owed her kindness. But I had no time.

The capstone shifted again. Further. With each shock, it slid from its bed. I watched, breathless. My love was coming closer.

A group of panicked citizens surged toward the throne, thinking it safe. They scrambled up the steps, crawling on hands and knees. I drove them back—hitting, shouting, tearing them away. I didn’t care what they thought. I only wanted them gone.

They called me a demon of the night. I didn’t argue.

The stone moved farther. Still too slow. Rage took over. I thrust against it with shoulder and head, clawed at it with hands till muscles bulged. The Gods must have laughed at my futility. But it was Their will—slow, sure, unstoppable.

Then—finally—the capstone reached balance. The next tremor threw it forward with a thunderous crash.

It fell.

And I froze.

All the effort, the planning, the prayers—it had come to pass. Yet my soul sank. Would Nais be gone? Had Phorenice taken her? Or left her dead as a cruel joke?

I couldn’t know. I dared not guess.

For the first time in my life, I was undone by love.

But the tremors eased. Zaemon had done his work. I gathered courage. I climbed onto the flat stone.

Darkness pressed in. Dust choked the air. But lightning might strike again. If I were seen—shaggy, wild, changed—Phorenice would recognize me.

The idea of fighting made me sick. I just wanted to take her and leave.

With trembling hands, I found the metal clamps—still locked as I had left them. I slipped my fingers between them, touched the curve of her body beneath. Joy surged through me. Alive or dead, she was mine. I could have screamed.

But the earth still shook. I clung with one hand, unhooked the clamps with the other. Finally, I lifted the top half clear. Left the lower ones. I pulled her soft form onto the stone, kissed her face a hundred times—blindly, desperately.

Some madness seized me. I thought my kisses alone might wake her. But she was cold. Lifeless. Slowly, reason returned.

This was no place for either of us. Daylight would come. The city would recover. Someone would see her. Recognize her. And then—me. Under the beard, the dirt, the savage clothes—they’d know.

I couldn’t stay.

I slung her over my shoulder, free arm ready. I stumbled through the ruins, blinded by dust, feet slipping on cracked stone. I fell many times—but she never bruised.

I reached the edge of the square, found a known street. The worst of the shaking passed. No buildings fell. But rubble littered the road. Sweat poured from me as I pushed forward.

No gate. No wall. The city’s defenses had been torn apart. The Gods had plowed through it. Atlantis stood open, defenseless.

I felt sorrow—not for the pyramid, nor the palaces—but for the ramparts. The soldier in me grieved. This mighty city, once my only master, was humbled.

But it was fleeting. I had another love now—one that wrapped around me tighter than any duty. Dawn approached. Danger loomed.

Raiders would come. Soldiers would stop anyone fleeing with loot. I wouldn’t fight them. I ran—swiftly, silently—through back alleys, calling to strangers, “She has the plague! Shelter us!” They opened their doors. I kept moving.

Finally, I reached the cliff. The rope dangled. I hoisted Nais up. Then I stepped into the loop and followed.

Now came the true trial.

We laid her in a bed in one of the mountain houses. And there, for the first time in nine years, I saw her clearly.

Her beauty was faded, drawn thin. Her eyes were closed, but the lids were so translucent, I could see the brown of her pupils beneath. Her hair had grown wild, thick, long—spreading like a cushion around her head.

No breath. No pulse. Yet she did not look dead. The woman I buried was full-figured, strong. This one—half her weight. I could carry her easily now.

Her fingers—nails twisted into spirals, skin waxen, bones visible beneath. Lips pale as snow, once crimson. Shoulders hollow, dust collecting in the crevices.

I knelt beside her. Reverently, I prepared the bath. I heated water, mixed it with the sacred essences the Priests preserved—life extracts from beasts, stored for desperate times. I placed her chin-deep in the warm liquid, kept the temperature steady, constant—like blood.

And I sat beside her, watching.

Waiting.

For life to return.
