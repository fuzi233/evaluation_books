The princess's command to commission a full-length portrait of herself arrived like a gentle breeze on a summer's day, rustling the leaves of Gerard's artistic ambitions. His friend, Pietro Vanucci, was recommended to take on the task, but the princess declined, her wit sharp as a razor's edge. "Let's not hasten to affix a stain to the good name of Gerardo until his work discontents me," she said, a sly smile playing on her lips. Gerard, aware of his skill with lines and shapes, but less confident in his ability to capture the subtleties of color, humbly requested that the princess pose for him as a Roman statue. He demonstrated his technique, mimicking the smooth curves of marble on paper, and for a moment, she consented.

However, upon hearing his explanation that she would need to don the tunic, toga, and sandals of the ancients, her expression turned skeptical. "I'd rather be presented in my everyday smock," she said with unflinching candor. Gerard, undeterred, waxed poetic about the elegance of the ancient attire, its simplicity and nobility a perfect match for her classical features and lithe form. He pleaded for time to think on the matter, and the princess agreed to make a decision by Saturday, granting him a brief reprieve.

During this respite, Gerard devoted himself to crafting the tunic and toga, carefully testing them on the princess in her chambers to gauge whether they would complement her beauty without appearing anachronistic. As he hurried to the princess's chambers on the designated day, his gaze landed on a nearby shop window, where a book caught his attention. A copy of Lactantius lay open, its pages inviting him to peruse its contents. Gerard's trained eye scrutinized the text, his mind racing with the implications. That was when he noticed something peculiar – the text was not handwritten, but printed.

A jolt ran through his body like a spark of electricity. The printing press, he thought, was a mythical creature, a fantastical beast conjured from the depths of imagination. And yet, the title page revealed the truth: Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz, Alumnorum Joannis Fust, Impressum Subiacis, A.D. 1465.
