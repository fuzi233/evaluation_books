Colonel Esmond—whose name, like a well-worn family seal, had been pressed into this tale so often already—had carried into his long American leisure a habit of setting things down. In Virginia, where the summers were loud with cicadas and the nights rang with frogs and distant drums from quarters, he had written his memoirs in a firm, old-fashioned hand, as if to keep the past from washing away like a footprint in tide-sand. In those pages Madame de Bernstein—then Beatrice Esmond—moved with a brightness that seemed, even on paper, to throw candle-glare on the surrounding sentences. George Warrington had read that manuscript many times, and so came to know his aunt twice over: first as the young, handsome, wilful woman of fifty years ago; then as the lady who now sat near him in rouge and jewels, with age at her temples and malice and wit still quick as a knife.

There is a particular cruelty in time: it does not merely dim a face; it changes the sound a room makes around that face. Where once there might have been music and the soft rush of admirers’ feet, now there were the tired creak of chair-legs, the click of a cane, the dry cough of servants keeping wakefulness by habit. George, watching his aunt come in late from her rout, thought—against his will—of fireworks he had seen as a boy at Williamsburg: the rockets’ brief splendor, and afterward the blackened frame, the empty sockets, the wheel that had been a sun and now was only a skeleton.

He had kept to himself how closely he knew her early history. It did not seem to him a kindness to tell an old woman—however formidable—that her youth had been long since rehearsed and bound between covers in his grandfather’s study.

When their supper was done, Madame Bernstein tapped her cane toward her escritoire, and ordered her attendant to bring the letter lying beneath the inkstand. George knew the superscription at once. It was the letter he had carried from Virginia.

“It would appear by this,” said the Baroness, fixing him with a stare that seemed to search for lies among his eyelashes, “that since your return you and my sister have had differences.”

“Indeed? I did not know Madam Esmond had chosen to name them,” George answered.

She set a great pair of glasses upon her nose—an absurd harness for eyes that, in their day, had made men reckless—and after a glance, passed the paper to him. He read.

“RICHMOND, VIRGINIA, December 26th, 1756.

“HONOURED MADAM! AND SISTER!—I have received, and thankfully acknowledge, your ladyship’s favour….”

It was his mother’s voice upon the page: pious, exact, tender where it chose, and firm as a locked pantry where it must. She spoke of the season of goodwill, and of sorrow still decreed. She told how her eldest son, returned “miraculously out of the Valley of the Shadow of Death,” would bring her answer. She mourned that Harry, in England, had been presented “under false colours,” as heir, while George yet lived, though “dead to us” in captivity. She praised Harry’s tenderness—his heart, she insisted, the softest alive—and assured her sister that in the joy of George’s return, Harry would accept his altered place without a pang, obedient, generous, giving up the inheritance that had seemed his “but for the accident of birth.”

Then came money—always money beneath the prayers, as sure as salt beneath sweetness in a sauce. She reminded her sister how an opportunity had once arisen to establish Harry in Virginia with “a noble estate and negroes,” had her wishes been complied with and his portion invested, but “the forms of the law” and “my elder son’s scruples” had prevailed. She sent a sum now—carefully entrusted—meant to cover Harry’s expenses in fine company, for surely a grandson of the Marquis of Esmond “may claim equality with any other nobleman in Great Britain.” And she prayed the Baroness to add what she would.

George, unable to help himself, broke in, his tone controlled but edged. “It took him out of prison yesterday, madam. I think that was the best use to which we could put it.”

Madame Bernstein lifted her brows as if he had spoken heresy over the pudding. “Nay, sir, I don’t know any such thing! Why not keep it to buy him a pair of colours—or to help toward another estate and some negroes, if he has a fancy for home? Besides, I had a fancy to pay that debt myself.”

“I hope you will let his brother do that. I ask leave to be my brother’s banker in this matter—and consider I have borrowed so much from my mother, to be paid back to my dear Harry.”

“Do you say so? Give me a glass of wine!” she cried. “You are an extravagant fellow. Read on, and you will see your mother thinks so. I drink your health, nephew George. ’Tis good Burgundy. Your grandfather never loved Burgundy. He loved claret—the little he drank.”

George read on. His mother’s letter, like one of her own Virginia broths, had been skimmed and strained until only her chosen flavors remained. She professed fear of George’s prudence. She spoke of humility, of fond blessings, of every act dictated by love for her “dearest boys’ welfare.” Yet between the lines, George saw the scratches and blots—literal upon the page and figurative in the heart that wrote it: the penknife mark, the heavy stain as of water fallen, as though the ink had been forced to run by a tear or an angry hand.

“There is a scratch with a penknife, and a great blot upon the letter there, as if water had fallen on it,” his aunt remarked, not unkindly. “Your mother writes well, George. I suppose you and she had a difference?”

“Yes, ma’am—many,” George said quietly.

“The last was about money,” he continued, because the truth had a pressure of its own and would not be kept down. “About the ransom I promised the old lieutenant at the fort—Museau—who aided my escape.”

He told her what had happened: how, six weeks after his return, the Indian woman—Museau’s poor mistress—had found her way through the woods to Richmond, guided by the same perilous track he had taken, bearing his grandfather’s watch and a line scrawled from a prison-bed. A new commandant had arrived at Duquesne; Museau had been accused—of what peculation the messenger could hardly make clear—put under arrest, attempted to flee, and been shot on the rampart. With his last breath he begged George to send “ce que je scavais” to a notary at Havre de Grace, to be transmitted to his kin at Caen.

Silverheels, the hunter, had helped the woman; without him, she might have been scalped and left to the crows. But in Richmond, she met not gratitude but suspicion—suspicion that had its own harsh music: whispers in doorways, laughter from servants, pulpit-thunder from a sanctimonious fellow who used a sermon like a cudgel. She drank when she could. George ordered her food and shelter, and she became a jest among their negroes and a subject for the old town’s sharp tongues. At last he obtained a pass from the Governor—“a man of sense,” he said—to send her back to her people. Her grief at parting from him only confirmed those who wished to be scandalised. He had to cane one man; he believed another preached against him. And at home, Madam Esmond and he had come to a violent quarrel because he insisted on paying Museau’s heirs the money he had promised.

“You see,” George concluded, “that scandal flourishes at the borders of the wilderness, and in the New World as well as the Old.”

“I have suffered from it myself, my dear,” Madame Bernstein said, demure as a saint in a comedy. “Fill thy glass, child! A little tass of cherry-brandy! ’Twill do thee all the good in the world.”

He tasted it—hot and perfumed, with that winter-stored sweetness of cherries steeped until they surrender their color—and the room seemed for a moment to change its sound: the crackle of the fire, the faint far-off rumble of carriages, the rustle of silk as the Baroness adjusted herself. London at night had its own ecology of noise—less honest than Virginia’s, more mechanical, more restless—wheel on cobble, chairmen calling, a drunken catch from a side-street, all of it like commerce turned into weather. In such a city, even art was hawked and shouted, and song itself could feel like a thing for sale.

His mother’s letter went on to speak of Harry’s marriage. Madam Esmond declared herself shocked at the match with Lady Maria Esmond, “out of the question” because of her age—almost the same as Rachel’s—and because Castlewood’s daughters had been left with the smallest portions. Harry, she said, was so obedient that a desire from her would suffice to end the engagement. She reminded her sister—unnecessarily—that she herself had never contemplated a second union, whatever foolish people had supposed; she knew what was due to her children. George, she wrote, was amply provided for as heir; her task now was to save for the younger brother, and to have him return without delay to his “fond and loving mother,” since she did not love to live alone.

She praised Harry’s refinement gained in Europe, spoke with gratitude of his time among his English cousins, and ended with gratitude to her sister for kindness, with a pious flourish over their father—“one of the best of men”—and a postscript: she had communicated with Lady Maria, and there was no need to tell her or Harry that his mother or his aunt hoped to increase his small fortune. “The match is altogether unsuitable.”

George laid the paper down. “As far as regards myself, madam, my mother’s letter conveys no news. I always knew Harry was the favourite son—he deserves it. He has a hundred good qualities I have not. He has better looks—”

“Nay, that is not your fault,” the Baroness said, looking him over with a sly approval. “And but that he is fair and you are brown, one might almost pass for the other.”

George bowed; a faint color came to his cheek. “His disposition is bright, and mine is dark. Harry is cheerful, and I am otherwise. He knows how to make himself beloved by every one, and it has been my lot to find but few friends.”

“My sister and you have pretty little quarrels. There were such in old days in our family,” Madame Bernstein said. “And if Madam Esmond takes after our mother—”

“My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth,” George said quickly.

“Eh! That is a common character for people when they are dead!” cried the Baroness. “Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like—at least your grandfather thought so. But angels are sometimes not very commodes à vivre. They are too good to live with us sinners; the air down below doesn’t agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she never could forgive me for being otherwise. Ah, mon Dieu, how she used to oppress me with those angelical airs!”

George’s eyes dropped. He thought of his own youth, of prayers measured out like rations, of moral precepts spoken in a tone that allowed no answering. It was not that he wished to blacken his mother; rather, he could not bear to have her anatomised by this glittering cynic, who seemed, for all her worldliness, to read him as easily as if he were print.

“I comprehend you, sir, though you hold your tongue,” the Baroness went on. “A sermon in the morning, a sermon at night, and two or three of a Sunday—that is what people call being good. Every pleasure cried fie upon; all us worldly people excommunicated; a ball an abomination of desolation; a play forbidden; a game of cards perdition. Mon Dieu, what a life!”

“We played at cards every night, if we were so inclined,” George said, allowing himself a small smile; “and my grandfather loved Shakespeare so much, my mother had not a word to say against her father’s favourite.”

“I remember. He could say whole pages by heart—though for my part I like Mr. Congreve better. And then there was that dreadful, dreary Milton, whom he and Mr. Addison pretended to admire!” Madame Bernstein tapped her fan as if beating time to an unwanted hymn. “If your ladyship does not like Shakespeare, you will not quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him too.”

“And indeed,” George added, attempting a defence that sounded, even to himself, like dutiful recitation, “you don’t do her justice. Wherever there are poor she relieves them; wherever there are sick she—”

“She doses them with horrible purges and boluses!” the Baroness broke in. “Of course, just as my mother did!”

“She does her best,” George said, steadying his voice. “She acts for the best and performs her duty as far as she knows it.”

“I don’t blame you for doing yours and keeping your own counsel about Madam Esmond,” the old lady returned. “But at least there is one point upon which we all three agree—that this absurd marriage must be prevented. Do you know how old the woman is? I can tell you—though she has torn the first leaf out of the family Bible at Castlewood.”

George said his mother had not forgotten her cousin’s age and was shocked at the disparity. And beyond age there was the matter of place: a London-bred lady, used to luxury and routs, might find little comfort in the plain routine of a Virginian plantation, where the day began with horns and hammering, where even the air had a different weight and smell—woodsmoke, damp earth, boiled hominy, curing meat. Besides, the house was not Harry’s; he was welcome there—more welcome, perhaps, than George, though George was the natural heir. But, George admitted with a blush and a stumble of speech, he doubted how Harry’s wife would like their colony.

The Baroness laughed—shrill, pleased, a laugh that made the glasses on the sideboard tremble. “He, he! Nephew Warrington! Speak your mind out. I shall tell no tales to your mother—though it is no news to me she has a high temper and loves her own way. Harry held his tongue; but it needed no conjurer to see who was mistress at home and what sort of a life my sister led you. I love my niece, my Lady Molly, so well I could wish her two or three years of Virginia with your mother reigning over her. You may well look alarmed, sir! Harry has said quite enough to show me who governs the family.”

“I may say as much as this,” George replied, forcing a smile, “that I don’t envy any woman coming into our house against my mother’s will. And my poor brother knows it perfectly.”

“What? You two have talked it over? No doubt you have. And the foolish child considers himself bound in honour—of course he does, the gaby!”

George told her Harry said Lady Maria had behaved nobly: when he was sent to prison she brought him her trinkets and jewels and every guinea she had. It touched him; he felt bound more than ever. Yet George owned that his brother seemed bound by honour rather than by love—at least in his present feeling.

“My good creature,” cried Madame Bernstein, leaning forward as if to press the point physically into him, “don’t you see Maria brings a few twopenny trinkets and a half-dozen guineas to Mr. Esmond—the heir of the great estate in Virginia—not to the second son who is a beggar and has just squandered away every shilling? I swear to you, on my credit as a gentlewoman, knowing Harry’s obstinacy and the misery he had in store, I tried to bribe Maria to give up her engagement and only failed because I could not bribe high enough! When he was in prison I sent my lawyer with orders to pay his debts immediately if he would part from her—but Maria had been beforehand with us, and Mr. Harry chose not to go back from his stupid word. Let me tell you what has passed in the last month!”

And she told it—what the reader already knows, though spoken now in that bold, unblushing idiom of her world, where even delicate ladies named greed and lust as if they were dishes on a bill of fare. Her phrases came out sharp, seasoned with contempt; she served up motives as if carving a fowl, laying bare joints and sinews that polite speech pretends are not there. She talked long, and cleverly, and with the satisfaction of one who believes herself the only honest tongue in a room of liars.

At last she surrendered herself to Mrs. Brett to be undressed, and announced, with the briskness of one who changes her mind as she changes gloves, that she had altered her opinion of her eldest nephew. Mr. George, she said, was handsome; certainly wittier than poor Harry (whom Heaven, it must be confessed, had not furnished with a great supply of brains); and he had the bel air—a melancholy, noble, distinguished je ne sais quoi—which reminded her of the Colonel. Had she ever told Brett about the Colonel? Scores of times, no doubt—and now she told her again, as if repeating a recipe whose taste she could not forget though the ingredients had long since soured.

George, walking home late through the city, was less pleased with her than she with him. The night had thinned; fashion’s last guests issued from bright doorways into cold air; chairmen yawned over expiring torches; somewhere a watchman’s call threaded through the street’s rumble like a mournful refrain. He felt as if the Baroness had peeled back a painted panel and shown him not merely her own face but the structure beneath their entire notion of nobility: family after family scrambling for small advantages, trampling through stale dissipations and faded pleasures, turning “ancient names” into counters in a game.

Are these the inheritors of noble blood? he asked himself. In the old romances, when a knight found a friend in want, did he turn away? When a nobleman received a young kinsman, did he cheat him at dice, trick him in horseflesh, buy and sell him with smiles? Had his poor Harry been inveigled into this engagement by a worldly woman’s skill—and would she, finding him poor, desert him? Would it not be better to pack trunks at once and take a cabin in the next ship home, to the quieter soundscape of fields and rivers, where the loudest thing at dawn might be geese, not coaches?

His own door answered these thoughts with domestic plainness. Gumbo came up yawning with a candle, the small flame making the hallway smell of tallow and smoke. In the sitting-room Harry lay asleep before the extinguished fire, his pipe emptied, its grey ashes spilled on the table like sifted flour. The city’s roar was muffled here; only the faint crack of settling wood and the far-off wheels remained, like surf heard indoors.

Harry started up at the candle’s movement. For a moment his eyes were dulled by sleep; then they brightened with honest pleasure at sight of George. He laughed like a boy and put his arm round his brother.

“There he is in flesh and blood, thank God!” he cried. “I was dreaming of thee but now, George, and that Ward was hearing us our lesson! Dost thou remember the ruler, Georgy? Why, bless my soul, ’tis three o’clock! Where have you been a-gadding, Mr. George? Hast thou supped? I supped at White’s, but I’m hungry again.”

His words tumbled out with that cheerful appetite for life that belonged to him: the hunger that was not only for food, but for company, for warmth, for reassurance.

“I did not play, sir—no, no; no more of that for younger brothers!” he went on quickly, as if reporting a victory. “And my Lord March paid me fifty he lost to me. I bet against his horse and on the Duke of Hamilton’s! They both rode the match at Newmarket this morning, and he lost because he was under weight. And he paid me, and he was as sulky as a bear. Let us have one pipe, Georgy—just one.”

And so, in a room that smelled of cold hearth and stale tobacco, with London’s restless noises held at bay by a closed door, the brothers shared their smoke like a small rite—one more thread binding them back to childhood and to home. Afterward they went to bed; and it is a good and pleasant thing, indeed, to see brethren who love one another.
