Over at Fairoaks, everyone was starting to side-eye the empty armchair Major Pendennis usually occupied at dinner. Dr. Portman and his wife popped by Helen’s lodge on their drive home to Clavering, bearing a quick scribbled note from the Major: he’d be lingering in Chatteris one more day, he claimed, to chat with the Tathams, the family solicitors, that very afternoon. Not a single word about the absolute knock-down, drag-out fight he’d already lost to Captain Costigan that very morning.

Pen, for his part, had zero urge to wander into Chatteris while his uncle was still there. The very thought of the Major lurking smugly on the Dean’s neatly mowed lawn, spying on him while he made moon eyes at Miss Costigan in her drawing room, would’ve turned a perfectly pleasant stroll (a treat he barely ever got to enjoy) sour in two seconds flat. His silly little boyish crush was far too shy to show off in public anyway, beyond the frankly overzealous ogling he did every time Emily so much as handed him a cup of tea. All giddy new love talk sounds like absolute gibberish to anyone not currently smitten, after all: it’s a vocabulary book with no key, a lamp with no flame. Go dig up that box of old love letters you hid in the attic from when you were 17, if you don’t believe me. You’ll read those gushing lines and wonder what sort of hallucination made you think that level of puling nonsense was romantic. We’re not even going to bother printing the terrible, over-the-top flowery verses and letters Pen churned out at this point in his life, purely to save him from lifelong public embarrassment. They’re far too spooney for even the softest-hearted young lady to read in cold blood. We’ve locked them up tight, tied with Emily’s sewing cotton and sealed with Captain Costigan’s giant dented silver flask cork, and no one’s getting the key.

The Major, meanwhile, had stormed out of his interview with Costigan in such a towering rage that even his own shadow would’ve crossed the street to avoid him. “That impudent bog-trotting scoundrel,” he fumed to himself, “dared threaten me? Dared say he’d let his lot of Costigans marry into the Pendennis family, and then challenge me to a duel? If he can dig up even one man who counts as a gentleman to carry that challenge, I’m half tempted to say yes—pshaw, though! Can you imagine the county gossip? Me, a respectable retired officer with polished boots and lavender cologne, dueling a tipsy hack actor’s dad over a row with a barn theatre actress?”

When Dr. Portman asked him how the fight against the dragon Costigan had gone, the Major didn’t have the heart to tell the clergyman about the drunken Irishman’s insults, just said the whole mess was ugly, far from over, and swore both the Doctor and his wife to not breathe a word of the business to Helen at Fairoaks. He sent off that tame, vague note we already mentioned, then trundled back to his hotel to take his wrath out on his valet Morgan, who later gossiped to Foker’s footman over their dinner in the servants’ hall that the Major was “dammin and cussin upstairs and downstairs like a sailor on shore leave.”

That gossip made its way straight to Foker, who was just finishing his breakfast (it was two o’clock in the afternoon, as per usual for him) and decided he was dying to know how the Major’s interview with Costigan had turned out. He traipsed down the hall in his fancy brocade dressing gown and the hand-embroidered fez his doting mother had stitched for him, and knocked on the Major’s door.
The Major was deep in consultation with old Mr. Tatham, the white-haired family solicitor who’d managed his late brother’s affairs, sorting out a new lease for Helen’s Fairoaks property. Foker, embarrassed to intrude, started to back off, but the Major waved him in. Foker doffed his fancy fez, bowed, spread his crimson dressing gown skirts over the armchair he sank into, and beamed at the two men so warmly he could’ve sweet-talked a grumpy inquisitor into a good mood.
After Foker made them all laugh about how nice his robe was, the Major spilled the whole messy story. “That Irishman, Emily’s father? Refuses to believe my nephew’s broke, called us both liars, even hinted I was a coward before he kicked me out. When you knocked, I half thought you were his second come to deliver the duel note.”

Mr. Tatham, a stern Quaker-adjacent type who never set foot inside a theatre, was horrified. He dropped a very useful little fun fact: “I know that Costigan personally. He’s a total deadbeat, wine soaked, never pays his bills. We’ve got an outstanding writ out against him and one of his actor pals, Garbetts the tragedian, for a bill they owed our local respectable Quaker grocer Mr. Skinner. Costigan even cried in the man’s shop to beg for extra time, but neither of them are worth the trouble of sending the bailiffs after.”

Right on cue, there was another knock at the door. In lumbered a burly shabby-looking man in a braided frock coat, holding a letter with a huge blotched red wax seal. “Can I speak to Major Pendennis in private? I’m bearing a message from my good friend Captain Costigan—” He stopped dead, turned paler than a ghost, locked eyes with Mr. Tatham. “Hullo, Garbetts!” Foker cackled, delighted. Garbetts didn’t say another word, gasped some unintelligible gibberish, and bolted out of the room before anyone could grab him.
All three men burst out laughing. “No duel today, Major,” Foker wheezed, already mimicking Garbetts’ horrified face. “If they tried to fight it out, Mr. Tatham here could just send the bailiffs to arrest both seconds halfway through the first shot.” The Major couldn’t have been more relieved.

Foker, being the chaotic good little rich boy who could get into any room, decided to pop over to Costigan’s lodgings later that afternoon to snoop. Costigan was already at the Magpie Hotel pub, bragging to his drinking buddies about how he was gonna blow the Major’s brains out, so only Emily was home, washing teacups while old Bows the fiddler sat across from her smoking his pipe. Foker poked his silly little head in the door, did a ridiculous Harlequin spin so fast his head looked like it was about to fall off, and made Emily laugh so hard she snort-laughed, the kind of laugh even Bows’ wittiest jokes could never drag out of her, the kind Pen’s most dramatic love speeches just confused her.
Foker spilled the whole story of Garbetts’ failed duel note delivery, told Emily straight that Pen was dead broke, not the 2,000 a year rich husband her dad had promised her. Emily’s face fell. “Oh, he’s a nice sweet boy,” she said, a little sad, “but no 2,000 a year? Sure I was an idiot for believing all my da’s silly stories. Next time I’ll pick my own husband.” The very next second, she was probably mentally picturing that handsome rich Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks she’d danced with at the last assembly.

Then Foker dropped the master bait: he’d booked a private room back at The George, they could have lobster salad and unlimited champagne for dinner, come meet the Major there, bury the hatchet. No one has ever turned down free lobster salad and bubbly, especially not Emily, so she agreed immediately, even dragging grumpy Bows along. Foker sent his servant Stoopid down to the Magpie to fish Costigan out mid-drunk ballad, and by seven o’clock that evening, the Major found himself sat at a dinner table with the fiddler, the actress Costigan had demanded he duel over, and 10 minutes later Costigan himself stumbled in, so drunk he’d already been crying over his own sad Irish song at the pub. He grabbed the Major’s hand, squeezed it so hard his knuckles went white, and called him his dearest, oldest, most beloved friend. No one mentioned the duel. Everyone ate lobster. A grand old time was had by all.

The Major rolled back to Fairoaks the next day, acting so casual no one would’ve guessed he’d spent the previous night drinking champagne with the man who’d threatened to murder him 24 hours earlier. He was extra sweet to Pen, gave him a very emphatic “God bless you” before he went to bed, almost told Helen the whole messy story then decided not to ruin her night’s sleep.
The next morning at breakfast, the post arrived. Pen grabbed a letter, blushed bright red, recognized Costigan’s scrawled handwriting immediately, and didn’t dare open it in front of everyone. The Major sent little Laura off to play (she hated him anyway, so she went without arguing), pulled Helen aside into the hall, and told her plainly: “It’s done. The affair’s over. I dictated that breakup letter myself, got Emily to add a couple lines of her own. The engagement’s off for good.”
Helen ran back into the dining room, just in time to see Pen ripping the letter open, face gone stupefied. The note said they’d just found out Pen was far too poor to support a wife, Emily was 10 years older than him, marriage was completely impossible, and he ought to stop visiting their house at once. Emily’s tiny little added note said she agreed with her father, she was grateful for all his kindness, but they shouldn’t meet until the pain wore off.
Pen flew into a wild panic. “This is a trick! That’s not her handwriting! Someone’s playing a joke on me!” He raged, yelling that he was going to ride straight to Chatteris, confront Emily, make her tell him to his face it was over. The Major just sighed, pointed out that Emily spelled “affection” with one F, that she was ten years his senior, that her father owed every shopkeeper in three towns money, and she was never going to be a suitable companion for Helen.
Pen stormed off to Chatteris anyway, banged on Costigan’s door, got all his letters returned unopened, and eventually ran into Emily on the street walking with her father. She looked right through him, didn’t even nod. Pen pined so hard he got a full fever after he went to see her perform one night where the audience was so tiny the manager gave everyone their ticket money back. Everyone begged him to travel abroad for a while to get over her, but he flat-out refused. Heartbreak hurts, but let’s be honest—plenty of people have gotten dumped before Pen Pendennis, and none of them died of it, right?
