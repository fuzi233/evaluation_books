The following morning, Mr. Moore rose before the dawn. By the time his sister had prepared the *café au lait* or sliced the tartines for breakfast, he had already ridden out to Whinbury and returned. He kept the nature of his business dealings strictly to himself. Hortense, ever respectful, offered no inquiries; it was not her custom to comment on his movements, nor was it his to provide an account of them. The complexities of commerce—often dismal and intricate mysteries—remained buried in his chest, surfacing only occasionally to frighten Joe Scott or startle a foreign correspondent. Indeed, a profound reserve regarding matters of importance seemed woven into the very fabric of his mercantile blood.

Once breakfast concluded, Moore proceeded to his counting-house. Henry, Joe Scott's son, delivered the letters and daily newspapers. Moore seated himself at his desk, broke the seals of the documents, and scanned their contents. They were all brief, yet seemingly unsweet; rather, they appeared sour. As he laid down the final letter, his nostrils flared with a derisive, defiant sniff. Though he uttered no soliloquy, a glint in his eye seemed to summon the devil, charging him to sweep the entire enterprise into Gehenna. With a brief spasm of fury limited to his fingers, he stripped the quill of its feathered tip and dashed off a batch of replies. Sealing them, he stepped outside to walk through the mill. Upon returning, he settled down to read his newspaper.

The content failed to hold his attention. More than once, he draped the paper across his knees, folded his arms, and stared into the fire. Occasionally, he turned his gaze toward the window; at intervals, he consulted his watch. Clearly, his mind was elsewhere. Perhaps he was contemplating the beauty of the weather—for it was a fine, mild morning—and longing to be out in the fields. The door to his office stood wide open. Breeze and sunlight streamed in freely, yet the first visitor brought no spring fragrance, only an occasional puff of sulfur carried on the wings of thick, black smoke billowing from the gaunt chimney.

A dark-blue figure, freshly emerged from a dyeing vat, appeared briefly at the threshold. "He's comed, sir," the figure muttered before vanishing.

Mr. Moore did not lift his eyes from the paper. A large, broad-shouldered man, clad in fustian garments and gray worsted stockings, entered. Received with a nod, he was invited to sit. As he removed his hat—a notably poor specimen—he stowed it beneath his chair and wiped his forehead with a spotted cotton handkerchief pulled from the crown. "It's raight dahn warm for Febewerry," he remarked. Mr. Moore assented with a slight, inarticulate sound. The visitor then carefully deposited an official-looking staff in the corner beside him, whistled softly to signal his ease, and settled in.

"You have what is necessary, I suppose?" asked Mr. Moore.

"Ay, ay! All's right."

He resumed his whistling; Moore returned to his reading. The paper suddenly seemed more engaging. Presently, however, Moore reached into his cupboard, retrieved a black bottle—the same he had used previously for Malone's benefit—a tumbler, and a jug. Placing them on the table, he gestured to his guest.

"Help yourself; there's water in that jar in the corner."

"I dunnut knaw that there's mich need, for all a body is dry (thirsty) in a morning," said the fustian-clad gentleman, rising to comply.

"Will you tak naught yourseln, Mr. Moore?" he inquired, mixing a portion with practiced skill. After testing it with a deep draught, he sank back, satisfied and bland. Moore, sparing with words, replied only with a negative murmur.

"Yah'd as good," persisted the visitor. "It 'uld set ye up wald a sup o' this stuff. Uncommon good hollands. Ye get it fro' furrin parts, I'se think?"

"Ay!"

"Tak my advice and try a glass on't. Them lads 'at's coming 'll keep ye talking, nob'dy knows how long. Ye'll need propping."

"Have you seen Mr. Sykes this morning?" Moore inquired.

"I seed him a hauf an hour--nay, happen a quarter of an hour sin', just afore I set off. He said he aimed to come here, and I sudn't wonder but ye'll have old Helstone too. I seed 'em saddling his little nag as I passed at back o' t' rectory."

The speaker proved a true prophet. Five minutes later, the trotting hooves of a small nag echoed in the yard. A familiar nasal voice cried out, "Boy," presumably addressing Harry Scott, who usually hovered around the premises from nine until five, "take my horse and lead him into the stable."

Helstone entered, marching nimbly and erect. He looked browner, keener, and livelier than usual.

"Beautiful morning, Moore. How do, my boy? Ha! whom have we here?" Turning to the man with the staff, he added, "Sugden! What! you're going to work directly? On my word, you lose no time. But I come to ask explanations. Your message was delivered to me. Are you sure you are on the right scent? How do you mean to set about the business? Have you got a warrant?"

"Sugden has."

"Then you are going to seek him now? I'll accompany you."

"You will be spared that trouble, sir; he is coming to seek me. I'm just now sitting in state waiting his arrival."

"And who is it? One of my parishioners?"

Joe Scott had entered unnoticed. Now standing like a sinister phantom, half his body stained the deepest tint of indigo, he leaned against the desk. His master's answer to the rector's question was merely a smile. Joe seized the opportunity. Adopting a quiet yet cunning expression, he declared,

"It's a friend of yours, Mr. Helstone, a gentleman you often speak of."

"Indeed! His name, Joe? You look well this morning."

"Only the Rev. Moses Barraclough; t' tub orator you call him sometimes, I think."

"Ah!" said the rector, producing his snuff-box and administering a very long pinch—"ah! couldn't have supposed it. Why, the pious man never was a workman of yours, Moore. He's a tailor by trade."

"And so much the worse grudge I owe him, for interfering and setting my discarded men against me."

"And Moses was actually present at the battle of Stilbro' Moor? He went there, wooden leg and all?"

"Ay, sir," said Joe. "He went there on horseback, that his leg mightn't be noticed. He was the captain, and wore a mask. The rest only had their faces blackened."

"And how was he found out?"

"I'll tell you, sir," said Joe. "T' maister's not so fond of talking. I've no objections. He courted Sarah, Mr. Moore's servant lass, and so it seems she would have nothing to say to him; she either didn't like his wooden leg or she'd some notion about his being a hypocrite. Happen (for women is queer hands; we may say that amang werseln when there's none of 'em nigh) she'd have encouraged him, in spite of his leg and his deceit, just to pass time like. I've known some on 'em do as mich, and some o' t' bonniest and mimmest-looking, too--ay, I've seen clean, trim young things, that looked as denty and pure as daisies, and wi' time a body fun' 'em out to be nowt but stinging, venomed nettles."

"Joe's a sensible fellow," interjected Helstone.

"Howsiver, Sarah had another string to her bow. Fred Murgatroyd, one of our lads, is for her; and as women judge men by their faces--and Fred has a middling face, while Moses is none so handsome, as we all knaw--the lass took on wi' Fred. A two-three months sin', Murgatroyd and Moses chanced to meet one Sunday night; they'd both come lurking about these premises wi' the notion of counselling Sarah to tak a bit of a walk wi' them. They fell out, had a tussle, and Fred was worsted, for he's young and small, and Barraclough, for all he has only one leg, is almost as strong as Sugden there--indeed, anybody that hears him roaring at a revival or a love-feast may be sure he's no weakling."

"Joe, you're insupportable," interrupted Mr. Moore. "You spin out your explanation as Moses spins out his sermons. The long and short of it is, Murgatroyd was jealous of Barraclough; and last night, as he and a friend took shelter in a barn from a shower, they heard and saw Moses conferring with some associates within. From their discourse it was plain he had been the leader, not only at Stilbro' Moor, but in the attack on Sykes's property.
