Let us suppose that the farewell between the brothers has passed, and George now takes his place within Mr. Braddock’s household, while Harry returns to Castlewood, where duty and quiet life await him. Yet though he is home, his heart remains with the army—his true calling, his passion. The tasks of country life offer no joy; they feel like idle shadows compared to the grand purpose of war. He does not admit how deeply he grieves at being bound to this peaceful, homely roof—now heavier with silence than ever since George departed.

Harry walks past his brother’s empty chamber without looking in, avoids the door as if it might betray a wound. At meals, he sits at the head of the table, once occupied by George, and sips his drink from the silver tankard, his eyes distant, his breath slow. Each day, Madam Warrington raises her voice in firm salute: “The King!” And on Sundays, when Harry reads the service aloud, she joins in prayer for all travelers—by land and sea—with an extra weight of solemnity. She speaks of George constantly, cheerfully, confidently—as if his return were certain. She enters his room with her head high, untouched by outward sorrow. His books are arranged neatly, his linen folded with care. She talks of him with reverence, asking the old servants what must be done “when Mr. George comes home,” as though he were already returning through the garden gate.

Mrs. Mountain weeps whenever George’s name is mentioned. Harry’s face grows pale, almost grim, as if he fears some unseen blow. But his mother? She remains calm, composed. She makes more mistakes at piquet and backgammon than one would expect from her, and the servants find her awake and dressed before dawn. She has asked Mr. Dempster to return to live at Castlewood, and though she was once known for her sharpness and pride, she now speaks gently, listens quietly, and responds with soft authority. She often recalls her father—his campaigns, his scars, his survival—and says, “He came out of every battle unbroken, and so I trust my eldest son will.”

George writes frequently. Once the army began its march, he began keeping a rough journal, sending it home when possible. These letters are read with great eagerness by Harry, and more than once, on long summer evenings, the whole household gathers around the tea-table as Harry reads aloud. Madam Esmond sits upright—never leaning back, never yielding to comfort—as little Fanny Mountain sews beside her, and Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain sit over their cards. The old servants move softly in the dim light, listening intently.

Hear now Harry Warrington reading his brother’s words:

“It must be said that the provinces are behaving shamefully toward His Majesty King George II., and his representative here is furious. Virginia is bad enough, Maryland worse, but Pennsylvania is the worst of all. We beg them to send troops from England, promising to support them once they arrive. Yet not only do they fail to keep their word, but they charge exorbitant prices for cattle and supplies—some even cheat the soldiers who have come to defend them. No wonder the General swears, and the men grow restless. Delays have been endless. Because the colonies failed to deliver promised provisions or transport, weeks and months have passed—time during which, no doubt, the French have strengthened their positions on our frontier and in the forts they seized from us.

Though I’ve never liked Colonel Washington, I must acknowledge that your favorite (I’m not jealous, Hal) is a brave man and a capable officer. The family respects him greatly, and the General often seeks his counsel. Indeed, he’s nearly the only one who has seen the Indians in full war paint, and I confess I think he was right to fire upon Monsieur Jumonville last year.

There will be no further trouble over that brawl at Benson’s Tavern—not any more than there was over the proposed duel between Colonel W. and a certain young gentleman whose name I’ll omit. Captain Waring wanted to renew the quarrel when he arrived in camp, bringing a message from Captain Grace, whom your friend—brave as Hector—is eager to confront. He even called upon Colonel Wingfield, another aide-de-camp, to stand by him. But when Wingfield learned the truth—that the quarrel began because Grace was drunk, fanned by Waring’s own tipsiness, and that two officers from the 44th had insulted a militia man—he swore Colonel Washington would not fight them. He threatened to report them directly to His Excellency, who would bring both captains before a court-martial for drunkenness, disorderly conduct, and disrespect. They had no choice but to drop their swords and swallow their anger. Out of their cups, they were civil enough, and ate humble pie with good appetites at a reconciliation dinner hosted by Colonel W., where he was as perfectly correct and stiff as Prince Prettyman himself. Hang him! He has no faults—and that’s why I dislike him. When he marries that widow—ah me! What a dreary life she will lead.”

“Now, I wonder at the taste of some men—and the boldness of some women,” says Madam Esmond, setting down her teacup. “What woman, once married, could forget herself so completely as to marry again? Don’t you agree, Mountain?”

“Monstrous!” replies Mrs. Mountain, with a peculiar expression.

Mr. Dempster stares into his glass of punch, silent.

Harry looks as though he is choking—either with laughter or something deeper—but his mother says, “Go on, Harry. Continue with your brother’s journal. He writes well—but ah, will he ever write like my father?”

Harry resumes:

“We maintain strict order in camp. Orders against drunkenness and misconduct are strictly enforced. Rolls are taken three times daily—morning, noon, and night—and reports of absentees or offenders are submitted to the regimental commander, who ensures punishment. Men are disciplined, and the drums are always beating. Oh, Harry, it made me sick to see the first blood drawn from a strong white back, and to hear the pitiful cry of the poor fellow.”

“Oh, dreadful!” cries Madam Esmond. “I’d have killed Ward if he’d flogged me. Thank Heaven I escaped with only a crack of the ruler! The men are cared for well enough. I wish the officers were too. The Indians just broke camp and withdrew in anger—because the young officers were constantly drinking with the squaws—and—well—hum—ha.”

Here Harry pauses, perhaps unwilling to continue in front of little Fanny, who sits primly by her mother’s side, stitching her sampler.

“Skip that part about the disgusting drunkards,” says Madam Esmond. Harry clears his throat and continues, louder now:

“Each regiment holds Divine Service every Sunday under its colors. The General does everything in his power to prevent looting and to encourage locals to bring in supplies. He has declared that any soldier who molests market traders shall be shot. He raised the price of provisions by a penny per pound and lent money from his own pocket to supply the camp. All in all, this General is a strange mix. He flogs his men without mercy, yet gives freely. He swears frightful oaths and tells stories that would shock Mountain—”

“Why me?” asks Mountain. “What do I have to do with the General’s foolish tales?”

“Never mind the stories,” says the mistress of the house. “Just go on, Harry.”

“He tells stories that would shock Mountain after dinner—but never misses church. He adores his Great Duke, and speaks his name constantly. Our two regiments served in Scotland—where I imagine Mr. Dempster knew the color of their facings.”

“We saw the tails of their coats, as well as their facings,” grumbles the Jacobite tutor.

“Colonel Washington has suffered a severe fever and has barely been able to keep up with the march. Shouldn’t he return home and be cared for by his widow? When either of us falls ill, we’re soon friends again. But I can’t forgive him for having wronged him. Good heavens! How I’ve hated him these past months! Oh, Harry, I was furious at the tavern the other day when Mountain interrupted us so quickly. We should have fired a bit of powder between us and cleared the air. But though I don’t love him as you do, I know he’s a good soldier, a decent officer, and a brave, honest man. And I’ll respect him no less for not wanting to be our stepfather.”

“A stepfather indeed!” cries Harry’s mother. “Jealousy and prejudice have driven the poor child mad! Do you truly believe the Marquis of Esmond’s daughter and heiress could not find better stepfathers for her sons than a mere provincial surveyor? If there are any more such remarks in George’s journal, Harry, I beg you skip them.”

“‘Tis a fine sight,” Harry continues, reading from the journal, “to see a long line of redcoats winding through the woods or taking position after the march. Our vigilance against surprise is constant. We dare the Indians to attack us unawares, and our advance scouts and native allies have actually encountered the enemy, taking scalps. These French and their painted allies are cruel villains—we give them no mercy. Just yesterday, we found a small boy scalped but still alive in a lone house, where his parents had been murdered by savage foes. So angry is our General at their brutality that he has offered five pounds for every Indian scalp brought in.”

“When the march ends, you should see our camp—the care taken in its layout. Our baggage and the General’s tents are placed at the center. Outlying sentries are posted in pairs, threes, tens, even entire companies. In case of surprise, they are ordered to rush to the main body and rally around the tents and baggage, which are arranged to form a strong fortress. Sady and I are now marching on foot—my horses carry supplies. The Pennsylvanians sent such poor animals that they quickly gave out. The best horses were given up, and Roxana now carries packs instead of her young master. She knows me well, whinnies when she sees me, and I walk beside her, talking as we go.”

“July 4. To guard against surprise, we are warned to pay close attention to the drumbeat—halting at the long roll, marching at the long march. We are more alert now. Our advanced pickets are doubled, two sentries at each post. Men on picket duty remain armed with fixed bayonets all night, relieved every two hours. Those relieved lie down near their arms but may not leave their posts. It’s clear we are nearing the enemy. This packet goes with the General’s orders to Colonel Dunbar’s camp, thirty miles behind us, then to Frederick, then to my honored mother’s house at Castlewood. I send my duty and kindest regards to all friends there, and much love to my dearest brother—from his affectionate—GEORGE E. WARRINGTON.”

By July, the land lay scorched under the sun. Ten days had passed without news from the army advancing on the Ohio. Though progress through the forest was slow, the troops—led by seasoned officers—were now accustomed to the wilderness and feared no ambush. Precautions were thorough. Scouts and skirmishers had already discovered and destroyed enemy outposts. The latest intelligence said the army had moved far beyond the site of Washington’s defeat the previous year, and within two days would be within a day’s march of the French fort. No one doubted they would take it. The number of French reinforcements from Montreal was known. With two veteran British regiments, plus allies from Virginia and Pennsylvania, Braddock’s force was clearly superior.

This was the talk in the scattered towns of Virginia—at gentlemen’s homes, roadside taverns, wherever people gathered to discuss the war. Messengers sent back by the General reported well. It was believed the enemy would not resist. Had they meant to attack, they could have struck at dozens of points where the British had passed unchallenged.

And George—had he really given up his beloved mare, Roxana, and marched on foot? Madam Esmond vowed he should have the finest horse in Virginia or Carolina. Horses were plentiful, and for money, they could be had. Only for the King’s service were they withheld.

At Castlewood, the family spoke cheerfully at meals, never admitting fear, never doubting victory. Yet privately, they were restless, constantly leaving the house, riding from neighbor to neighbor in search of news. Rumors spread fast. When Colonel Jack, a famed border warrior, offered his hunters to the General—only to be refused—the news of his defection lit a thousand tongues.

The enslaved men, galloping through the countryside at night for pleasure or love, carried whispers across vast distances. They knew the army’s movements for at least two weeks after departure. They laughed at the tricks played on the troops—overcharging for horses, food, and supplies. A bargain over a foreigner was common among New Yorkers, Pennsylvanians, and Marylanders—though in later times, Americans became innocent, fair, and free from greed.

For three weeks, all news was hopeful. At supper, the Castlewood circle spoke with confidence. But on July 10th, darkness fell over the province. Fear touched every face. Negroes looked at their masters with wide eyes, then whispered among themselves. The fiddles stopped. Songs ceased. Servants rode off in haste, seeking news.

Taverns were packed with riders, drinking, cursing, shouting their grim tales. The army had been ambushed. Cut down almost to the last man. Officers were picked off by French marksmen and savages. The General was wounded, carried from the field in his sash. Four days later, the report came: the General was dead—scalped by a French Indian.

Ah! Poor Mrs. Mountain screamed when Gumbo brought the news from across the James River, and little Fanny leapt into her mother’s arms, crying.

“Lord God Almighty, watch over us, and defend my boy!” cried Mrs. Esmond, falling to her knees, hands lifted to heaven.

The gentlemen were away when the rumor reached them, but returned within hours, each from his own hunt for truth. The Scots tutor dared not meet the widow’s agonized gaze. Harry Warrington was as pale as his mother. The manner of the General’s death might be false—but he was dead. The army had been surprised, fled, slaughtered without seeing the enemy.

An express had arrived from Dunbar’s camp. Fugitives were streaming in. Should Harry go? He must go. He and stout little Dempster armed themselves and mounted, taking two servants with them. They followed the trail the expedition had carved through the woods. With every mile, the horror of the battle grew greater.

The day after the defeat, survivors had reached Dunbar’s camp, fifty miles from the battlefield. There, Harry and his companions rode, stopping stragglers, asking for news, giving coin, hearing again and again the same terrible tale: a thousand men slain, two-thirds of the officers dead, all the General’s aides-de-camp hit. Hit? But were they killed? Those who fell did not rise. The tomahawk had done its work.

O brother, brother!

All the memories of youth flooded back—the laughter, the vows, the tender bonds of childhood. Harry’s grief was unbearable. Wounded men looked up and softened at his sorrow. Rough women wept at the sight of his grief. Even the hardened old tutor could scarcely look at him, overwhelmed with tears—for he mourned not only the pupil he loved, but the noble young man who now lay dead beneath the savage blade.
