Her posture was one to stir pity rather than fear—unless the eyes that beheld her were clouded by suspicion. Her bosom fluttered like a trapped bird, her cheeks flushed and paled in turns, and her trembling hand braced against the wall for support, so unsteady were her limbs. The look in her eyes—purple as twilight—was a marvel of mingled love, reverence, pity, and tender memory, all fixed upon the gaunt and hollow-eyed hermit before her.

"Aha!" he cried. "Thou comest at last in flesh and blood—come to me as thou camest to holy Anthony. But I am armed against thee." He snatched up his crucifix and thrust it toward her, the candle in his other hand shaking violently. "*Exorcizo te!*"

"Ah, no!" she pleaded, lifting her hands in supplication. "Do me no harm! It is Margaret."

"Liar!" he shouted. "Margaret was fair, but not so radiant as thou. Thou flinchest at that holy name—thou subtle hypocrite! *In Nomine Dei, exorcizo vos!*"

"Jesu, mercy!" gasped Margaret, terror-stricken. "Curse me not! I will go home. I thought—I hoped—" Her voice broke. "For the love of manhood, do not Latin me away! Gerard, is this how we meet again, after all this time, after all?" She sank nearly to her knees, sobbing—half in wounded love, half in superstitious dread.

Had his mind not been steeped in visions of demons, he might have doubted longer whether this trembling, grief-stricken creature was truly woman or fiend. But the sacred name she invoked shattered his delusion. The truth struck him like lightning.

With a cry of remorse, he tried to lift her, but his limbs, weakened by fasting and the shock of emotion, failed him. "What—is it truly thee?" he gasped. "Have I met thee with such cruelty? Forgive me!"

For a long moment, neither could speak, choked by the weight of feeling.

"I do not blame thee much," Margaret said at last, mastering her tears with effort. "Thou tookest me for another—for a devil! Oh—oh!"

"Forgive me, gentle soul." When at last he could form more than broken words, he said, "I have been sorely plagued by the Evil One since I came here."

Margaret shuddered, glancing around the dismal cell. "No wonder. Then take my hand, I beg thee, and let me lead thee from this foul place."

He stared at her in astonishment. "What—desert my cell? Return to the world? Is this why thou hast sought me?" His voice was heavy with reproach.

"Ay, Gerard! I come to take thee to thy vicarage at Gouda—thanks to Heaven and thy brother Giles. Thy mother and I have made it fair for thee. 'Tis snug enough in winter, I promise thee. But wait till spring, when the hawthorn blooms and roses climb thy walls. Didst think we had forgotten thee—forgotten all the foolish things thou lovest?" Her voice softened. "And thy mother waits even now—'tis late for her to be abed. Prithee, come! The moment we quit this wretched hole, I will show thee a treasure thou hast—unknowing—given thyself. Else thou wouldst never have fled from us so!"

Her words faltered. "Alas, what is wrong? What have I said to make thee look so?"

For he had drawn back, his face a mask of suspicion and dread.

"Unhappy girl," he said solemnly, though his voice quaked, "wouldst have me risk my soul—and thine—for a paltry vicarage and its flowers? This is not thy doing. The pitiless Fiend sends thee, his unwitting tool, to tempt me. But I will unmask him even to thee, that thou mayst loathe him as I do." He drew a sharp breath. "Margaret, my lost love—knowest thou why I am here? Because I love thee."

"Oh, no! If thou lovedst me, thou wouldst not have hidden from me. There was no need!"

"Let there be no deceit between us, who have loved so truly—and who, after this night, must meet no more on earth."

"God forbid!" she cried.

"I love thee still—else thou wouldst have wed another by now, nor been the one to seek me in this desolate place. But I am a priest, a monk. What can come of us living side by side, feeding a passion once innocent, now forbidden? No—though my heart break, I must be firm. 'Tis I who am the man, the priest. We must not meet again—not till solitude has schooled me, and thou art wed to another."

"I will bear my doom—but not thine. I would die ten times over rather than see thee rot in this dismal den when a fair manse awaits thee!"

Clement groaned. Each word she spoke only sharpened the agony of duty.

"My beloved," he said, torn between tenderness and iron resolve, "I bless thee for this one more glimpse of thy sweet face. And may God forgive thee—and bless thee—for shattering in a moment the peace I have built through six months of solitude. No matter. A year of penance may restore me." His voice broke. "Poor Margaret, I seem cruel—yet I am kind. 'Tis best we part—now, this moment."

"Part? Never! We have seen what comes of parting. Gerard, thou hast not heard half my tale—no, not a tenth! 'Tis not for comfort alone I bring thee to Gouda. Hear me!"

"I dare not. Thy very voice is temptation—its music a snare to memory."

"Then I say thou *shalt* hear me, Gerard! I will not leave this place unheard!"

"Then must we part by other means," he said sadly.

"What means? Wouldst thrust me out by force—being the stronger?"

"Nay, Margaret. Thou knowest I would suffer death rather than harm thee. Thy sweet body is dearer to me than mine own—but a thousand times dearer are our souls, both thine and mine. I have endured this direst temptation long enough. Now I must fly. Farewell!"

He lunged for the door, flung it open, and was halfway out when she darted after him, seizing his arm.

"Then another must speak for me! I meant to reward thee for yielding—but since thou wilt not, his voice must plead. Turn but a moment—look!"

"Nay—"

"I say *ay*! And then turn thy back if thou canst!"

She loosened her grip, trusting he would not deny so small a kindness. But seeing his chance, he wrenched free with a cry:

"Fly, Clement, fly!"

His monastic fervor lent him a burst of strength. He tore from her grasp, leaped the brook, and ran. But hearing no pursuit, he halted and looked back.

She lay prone, hands outstretched. In his haste, he had thrown her down.

The sight struck him like a blade. He took a step toward her—then, with a wild impulse, flung himself into the icy stream instead.

"Here, kill my body!" he gasped, half-submerged. "But save my soul!"

As he stood there, the freezing water numbing his limbs, Margaret uttered a long, broken moan and pushed herself to her knees. The moonlight showed her face—pale, eyes glistening with tears. Then came her words, clear in the silent night:

"Oh, God! Thou who knowest all—Thou seest how I am used. Forgive me, then! For I will not live another day!"

With that, she sprang to her feet and fled past him like a wounded creature, her cry ringing in his ears:

A single syllable, yet brimming with anguish—wounded love, wounded pride, despair, the brink of madness.

Clement heard, and terror and remorse froze him worse than the water. He had driven her away—forever. In the midst of his grim triumph, a black impulse rose to curse the Church, to curse Heaven itself, for demanding such cruelty of mortal flesh.

At last, half-dead, he dragged himself from the stream and stumbled back to his cell.

"I am safe now," he groaned. "She will never return. Unmanly, ungrateful wretch that I am!" He collapsed on the floor, half-hoping never to rise again.

But glancing at the hour-glass, he saw midnight had passed. Slowly, stiffly, he peeled off his soaked garments, moaning at the pain he had caused her. Then he donned the old hermit's bristled cilice and over it the breastplate—arms he had never dared wear before, doubting his worth. Now he needed every defense: the cilice to torment his flesh, the breastplate for holy strength.

Kneeling, he prayed humbly for death that very night. Then, lighting all his candles, he recited psalms mechanically, each word leaden on his tongue. Between verses, he groaned aloud.

Then his eye caught something in the corner—a small bundle, unnoticed before. At one end gleamed threads of gold amid silk.

Curious, he crept closer—and his breath caught.

"A seraph!" he whispered. "A heavenly seraph! Heaven has witnessed my trial and approves my harshness—sending this angel to cheer me."

Kneeling in awe, he gazed at the child—golden hair, skin like dawn, cheeks like ripened fruit.

"Let me feast my sorrowing eyes on thee," he murmured, "ere thou wingest back to thy blessed home, leaving my cell darker than before."

But his movement disturbed the little visitor. Two eyes, blue as heaven, flew open. Seeing the wild figure looming, the child whimpered, "*MUMMA! MUM-MA!*" Tears spilled down his cheeks.

Perhaps, after six months of solitude, Clement saw childish beauty more vividly than we. This fair-haired boy seemed more angelic than any painted cherub.

Yet the spell was broken—though not unhappily. Clement had always loved children, and monastic life had nurtured that tenderness. The child's innocent distress, his trembling lips and helpless cry, pierced the hermit's heart.

Softly, gently, he soothed him, and soon wonder replaced fear. The boy studied this strange, armored man who spoke so kindly.

Clement, perplexed, could only guess how this little one had come here. The open door—some desperate mother abandoning her child? The thought made his heart ache.

"Now bless thee, bless thee," he murmured. "I would not trade thee for a cherub in paradise."

"*At's pooty*," the boy declared, pointing to the breastplate.

Clement almost laughed. "What else here is pretty, little one?"

"*Ookum-gars*," the child said gravely.

"So thou likest my armor?"

"*Nay. Ay. En um ittle, ery ittle? Not ike torks. Hate torks um bigger an baby.*"

In broken words, the child confided his fear of storks—their great flapping wings, their looming size.

Clement smiled. "But my birds are tiny and sweet. Wouldst like them?"

"*Den I ikes 'm. I ont my mammy.*"

"Ah, sweet one, I fear I must serve in her stead. Hast thou no father?"

The question hung in the air—and at that very moment, unseen, a third listener stood outside, every word striking her heart like a hammer.

For Margaret, fleeing in despair, had not gone far. Love and fear for her child had drawn her back. Now she crouched by the door, breathless, eyes wide, drinking in the scene within.

The child, asked about his father, shook his head at first. Then, as if recalling something, he said, "*Dad-da ill man; run away and left poor mum-ma.*"

Margaret winced. Some meddling fool had put those words in his mouth—and now they spilled out before Gerard.

His response stunned her.

"The villain!" he burst out. "The heartless wretch—to desert thee, sweet one! Ah, he knows not what joy he cast away. Well, little dove, I must be father and mother to thee now."

Her heart swelled. He did not know. He *did not know*.

As he spoke of fetching eggs and milk for the child, teaching him to read, her eyes burned with tears.

"*I can yead*," the boy boasted.

"Can thou? All the better. We shall read good books together. And I will show thee the way to Heaven—a fair place, full of little ones like thee, waiting to love thee. Wouldst like to go there one day?"

"*Ay, along wi' my mammy.*"

"What, not without her?"

"*Nay. I ont my mammy. Where is my mammy?*"

Oh, how it cost her not to rush in!

"Fret not," he soothed. "Mayhap she will come when thou sleepest. Wilt thou be good and rest?"

"*I not eepy. Ikes to talk.*"

"Then talk we shall. What is thy name?"

"*Baby.*"

"None other?"

"*Nay.*"

"Shall I tell thee a story? Or sing thee a song?"

The child clapped his hands. "*I ikes tories. I ikes tongs.*"

"Choose, then—a song or a story?"

The boy’s face twisted in indecision. "*Ting I a tong. Nay, tell I a tory. Nay—*" His lip quivered, torn between choices.

Then, brightening: "*Ting I a tory.*"

"Sing thee a story? Well, why not?"

He shed his breastplate and rough cilice, donning a softer garment. Then, seating the boy on his knee, he took up his psaltery and played a gentle tune.

"*I ikes dat,*" the child approved.

"Then list the story."

He chanted a tale in a lilting cadence, the boy enraptured.

"*I ikes oo,*" came the sleepy murmur. "*Ot is oo? Is oo a man?*"

"*Ay, little heart—and a great sinner too.*"

"*I ikes great tingers. Ting one other tory.*"

A second story followed, then a drowsy protest: "*I is not eepy...*" before the child succumbed, eyelids fluttering shut.

Clement laid aside the psaltery and cradled him, humming a lullaby from his own childhood. As the boy sank into deep sleep, he gazed down, his face a blend of tenderness and sorrow.

A sigh escaped him.

Then—like moonlight given form—Margaret stood before him, her hand on his shoulder, her voice trembling.

"Gerard... thou dost not reject us. Thou *canst* not."
