I rose next morning while the dormitory still breathed its last, sour-sweet draught of night—lamp-oil, washed boards, the faint iron of cold. An hour before dawn, I finished my vigil kneeling on the floor by the central stand, working not by day’s honest breadth but by that dying wick’s reluctant glow, as if I were salting meat in a cellar and must not be seen.

All my poor wealth of colour—beads, silk, the very last lengths of thread—went under my fingers before the chain came to the weight and richness I had determined on. I made it double on purpose: I knew the taste I meant to meet; for certain spirits, contrariness is the surest seasoning, and nothing looks so persuasive as what has been laboured upon till it shines. When the last knot was drawn tight, when the braid lay pliant and firm as a well-plaited loaf, I wanted only a clasp. I had one, but it was the fastening of my sole necklace; I unhooked it as one takes the last pinch of spice from a jar kept for Sunday, and fixed it where it would do the most good. Then I coiled the guard compact, like a ribbon of burnished fruit-skin, and put it in a small box chosen for its insolent brilliance—tropic shell, nacarat-hued, warm as coral under milk, set round with a little coronet of blue stones that caught what light there was and answered it.

Inside the lid, with the point of my scissors, I scratched certain initials, neat and secret, as a cook will mark her own tin by a nick in the rim. I shut it, and the click sounded louder than it should have done in the sleeping house.

There are days in Madame Beck’s school that turn, with regularity, like the seasons of a kitchen: markets, masses, examinations, fêtes. The reader will remember Madame Beck’s anniversary—how, at each return, the establishment subscribed a handsome offering. This honour, full and formal, belonged to her alone; but in a modified form, by the natural drift of affection rather than any plotted ceremony, it had come to attach itself also to her kinsman and counsellor, M. Emanuel. He had made it plain that he would accept neither plate nor jewellery; the money-value—so much gold, so many stones—left him cold. Yet a slight tribute, plainly offered, touched him: a flower, a sketch, a thing made with one’s own patience and meaning, would please him more than a ring presented with bows and speeches. Such was he: not wise as his own generation counts wisdom, but capable of a childlike susceptibility to whatever dawns pure and lifts.

His fête fell on the first of March, a Thursday. The day came clear and sunny, with that thin, clean warmth that seems to rinse the very air. It was also the morning when we attended mass, and it carried besides the privilege of a half-holiday—an afternoon for walking, shopping, visits. The whole house, as if tuned by the same hand, woke to smartness. Collars—so often grey with work—came out white; the dingy classe-dress gave way to lighter stuffs. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, on this Thursday, went further: she appeared in a robe de soie—an article in economical Labassecour accounted as rash as buying strawberries in Lent. Nay, she sent for a coiffeur to dress her hair; and even dull noses could tell she had bedewed her handkerchief, her hands, with a new perfume—sharp-sweet, too assertive, like candied violets laid on hot metal.

Poor Zélie! It was her habit to say, at this time, that she was weary to death of seclusion and labour; that she longed for leisure, for means, for some one to work for her—a husband who would pay her debts (she was heavily entangled), supply her wardrobe, and leave her free, as she phrased it, to goûter un peu les plaisirs. Rumour had long declared her eye set on M. Emanuel. His eye was certainly often set on her. I had seen him sit and watch her, perseveringly, minute after minute, while the class composed in silence and he, on his estrade, seemed unoccupied. Under that basilisk attention she would writhe—half flattered, half at a loss; and he would follow every shade of her sensation with a keen, almost cruel acuteness. For in some cases he had an instinct that pierced like a skewer through a joint, finding the bone, the hidden gristle, the place where the meat is not sound. Under florid veilings he could discern the bare, barren places of spirit; the twisted tendency; the stain self-brought and carefully covered. Any acknowledged calamity he could pity and forgive; but where he met denial, where he found deceitful concealment, he could be merciless. He would snatch away the screen, hurry a shrinking creature up to the very peak of exposure, and show her there—false, naked, poor living lie—spawn of that horrible Truth which cannot be looked on unveiled. He believed it justice. I have doubted whether man has the right to do such justice upon man; more than once I have given tears to his victims and not spared keen reproach to himself. He deserved it; yet it was hard to shake his conviction that the work was righteous.

After breakfast and mass, the bell rang; the rooms filled; and classe offered, for once, a pretty spectacle. Teachers and pupils sat neat, orderly, expectant; in each hand, a bouquet of felicitation—spring flowers, fresh and damp at the stems, their scent rising and mingling: the green sting of cut leaves, the honey of forced blossoms, the faint earthy breath that clings to roots even when roots are gone. The air felt seasoned with sweetness, as if someone had opened a cupboard where sugared things are kept.

In my hands there was nothing.

I have always liked flowers best when they grow. Once gathered, they cease to please me; they look to me like lives torn up and sentenced—rootless, perishing. Their likeness to living makes me sad. I never offer flowers to those I love; I never wish to receive them from hands dear to me. Zélie marked my empty hands; she could not believe I had been so remiss. Her eye ran over and round me with greedy curiosity: surely I must have some solitary symbolic blossom tucked away—some shy knot of violets to win praise for taste, commendation for ingenuity. But the unimaginative Anglaise proved barer than the Parisienne’s fears: I sat literally unprovided, as leafless as winter timber. When she ascertained it, Zélie smiled with satisfaction.

“How wisely you have acted to keep your money, Miss Lucie,” she said. “Silly I have gone and thrown away two francs on a bouquet of hot-house flowers!”

And she displayed her splendid nosegay with pride.

Hush—there was a step in the corridor: the step. Prompt as always, but to our fancy this morning it had in it something more than mere vehemence of nerve; we flattered ourselves it carried a friendly promise. It did.

He entered with a mood that made him, in that already well-lit room, like an added sunbeam. The morning light played amongst our plants and laughed on the walls; it seemed to take an extra lustre from his benignant salute. Like one who understands the ceremony of a table and dresses for it—not to boast, but to honour—he had put aside the soot-dark paletôt whose vague folds gave him a conspirator’s air. His figure (such as it was) was set off by a civilized coat and a silken vest not unpretty to behold. The defiant bonnet-grec had vanished; bare-headed he came in, carrying a Christian hat in his gloved hand. The little man looked well—very well. His blue eye held a clearness of amity; his dark complexion carried a glow of good feeling that passed in place of beauty. One ceased to note that his nose, though far from small, had no fine shape; that his cheek was thin, brow square, mouth no rose-bud. He was accepted as he stood; his presence seemed the reverse of insignificant.

He went to his desk, set down hat and gloves.

“Bon jour, mes amies,” said he—and though it was not jovial, nor unctuous, it had that tone he reserved for words his heart consented to utter. That heart did speak sometimes. Irritable, it was not ossified; in its core lay a tenderness beyond a man’s tenderness—something that humbled him to little children and bound him, in spite of himself, to girls and women; he could not deny the affinity, nor quite deny that, on the whole, he was better with them than with his own sex.

“We all wish Monsieur a good day,” said Mademoiselle Zélie, who constituted herself spokeswoman of the assembly, “and present to him our congratulations on the anniversary of his fête.”

Advancing with only so many twists of affectation as were, to her, the indispensable machinery of motion, she laid her costly bouquet before him. He bowed over it.

Then followed the long train of offerings. Pupils swept past with that gliding step foreigners practise, leaving their tributes as they went; each adjusted her separate gift with dexterity, so that when the last nosegay lay on the desk it formed the apex of a blooming pyramid—spreading, towering, exuberant enough, at last, to eclipse the hero behind it. Ceremony ended, seats were resumed; we sat in dead silence, expectant of a speech.

Five minutes might have passed, and the hush remained unbroken; ten—and still no sound.

Many must have begun to wonder what Monsieur waited for; and well they might. Voiceless and viewless, stirless and wordless, he kept his station behind the piled flowers.

At last a voice issued—deeper than his usual, as if it spoke from a hollow place.

“Est-ce là tout?”

Zélie looked round.

“You have all presented your bouquets?” she inquired of the pupils.

Yes; all had given—from eldest to youngest, from tallest to smallest. The senior mistress signified as much.

“Est-ce là tout?” came again; and the intonation, deep before, had now sunk lower.

“Monsieur,” said Mademoiselle St. Pierre, rising, and speaking with her own sweet smile, “I have the honour to tell you that, with a single exception, every person in classe has offered her bouquet. For Meess Lucie, Monsieur will kindly make allowance; as a foreigner she probably did not know our customs, or did not appreciate their significance. Meess Lucie has regarded this ceremony as too frivolous to be honoured by her observance.”

“Famous!” I muttered between my teeth. “You are no bad speaker, Zélie, when you begin.”

The answer vouchsafed from the estrade was only a hand, emerging from behind the pyramid, gesturing as if to deprecate words, to enjoin silence.

A moment after, the form followed. Monsieur emerged from his eclipse; stepping to the front of his estrade, he fixed his gaze straight upon the vast mappe-monde opposite, as if that painted globe could give him patience; and demanded a third time, now in tones genuinely tragic:

“Est-ce là tout?”

Even then I might have set all right. In my hand, tight-held, I had the ruddy little shell-box with its blue stones—my secret tribute, my labour. I had fully purposed to step forward and slip it into his hand. But first the comic side of his behaviour tempted me to delay; then Mademoiselle St. Pierre’s interference provoked contumacy. The reader has had no reason to ascribe to me pretensions to perfection; he will not be surprised to learn I felt too perverse to defend myself from any imputation the Parisienne chose to insinuate. Besides, M. Paul took my defection so seriously—made tragedy of it—that he deserved, I thought, to be vexed. I kept my box and my countenance, and sat as insensible as a stone.

“It is well!” dropped at length from his lips; and as he said it, the shadow of some paroxysm—wrath, scorn, resolve—passed over his brow, rippled his mouth, lined his cheeks. He swallowed down further comment and launched into his customary discours.

I do not remember the discours; I did not listen. The very act of gulping down his vexation, of dismissing it abruptly, gave me a sensation that half counteracted the ridiculous effect of the reiterated “Est-ce là tout?”

Toward the close, my attention was caught again, and in a manner which diverted it at first, then pricked it sorely. By some little accident—my thimble fell; I stooped to regain it, and struck the crown of my head against the sharp corner of my desk—petty mishaps that made a small bustle. M. Paul, already strained, became irritated; flinging away the dignity and self-control he never long consented to wear, he broke forth into the strain that gave him ease.

Somehow, in the course of his speech, he had crossed the Channel and landed on British ground. There I found him when I began to listen.

With a quick cynical glance round the room—a glance meant to scathe as it crossed me—he fell with fury upon les Anglaises. Never had I heard English women handled as he handled them that morning. He spared neither mind, morals, manners, nor appearance. He railed at their tall stature, long necks, thin arms, slovenly dress; at their pedantic education; at their impious scepticism; at their insufferable pride; at their pretentious virtue. Over that last he ground his teeth and looked as if, had he dared, he would have said things worse. He was spiteful, acrid, savage—and, as a natural consequence, detestably ugly.

“Little wicked venomous man!” I thought. “Am I going to harass myself with fears of displeasing you, of hurting your feelings? No. You shall be indifferent to me, as the shabbiest bouquet in your pyramid.”

I grieve to say I could not carry out the resolution. For a time his abuse found me stolid; I bore it a quarter of an hour. But he was determined to sting. He fastened not only upon women, but upon our greatest names and best men—sullying the shield of Britannia, dabbling the union jack in mud. With vicious relish he brought up the spiciest continental historical falsehoods—than which nothing can be more offensive. Zélie and the whole class became one grin of vindictive delight; it is curious how these clowns of Labassecour secretly hate England. At last I struck a sharp stroke on my desk, opened my lips, and let loose this cry:

“Vive l’Angleterre, l’Histoire et les Héros! A bas la France, la Fiction et les Faquins!”

The class was struck of a heap; they thought me mad. The Professor put up his handkerchief and smiled fiendishly into its folds. Little monster of malice! He believed he had the victory because he had made me angry. In a second he grew good-humoured. With great blandness he resumed the subject of his flowers; talked poetically and symbolically of sweetness, perfume, purity; made Frenchified comparisons between the jeunes filles and the blossoms before him; paid Mademoiselle St. Pierre a full-blown compliment on the superiority of her bouquet; and ended by announcing that on the first really fine, mild, balmy spring morning he intended to take the whole class out to breakfast in the country—such of the class, he added with emphasis, as he could count amongst the number of his friends.

“Donc je n’y serai pas,” I declared, involuntarily.

“Soit!” was his response; and gathering his flowers in his arms, he flashed out of classe. I, consigning my work, scissors, thimble, and the neglected little box to my desk, swept upstairs. I do not know whether he felt hot and angry; I confess that I did.

Yet my anger was strangely evanescent. I had not sat an hour on the edge of my bed—picturing and re-picturing his look, manner, words—before I smiled at the whole scene. A little pang of regret came with the smile: the box had not been offered. I had meant to gratify him. Fate would not have it so.

In the afternoon, remembering that desks in classe were not inviolate repositories, and thinking it well to secure the box—because of the initials I had cut inside the lid, P. C. D. E., for Paul Carl (or Carlos) David Emanuel; these foreigners must always have a string of baptismals—I went down to the schoolroom.

It slept in holiday repose. The day-pupils were gone home; the boarders were out walking; the teachers (save the surveillante of the week) were in town, visiting or shopping. The suite of divisions was vacant; so was the grande salle, with its huge solemn globe hanging in the midst, its many-branched chandeliers, and its horizontal grand piano closed and silent, as if enjoying its mid-week Sabbath. In that stillness the building’s sounds stood out—boards settling, a distant door’s sigh, the faint echo of street-noise filtered through thick walls; and I could not help thinking how different a mind feels when spared the continual clatter of voices and footsteps. A school is a kind of managed din, and even its order has its own oppression.

I wondered to find the first classe door ajar; that room was usually locked when empty and accessible only to Madame Beck and myself, who held a duplicate key. I wondered more, as I approached, to hear a vague movement as of life—a step, a chair stirred, the sound of a desk opening.

“It is only Madame Beck doing inspection duty,” I decided.

The half-open door permitted certainty. I looked.

Not Madame Beck’s inspecting garb—not the shawl and clean cap—but a man’s coat and close-shorn dark head. He sat in my chair; his olive hand held my desk open; his nose was lost among my papers. His back was toward me; there was no room for doubt. The attire of ceremony had been discarded; the cherished ink-stained paletôt was resumed; the perverse bonnet-grec lay on the floor as if dropped from a hand too busy to care.

I knew—had long known—that M. Emanuel’s hand was on intimate terms with my desk. It raised and lowered the lid, ransacked and arranged the contents almost as familiarly as my own. He did not wish the fact to be dubious: he left signs of each visit palpable and unmistakable. Yet I had never caught him in the act. Watch as I would, I could not detect the hours and moments of his coming. I saw only the brownie’s work: exercises left overnight full of faults and found next morning carefully corrected; loans that came capriciously, full welcome and refreshing. Between a sallow dictionary and worn-out grammar would spring, as if grown in the dark, a fresh interesting new work, or a classic mellow and sweet in its ripe age. Out of my work-basket would peep a romance, under it would lurk a pamphlet or magazine from which last evening’s reading had been taken.

Impossible to doubt the source; and one trait, condemning and traitorous, settled the question beyond dispute: they smelt of cigars. This, at first, had shocked me. I used to open the window with bustle and air my desk; with fastidious finger and thumb I would hold the peccant brochures out to the breeze as if smoke were moral infection. I was cured of that formality suddenly. Monsieur caught me at it one day, understood the inference, and in an instant relieved my hand of its burden; another moment, and he would have thrust the same into the glowing stove. It happened to be a book I was bent on reading; for once I proved more decided and quicker than he. I recaptured the spoil; and, having saved that volume, I never hazarded a second.

With all this, I had never been able to arrest, in his visits, the freakish, friendly, cigar-loving phantom.

But now at last I had him. There he was—the very brownie; and there, curling from his lips, was the pale blue breath of his Indian darling. He was smoking into my desk; no wonder the books betrayed him. Provoked at that particular, yet pleased to surprise him—pleased, with the mixed feeling of a housewife who discovers at last her strange elfin ally busy in the dairy at the untimely churn—I stole forward softly, stood behind him, and bent with precaution over his shoulder.

My heart smote me. After this morning’s hostility, after my seeming remissness, after the puncture to his feelings and the ruffling of his temper—he, willing to forget and forgive, had brought me a couple of handsome volumes, whose titles and authorship promised interest. As he sat bending above the desk, he stirred its contents with a gentle careful hand; disarranging, but not harming. My morning’s anger melted as easily as salt in warm broth. Whatever else I might say, I did not dislike Professor Emanuel.

I think he heard me breathe. He turned suddenly. His temperament was nervous, yet he never started, and seldom changed colour; something hardy in him caught and held itself.

“I thought you were gone into town with the other teachers,” said he, as if gripping his self-possession lest it slip. “It is as well you are not. Do you think I care for being caught? Not I. I often visit your desk.”

“Monsieur, I know it.”

“You find a brochure or tome now and then; but you don’t read them, because they have passed under this?”—and he touched his cigar.

“They have, and are no better for the process; but I read them.”

“Without pleasure?”

“Monsieur must not be contradicted.”

“Do you like them, or any of them—are they acceptable?”

“Monsieur has seen me reading them a hundred times, and knows I have not so many recreations as to undervalue those he provides.”

He looked at me—quick, searching, as if he listened not only for words but for what they could not translate.

“I mean well,” he said. “And if you see that I mean well, and derive some little amusement from my efforts, why can we not be friends?”

“A fatalist would say—because we cannot.”

“This morning,” he went on, “I awoke in a bright mood, and came into classe happy; you spoiled my day.”

“No, Monsieur—only an hour or two of it, and that unintentionally.”

“Unintentionally! No. It was my fête-day; everybody wished me happiness but you. The little children of the third division gave each her knot of violets, lisped each her congratulation: you—nothing. Not a bud, leaf, whisper—not a glance. Was this unintentional?”

“I meant no harm.”

“Then you really did not know our custom? You were unprepared? You would willingly have laid out a few centimes on a flower to give me pleasure, had you been aware that it was expected? Say so, and all is forgotten, and the pain soothed.”

“I did know that it was expected: I was prepared; yet I laid out no centimes on flowers.”
