Over our supper, Dan and I swapped our life stories, discovering a strange kinship in our wanderlust. He had left school years before me, attended Cambridge, but fate—specifically his father's death and a wound from the Indian Mutiny that never healed—cut his academic career short.

"You might not have expected that to grieve me," Dan said, smiling. "But it was a severe blow. At Cambridge, I finally realized I was a scholar by temperament. School had been designed to make learning boring; we were rushed through education like tourists in a gallery, told to say we'd seen it rather than actually understanding it. At college, I chose my own subjects and fed on knowledge instead of being stuffed like a Strasbourg goose."

Back in London, he worked in a bank where the chairman was his father's old friend. While earning a small income, he studied, but the deadly monotony of banking appalled him. After his mother died, he shook the "cloying dust" of the City from his brain and joined a small theatrical company. For eighteen months, he played everything from Romeo to Paul Pry, painted scenery, and even helped post bills—a job he found uniquely difficult because the paste-laden posters had an innate tendency to recoil onto the amateur's head.

Weary of the stage, he tried a circus as "Signor Ricardo," the daring bare-back rider, and performed in a trapeze act. Seeking respectability, he became a waiter in Ostend, then a footman.

"One never knows," Dan remarked. "I might become a society novelist; inside knowledge of the aristocracy would give me an edge."

He had tried it all: tramping Ireland with a fiddle, lecturing in Scotland on Palestine with dissolving views, marking billiards, teaching school, and finally, for the last three months, working as a dramatic and musical critic for a Sunday newspaper.

"How did you get it?" I asked.

"The idea struck me late one afternoon," Dan replied. "I was broke, sauntering down the Strand. I walked into a newspaper office, went upstairs, and opened the first door without knocking. Inside, a young man was running in circles, cursing because he'd spilled ink, lost his matches, and broken the bell-pull. Thinking he was the office boy, I assumed he was the editor. I lit the gas for him and found him a new inkpot. He was a slim young man with the voice of a schoolboy, likely in his mid-twenties, owing his job to his aunt, the proprietress.

"He complained that strangers treated the office as a shelter from the rain, marching straight up to the editor's door. I suggested he make strangers write their business on a slip of paper. He agreed. Just then, another knock came. The young editor screamed, 'I won't see him! Send everybody away!'

"I barred the door for an elderly gentleman, telling him the editor was unavailable and handing him a slip of paper. I stayed in the ante-room for half an hour, sending away about a dozen people. The last visitor was a tired gentleman smoking a cigarette. When I asked his name, he looked at me in surprise and said, 'Idiot!'

"I refused to let him pass. He asked if I could make an exception for the sub-editor on press night. I said no. He then asked who was going to the Royalty Theatre that night. It was seven o'clock.

"I realized: if the sub-editor didn't know who to send, the dramatic critic's post must be vacant. 'Oh, that's all right,' I told him. 'I'll be in time.'

'Have you arranged with the Guv'nor?' he asked.

'I'm just waiting to see him again,' I lied. 'Have you got the ticket?'

'Haven't seen it,' he replied.

'About a column?' I suggested.

'Three-quarters,' he said, and left.

The moment he was gone, I met a printer's boy. 'What's the name of your sub?' I asked. 'Tall man with a black moustache.' 'Oh, you mean Penton,' the boy explained. 'That's the name.'

I walked straight into the irritable editor. 'I only want the ticket for the Royalty Theatre. Penton says you've got it.'

'I don't know where it is,' he growled.

I found it on his desk. 'Who's going?' he asked. 'I am,' I said. And I went.

They never discovered I appointed myself. Penton thinks I'm a relation of the proprietress, and everyone treats me with marked respect. Mrs. Wallace, the proprietress, thinks I'm Penton's discovery. The salary is small, but journalism suits my temperament."

"You've been somewhat of a rolling stone," I commented.

He laughed. "From the stone's point of view, I never saw the advantage of being smothered in moss. I prefer remaining the stone, unhidden, able to move."

He proposed we set up an establishment together. "There are four delightful rooms in Queen's Square," he said. "Charming furniture, a fine sitting-room, two bedrooms, and a kitchen. The last tenant was a Polish Revolutionary who fled back to Russia. The landlord is an original old fellow, Deleglise the engraver. He occupies the rest of the house. He'll let us have the rooms for thirty shillings a week, provided we 'do for' ourselves. He hates petticoats, especially elderly ones. He has one servant, an old Frenchwoman who was his mother's housekeeper, and they quarrel over the housework. Nothing else domestic female is allowed inside. It's a beautiful Georgian house with Adams mantelpieces and oak paneling. No pianos, no landlady. He's a widower with a daughter of about fourteen. Will you be house-and-parlour-maid?"

I needed no pressing. A week later, we were installed. For nearly two years, we lived there. Without female assistance, we somehow survived, proving Dan's boast correct. His cooking was excellent—better plain cooking I never want to taste. But when stirred into dangerous ambition by occasional tea parties or the example of old Deleglise, Dan ventured into pastry. His hand was too heavy; his temperament too serious. Lemon sponge, described in cookbooks as an airy structure of dazzling whiteness, emerged from his kitchen as a miniature earthquake, yielding with a gentle tearing sound. His favorite, manna-cake, was substantial enough to satisfy anyone, even if it resembled the food of the Children of Israel, offering an excuse for their ancient discontent. For supper parties, he attempted soufflés that guests insisted were puddings, and pancakes. Initially, the tossing was perilous; he often caught half-baked things on his head or dropped them into the fire. But "Thorough" was always his motto. Small particles of coal or hairs could always be detected by the careful feeder and removed.

Dan was an even-tempered man for twenty-three hours a day. The other hour, however, he could be uncertain, irritable, and ungrateful. When I offered advice on pimply custards or mayonnaise resembling hair oil, I was met with sneers, insults, and spoiled tomatoes. His greatest enemy was the oven, which seemed determined to thwart his best schemes, turning snowy buns into red sandstone and macaroni cheese into Cambrian clay. The virulence of his reproaches almost made one take the oven's side.

Regarding our house-maid, I speak with unqualified praise. Unlike fussy maids who disturb the delicate domestic bloom of settled dust, I believed in letting sleeping dogs lie. Dust, crumbs, and matchsticks should remain under the nearest mat. To sweep them up is tiresome work that breeds bitterness. Compromise is true statesmanship. There will come a day when you are glad of an excuse to stop cleaning, then you can take up the mats and feel industrious, contemplating the work that *really* needs doing.

Differentiating between the essential and the non-essential is where women fail. Why wash a cup that will be dirty again in half an hour? If a bed looks made, it is made. If a cat cleans a plate, let her have the pleasure.

I never acquired skill in needlework. Dan handled a thimble well; my second finger was ever reluctant, finding itself pricked by the needle's head before it could push through. More practical was pushing the needle through with the aid of a door or table.

As Dan predicted, the opera ran far into the following year. When it ended, another production featuring "Goggles" as a principal took its place, even more successful. My salary rose to thirty-five, occasionally forty shillings a week. On six hundred pounds a week, I felt I could be content. But the O'Kelly set himself to dispel this dream.

"Ye'd be making a mistake, me boy," he explained. "Ye'd be wasting ye're time."

"It might be good enough for comic opera," I argued.

"Sure, there ye're right," agreed the O'Kelly. "But it would depend upon your acting."

I dreamed of becoming a second Kean, filling Drury Lane in the evening and writing epoch-making novels in the morning. Every day I studied in the British Museum reading-room. I continued French and German with Ollendorf. Perhaps diplomacy would appeal to me in old age. An ambassadorship! A pleasant termination to a brilliant career.

All things were going well. A story of mine had been accepted. Proofs had been sent, corrected, and returned. But proofs are not facts. This had happened before; the paper had collapsed before my story appeared. This time, I remembered the proverb and kept my counsel. I slipped out each morning to buy the paper, scanning eagerly for my name. For weeks, I suffered hope deferred.

Finally, one bright winter's day in January, walking down the Harrow Road, I stopped before a bill outside a news-vendor's shop. It was the first time I had seen my real name in print: "The Witch of Moel Sarbod: a legend of Mona, by Paul Kelver." My legs trembling, I entered the shop. A ruffianly-looking man found a copy on the floor underneath the counter. With it in my pocket, I retraced my footsteps as in a dream. Sitting on a seat in Paddington Green, I read it.

The story was a sad, sentimental composition about a mighty King who forgot his honor and fell in love with a wicked Sorceress, the Witch of Moel Sarbod. Only a beautiful Princess named Purity could save him. She must climb a rocky path to the Witch's dwelling and kiss her upon the mouth. In this way, the spirit of Purity would pass into the Witch's soul, transforming her into a woman, while the Witch's form and spirit would pass into Purity, condemning her to forever abide in the Cave of the Waters. Purity gave herself so the King might live. With bleeding feet, she climbed, clasped the Witch, and kissed her. The Witch became a wise queen; Purity became a hideous witch, sobbing above the roaring cataract.

No writer worth his salt is ever satisfied with his work, I was told. Candid friends assured me my first story was a feeble, ill-shaped child, its attempted early English a cross between "The Pilgrim's Progress" and "Old Moore's Almanac," its scenery an attempt to sum up the Garden of Eden combined with Dante's Inferno. Maybe they were right. Much have I written since that appeared good to me, which I later read with regret. But of this, my first-born, I am no judge. Touching the yellowing, badly-printed pages, I felt the deep thrill of joy with which I first unfolded them. Again, I was a youngster, and life opened out before me—inmeasurable, no goal too high. This child of my brain shall spread my name throughout the world. It shall be a household word in lands I shall never see. Friends whose voices I shall never hear will speak of me. I shall die, but it shall live, yield fresh seed, bear fruit I know not of. Generations yet unborn shall read it and remember me. My thoughts, my words, my spirit: in it I shall live again; it shall keep my memory green.

The long, long thoughts of boyhood! We elders smile at them. The little world spins round; the little voices of an hour sink hushed. The crawling generations come and go. The solar system drops from space. The eternal mechanism reforms and shapes itself anew. Time, turning, ploughs another furrow. So, growing sleepy, we murmur with a yawn. Is it that we see clearer, or that our eyes are growing dim? Let the young men see their visions, dream their dreams, hug to themselves their hopes of enduring fame; so shall they serve the world better.

I brushed the tears from my eyes and looked up. Half-a-dozen urchins, male and female, were gaping at me open-mouthed. They scattered shouting, probably insulting me. I flung them a handful of coppers, which silenced them for the moment, and went on my way. How bright, how fair the bustling streets, golden in the winter sunshine, thronged with life and effort! Laughter rang around me. Sweet music rolled from barrel-organs. The strenuous voices of costermongers called invitation to the fruitful earth. Errand boys passed whistling shrilly joyous melodies. Perspiring tradesmen shouted generous offers to the needy. Men and women hurried by with smiling faces. Sleek cats purred in sheltered nooks, till merry dogs invited them to sport. Sparrows, feasting in the roadway, chirped their hymn of praise.

At the Marble Arch, I jumped upon a 'bus. I mentioned to the conductor that it was a fine day. He replied that he had noticed it himself. The retort struck me as a brilliant repartee. Our coachman, almost run into by a hansom cab driven by a surly old fellow of patriarchal appearance, remarked upon the danger of allowing horses out in charge of bits of boys. How full the world was of wit and humor!
