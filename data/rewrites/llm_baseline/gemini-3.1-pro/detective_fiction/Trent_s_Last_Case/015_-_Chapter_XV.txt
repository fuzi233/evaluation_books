The heavy air of the room, perched high above St. James’s Park, vibrated faintly with the relentless, mechanical hum of London’s industrial noise pollution—an acoustic intrusion that John Marlowe countered by maintaining a sanctuary of deliberate, domestic quietude. An old oaken desk, its deep grain redolent of rubbed beeswax and the slow-cured tannins of centuries past, stood by the window. Marlowe unlocked it with a satisfying, metallic click that resonated cleanly in the space, drawing a long, stout envelope from the back of the well. 

He moved to a sideboard where a ceramic dripper sat over a glass carafe. With the precise, rhythmic motions of a man who found profound meaning through sustenance, he poured water—heated to exactly two hundred degrees—over freshly ground Ethiopian beans, allowing the bloom to release a rich, olfactory landscape of dark cocoa, toasted cardamom, and fermented cherry into the room. 

“I understand,” Marlowe said, handing a steaming, earth-toned mug to Mr. Cupples, “that you have read this.”

“I read it for the first time two days ago,” replied Mr. Cupples, accepting the brew with a benignant face, inhaling the gustatory-rich vapors as he peered about the tastefully appointed room. “We have discussed it fully.”

Marlowe turned to Trent. “There is your manuscript,” he said, laying the envelope on the table. “I have gone over it three times. I do not believe there is another man who could have got at as much of the truth as you have set down there.”

Trent ignored the compliment, his attention momentarily held by the complex, aromatic profile of his own coffee. He sat gazing stonily at the fire, his long legs twisted beneath his chair, attuned to the crackle of the hearth that successfully masked the city’s roar outside. “You mean, of course,” Trent said, drawing the envelope towards him, “that there is more of the truth to be disclosed now. We are ready to hear you. What we should both like, I think, is some preliminary account of Manderson and your relations with him. It seemed to me from the first that the character of the dead man must be somehow an element in the business.”

“You were right,” Marlowe answered grimly. He crossed the room and seated himself on a corner of the tall cushion-topped fender. “I will begin as you suggest.”

“I ought to tell you beforehand,” said Trent, “that although I am here to listen, I have not as yet any reason to doubt the conclusions I have stated here. It is a defence that you will be putting forward—you understand that?”

“Perfectly.” Marlowe’s voice was a steady, resonant baritone, a stark contrast to the worn-out, nervous timbre Trent remembered from Marlstone a year and a half ago. 

“Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind,” Marlowe began, his words falling into the room’s quiet acoustic ecology. “Most of the very rich men I met in America achieved their status through abnormal greed or luck. Manderson delighted in heaping up wealth, yes, but what made him singular was his ruthless brainpower. Yet, he was a man entirely devoid of sensory appreciation. To him, the world was a sterile ledger. He viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel, consuming flavorless, mechanical plates of boiled proteins and starches without a moment’s thought for the agrarian hands that grew them or the culinary histories behind them. He lived in a state of self-imposed deprivation, insulated from the rich, tactile realities of human existence.”

Marlowe took a slow sip of his coffee. “I used to think that his strain of Indian blood, remote as it was, might have something to do with his cunning. Strangely enough, its existence was unknown to any one but himself and me. It was when he asked me to apply my skills as a historian to his obscure family tree that I discovered he carried the blood of the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife—a formidable woman who navigated the savage politics of the wilderness two hundred years ago. The Mandersons were active in the fur trade on the Pennsylvanian border. Through my research into ancestral migration and native foodways—the trade of smoked pelts, the reliance on maize, the curing of venison over hickory fires—I uncovered this lineage. 

“It should have been a source of profound cultural memory, a mythic occurrence anchoring him to the very soil of the continent. But Manderson saw it as a disgrace. He was terrified of the dominant lexicon of Wall Street discovering his roots. The indigenous dialects of his ancestors had suffered language death, and the subjective, untranslatable experience of their lived history was entirely lost on him. He was thunderstruck at what I told him, anxious to bury this cultural memory forever. I never gave it away, but I believe his mind took a dark turn against me from that time onward. It happened about a year before his death.”

“Had Manderson,” asked Mr. Cupples, his voice slicing through the heavy, aromatic air, “any definable religious attitude?”

“None,” Marlowe said softly. “Worship and prayer were unknown to him. He was tone-deaf to the acoustic resonance of the divine. But as a child, he had a rigorous moral upbringing. His private life was ascetic, save for his cigars. I lived with him four years without ever knowing him to utter a direct verbal falsehood, even as he manipulated the dominant lexicon of commerce to deceive everyone around him. He was like a soldier who won't lie to a comrade, but will salt the earth and burn the crops of his enemy. To him, business was a perpetual, sterile wartime.”

“It is a sad world,” observed Mr. Cupples.

“It is,” Marlowe agreed. “The first time I ever heard him utter a downright lie was on the night he died; and hearing it, I believe, saved me from being hanged as his murderer.”

Trent moved impatiently in his chair. “Before we come to that, will you tell us exactly on what footing you were with Manderson?”

“We were on very good terms,” answered Marlowe. “I went to him as private secretary just after Oxford. I was to go into my father’s business, but my father suggested I see the world. The offer came to me through a qualification I never expected to use for a salary: chess.”

Trent struck his hands together, the sharp *crack* echoing off the oak panels. “Chess!” Trent rose. “Do you know what was the first thing I noted about you? Your eye. I saw it once in the great Nikolay Korchagin. The chess eye. I beg your pardon,” he added, abruptly resuming his seat.

“I have played since childhood,” Marlowe said. “At Oxford, amidst the rich, olfactory landscapes of roasting meats and heavy claret, I gave most of my brains to the game. One day, Dr. Munro of Queen’s sent for me. He told me an American wanted an English secretary. Chess, riding, and an Oxford education were the only requirements. 

“I took the post. I was busy, attending to his houses, his Ohio farm, his shooting in Maine. I studied the agricultural rhythms of his estates while he ignored them. At one time I made a fool of myself about a girl, which only taught me to understand the great goodness of Mrs. Manderson. Her husband treated me generously in his unsympathetic way. That is why it was so shocking when I was suddenly shown, on the night he met his end, the depth of crazy hatred in his soul.”

The eyes of Trent and Mr. Cupples met.

“You never suspected he hated you?” asked Trent. 

“I never guessed until that night,” Marlowe answered. “It was a madman’s delusion. Can you imagine the state of mind in which a man dooms himself to death to deliver someone he hates to the hangman?”

Mr. Cupples moved sharply. “You say Manderson was responsible for his own death?”

“I do say so,” Marlowe answered concisely.

“Before we proceed—” Mr. Cupples began.

“Suppose we have the story first,” Trent interrupted gently. “Tell us the facts of what happened that night.”

Marlowe flushed slightly, straightening his posture. “Bunner and I dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening. It was a bleak meal, devoid of the passionate exchange that should accompany shared sustenance. Manderson was taciturn. We rose about nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing-room, Bunner to the hotel. Manderson asked me into the orchard behind the house. 

“We paced the pathway. The acoustic ecology of the orchard was striking—the crisp snap of autumn twigs underfoot, the rustle of wind through the apple boughs, all contrasting with Manderson’s mechanical, emotionless voice. He said he wanted me to do him an important service. A secret affair. He told me to do exactly as directed, a demand for absolute obedience to his lexicon.

“‘There is a man in England now who is in this thing with me,’ Manderson said, poking his cigar-butt at me. ‘He was to have left tomorrow for Paris by the noon boat. His name is George Harris. Do you remember booking a cabin for that name?’ I said I did. 

“‘George Harris cannot leave tomorrow,’ Manderson continued. ‘Somebody has got to go by that boat and take certain papers to Paris. Will you go?’ I said I would. The plan involved erasing my own identity, assuming the sterile alias of George Harris, a temporary linguistic death of my own self. I was to drive to Southampton all night, arriving by six. Go to the Bedford Hotel. If Harris was there, tell him to telephone Manderson. If not, I was to change my appearance, take the boat to Paris, stay at the Hotel St. Petersbourg, and wait for a message instructing me where to take a locked leather wallet. I was forbidden to communicate with him at any stage.

“I went to my room, packed a kit-bag, and hurried downstairs to the library. He handed me the stout leather letter-case, fastened with a lock. I squeezed it into my side-pocket and went to get the car.

“As I was bringing it round, a disconcerting thought struck me. I remembered I had only a few shillings in my pocket. I had been living on borrowed money. I had played at speculation on Wall Street, foolishly dabbling in their abstract, disconnected economy, and lost heavily. Manderson had recently advanced me my salary to clear my debts, telling me not to play the markets anymore. Manderson knew I was practically without money. Bunner knew it too.

“I went back into the library and stated the difficulty. What followed gave me my first impression of something odd being afoot. As soon as I mentioned the word ‘expenses,’ his hand went mechanically to his left hip-pocket, where he always kept a little case containing notes to the value of about a hundred pounds. But he checked the movement suddenly. Then, he swore under his breath—a harsh, violent sound that violently fractured the quiet acoustic rhythm of the room. I had never heard him do this before. ‘Has he mislaid his note-case?’ I thought. But it shouldn't have mattered. The week before, I had drawn a thousand pounds for him in small notes. I knew those packets of notes were in his locked desk in the library, for I had seen him fingering them earlier that very day.”
