It was no safe matter in those days to travel from Plymouth northward through Devon. Unless one wished to circle many miles, the road led straight through the domain of a foreign potentate, a man who had ravaged the realm and defeated the forces of Queen Elizabeth. Behind his back, at least, he was known as the King of the Gubbings. As Fuller noted, they were secured by distance, for those of more valor dared not face them openly for fear of their fury. The name itself was a mark of shame; "gubbings" were the shavings of fish, worthless and despised, and the inhabitants of this Scythia within England seemed to sense the disgrace attached to their title. They lived in cots that were more holes than houses, multiplying without marriage, their language a dross of vulgar Devonian that the more learned found hardest to understand. They were a peculiar breed, exempt from all authority, living by theft and outrunning horses, holding together like bees to revenge any wrong done to one of their own. Yet, rumors circulated that they were beginning to be civilized, offering their children for baptism.

Amyas Leigh, clad in his full suit of armor and armed with lance, sword, and two great dags, rode out of Plymouth with Salvation Yeo and a handful of north Devon men returning from Ireland. They pushed on as fast as they could, aiming to reach Lydford before nightfall, but delays with their horses meant they arrived at the frontier just as the sun dipped below the wild heath. The landscape was a dreary tableland banked by the crags of Dartmoor and sloping toward the great cone of Brent-Tor, an extinct volcano crowned by a tiny church vowed by a distressed merchant. From the waste slopes, tiny threads of blue smoke rose from the dens of the Gubbings, and the travelers halted frequently, mistaking distant furze-bushes and ponies for the patrols of an advancing army.

Their destination was a wayside inn, a desolate lump of lichen-spotted granite with paper-patched windows and rotting thatch. At the door stood the innkeeper, a brawny, sodden-visaged brute holding up his hose for lack of points, his head adorned with feathers that testified to a night spent sheep-stealing. He spotted Amyas's party, counted them, and seeing the armor, let out a sleepy howl. His wife, Mary, a strapping lass in a green bodice and red petticoat, emerged with a fishing rod, complaining that the "dun fly" had been eaten. The host, enraged that his catch was gone, struck her, and she returned the blow with good effect before he shuffled off to summon his accomplice, Patrico.

Inside, the inn was a "mucksy sort of a place," but the travelers preferred it to bivouacking near the enemy. The host's missing catch was roasted alongside "black Dartmoor mutton," which, under the pressure of the gridiron, confessed itself to be red-deer venison. Amyas secured his horses in a shed lacking lock or key, while Yeo posted himself at the door. Soon, Yeo returned in high excitement, reporting that a gentleman had entered the back way. When Amyas asked him to invite the stranger, Yeo explained that the man had slipped away, only to return moments later.

"He's gone in back ways, by the court," Yeo reported.
"Well, he has an odd taste," Amyas replied.

Yeo returned once more, breathless. "Come out, sir! I've got him. Safe as a rat in a trap!"
"Who?"
"A Jesuit, sir."
"Nonsense, man!"
"I tell you truth, sir. I saw him turn in his toes, put down his feet so carefully as if afraid of offending God at every step. He called 'Mary!' quite still, and she flew out ready to eat him. He gave her a queer cuffin'—that's a justice in these canters' thieves' Latin—and she kissed it, crossed herself, and put it in her bosom. Then he slipped to the stable, peeped in, and went in when he saw no one. I shut the door and backed a cart against it."

"Fool's trick, man! How do you know he is not some honest gentleman?"
"Honest gentlemen don't give maidens Agnuses," Yeo insisted. "I've put him in. If you want him let out, you must do it yourself, for my conscience is against it. 'If any let one of them go, his life shall be for the life of him.'"

Amyas hurried out, apologizing to his cousin Eustace, who sat disconsolately in the stable. Eustace, with a countenance half foolish, half venomous, looked like a fox trapped by terriers. After a tense exchange where Eustace claimed he was bound to know no prudence save for the Lord's work, and Amyas accused him of hunting down Rose Salterne as an outlaw, the two cousins found common ground over supper. They spoke guardedly at first, but Amyas's straightforward kindness thawed Eustace's frozen heart, and they drifted into talk of old haunts and boyhood memories.

Yet, beneath the surface, the tension remained. Eustace asked why Amyas was going to Bideford while Frank and his mother were in London. Amyas admitted he could not rest until he heard the whole story about Rose Salterne. Eustace shrieked, realizing he had betrayed himself, and paced the room in despair, clutching at empty air. He knew of the Brotherhood of the Rose, and when Amyas pressed him about meeting a Spaniard at Lundy, Eustace refused to answer, warning that Amyas was within a few miles of those who would obey him.

Amyas, though angry, restrained himself, knowing that capturing Eustace would bring certain death upon him. He dismissed his cousin, telling him to go to the devil, and turned his attention to the men. They settled for the night, but at midnight, Amyas was awakened by the sound of flying dragons—terror-stricken geese—and the roar of a fight in the courtyard.

Yeo was holding his own against a dozen men, sword and buckler in hand, while the entire household screamed in chaos. The ruffians fled over a low hedge, leaving two wounded behind. One was a huge man pierced through the body; the other lay dead in the straw. Yeo's story revealed that he had expected a night-attack on the horses, suspecting Eustace's involvement, and had taken lodging in the stable. The attackers had been led by the innkeeper, who had slipped away to call neighboring outlaws to the spoil.

Before they could secure the scene, a fresh hubbub arose from the kitchen. Mr. Evan Morgans, alias Father Parsons, stood foaming with rage, captured by sturdy sailors, with Eustace Leigh standing behind him. Parsons claimed he was preparing his "wounded lamb" for the account to which Yeo's cruelty had sent him. The dying man, Ballard, the leader of the Gubbings, lay on the floor, moaning for Patrico. Parsons, kneeling beside him, listened to the thief's gibberish confession, while Eustace interpreted the thieves' dialect into Latin.

"It's a false peace," Yeo protested, watching the soul perish before his eyes.
"At all events, he is confessing his sins," Amyas argued, holding Yeo back. "If that's not good for him, what is?"

But the dark catalogue of brutal deeds had been gasped out, and ere the words of absolution could follow, the head fell back, and all was over. Parsons declared that confession in extremis was sufficient, while Yeo insisted the man's soul was lost. Amyas, furious, ordered Parsons to accompany them, threatening to shoot him if he delayed their journey. Reluctantly, Parsons agreed, and Eustace volunteered to carry the message to the remaining Gubbings.

They marched through the mire toward Okehampton, with Parsons tied to Amyas's saddle. Yeo walked alongside, mocking the Jesuit with tales of bogs and martyrdom, his laughter echoing under the moon as they approached Lydford Castle. Once across the threshold, Yeo and Simon Evans stopped, claiming they had saved the Jesuit's fine clothes. They had hove the man over the bank into a furze-bush, leaving him to his fate, for a Jesuit, Yeo coolly remarked, had as many lives as a cat.

Leaving Lydford and its ill-omened castle behind, Amyas and his party trudged on toward Okehampton. The voice of the Torridge stream recalled the nymph's words to him, and his mind wandered to Rose Salterne. He had never seen her since that day six years prior, and the chain of petty accidents that kept them apart seemed cruel. He wondered if his old oath meant anything more than a tavern frolic, but as he looked at it, he felt it was a sacred duty. He had nothing but his sword, yet he vowed to find the man who had ruined her, for if there was no law on earth, there was law in heaven.

He rode into the ugly, dirty town of Okehampton, detained by Justice Shallow, who refused to take depositions without a brother justice present. The wrangling nearly exposed the presence of Jesuits in the Moorland Utopia, but Shallow, citing the proverb "leave ill alone," dismissed the matter. Amyas continued his weary journey until he saw the broad shining river and the white houses of Bideford.

When he arrived at Sir Richard's door, he found the knight still in Ireland and Lady Grenville at Stow. Disappointed, he returned to the Ship Tavern, where the host chattered about the emptiness of the town and the strange doings among the ladies. Amyas, disturbed, asked about the old man, Mr. Salterne. The host described him as a changed man, sitting up all night in Rose's room, turning over her garments, and refusing to drink.

Amyas went to see Salterne himself. The old merchant opened the door with stern courtesy, welcoming Amyas into his lonely house. He led him to the parlor, where he ordered the apprentices to bring food and wine, his sudden hilarity contrasting sharply with the host's earlier description. He fed Amyas like a fighting-cock, boasting of his own past voyages and his ambition to have married his daughter to an earl.

Then, Salterne led Amyas upstairs to a room untouched since Rose's departure. The bed was made, the books arranged, the dressing-table set with pins and rings, and the dolls lined up in a cupboard. Salterne opened a chest filled with necklaces, jewels, furs, and cloth of gold, treasures gathered over twenty years for her wedding day.

"And whom do you think I kept all these for?" Salterne whispered, his voice trembling. "For her wedding-day. For your wedding-day, if you'd been minded, sir! Yes, yours, sir! And yet, I believe, I was so ambitious that I would not have let her marry under an earl... Ah, well! There was my idol, sir. I made her mad, I pampered her up with gewgaws and vanity; and then, because my idol was just what I had made her, I turned again and rent her."

He pointed to the open chest and the empty bed. "That was what I meant; and that was what God meant. Never mind. Come downstairs and finish your wine. I see you don't care about it all. Why should you! you are not her father, and you may thank God you are not. Go, and be merry while you can, young sir! . . . And yet, all this might have been yours. And--but I don't suppose you are one to be won by money--but all this may be yours still, and twenty thousand pounds to boot."

"I want no money, sir, but what I can earn with my own sword."
"Earn my money, then!" Salterne demanded, clutching Amyas's arm. "To keep your oath."
"My oath! How did you know that I had one?"
"Ah! you were well ashamed of it, I suppose, next day! A drunken frolic all about a poor merchant's daughter! But there is nothing hidden that shall not be revealed."

Salterne revealed that he had brought John Brimblecombe to him, showing him the room and the treasure, and that the clown had wept and prayed until Salterne fell to his knees. "Bring me home that man's head, and take ship, prizes--all! Keep the gain, sir, and give me the revenge!"

Amyas was silent, the weight of his mother's permission pressing on him. "I dare not go without her leave," he said.
"I dare not, sir; I must obey my parent, whatever else I do," Salterne replied, his voice heavy with sorrow.

Amyas departed to the inn, where he found Cary waiting for him, ready to share details that would be told in another chapter. For now, the night deepened, and the path ahead remained uncertain, but the vow was sealed.
