The quickest route from my lady's sitting-room to the garden lay through the shrubbery path, a thoroughfare familiar to anyone who knows the estate. For clarity's sake, I must mention that this was Mr. Franklin's favorite promenade. Whenever he wandered the grounds and we could not locate him elsewhere, he was invariably found strolling here.

I confess, I am somewhat of an obstinate old man. The more resolutely Sergeant Cuff endeavored to keep his thoughts hidden from me, the more determinedly I pressed against the barrier, striving to glimpse what he concealed. As we turned onto the shrubbery path, I attempted another maneuver to bypass his caution. "Given the current state of affairs," I remarked, "if I were in your shoes, I should be utterly at my wits' end."

"If you were in my position," replied the Sergeant, "you would have already formed an opinion. Furthermore, whatever doubts you harbored regarding your own deductions would now be entirely dispelled. Let us not dwell on those conclusions for the moment, Mr. Betteredge. I did not bring you out here to harry me like a badger; I brought you to request certain information. You could certainly have provided it within the house, yet doors and eavesdroppers have a peculiar tendency to congregate. Moreover, in my profession, we cultivate a distinct appreciation for the open air."

Who, indeed, could withstand *this* man? Reluctantly, I surrendered and waited with as much patience as possible for his next move.

"We need not speculate on your young lady's motives," the Sergeant continued. "It is merely a pity she refuses to assist me, for her reluctance complicates this inquiry unnecessarily. We must now attempt to unravel the mystery of the smear on the door—which, trust me, signifies the mystery of the Diamond itself—by alternative means. I have resolved to interrogate the servants, examining their thoughts and actions, Mr. Betteredge, rather than rummaging through their wardrobes. Before I commence, however, I require answers to a few questions. You are an observant man; did you detect anything unusual among the staff following the discovery of the Diamond's loss? Allow for the natural effects of fright and agitation, of course. Was there any specific discord? Did anyone appear out of sorts, unusually irritable, or inexplicably ill?"

I barely had time to recall Rosanna Spearman's sudden illness at yesterday's dinner before I realized I could not formulate an answer. At that precise moment, I observed Sergeant Cuff's gaze shift abruptly toward the shrubbery. Softly, to himself, he muttered, "Hullo!"

"What is the matter?" I asked.

"A slight twinge of rheumatism in my back," the Sergeant announced loudly, seemingly inviting a third party to overhear. "We shall likely witness a change in the weather soon."

Proceeding a few steps further, we reached the corner of the house. Turning sharply to the right, we ascended the terrace and descended via the central steps into the garden below. Stopping in the open space, where we could survey the surroundings on all sides, the Sergeant addressed me.

"Regarding that young person, Rosanna Spearman?" he inquired. "Considering her appearance, it is unlikely she possesses a suitor. Nevertheless, for her own sake, I must ask immediately: has she, poor wretch, secured a sweetheart like the others?"

Good heavens, what did he imply by such a query under the present circumstances? I stared at him, unable to speak.

"I witnessed Rosanna Spearman concealing herself in the shrubbery as we passed," the Sergeant stated. "Was this when you uttered 'Hullo'?"

"Yes—precisely when I shouted 'Hullo!' If a sweetheart were involved, her hiding would be irrelevant. But given the atmosphere in this household, if no suitor exists, her concealment constitutes a highly suspicious circumstance. It will be my painful duty to act upon it accordingly."

What, in God's name, was I to reply? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin's preferred walk; I knew he would almost certainly return that way upon arriving from the station; and I knew that Penelope had repeatedly caught her fellow-servant lingering there, declaring to me that Rosanna's intent was to attract Mr. Franklin's attention. If Penelope was correct, the girl might well have been waiting for Mr. Franklin's return when the Sergeant spotted her. Caught between the dilemma of voicing Penelope's speculation as my own or allowing an unfortunate creature to suffer grave consequences by arousing the Sergeant's suspicion, I acted purely out of pity. On my soul and reputation, driven solely by compassion for the girl, I supplied the necessary explanations, informing the Sergeant that Rosanna had been foolish enough to set her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff did not laugh. On the rare occasions when amusement struck him, he merely curled the corners of his lips upward. This time, he did so.

"Would it not be wiser to suggest she is mad enough to be an ugly girl and merely a servant?" he asked. "Falling in love with a gentleman possessing Mr. Franklin Blake's manners and appearance does not strike me as the most insane aspect of her behavior. However, I am glad the matter is clarified; it brings a sense of relief to have uncertainties resolved. Yes, I shall keep this confidential, Mr. Betteredge. I possess a fondness for tenderness toward human frailty, though opportunities to exercise this virtue are scarce in my line of work. Do you truly believe Mr. Franklin Blake remains unaware of the girl's affection? Ah! He would have discovered it swiftly had she been beautiful. Ugly women endure a harsh existence in this world; let us hope it is compensated for in the next. You maintain a lovely garden here, with a meticulously kept lawn. Observe how superior the flowers appear amidst grass rather than gravel. No, thank you. I shall not accept a rose; it breaks my heart to sever them from the stem. Just as it breaks yours, I imagine, when trouble brews in the servants' hall. Did you notice anything inexplicable regarding any of the servants when the loss of the Diamond was first discovered?"

Our rapport with Sergeant Cuff had progressed reasonably well until that final, insidious question placed me on guard. Plainly put, I disliked the prospect of aiding his investigations when they directed him, like a snake in the grass, among my fellow-servants.

"I noticed nothing," I replied, "save that we collectively lost our composure, myself included."

"Oh," the Sergeant mused, "that is all you have to report?"

"That is all," I answered, maintaining what I hoped was an impassive expression.

Sergeant Cuff's somber eyes fixed upon me intently. "Mr. Betteredge," he said, "do you object to oblige me by shaking hands? I have developed an extraordinary affinity for you." (One wonders why he chose the exact moment when I was deceiving him to offer such proof of his esteem! I felt a surge of pride—I genuinely felt proud of having bested the celebrated Cuff!)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant requested a private room, instructing me to summon the indoor servants sequentially, ranked from highest to lowest. I escorted Sergeant Cuff into my study and then gathered the servants in the hall. Rosanna Spearman appeared among them, appearing as usual. She moved with the same swiftness as the Sergeant, and I suspected she had overheard his remarks concerning the servants prior to his discovery of her. Yet, there she stood, feigning ignorance of the shrubbery's existence.

I admitted them one by one. The cook entered first, effectively stepping into the Court of Justice, which was my study. Her visit was brief. Upon exiting, her report was: *"Sergeant Cuff is depressed in spirit, yet he is a perfect gentleman."*

My lady's maid followed. She lingered considerably longer. Her exit report read: *"If Sergeant Cuff cannot believe a respectable woman, he should keep his opinions to himself, at least!"*

Penelope entered next. She stayed only a moment or two. Her verdict: *"Sergeant Cuff deserves pity. He must have been jilted in love when he was a young man."*

The first housemaid followed Penelope. Like my lady's maid, she remained for an extended period. Her departure announcement was fiery: *"I did not enter my lady's service, Mr. Betteredge, to be doubted in my face by a low police officer!"*

Rosanna Spearman entered subsequently. She stayed longer than any of the others. There was no report upon her exit—only dead silence and lips pale as ash.

Samuel, the footman, followed Rosanna. He remained for a minute or two. His comment: *"Whoever polishes Sergeant Cuff's boots ought to be ashamed of themselves."*

Nancy, the kitchen-maid, was last. She stayed briefly. Her observation: *"Sergeant Cuff has a heart; he does not cut jokes with a poor, hardworking girl, Mr. Betteredge."*

Upon entering the Court of Justice to await further instructions, I found the Sergeant engaged in his customary pastime: gazing out the window while whistling "The Last Rose of Summer."

"Any discoveries, sir?" I inquired.

"If Rosanna Spearman requests permission to leave," the Sergeant said, "grant it to the poor soul, but inform me beforehand."
