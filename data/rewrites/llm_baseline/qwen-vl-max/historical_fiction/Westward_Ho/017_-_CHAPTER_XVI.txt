“He is brass within, and steel without,
With beams on his topcastle strong;
And eighteen pieces of ordinance
He carries on either side along.”

—Sir Andrew Barton.

Let us follow Amyas’s path and take a boat at Whitehall stairs, slipping quietly beneath the ancient arches of London Bridge, then drifting down to Deptford Creek. There, preserved as if in time’s own vault, rests the famed ship *Pelican*, the very vessel in which Sir Francis Drake had sailed around the world. She lies high and dry upon the muddy banks of the Thames, like a veteran warrior laid to rest after years of storm and glory. Upon her mainmast are nailed verses and epigrams in honor of both her and her captain—three of them recorded by Camden, the Winchester scholar, in his history. Queen Elizabeth herself once blessed the ship with solemn ceremony, feasting aboard her decks before knighting Drake there and then. “At that moment,” Camden writes, “a plank bridge broke under the crush of people, sending a hundred men plunging into the river below—but not one was harmed. So it seems this ship was built under a lucky star.”

Since that day, the *Pelican* has stood as a monument, a curiosity, and a place of revelry for noble gatherings from the city. Today, she is alive with festivity: flags flutter from her masts, laughter echoes through her old hull, and savory aromas drift from windows that once were portholes. Waiters in white aprons dart between the riverbank and the ship, crossing the same “lucky bridge” that once collapsed under the weight of royal celebration. A grand feast is clearly underway—judging by the city badges worn by the serving men, the presence of civic dignitaries, and the unmistakable air of importance that clings to every guest.

The occasion? The Lord Mayor hosts a dinner for gentlemen connected to the Leicester household, all deeply engaged in dreams of foreign discovery. What better setting than the *Pelican* herself?

Look about you—the company is no ordinary gathering. Few nobler assemblies have ever met beneath a single roof. And if you are an American, look closely at these faces. In their eyes and brows, you see the fathers of your nation—men whose courage, vision, and daring laid the foundation for the lands you now call home.

At the head of the table sits Sir Edward Osborne, Lord Mayor of London—a man whose name still rings in history. He is clothed in the dignity of age and wealth, yet his spirit remains unbroken. Once, he leapt from a window on London Bridge into the roaring tide to save a child—now that child is his wife. That youthful act of chivalry has ripened into the steady resolve of a merchant-adventurer. He sits regally, flanked by the Earl of Cumberland on his right and Sir Walter Raleigh on his left. Together, they speak in low voices of undiscovered lands—vast territories rumored to lie between Florida and the River of Canada. Raleigh, ever the dreamer, cites Doctor Dee, the mystic philosopher, but Osborne listens with quiet skepticism. It is only when he turns to Richard Hakluyt, the Oxford scholar seated beside him, that Raleigh finds a listener who shares his passion. But Hakluyt is deep in conversation with Anthony Jenkinson, the great traveler of Asia, whose beard flows like snow and whose face bears the marks of countless storms. Jenkinson speaks of reindeer sledges across Siberia, of Arctic ice cliffs where Tungoos unearth fossil ivory—proof, he says, of Noah’s flood.

Beside him sits Christopher Carlile, son-in-law to Walsingham and soon to command troops in Drake’s 1585 West Indian campaign. He talks now with Alderman Hart, Sheriff Spencer, Charles Leigh (Amyas’s cousin), Aldworth of Bristol, and William Salterne—another kinsman of Amyas’s friend from Bideford. For two years, Carlile and Walsingham have worked tirelessly to raise funds for Humphrey and Adrian Gilbert’s voyages to the North-West. Carlile himself was meant to sail—but did not. Why? No one knows.

On the opposite side of the table, another circle of minds gathers. Martin Frobisher and John Davis, explorers of the Arctic passage, debate with Alderman Sanderson, the renowned geographer and globe-maker. With them are Mr. Towerson, Sir Gilbert Peckham, Captain John Winter, and lastly, Philip Sidney—whose courteous nature has caused him to give up his rightful seat near the head so he might sit among scholars and adventurers. He brings with him his closest friends: Edward Dyer and Francis Leigh. They too discuss the North-West Passage. Sidney laments his own constraints—bound to court and diplomacy—while envying Frobisher’s life at sea.

“'Tis fine talk here,” Frobisher replies, “sailing on dry land with a glass of wine. But try it among icebergs, your beard frozen to your ruff—especially if you’re faint of stomach.”

“That would be a small trial,” Sidney answers, “if it earned me the honor you received—when the queen herself waved farewell from Greenwich Palace with her ivory hand.”

“Well,” says Frobisher, “they say you’ve no reason to complain of favors. If you can win them ashore, why go to sea?”

Towerson nods gravely, his gray beard having seen many a voyage.

Then a waiter enters.

“My lord mayor, a tall gentleman outside requests to speak with Sir Walter Raleigh.”

“Show him in,” says Osborne. “Sir Walter’s friends are ours.”

Amyas steps into the cabin, hesitating at the door.

“Captain Leigh!” cry several voices.

“Why didn’t you come in?” asks Osborne. “You know your way well enough on these decks.”

“I do, my lords,” Amyas replies. “But Sir Walter—excuse me.” His glance speaks volumes. Raleigh, pale as death, rises and follows him into a private cabin. Five minutes pass. Then Amyas emerges alone.

In brief words, he tells the company what we already know—the tragic news of Sir Humphrey Gilbert’s death. As the tale unfolds, tears glisten on some of the sternest faces.

“The ancient Egyptians,” says Osborne, “placed a corpse at their banquets to remind them of mortality. Have we forgotten God and our frailty in this feast? Has He sent us this message from beyond the grave?”

“No, my lord,” says Sidney. “Not from the dead—but from eternal life.”

“Amen,” Osborne replies. “But our feast ends here. Some may drink on bravely despite personal loss—but none can celebrate while the memory of such a man hangs heavy in the air.”

Indeed. Though many had lost money in the failed expedition, none could forget the shock of Gilbert’s passing. The gathering breaks up quickly, each man whispering to his neighbor, “What will the queen say?”

Raleigh returns shortly, silent, shaking hands with those he passes. He beckons Amyas to follow him to a wherry. Sidney, Cumberland, and Frank join them in another boat. The two brothers travel together, talking in hushed tones.

They disembark at Whitehall stairs. Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland go to the palace. Amyas and Frank walk to their mother’s lodgings.

Amyas had prepared a speech for Frank about Rose Salterne—but now, standing before him, he lacks the courage to begin. He hopes Frank will speak first. But Frank, too, dreads what must come—especially since he knows nothing of Amyas’s journey to Bideford or the fate of the *Rose*.

Upstairs, they find their mother away at the Abbey—relief for both. They stand at the bay window, looking out over the river, speaking of trivial things, studying each other’s faces in the fading light. Three years have passed since they last met.

Time has changed them both. Frank looks up at Amyas with affectionate pride. The rollicking sailor-boy is gone. In his place stands a confident, stately warrior—every gesture marked by strength and purpose:

“The reason firm, the temperate will,
Endurance, foresight, strength, and skill.”

Trained by Drake and Grenville, refined by Raleigh and Gilbert, Amyas is a man forged in fire. His long hair is now cropped short. A rich golden beard frames his lips and chin. His face is weathered by sun and storm. A scar crosses his temple—a trophy from an Irish battle. His broad frame has grown even broader. His hand, resting on the sill, is hard and massive like a blacksmith’s. Frank places his own hand upon it—and sighs. Amyas looks down, startled by the contrast: Frank’s fingers are slender, pale, almost translucent—those of a courtier, not a sailor.

Amyas gazes into his brother’s face. It has changed. The flush remains, but the skin is dull, the lips pale, the features sharper. His eyes burn with unnatural fire. When Frank says Amyas looks older, Amyas cannot help thinking the same applies to Frank.

Trying to ignore the truth, he begins asking names of buildings along the river.

“And so this is old Father Thames, with his bank of palaces?”

“Yes,” Frank replies. “His banks are grand—but he does not linger. He rushes toward the sea, the sea into the ocean, and the ocean westward—forever. All things move westward. Perhaps we shall too, Amyas.”

“What do you mean?”

“Only that the ocean mirrors the heavens’ motion—flowing east to west. Is it strange I should think of it, just after drinking to ‘Westward-ho’?”

“And much good came of it! I lost my best friend, the noblest captain, and all my savings—all in that cursed gulf of Westward-ho.”

“Sir Humphrey Gilbert’s star set in the west—why not? Sun, moon, planets all sink westward. Why not the sparks of this world? Why not a will-o’-the-wisp like me, Amyas?”

“God forbid!”

“Why? Is not the west the land of peace, of dreams? Does not every heart leap at the setting sun, longing to float with him on golden clouds? Men are buried facing east. I’d rather face west, Amyas. For I believe the ancients guessed rightly—that Elysium lies beneath the sunset. It is written in the soul of man: the longing for the west. I blame no one who flees thither beyond the sea, as David wished to flee—to be at peace.”

“You blame no one?” Amyas asks.

“No. If I blamed anyone, it would be you—for seeming tired of going Westward-ho.”

“Do you wish me to go?”

“God knows,” Frank says after a pause. “But I must tell you now—once and for all. Something has happened at Bideford…”

“Spare us both, Frank—I know all. I passed through Bideford on my way here. I came not just to see you and Mother—but to ask your advice, and hers.”

“True heart! Noble heart!” Frank exclaims. “I knew you would be loyal!”

“So—Westward-ho it is?”

“Can we escape?”

“We?”

“Amyas—what binds you binds me.”

Amyas steps back, gripping Frank’s shoulders. Through the thin fabric, he feels the bones beneath—skin and bone.

“You? Dearest man—this voyage would kill you in a month!”

Frank smiles, tossing his head.

“I belong to Thales, who said the ocean is the mother of all life. I feel no more repugnance returning to her arms than Humphrey Gilbert did.”

“But—Mother?”

“Mother knows. And she would not have us unworthy of her.”

“Impossible! She’ll never let you go!”

“All things are possible to those who believe in God, my brother—and she believes. Besides, Doctor Dee told her this summer of certain signs concerning me. I am born under a cold, watery planet. To live long, I must seek the sun’s warmth—bask in its heat like a fly on a wall. He warned me to avoid winters here. Return to the sea-breezes, he said, to warm my frozen lungs. He told stories of men given up for dead in Germany or France, who renewed their youth in Italy, Spain, or the Canaries. He has filled Mother’s mind with hope. She may be more willing to let me go than I am to leave her.”

“And yet—you must go. Not only because my heart burns, like Sidney’s, to join your noble band of Argonauts—men who are filling the earth and subduing it for God and the queen. Not only because love calls me—love fierce and unyielding, made stronger by absence, deeper by despair. But honor, Amyas—my oath—”

He stops, gasping, then collapses into a fit of coughing. Amyas supports him.

“Fools—fools we were—fools I was—to take that foolish vow!”

“No,” a gentle voice replies from behind. “You vowed for peace on earth, and goodwill toward men. 'Blessed are the peacemakers,' for they shall be called children of God. My sons—be sure such sacrifice will be rewarded by Him who gave Himself for you.”

“Oh, Mother! Mother!” Amyas cries. “Do you not hate seeing me—coming to take your firstborn?”

“My boy, God takes him—not you. And Doctor Dee said something extraordinary: great honors await you both in the West—each according to your worth.”

“Ah,” says Amyas. “My blessing will be like Esau’s—to live by the sword. While Jacob, the spiritual one, inherits heaven and an angel’s crown.”

“Be it what it may,” she says, “it will be a blessing—if you remain as you are. At least, Frank will be safe from court intrigues and worldly temptations. Would that I could go with you—share your glory! Come now,” she adds, laying her head on Amyas’s chest and smiling up at him, “I’ve heard of heroic mothers who went with their sons to war. Why not with you on a peaceful quest? I could nurse the sick. I could speak to that poor girl—perhaps win her back. Women understand women. Or I could mend and wash. Is it not as easy to be a good housewife on a ship as on land? Come!”

Amyas looks from one to the other.

“God only knows which is less fit to go. Mother—you don’t know what you ask. Frank—you don’t want to come. This is no gentle adventure. It demands sharp shot and cold steel.”

“How?” they cry together, horrified.

“I must pay my men, my partners. I must pay them with Spanish gold. And as a loyal subject, I cannot sail to the Spanish Main with a clear conscience unless I strike at her enemies—by day and night, with every weapon I have.”

“What nobler knight-errantry?” Frank says cheerfully.

Mrs. Leigh shudders.

“Frank too?” she murmurs. Her sons know her fear. Amyas’s life of war, though honorable, she bore as a necessary burden. But Frank—her gentle, poetic son—becoming a man of blood? That was unbearable. She had never known of his duel. Now, imagining him amidst carnage, she recoils. Yet, she whispers: “Is this another idol I must renounce?”

Then, grasping at hope: “Frank must ask the queen’s permission. If she allows it, how can I deny her wisdom?”

So the talk ends—sadly.

But now, a new worry grips Frank—one Amyas at first thinks jest, then realizes is serious. Frank fears the queen’s audience as if facing execution. Days pass before he gathers courage. He spends them building support through Leicester, Hatton, and Sidney—as if pleading for mercy.

“Why,” says Amyas, “can’t she cut your head off for wanting to go to sea?”

“But what axe sharper than her frown?” Frank replies, in deepest despair.

Amyas whistles tunelessly.

“Ah, brother—you cannot know the pain of parting from her.”

“No,” Amyas says. “I’d die for a hair of her royal head. But I could live till Doomsday without ever seeing it.”

“Plato’s Troglodytes regretted not sunlight they’d never seen.”

Amyas, baffled, says nothing.

Finally, Frank gets his audience. After two hours, he returns pale and spent.

“Thank Heaven—it’s over. She was furious at first—what else? She accused me of loving too low. I said my fault was committed before I knew true beauty. Then she charged me with disloyalty—swearing allegiance to another. I confessed with tears. When she threatened punishment, I said the offense had punished itself—exile from her light, into darkness. Then she asked: How could I, her sworn servant, desert her in these perilous times? What if I returned to find her dead by assassin’s hand? I fell to my knees, begging mercy. And she—turning to Hatton—asked, 'What say you, Mouton? Is he humbled enough?' Then she dismissed me.”

“Heigh-ho!” yawns Amyas.
“If the bridge had been stronger,
My tale had been longer.”

“Amyas! Amyas!” Frank says solemnly. “You do not know the power of royalty—its native majesty, magnified by wisdom and womanhood. If I had my way, every realm would have not a Salique law, but an anti-Salique: no kings, only queens. Then weakness, not power, would symbolize divinity. Love, not cunning, would decide every cause. Chivalry, not fear, would inspire obedience.”

“Humph,” says Amyas. “There’s sense in that. I’d run a mile for a woman when I’d not walk a yard for a man. But—who is this Mother brings in? The handsomest fellow I’ve ever seen!”

He was not wrong. Mrs. Leigh’s companion was none other than Mr. Secretary—Amyas’s old acquaintance from Smerwick Fort—also known as Colin Clout, Immerito, Edmund Spenser.

A half-jesting exchange had passed between poet and saint. As they entered, Mrs. Leigh smiled—though somewhat forced—“Well, my dear sons, you are assured of immortality. Mr. Spenser vows to dedicate an entire canto to your adventure in his *Faerie Queene*.”

“And you no less, madam,” Spenser replied. “What value has the story of the Gracchi without Cornelia? If I honor the fruit, I must not forget the stem. Frank, I congratulate you.”

“Then you know the result of my interview, Mother?”

“I know everything—and am content,” she said.

“Mrs. Leigh has reason to be content,” Spenser added, “for it is but her own likeness.”

“Spare your flattery, Mr. Spenser. When did I”—with a loving glance at Frank—“refuse knighthood for duty’s sake?”

“Knighthood?” Amyas cries. “You never told me that, Frank!”

“That may well be, Captain Leigh,” Spenser says, “but I’m told—by Hatton—that the queen informed him today that by taking this voyage, he forfeited the highest earthly honor, one even crowned heads seek from their subjects.”

Spenser spoke truly. Knighthood in that age was the reward of merit, so prized that Elizabeth once said, when asked why she didn’t make a favorite a peer, “I’ve already knighted him—there’s nothing higher to give.” It was not until Essex’s disastrous Irish campaign that the order began to decline—and James I completed its ruin by selling baronetcies, a mockery of nobility that brought shame upon its first holders.

Yet, in that moment, the honor remained sacred—and the dream of Westward-ho, alive.
