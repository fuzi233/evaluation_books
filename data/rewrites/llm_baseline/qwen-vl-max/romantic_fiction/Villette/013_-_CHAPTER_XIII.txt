I found myself smiling—nay, laughing—once more at Madame within twenty-four hours of the scene described in the previous chapter. Villette’s climate, though not as damp as England’s, is no less changeable. The soft glow of the sunset gave way to a night of fierce wind, and the following day brought a dry, tempestuous storm—dark and clouded, yet without rain. Dust swirled across the streets, lifted from the boulevards like a veil of grit, dimming the light and filling the air with a restless haze.

Even had the weather been fair, I doubt I would have ventured into the garden again that evening. My quiet alley, once so dear, now felt exposed. The seclusion I’d cherished was gone, replaced by an uneasy awareness. That casement, through which billets had rained down, had turned the once-private nook into something vulgar. The flowers seemed to watch; the knots in the tree trunks listened. Some plants bore the marks of Dr. John’s hurried search—trampled and bent—and footprints marred the beds. But before dawn broke, while the wind still howled, I managed to erase every trace, careful not to let any eye catch sight of it.

That evening, I sat at my desk, German grammar open before me, as the students settled into their lessons. The other teachers picked up their needlework, and silence fell over the refectory—a smaller room than any classroom, reserved only for boarders, whose number did not exceed two dozen. Two lamps hung above the tables, lit at dusk, marking the beginning of “la lecture pieuse,” or the devotional reading. It was here, each night, that we gathered under a solemn hush, books set aside, faces grave, and minds prepared for what followed.

The book used was always the same—an ancient volume, its cover worn as if by centuries, its pages yellowed like autumn leaves. I longed to hold it, to turn its sacred pages, to read the title myself. But I could only listen, as a heretic might hear scripture read aloud, my mind reeling under the weight of absurdity. These were legends of saints—tales so exaggerated, so far-fetched, they made one laugh inwardly. Yet beneath the monkish fantasy lay something darker: priestcraft, moral tyranny, stories of noblewomen humbled, reduced to servitude under the false piety of confessors who wielded power like weapons.

One tale, repeated often—the story of Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary—was especially harrowing. It spoke of forced renunciations, cruel penances, lives shattered in the name of faith. Such narratives felt like nightmares, dreams of oppression and suffering. I endured these readings for several nights, sitting quietly, pretending to be absorbed in my own work. Once, I snapped the points of my scissors, stabbing them deep into the worm-eaten table out of sheer tension. But eventually, the heat grew unbearable—my temples throbbed, my heart pounded, and sleep became fractured with unrest. I could stay no longer.

Prudence dictated escape. From that moment on, as soon as Rosine lit the lamps, I slipped away swiftly, silently—taking advantage of the brief flurry before silence descended. I vanished just as the students tucked their books away. There was no candlelight allowed outside the refectory; the teacher who left was cast into darkness, with only unlit halls, classrooms, or bedrooms to retreat to.

In winter, I walked the long classrooms, pacing quickly to keep warm. If the moon shone, I was grateful; if only stars glimmered, I adapted. In summer, when twilight lingered, I climbed to my chamber in the dormitory—its five large windows like doors to the sky. I opened my casement, leaned out, and gazed upon the city beyond the garden. Music drifted from the park or palace square, and I lived in my own silent world, thinking my own thoughts, free from intrusion.

This particular evening, I fled the refectory as usual, fleeing the Pope and his works. I ascended the stairs, approached the dormitory door—always kept shut—and pushed it open softly. The hinges, well-oiled, moved without a sound. Before I saw, I *felt* that someone was there. Not movement, not breath, but presence. The great room, usually empty, held life. All the white beds—called “lits d’ange”—were visible at a glance. They were all vacant. No sleeper rested.

Then came the soft scrape of a drawer being pulled open. I stepped sideways, and my vision cleared. I saw my own bed, my toilet, my locked work-box, and drawers beneath. And there stood Madame—dumpy, motherly, wrapped in a modest shawl, wearing a clean nightcap. She was searching my belongings with meticulous care.

The lid of the work-box was open. The top drawer was drawn out. One by one, she opened each drawer, lifting every item, unfolding garments, scanning papers, unlidding tiny boxes. Her movements were precise, unhurried, almost reverent. She worked like a true star—unhasting, yet never resting.

I watched, secretly amused. Had I been a man, I might have admired her. She was neat, thorough, efficient. Others’ clumsiness irritated me; hers pleased. I stood transfixed—but then I forced myself to move. If she turned, she might see me. Then there would be no hiding. We would confront each other—no masks, no pretenses. I would look into her eyes, and she into mine. We would know, instantly, that our roles could no longer continue. And we would part forever.

Why invite such a catastrophe? I was not angry. I had no desire to leave. This was the easiest employment I could hope for, and I respected Madame’s intelligence, even if I disagreed with her beliefs. Her system didn’t harm me. I was untouched by it. And since I had no lover, no hopes of love, my heart was safe—like a beggar’s purse, barren and thus immune to theft.

So I turned and fled, descending the stairs as silently as a spider slipping down the banister. When I reached the schoolroom, I laughed—quietly, but deeply. I knew now that she had seen Dr. John in the garden. I knew what she thought. The irony of her suspicion, so wildly misplaced, delighted me. But the laughter faded, replaced by a sudden anger, then bitterness. Like striking rock and watching water spring forth, my emotions surged.

Never had I felt such turmoil—laughter and pain, fire and grief, warring within me. I wept hot tears—not because of her mistrust, which meant nothing to me—but for deeper reasons. Complicated thoughts stirred, disrupting my peace. Yet by morning, the storm had passed. I was Lucy Snowe again.

Returning to my room, I found everything restored—drawers locked, clothes neatly folded. My dresses lay as I had left them. A small bunch of dried white violets—given anonymously once, preserved between the folds of my best dress—lay undisturbed. My black silk scarf, lace chemisette, collars—all untouched. Had even one article been creased, I might have resented it. But finding everything in order, I whispered, “Let bygones be bygones. I am unharmed. Why should I bear malice?”

Yet one mystery remained: how had Dr. John known the ivory box had fallen, if he hadn’t helped drop it? He arrived promptly to retrieve it. The question gnawed at me. Could I, if given the chance, ask him directly? While he was absent, I believed I had the courage.

Georgette, meanwhile, was recovering. Her doctor visits grew rare. He would have stopped entirely, had Madame not insisted on occasional calls until the child was fully well.

One evening, after I’d heard Georgette’s lisping prayer and tucked her into bed, Dr. John entered the nursery. He took the child’s hand and said, “Cette enfant a toujours un peu de fièvre.” Then, looking at me with a sharper glance than usual, “Le Docteur John l’a-t-il vue dernièrement? Non, n’est-ce pas?”

She knew better than anyone. “Well,” she continued, “I’m going out—to run some errands by carriage. I’ll call on Dr. John and send him to the child. I insist he sees her tonight. Her cheeks are flushed, her pulse quick. You will receive him—I shall be away.”

The child was merely warm from summer heat. Sending for a doctor was as unnecessary as calling a priest for extreme unction. Moreover, Madame rarely ran errands in the evening. This was the first time she’d chosen to leave during one of Dr. John’s visits. Clearly, this was deliberate. But I felt no anxiety.

“Ha! ha! Madame,” I thought, “your cleverness has misfired.”

She left dressed smartly—shawl of fine quality, a green hat that suited her fresh complexion. I wondered what she intended. Would she send Dr. John? Would he come?

Madame had told me not to let Georgette sleep until the doctor arrived. So I occupied myself with stories and gentle talk, soothing the child. I liked Georgette—she was sensitive, affectionate. Holding her in my arms was a comfort. Tonight, she insisted I rest my head on her crib pillow, even wrapping her tiny arms around my neck. The tenderness overwhelmed me. Tears pricked my eyes. Such pure emotion, so innocent, touched something deep within.

An hour passed. Georgette murmured, sleepy. “And you _shall_ sleep,” I decided, “despite maman and médecin, if they don’t arrive in ten minutes.”

Then—ringing! And footsteps, swift and startling, echoing up the stairs. Rosine ushered in Dr. John, staying behind, as was common among Villette’s servants. She wasn’t afraid of him—only of Madame’s presence. Smart, trim, and bold, she stood with hands in her apron pockets, eyeing him as if he were a portrait.

“Le marmot n’a rien, n’est-ce pas?” she asked, jerking her chin toward Georgette.

“Pas beaucoup,” replied the doctor, scribbling a harmless prescription.

“Eh bien!” she pressed, stepping close. “And the box—did you get it? Monsieur rushed off like a storm last night. I didn’t get to ask.”

“I found it,” he said simply.

“And who threw it?” she asked freely—words I longed to speak but lacked the courage.

“That may be my secret,” he replied, calm and unassuming.

“Mais enfin,” she persisted, “monsieur knew it was thrown—how did you know?”

“I was attending a young patient at the college nearby,” he explained. “I saw it fall from his window and came to retrieve it.”

Ah—so simple. The note had mentioned a physician examining “Gustave.”

“Ah ça!” Rosine exclaimed. “So there’s nothing hidden—no mystery, no romance?”

“Not more than on my palm,” he replied, showing his hand.

“Quel dommage!” she sighed. “And I—was beginning to imagine things.”

“Vraiment! You’re out of luck,” he said coolly.

She pouted. He couldn’t help laughing at her expression. His smile softened his face, revealing a warmth that made him seem kinder. I saw his hand go to his pocket.

“How many times have you opened the door for me this month?” he asked.

“Monsieur should have counted,” she replied.

“As if I had anything better to do!” he teased—but I saw him slip a gold coin into her hand. She took it without hesitation and danced off to answer the bell, ringing every five minutes as servants fetched half-boarders.

Do not judge Rosine too harshly. She was not a bad person—only practical. She saw no shame in taking what she could, nor fear in speaking plainly to a gentleman.

From this exchange, I learned more than about the ivory box: the fault did not lie in Madame’s wardrobe, nor in Rosine’s apron. Those garments were as blameless as Georgette’s blue tunic. So who then was responsible? What was the truth behind it all?

Some clues emerged, but many remained dark. “However,” I thought, “it’s none of my business.” I turned from the face I’d been staring at—Dr. John’s—and looked out the window toward the garden.

He stood by Georgette’s bed, slowly pulling on his gloves, watching her as her eyes closed and her lips parted in sleep. I waited for him to leave, as he always did—with a quick bow and a barely audible “good-night.”

Just as he reached for his hat, my gaze caught the lattice window across the garden—the one already noted—cautiously opening. A hand appeared, holding a white handkerchief. It waved.

I don’t know if it was answered from somewhere unseen in our house. But moments later, a white object fluttered from the window—billet number two.

“There!” I breathed.

“Where?” Dr. John asked sharply, turning toward the window.

“They’ve done it again,” I said. “A handkerchief waved—and something fell.” I pointed.

“Go at once,” he ordered. “Pick it up and bring it here. Nobody will notice you. I would be seen.”

I went. After a brief search, I found a folded paper lodged in a shrub. I brought it back.

He tore it into pieces without reading.

“It’s not her fault,” he said, looking at me. “You must remember.”

“Whose fault?” I asked.

“Who is it?”

“You don’t know yet?”

“Not at all.”

“Have you no guess?”

“None.”

“If I knew you better,” he said, “I might trust you with a confidence—entrust you to guard a most innocent, excellent, yet inexperienced creature.”

“As a duenna?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said absently. “What snares surround her!”

For the first time, he studied my face—perhaps seeking kindness, perhaps looking for someone worthy of such a charge.

I felt no calling to chaperone angels. But recalling the scene at the bureau, I felt I owed him something. If I could help, I would.

“I’m willing,” I said, “to do what I can to protect anyone you care about.”

“I’m not interested personally,” he replied, with a modesty I admired. “I know the character of the person from the opposite house—who has twice intruded here. I’ve met the object of these attempts in society. She is exquisite, refined—her very nature should repel such impertinence. Yet she remains unaware, innocent. I wish to shield her—if I could. But I cannot approach her myself.”

“Then tell me how I can help,” I said. “Only tell me who she is.”

“But you _must_ know,” he said earnestly, voice low. “So spotless, so good, so beautiful—impossible that one house could hold two like her. I allude, of course—”

At that moment, the latch of Madame Beck’s chamber door clicked—suddenly, as if startled. Then came the suppressed burst of a sneeze.

These little accidents happen to the best.

Madame—excellent woman—had returned quietly, crept upstairs on tiptoe, and entered her room. Had she not sneezed, she might have heard everything. But the sneeze betrayed her.

She stepped forward, composed, alert, as if she had just arrived. No novice would have guessed she’d been listening at the keyhole for ten minutes. She pretended to sneeze again, declared herself “enrhumée,” and began recounting her errands with cheerful detail.

The prayer-bell rang. I left her with Dr. John.
