The day following, a Sunday, dawned bright and clear over the Hoog Straet, where Catherine’s house hummed with quiet excitement. Mass had just ended, and now the air was thick with the promise of new beginnings. The shop stood ready—clean, orderly, and proudly displaying the names of its new masters: Cornelis and Sybrandt. Above the door hung their sign: a white lamb suckling a gilded sheep, a symbol both humble and hopeful. Eli had arrived earlier, bringing goods from his own store to help them begin on solid ground. Their parents watched with swelling hearts, proud not only of their children’s venture but of the change it marked—a turning away from the toil of the fields toward the steady rhythm of trade.

Cornelis and Sybrandt walked together in the garden, hands clasped, eyes bright. “I’m sick of that old life,” said one. “It wore us down like dry reeds.” “Aye,” replied the other, “and this—making money instead of spending it—feels like breathing clean air.” They vowed then and there to stand firm in their calling, as steadfast as wax set in the sun.

Catherine, ever alert, heard their words through an open window. She turned to Eli and smiled. “They mean well. Let us bless their efforts.”

That evening, the family would gather for supper—a celebration of sorts. Margaret and her young son were invited, and the meal would be more than mere food; it would be a feast of counsel, of encouragement, of shared wisdom. Wine would flow freely, and the elders would speak plainly of trade, of diligence, of the rewards that come to those who work with purpose. It was a toast to commerce, a farewell to the unyielding land—wild oats, they called it, sown without return.

Margaret had come early, eager to help Catherine in the kitchen and to shake off the heaviness that had settled upon her lately. Both women moved about with quiet energy, their cheeks flushed as if warmed by the hearth’s fire. Then Joan arrived, bearing a fresh salad from Jorian’s garden.

“He cut it for you, Margaret,” she said, grinning. “You’re all he talks of these days. I’ll be jealous. I told him you’d be feasting today. But oh, lass—what a sermon in the new kerk! Preaching? I never knew what it meant until today.”

“Would that I had been there,” sighed Margaret. “My soul feels parched, starved for the dew of heaven.”

“Why, he preaches again this afternoon,” Joan replied. “But perhaps you’re needed here?”

“No,” said Catherine firmly. “Come, go if you wish. You’ve no call to stay.”

“I think,” Margaret mused, “I should not dull the mood at table if I came straight from a good sermon.”

“Then you must hurry,” Joan urged. “See how the folk are moving that way—and look! There goes the holy friar. Oh, mercy! The hermit himself! We forgot!”

And with that, Joan darted out of the house, crossed the street, and stopped the friar in his path. A moment later, she returned, breathless.

“There—he spoke to me,” she declared. “He said, ‘My daughter, I will go to him ere sunset, God willing.’ Such a gentle voice. And oh, my mistresses—such thin cheeks for a young man, and such great eyes—almost your colour, Margaret.”

“I have a strong desire to go,” Margaret confessed. “But my cap is not clean, and everyone else will be in their snowy mutches.”

“Take mine,” Catherine said, pointing to the basket. “But you cannot take the child—I need him for poor Kate. She has one of her bad days.”

Margaret nodded, understanding, and carried the boy upstairs. In the bedroom, Kate lay in bed, pale but smiling faintly.

“How art thou, sweetheart?” Margaret asked softly.

“Nay,” Kate whispered, “you need not ask. I am in pain—but I smile because I know you care.” She held up a small hand. “See—I brought thee one thou lovest.”

“Two, by my reckoning,” Kate replied with an angelic smile. At that moment, a spasm gripped her, fierce and sudden—enough to make any woman cry out. But she bore it silently.

“What, in your lap?” Margaret asked, interpreting the gesture.

“Nay,” Kate murmured. “He is too heavy, and I am in such pain.”

“But I love him so dear,” she added, “that I feel no weight at all.”

Margaret took the chance to prepare herself. She washed her face, brushed her hair, and tied on a clean cap.

“I am going to the kerk,” she announced. “To hear that beautiful preacher.”

Kate sighed. “And just now, I longed to go myself. That’s how it’s been this month—up and down, like the waves of the Zuyder Zee. I’d rather stay with you. Say the word.”

“Nay,” Kate said gently. “Go. And bring me back every word. Alas, I cannot go myself.”

Tears gathered in her eyes.

That settled it. Margaret kissed her sister-in-law, looked tenderly at the sleeping child, and let out a soft sigh.

“I trow I must not,” she said. “I never could kiss him lightly. And my father always said: *Never wake a child by day or night unless it is thy pleasure.*”

She paused. “Speak to Aunt Kate the two new words you’ve learned.”

And then she left, glancing back once with deep affection, closing the door softly behind her.

Downstairs, Catherine turned to Joan. “Will you help me peel these?”

“Aye, mistress,” Joan replied. “With pleasure.”

And so they worked in silence, swift and sure, the kitchen alive with the scent of herbs and roasting meat.

“Now, Joan,” Catherine said, “those who help me cook and serve the meat—they help me eat it. That’s my rule.”

“There are worse laws in Holland,” Joan chuckled. “Your will is my pleasure, mistress. My Luke has his supper already—digging in the fields today, thank God.”

Margaret descended the stairs.

“Eh, woman,” Catherine said, “that’s a hard trade. But look at her—just washed her face, turned her hair, and now who is like her? Rotterdam itself couldn’t match her!” She snapped her fingers. “Give us a kiss, hussy!”

“And mind,” she added, “Eli won’t wait for the duke. So don’t linger after church.”

Joan and Catherine followed her to the door, standing there watching as she walked down the street—her figure small but resolute against the golden light.

For among plain housewives, stepping out into the world is half an event.

Catherine murmured, “There, Joan—it feels almost as though I’ve just sent my own daughter to church, and am standing here, watching her go. I’ve done it many times. And yet… this time, it stirs something deeper. Joan, she’ll never speak ill of Gerard—even though he treated her cruelly while he lived. That’s why my heart aches for her. I’m older, wiser. I’ll see her wed to that simple Luke. And there—an end. What’s one grandchild?”
