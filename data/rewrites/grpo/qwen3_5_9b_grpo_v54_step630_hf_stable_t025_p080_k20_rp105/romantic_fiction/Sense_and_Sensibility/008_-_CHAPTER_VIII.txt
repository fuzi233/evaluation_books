Mrs. Jennings, a widow blessed with a generous income and two daughters already happily wedded, found her sole purpose in life to be the architect of matrimonial unions for everyone else. With the fervor of a matchmaker possessed, she scoured her circle of acquaintances for eligible bachelors and beautiful maidens, her keen eye rarely missing a flutter of affection or a blush of vanity. Upon arriving at Barton, her discernment was swift and decisive: she declared without hesitation that Colonel Brandon was deeply enamored with Marianne Dashwood. Her conviction was forged early, sparked by his rapt attention while Marianne sang, and confirmed when he listened to her again during the Middletons' dinner. To Mrs. Jennings, this was a perfect pairing; he possessed wealth, and she possessed beauty. Having long admired the Colonel since his introduction by Sir John, she was eager to secure a husband for every pretty girl in her sphere.

The personal reward for Mrs. Jennings was substantial, providing her with endless ammunition for jests. At the park, she teased the Colonel; at the cottage, she mocked Marianne. While the Colonel likely found her raillery harmless, Marianne was initially baffled by it. Once the nature of the jokes became clear, she struggled to decide whether to laugh at their absurdity or condemn their cruelty, viewing them as a stingless barb at the Colonel's advanced years and his lonely status as an old bachelor.

Mrs. Dashwood, unable to conceive of a man five years younger than herself as ancient in the eyes of her daughter's youth, stepped in to defend the Colonel's reputation. "At least, Mama," Marianne insisted, "you cannot deny the sheer absurdity of the accusation, even if you don't mean it maliciously. Colonel Brandon is younger than Mrs. Jennings, yet he is old enough to be my father. If he ever possessed the fire for love, he must have long since extinguished it. It is ridiculous! When will a man be safe from such wit if age and infirmity offer no protection?"

"Infirmity!" Elinor interjected gently. "Do you truly consider Colonel Brandon infirm? I can easily imagine his age seems much greater to you than to my mother, but you cannot deceive yourself about his physical strength."

"Did you not hear him speak of rheumatism?" Marianne countered. "Is that not the common ailment of declining years?"

"My dearest child," Mrs. Dashwood laughed, "if you proceed at this rate, you shall live in constant terror of my own decay. It must seem a miracle to you that I have reached the advanced age of forty."

"Mama, you are not doing me justice," Marianne replied. "Colonel Brandon is not so old that his friends fear losing him soon. He may live another twenty years. But thirty-five has nothing to do with matrimony."

"Perhaps," Elinor reasoned, "thirty-five and seventeen should not be paired. Yet, if there were a woman single at twenty-seven, I should not find Colonel Brandon's age an obstacle to his marrying her."

"A woman of twenty-seven," Marianne mused, "can never hope to feel or inspire affection again. If her home is uncomfortable or her fortune small, she might submit to the duties of a nurse for the security of a wife. In such a case, there is nothing unsuitable. It would be a compact of convenience, satisfying the world. To me, however, it would not be a marriage at all, but a commercial exchange where each seeks benefit at the other's expense."

"It is impossible," Elinor conceded, "to convince you that a woman of twenty-seven could feel anything approaching love for a man of thirty-five. Still, I must object to condemning Colonel Brandon and his wife to a sickroom merely because he complained yesterday, on a cold, damp day, of a slight ache in his shoulder."

"But he spoke of flannel waistcoats!" Marianne protested. "To me, a flannel waistcoat is inextricably linked to aches, cramps, rheumatism, and every ailment that afflicts the aged and feeble."

"Had he been in a violent fever, you would not despise him half so much," Elinor observed. "Confess, Marianne, is there not something fascinating in the flushed cheek, hollow eye, and quick pulse of a fever?"

Shortly after Elinor left the room, Marianne turned to her mother with a new anxiety. "I have a fear regarding illness that I cannot hide from you. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We have been here nearly two weeks, and he has not come. Nothing but genuine indisposition could cause such an extraordinary delay. What else could detain him at Norland?"

"Had you expected him sooner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked. "I had none. On the contrary, if I felt any anxiety, it was recalling that he sometimes seemed reluctant to accept my invitation. Does Elinor expect him already?"

"I never mentioned it to her, but surely she must assume so."

"I doubt you are right," Mrs. Dashwood replied. "When I spoke yesterday of getting a new grate for the spare bedroom, she remarked there was no hurry, as the room would not be needed for some time."

"How strange!" Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed. "What is the meaning of this? Their behavior toward one another has been inexplicable! How cold and composed were their final farewells! How languid their conversation on the last evening! In Edward's goodbye, there was no distinction between Elinor and me; it was merely the warm wishes of an affectionate brother. Twice I left them alone during the last morning, and each time he most strangely followed me out of the room. And Elinor, when leaving Norland and Edward, did not cry as I did. Her self-command remains unbroken. When is she dejected or melancholy? When does she seek solitude or appear restless?"
