Our mate Arthur Pendennis rolled into London that very same day for a flying 48-hour visit, first dumping a crew of deeply boring fellow travellers he’d escorted up from the West Country at their hotel, then hightailing it straight to his Lamb Court chambers. That drab old Temple building only gets about 12 minutes of direct sunshine a day total, but its residents never complain too hard: total freedom from overbearing landladies and nosy neighbours is more than a fair swap for a bit of shade, and the lot of them grumble nonstop while lazing about as happily as cats in a sunbeam.

Pen’s harried landlady greeted him with the news that Warrington was home too, so Arthur bolted up the stairs two at a time, and sure enough found his oldest friend propped at his desk wreathed in pipe smoke thick enough to hang a hat on, half-buried under a pile of newspapers and quarterly reviews. Their greeting was peak unhinged British male stoicism: no dramatic hugs, no loud sobs over the months they’d spent apart. “Well, young one!” Warrington grunted, pausing to fish his pipe out of his mouth. “How are you, old boy?” Pen replied, sticking out a hand.

You’d never see two Frenchmen pull that: they’d be clinging to each other’s shoulders shrieking *“Mon cher Arthur! Mon coeur!”* loud enough to rattle the street lamps. A pair of German pals would have dotted six perfumed kisses on each other’s moustaches before they even said hello. But two proper English blokes? They’d give that exact same flat, no-frills greeting even if they’d just dragged each other out of a burning building the night before. By next week they won’t even shake hands, just nod at each other across the breakfast table. They’d both empty their wallets for each other, scream down anyone who dared badmouth the other, and write 10 pages of gushing letters when they’re apart – but in person? They’d die before they looked sappy. Hardman, the bloke who’s never felt a genuine friendly emotion in his entire life, loves bragging “See? We Britons aren’t all over-dramatic like those confounded continentals” like that’s a personality win.

“Been to Switzerland, then?” Pen asked.
“Aye,” Warrington said. “Couldn’t find a single half-decent bit of tobacco the whole trip, until I hit Strasburg and stocked up on caporal.” The man had seen the most jaw-dropping alpine peaks and sunlit Italian lakes that would make any normal person cry, but he’d rather die than gush about it out loud. He’d ramble about those sights late at night after half a bottle of claret, and stuff all that giddy wonder into his travel essays later when no one’s watching, but for now he mumbles through his whole itinerary: Switzerland, North Italy, the Tyrol, Vienna, Dresden, down the Rhine, like all of it was a massive boring chore he’d rather not talk about.

Pen then took his turn updating his pal: he’d been stuck in the countryside for months, bored out of his skull on the parliamentary canvassing trail, counting cracks in the village pub ceiling just to pass the time. Next he’s off to some party near Tunbridge Wells full of people who will do nothing but ramble on about turnip yields and gout for three days straight. It’s physically painful for any Englishman to admit he’s having fun, after all.

“Speaking of that seat you’re gunning for,” Warrington said, “is that all sorted?”
“100%,” Pen grinned. “As soon as Parliament sits and they issue a new writ, old Clavering retires and I slip right into his spot.”
“Who are you going to pretend to be to win votes, then?” Warrington teased. “Liberal Conservative? Loyal government man? The lone champion of the working man?”
Pen waved him off. “Oh, politics are all the same these days, aren’t they? I don’t own enough land to be a protectionist even if I tried. I’ll mostly vote with the government, but push ahead of them on the sanitation and colonisation stuff I’ve been cramming from blue books all vacation – stop snickering, you old cynic. I’m no Moses, I’m not about to haul a bunch of holy commandments down a mountain to give to the masses. I don’t have the vanity, or the hypocrisy, to pretend I’m a grand world reformer. I promise you, George, I won’t tell outright lies. If there’s a good speech to give in the Commons I’ll give it, if there’s a good bill to back I’ll back it, if there’s a decent government job going I’ll take it and be chuffed with my luck. But I won’t kiss up to some fancy lord any more than I’ll kiss up to a rowdy mob. That’s all my politics are, no tricks.”

Warrington snickered, then leaned back in his chair. “And after ambition? Love, I suppose? You gonna divorce me and the chambers to get hitched?”
Pen sighed so loud the pipe smoke rippled across the room. “I s’pose. Blanche’s good fun, sings, doesn’t yell if you smoke in the drawing room. She’s got a decent fortune, no exact numbers yet, but my uncle swears her mum the Begum is gonna dump a massive wad of cash on us when we tie the knot. And I think she’s dead keen on me.”
“Meaning you’re happy to take her caresses and her money?”
“Come on, we both agreed life’s just a transaction, isn’t it?” Pen shrugged. “I’m not pretending I’m madly head over heels, I told her exactly what my feelings are, and we’re engaged. Her letters – especially the ones to Laura – get softer every week, you can tell she’s falling harder. Mine’ve been plain as day, no fancy love poems, no grand vows, just treating the whole thing as done, no point rushing or dragging it out.”

Warrington went quiet for a second. “How’s Laura, then?”
Pen looked him dead in the eye, and his voice went soft, wobbly like he was about to crack. “Laura, George – she’s the best, noblest, sweetest girl the sun ever shone on. I’m an idiot, I only just figured it out, now it’s too late. I had an angel right under my nose for years and I let her slip away. Who doesn’t lose the best prize life ever offers them, right? Who gets to the grave without having to give up something they loved most just to pay Fate’s fine? I’m not the only one, am I, old boy?”
Warrington just nodded. He was thinking of his own lost Helen, the girl whose old handwriting in a bible and a glass that once held a posy of her flowers were all he had left of her. Neither of them said a word for a good five minutes, both lost in their own dumb sad memories.

Pen broke the silence first, saying he had to go track down his uncle, the Major, who’d been firing off grumpy letters nonstop lately. “Said he’s getting old, wants to see me in Parliament, settled down with a kid to carry the Pendennis name before he kicks the bucket,” Pen said.
Warrington snickered. “That old heathen’s got a good heart, though. He cares about something that’s not buttoned up inside his own waistcoat, at least – you, and the whole Pendennis line. He’ll die happy knowing the family’s multiplying, then get carried off to be buried under St James’s Church right by Piccadilly, where he can hear the carriage traffic even six feet under. Very edifying ending.”

Then Pen rambled on about Blanche’s chaotic family: her dad was an Indiaman lieutenant who drowned between Calcutta and Sydney, her mum the Begum has had the worst luck with husbands ever, and her current stepdad Sir Francis Clavering is the most useless, roguish baronet to ever pretend to be a respectable county gentleman.
“Funny you mention Clavering,” Warrington cut in. “I ran into Chevalier Strong at Baden a few weeks back, parading around all covered in his fancy medals. He told me he’s had a massive falling out with Clavering, said he’s a total utter scoundrel. That Bloundell bloke who taught you to cheat at cards back at Oxbridge was with him too – years have turned him into a way more polished, professional scammer than he was when we were students. And the king of the whole casino was this Colonel Altamont, who apparently breaks the bank every single night like he’s got a direct hotline to the devil.”

Pen hopped up to grab his hat, then stopped at his own downstairs chambers and started dousing himself in eau de Cologne, spritzing so hard he was basically fumigating his hair and whiskers.
“What the hell?” Warrington grumbled. “You weren’t even smoking. Did my pipe offend you that much?”
“Got to call on some ladies, and eat dinner with them later,” Pen said, preening as he slipped on a crisp new shirt, perfectly tied neckcloth, fresh gloves, and boots so polished you could see your own reflection in them. Warrington, meanwhile, was still wearing his beat-up old work shirt, frayed at the collar from months of his coarse blue stubble rubbing against it, and scuffed thick work boots.
“Go on, then, you fancy buck,” Warrington laughed. “I love walking around with you – makes me feel like I’ve got a fresh rose stuck in my buttonhole. You never turn your nose up at scruffy old me, do you?”
Pen was about to protest, when Warrington added quietly, “If you write to Laura, tell her God bless her from me, will you?”

Pen burst out cackling so loud he nearly dropped his cologne bottle. “You daft sod – I’m going to dinner with her right now. I drove her and Lady Rockminster up from the country this very day, we stopped overnight at Bath. You can come too! The old lady won’t mind, she never stops talking about you.”
Warrington made a big show of saying he had a urgent article to finish, hemmed and hawed for all of 10 seconds before caving, and the two of them trundled off to the Jermyn Street hotel in very high spirits. Laura’s face lit up when she saw them, her soft hand warm and welcoming in theirs, and there was half an hour still to go before dinner.
“Off you go now, Mr Pendennis,” old Lady Rockminster ordered him, waving a gnarled finger. “Go visit your uncle, and do not – I repeat, do not – bring that old Major back to dinner. I’ve heard his story about the time he met the Duke of York 47 times already. Mr Warrington can stay here and chat with us, you’ve bored us both silly solid for two whole days straight.”
Pen obeyed at once, grinning, and trotted off towards his uncle’s Bury Street lodgings, already mentally rehearsing all his good news to make the old man chuffed.
