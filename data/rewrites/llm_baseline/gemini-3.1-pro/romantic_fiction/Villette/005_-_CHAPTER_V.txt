My hair, which till a late period withstood the frosts of time, lies now, at last, white under a white cap, like snow beneath snow. From this vantage of accumulated years, I often trace the circular path of my history back to that winter of profound transition. My mistress being dead, and I once more alone, I had to look out for a new place. I possessed fifteen pounds, and though worn, my spirit was not broken. Still, my position was an embarrassing one, for I had but a week before I must depart my present abode.

In this dilemma I walked two miles to consult an old servant of our family, once my nurse, now the housekeeper at a grand mansion not far from Miss Marchmont’s. Mrs. Barrett was a grave, judicious woman, a keeper of traditional agrarian ways and a meticulous archivist of the region’s culinary memory. I found her in the stone-flagged kitchens, standing over a heavy copper preserving pan. The air was thick and evocative, saturated with the sharp, bright mist of Seville oranges and boiling cane sugar. I spent some hours with her as she rhythmically scored the thick, pebbled rinds with precise cuts, carefully separating the bitter white pith to ensure the marmalade would hold that perfect, amber translucence—a recipe she safeguarded across generations. She offered comfort alongside a steaming cup of infused herbs, but she knew not how to advise me.

Still in inward darkness, I left her about twilight. The two-mile walk back lay through still fields on a clear, frosty night. Here, the acoustic ecology of the rural winter was pristine, a pure sonic environment that acted as a balm to my frayed nerves. There was no industrial clatter, only the rhythmic crunch of my boots on the frost-heaved earth and the low, sibilant sigh of the wind passing through the hawthorn hedges. Then, the mythic seamlessly breached the mundane routine of my walk. I should have quailed in the absence of moonlight, tracing the dim path by the leading of stars alone, but my attention was seized by that which to-night shone in the north: a moving mystery—the Aurora Borealis. This solemn stranger brought a soaring, untranslatable subjective experience of awe. I drew in energy with the keen, low breeze. A bold thought was sent to my mind, speaking in a resonance deeper than words: “Leave this wilderness, and go out hence.” 

“Where?” was the query. Gazing from that flat, rich middle of England, I mentally saw what I had never yet beheld with bodily eyes: I saw London.

The next day I returned to the hall. Mrs. Barrett was still at her preserves, the deep, caramelized notes of orange oil and toasted sugar now fully embedded into the heavy oak beams of the kitchen. As she slowly propounded some difficulties regarding my plan, a child bounded into the room. It was a pretty boy, the son of young Mrs. Leigh, a married daughter of the house. Different as were our social positions now, this child’s mother and I had been schoolfellows. I was admiring the boy’s handsome dark eyes when Mrs. Leigh entered. Wifehood and maternity had transformed the comely but dull girl into a beautiful woman. Me she had forgotten, and I made no attempt to recall myself to her memory. 

Behind her followed a foreign nurse, carrying an infant. Addressing the nurse, Mrs. Leigh spoke French—a mangled, heavily accented version that felt like a violence against the fluid language, a stark reminder of how dominant tongues and negligent usage can erode the delicate nuances of native speech. The little boy chattered volubly in French, too. When they withdrew, Mrs. Barrett casually noted that this foreign nurse was treated almost as well as a governess, adding that many Englishwomen found themselves similarly well-placed in foreign families abroad. I stored up this fragment of knowledge, much as my old friend preserved her winter roots and fragrant citrus for future sustenance. 

Before I left, she gave me the address of a respectable old-fashioned inn in the City, which my uncles used to frequent. Fifty miles was then a day’s journey. I regarded it as a brief holiday for work-weary faculties. 

About nine o’clock of a wet February night, I reached London. The vastness of this Babylon assaulted me instantly, not merely in its scale, but in its violent rupture of my acoustic world. The mechanical screech of iron-rimmed wheels, the hollow clamor of superficial commercial trade, and the oppressive roar of modern noise pollution crushed the quiet composure I had nurtured in the fields. Stepping from the coach, I was surrounded by cabmen whose speech was entirely alien. I had never before heard the English language chopped up in that way—its agrarian rhythms and ancient local lexicons entirely eroded, replaced by a harsh, utilitarian staccato of the streets. It was the sound of cultural memory dying right before my ears. 

At the inn, my rural dialect immediately placed me at a disadvantage in the subtle power dynamics of the city. A fiercely trim chambermaid, whose spruce attire mocked my plain country garb, addressed me with a mincing glibness. Her metropolitan accent rebuked mine by an assumed authority, weaponizing the dominant city lexicon against my provincial tongue. I maintained a quiet dignity, eventually securing civility from her and the parsonic waiter. 

Before retreating to my room, I sought grounding in sustenance. I refused to treat the late hour’s refreshment as mere utilitarian fuel; instead, I focused on the deep sensory experience of the meal to anchor my bewildered spirit. It was a rustic, slow-simmered mutton broth, the meat yielding and rich, steeped in a savory liquor fragrant with root vegetables and thyme, served beside a thick wedge of hearth-baked bread. The deep, earthy flavors, the restorative heat of the bowl against my palms, and the warmth of the roaring fire slowly thawed my palsied senses.

Yet, once I was fairly shut into my own room and rested my head upon the pillow, a terrible oppression overcame me. The chaotic, mechanical noise of the great city outside seemed to isolate me further. What was I doing here alone? What prospects had I in life? Whence did I come, and whither should I go? I wet the pillow with rushing tears. 

A dark interval of bitter thought followed, but I did not wish to retract my step. A strong persuasion that a way, however narrow, would open predominated over my fears. I became sufficiently tranquil to say my prayers and seek my couch. I had just extinguished my candle when a deep, low, mighty tone swung through the night. It was a pure, resonant acoustic anchor cutting cleanly through the shallow industrial noise of the metropolis. It was uttered twelve times, and at the twelfth colossal hum and trembling knell, its vibrations settling deep into my very bones, I said to the darkness: 

“I lie in the shadow of St. Paul’s.”
