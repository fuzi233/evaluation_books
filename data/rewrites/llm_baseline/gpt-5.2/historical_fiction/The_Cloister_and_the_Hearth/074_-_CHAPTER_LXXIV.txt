The new Pontiff, being of a mind to strengthen the brethren of Saint Dominic, sent word to the convent that a capable friar must be furnished for the University of Basle. The summons fell upon Clement as naturally as a hand finds the right tool in a darkened chest: he was read in languages; in his lay days he had sat at learned men’s feet; and now his obedience was a thing as plain as bread.

So the visit to England was delayed—not relinquished—and, because Basle would not need him for three months, he was bidden to take the road and preach as he went.

He passed out by the northern gate barefoot, his eyes lowered, the cord at his waist hanging quiet as a plumb-line, and the whole man wrapped in that inward labour men call contemplation. If a painter could lay on fresco the story of a mind, what colours had been ground and mixed upon this one robe within the compass of a single year—hope, the quick wine of love, bereavement like a knife in warm flesh, despair, impiety, vice, the black thought of self-slaughter, remorse, the long sour bread of penitence, death to the world, and at last a kind of resignation that was not joy, yet was not emptiness.

He was a traveller again, and on foot again; but now the road had lost its teeth. No perilous adventure sprang at him from hedge or hollow. The very thieves, seeing the white habit and the bare feet, bowed as if a power stood behind the man; and more than once, instead of stripping him, they thrust stolen coin upon him with rough, shamefaced fingers, and begged his prayers. Such passages make little show; yet there are days on a road when nothing happens outwardly, and the inward world changes its climate.

The first Sunday of his journey was such a day.

After preaching in a small Italian town—where the air, being Sunday-clean, held incense mingled with the faint sour of pressed olives and the human breath of a crowded nave—he found himself mightily carried onward, not by praise (he had learned to dread it), but by a soft persuasion, like warm broth slipping into an empty stomach. That day he prayed for Margaret’s soul.

He had never done so before.

Not because her eternal welfare was less dear to him than life itself, but because humility, taken in him to an extreme, had become a kind of scruple. Those terrible impieties that had broken from him when he first heard of her death had horrified others; but, when the first storm passed, they horrified no one as they horrified him who had uttered them. During many weeks of novitiate he had been oppressed by religious despair and believed he had committed that sin against the Holy Ghost which shuts the gate for ever. Anselmo—patient as an old ox in a stony field—had helped him through that blackness; yet deep self-abasement remained. His own salvation seemed insecure to him; and, more than that, he thought it a mockery of Heaven that he, so stained, should lift up prayer for a soul so innocent, comparatively, as Margaret’s. He had used to coax good Anselm and another kindly monk to pray for her; and they, like men who know what hunger is, had not prayed by halves.

But now, after that Sunday sermon, a balmy sense of God’s forgiveness and love descended on him; and with it came this new courage: he prayed for Margaret. From that hour it became his daily habit—the one purified tie that by memory bound his heart to earth. His family were to him as if they had never been. The Church would not share with earth; nor could even the Church cure the great love without annihilating the smaller ones.

On most days of that journey he felt little spring of life within him, save when he stood in the pulpit and words, like water from a hill, ran clear. The other times were when he could ease another creature’s pain.

Once, in a poor village where the hens scratched under carts and the dust tasted of sunburnt clay, he found a young man tarantula-bitten—or, as often happens, persuaded by terror and gossip that he was. For two days the youth had had no sleep and was in convulsions most extraordinary, leaping, twisting, striking the wall with his fists as if he would beat a door through it. The village musicians had tried their art—pipes and a shrill fiddle—and only whipped the frenzy higher; for there are sounds that heal and sounds that wound, and the difference is as subtle as the difference between salt that seasons and salt that scalds.

Exhaustion and death follow such a disorder when it mounts so far.

Clement, hearing the tale, sent for a psaltery. The instrument came, smelling of old wood and sweat from many hands, its strings dulled by dust. He tried the sufferer with soothing melodies such as had calmed novices in their first nights, and had made even an angry dog lay his head down; but if the village tunes had maddened the patient, Clement’s seemed to crush him. The youth groaned and moaned under them and fell grovelling on the floor as if the very harmony pressed him down.

Clement listened—listened not only with ear but with that inward attention a preacher learns from long silence. At intervals the young man’s lips moved. Clement bent; he caught it: the patient was whispering a tune, a strange crooked little air that had no existence in the world. It was born of fever, stitched together by a mind turned in upon itself—yet it had its own logic, its own small home of consonance.

Clement learned it, phrase by phrase, and played it back to him.

At once the young man’s face brightened, as if a window had been opened. He rose and marched about the room on the light fantastic toe, enjoying his own creation returned to him in sound. Clement played until his fingers ached as if pricked by needles; yet at last he had the satisfaction of seeing the youth sink, quiet and complacent, into sleep—lulled by the strange cradle-song his own mind had made. That sleep saved his life. Clement taught the tune to another in case it should be needed again, and went forward with his heart a little warmer, pondering how a man may be torn to pieces by noise, and yet gathered whole by the one sound that matches his inner weather.

On another day he came upon a mob dragging a decently dressed man along the street. The man struggled and shouted, but in a language none there understood; it rang sharp and island-hard among the Italian vowels, as a crow’s caw among doves. He had walked into their town erect and sprightly, waving a mulberry branch over his head to shade himself from the heat. The townsfolk had first stared as if their eyes lied to them; then, as if one mind had leapt from body to body, they pounced and hauled him before the podestà.

Clement went with them. On the way he edged near and spoke quietly to the prisoner in Italian. No answer. In French; in German; in Dutch. Still none. At last the man tried Clement in tolerable Latin, with a sharpish accent, as of a blade honed on stone.

He said he was an Englishman, oppressed by the Italian sun, and had taken a bough from the nearest tree to save his head.

“In my country,” he cried, “anybody is welcome to what grows on the highway. Confound the fools; I am ready to pay for it. But here is all Italy up in arms about a twig and a handful of leaves!”

The podestà, pig-headed as an ox and as proud, would have clapped the dogged islander into prison; but Clement mediated. With some difficulty he made the stranger understand that silkworms—and therefore mulberry leaves—were held sacred, being under the Sovereign’s protection and a source of revenue; and he urged upon the podestà that ignorance of such laws was natural in a far country where the tree itself might be unknown.

The islander, opinionated even in humility, tipped the trembling scale by drawing out a long purse and repeating his first theory, that the whole matter was mercantile.

“Quid damni?” he demanded. “Dic; et cito solvam.”

The podestà sniffed the gold as a dog sniffs meat: he fined him a ducat for the Duke—about the value of the whole tree—and quietly pocketed the coin.

The Englishman shook off his ire the instant he was free. He laughed heartily at the affair and was grateful to Clement with that open-handed gratitude which, like good ale, makes no secret of itself.

“You are too good for this hole of a country, father,” said he. “Come to England. That is the only place in the world. I was an uneasy fool to leave it and wander among mulberries and their idiots. I am a Kentish squire, educated at Cambridge. My name is Rolfe; my place Betshanger. The man and the house are both at your service. Come over and stay till Doomsday. We sit down forty to dinner every day at Betshanger. One more or one less at the board will not be seen. You shall end your days with me and my heirs if you will. What an Englishman says he means.”

And he gave Clement a hearty grip to seal it.

“I will visit thee some day, my son,” said Clement; “but not to weary thy hospitality.”

The Englishman then begged him to shrive him.

“I know not what will become of my soul,” said he; “I live like a heathen since I left England.”

Clement consented gladly, and soon the islander was on his knees by the roadside, confessing the last month’s sins with the same directness with which he had spoken of mulberries and money. When Clement found him, for all his bluster, a pious son of the Church, he let slip that he too was bound for England.

Then he asked, as men asked in every cloistered corridor, whether it was true that England was overrun with Lollards and Wickliffites.

The other coloured a little, as if the question had touched not the skin but something beneath.

“There be black sheep in every land,” said he; but after a moment he added gravely, “Holy father, hear the truth about these heretics. None are better disposed towards Holy Church than we English. But we are ourselves, and by ourselves. We love our own ways, and above all, our own tongue. The Norman could conquer our bill-hooks, but not our tongues; and hard they tried it for many a long year by law and proclamation. Our good foreign priests utter God to plain English folk in Latin, or in some French or Italian lingo—like the bleating of a sheep. Then come the fox Wickliff and his crew, and read Him out of His own book in plain English, that all men’s hearts warm to. Who can withstand this? God forgive me, I believe the English would turn deaf ears to Saint Peter himself, spoke he not to them in the tongue their mothers sowed in their ears and hearts along with mothers’ kisses.”

He broke off, then added, as if to save his own orthodoxy, “I say not this for myself. I am Cambridge-bred, and good words come not amiss to me in Latin; but for the people in general. Clavis ad corda Anglorum est lingua materna.”

Those words struck Clement with a force that had nothing of argument in it; it was like recognizing a scent long lost—the first loaf of one’s childhood, the smoke of one’s own hearth. He, who had suffered the tearing of his own speech in foreign lands and had clung to Latin as a plank in a flood, heard in this blunt squire’s saying the whole sorrow of tongues crushed under dominant tongues, and the hidden power that lies in the mother-syllables.

“My son,” said Clement, “blessed be the hour I met thee; for thy words are sober and wise. But alas! how shall I learn your English tongue? No book have I.”

“I would give you my book of hours, father,” cried Rolfe, quick as bargaining blood. “’Tis in English and Latin, cheek by jowl. But then—what would become of my poor soul, wanting my ‘hours’ in a strange land? Stay: you are a holy man, and I an honest one. Let us make a bargain. You to pray for me every day for two months, and I to give you my book of hours. Here it is. What say you to that?”

His eyes sparkled; he was all on fire with mercantility, even at the gate of confession.

Clement smiled gently, and from his girdle drew a manuscript of his own, written in Latin and Italian.

“See, my son,” said he, “Heaven hath foreseen our several needs and given us the means to satisfy them. Let us change books; and, my dear son, I will give thee my poor prayers and welcome, not sell them thee. I love not religious bargains.”

The islander was delighted.

“So shall I learn the Italian tongue without risk to my eternal weal. Near is my purse, but nearer is my soul.”

He forced money on Clement. In vain the friar told him it was contrary to his vow to carry more than was barely needful.

“Lay it out for the good of the Church and of my soul,” said Rolfe. “I ask you not to keep it, but take it you must and shall.”

He gripped Clement’s hand again; Clement kissed him on the brow and blessed him; and they went each his way, the one carrying away Italian words as if they were spices, the other carrying away English prayers as if they were stored grain.

A mile or so after they parted, Clement saw, in the deep shade of a great chestnut-tree at the edge of a grove, two tired wayfarers lying like men felled by heat. Beside them stood a little cart, and in it a printing-press—rude, clumsy, built almost like a vine-press, as if some peasant’s knowledge of crushing grapes had been turned to crushing ink upon paper. A jaded mule drooped in the shafts.

Clement stopped.

So he stood face to face with his old enemy.

As he looked on the press and on the honest blue-eyed faces of the wearied craftsmen, he looked back as upon a dream at the bitterness he had once felt against that machine—this contrivance that could set words free like swarming bees, sweet or stinging. Now his eyes softened.

“Sweynheim,” he said softly.

The men started up as if an arrow had struck the air.

“Pannartz!” he added.

They scuttled into the wood like startled deer and were seen no more.

Clement stood amazed, puzzling. Presently a face peeped from behind a tree, pale with superstition and fear.

“What fear ye?” Clement called.

A quavering voice answered, “Say rather, by what magic you—a stranger—can call us by our names! I never clapt eyes on you till now.”

“O superstition!” Clement sighed. “I know ye, as all good workmen are known—by your works. Come hither and I will tell ye.”

They advanced gingerly from different sides, each measuring his courage by the other’s step, until they stood within speaking distance.

“My children,” said Clement, “I saw a Lactantius in Rome, printed by Sweynheim and Pannartz, disciples of Fust.”

“D’ye hear that, Pannartz?” cried Sweynheim, his face lighting as a hearth does when a coal is roused. “Our work has gotten to Rome already.”

“And by your blue eyes and flaxen hair I wist ye were Germans,” Clement went on; “and the printing-press spoke for itself. Who then should ye be but Fust’s disciples, Pannartz and Sweynheim?”

The honest men were abashed that they had suspected magic in plain wit.

“The good father hath his wits about him, that is all,” said Pannartz.

“Ay,” said Sweynheim, rubbing his neck, “and with those wits would he could tell us how to get this tired beast to the next town.”

“Yea,” added Pannartz, “and where to find money to pay for his meat and ours when we get there.”

“I will try,” said Clement. “Free the mule of the cart, and of all harness but the bare halter.”

They did so; and the animal, feeling the sudden liberty, lay down at once and rolled on his back in the dust like a kitten, legs in the air, as if joy were nothing but soundless relief in the spine. Clement watched, and his mouth softened.

“I told ye,” said he, “I would rise up a new mule. His Creator hath taught him this art to refresh himself, which the nobler horse knoweth not.”

Then, as to money, he remembered Rolfe’s forced charity.

“Know,” said he, “that a worthy Englishman hath entrusted me with a certain sum to bestow in alms. To whom can I better give a stranger’s money than to strangers? Take it; and be kind to some Englishman—or other stranger—in his need. May all nations learn to love one another one day.”

Tears stood in the workmen’s eyes. They took the money with heartfelt thanks.

“It is your nation we are bound to thank and bless, good father—if we but knew it.”

“My nation is the Church,” said Clement simply.

He would have gone then; but the honest fellows would not let him depart empty-handed. They had no silver nor gold to offer, but they had their craft. They took the press out of the cart and, while Clement fed the mule and rubbed its ears until it stopped shivering at flies, they bustled about—now on the white-hot road, now in the deep green coolness under the chestnuts, now half in and half out like men at the door of a bakehouse on a cold day—setting, inking, adjusting. The smell of ink rose sharp as pitch, and the press’s wooden throat groaned in the heat as they worked its screw.

Presently they printed a quarto sheet of eight pages that was already set up; they had not type enough to print two sheets at once. After the slow preliminaries—letters arranged, ink spread, paper laid—came the marvel: the pull, all in a moment, like the swift closing of a lid.

Clement stared as if he had seen bread rise in a cold oven.

“What,” he cried, “are all these words really fast upon the paper? Is it verily certain they will not go as swiftly as they came? And you took me for a magician! ’Tis Augustine, De Civitate Dei. My sons, you carry here the very wings of knowledge. Oh, never abuse this great craft. Print no ill books. They would fly abroad countless as locusts, and lay waste men’s souls.”

The workmen swore, in the simple fierce sincerity of good craftsmen, that they would sooner put their hands under the screw than so abuse their goodly art.

So they parted. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this world; and the road, like a long table, seats companions for a moment, then shifts them away.

At a town in Tuscany the holy friar had a sudden and strange encounter with the past. He fell in with one of those motley companies of pilgrims—patricians and plebeians, piety and profligacy mingled like flour and grit in the same sack—who travel with prayers on their lips and gossip in their pockets. They lodged in an immense barn belonging to the inn. The place smelled of straw, old wine, damp cloaks, and the cook’s onions frying somewhere beyond a wall—homely scents, threaded with the sour breath of too many bodies. Above it all ran the soundscape of travel: coughing, rosaries clicking like dried seeds, boots scraped on boards, a distant mule braying in complaint, and a fife somewhere playing too sharply for the hour.

Clement, dusty and wearied and no lover of idle talk, sat in a corner with Rolfe’s book of hours, studying the English page and making it out as much by his own Dutch as by the Latin beside it. He tasted each foreign word as if it were a new herb—recognizable in family, yet strange on the tongue—and thought how little of a man’s soul can be carried over when the mother-speech is taken away.

Presently a servant brought a bucket half-full of water and set it at Clement’s feet. Another followed with two towels. Then a woman came forward. She crossed herself, knelt at the bucket without a word, pushed her sleeves up—nay, drew them wholly off—and motioned to him to put his feet into the water.

It was some lady of rank doing penance. She wore a mask scarce an inch broad, yet effectual. And she handled the friar’s feet more delicately than those do who are born to such offices, as if the very touch were part of her prayer. Such penances were not uncommon. Clement, though he had small faith in contrition that shows itself chiefly in spectacle, received her service as a matter of course.

But presently she sighed—deeply; and with that sigh, her head bowed low over her menial office, she seemed so broken with inward weight that he pitied her.

“Can I aught for your soul’s weal, my daughter?” he asked calmly.

She shook her head with a faint sob.

“Nought, holy father, nought—only to hear the sin of her who is most unworthy to touch thy holy feet. ’Tis part of my penance to tell sinless men how vile I am.”

“Speak, my daughter.”

“Father,” she whispered, bending lower and lower, “these hands of mine look white, but they are stained with blood—the blood of the man I loved.”

Clement’s foot drew back an instant, a movement not of doctrine but of flesh; and the lady, seeing it, gave a little moan as if struck.

“Ah me! What shall I do? All holy things shrink from me.”

“Culpa mea! culpa mea!” cried Clement eagerly. “My daughter, it was an unworthy movement of earthly weakness, for which I shall do penance. Judge not the Church by her feebler servants. Not her foot, but her bosom, is offered to thee, repenting truly. Take courage, then, and purge thy conscience of its load.”

Then the lady, in a trembling whisper, hurried and cringing, as if she feared the Church would strike her for what she had done, poured out her confession.

“He was a stranger, and base-born, but beautiful as Spring, and wise beyond his years. I loved him. I had not the prudence to conceal my love. Nobles courted me. I ne’er thought one of humble birth could reject me. I showed him my heart—oh, shame of my sex! He drew back; yet he admired me; but innocently. He loved another; and he was constant. I resorted to a woman’s wiles; they availed not. I borrowed the wickedness of men, and threatened his life, and to tell his true lover he died false to her.”

Clement’s toes curled in the water; not from the woman’s touch, but from the picture she painted.

“Ah! you shrink—your foot trembles. Am I not a monster? Then he wept and prayed to me for mercy; then my good angel helped me; I bade him leave Rome. Gerard, Gerard, why did you not obey me? I thought he was gone. But two months after this I met him. Never shall I forget it. I was descending the Tiber in my galley when he came up it with a gay company, and at his side a woman beautiful as an angel, but bold and bad. That woman claimed me aloud for her rival. Traitor and hypocrite, he had exposed me to her, and to all the loose tongues in Rome. In terror and revenge I hired a bravo.”

Her hands, so gentle upon his skin, trembled as she spoke, and the water in the bucket made faint rings.

“When he was gone on his bloody errand I wavered—too late. The dagger I had hired struck. He never came back to his lodgings. He was dead. Alas! perhaps he was not so much to blame; none have ever cast his name in my teeth. His poor body is not found—or I should kiss its wounds, and slay myself upon it. All around his very name seems silent as the grave to which this murderous hand hath sent him.”

As she moved, Clement’s eye was drawn to her arm—shapely, white, and familiar in its grace. A recognition, like a sudden taste of bitter almond, went through him.

“And oh! he was so young to die,” she whispered. “A poor thoughtless boy that had fallen a victim to that bad woman’s arts, and she had made him tell her everything. Monster of cruelty, what penance can avail me? Oh, holy father, what shall I do?”

Clement’s lips moved in prayer, but no words came. Duty lay before him, yet he could not see it clear. Then the woman took his feet and began to dry them. She rested his foot upon her soft arm and pressed it with the towel so gently she seemed incapable of hurting a fly. And yet her mouth had just spoken murder.

While Clement still prayed for wisdom, a tear fell upon his foot—hot as broth on cold skin.

It decided him.

“My daughter,” said he, “I myself have been a great sinner.”

“You, father?”

“I; quite as great a sinner as thou, though not in the same way. The devil hath gins and snares, as well as traps. But penitence softened my impious heart, and then gratitude remoulded it. Therefore, seeing you penitent, I hope you can be grateful to Him who hath been more merciful to you than you have to your fellow-creature. Daughter, the Church sends you comfort.”

“Comfort to me?” she breathed. “Ah, never—unless it can raise my victim from the dead.”

“Take this crucifix in thy hand, fix thine eyes on it, and listen to me.”

“Yes, father—but let me thoroughly dry your feet first; ’tis ill sitting in wet feet; and you are the holiest man of all whose feet I have washed. I know it by your voice.”

“Woman, I am not. As for my feet, they can wait their turn. Obey thou me.”

“Yes, father,” she said humbly; yet with a woman’s evasive pertinacity she wound one towel swiftly round the foot she was drying and placed his other upon the dry napkin; then took the crucifix and bowed over it.

The barn’s noises—snoring, murmured prayers, a cough that scraped like a file—fell away from Clement as if a door had been shut. In the low solemn tones of the friar, words dropped, one by one, like measured grains into a scale.

“My daughter, he you murdered—in intent—was one Gerard, a Hollander. He loved a creature as men should love none but their Redeemer and His Church. Heaven chastised him. A letter came to Rome. She was dead.”

“Poor Gerard! Poor Margaret!” moaned the penitent.

Clement’s voice faltered at the name, as if his throat had been touched by ash. But by a strong effort he recovered his calm.

“His feeble nature yielded, body and soul, to the blow. He was stricken down with fever. He revived only to rebel against Heaven. He said, ‘There is no God.’”

“Poor, poor Gerard!”

“Poor Gerard?” Clement’s tone sharpened, not with cruelty, but with truth. “Thou feeble, foolish woman! Nay—wicked, impious Gerard. He plunged into vice and soiled his eternal jewel. Those you met him with were his daily companions. But know, rash creature, that the seeming woman you took to be his leman was but a boy dressed in woman’s habits to flout the others—a fair boy called Andrea. What that Andrea said to thee I know not; but be sure neither he nor any layman knows thy folly. This Gerard, rebel against Heaven, was no traitor to thee—unworthy.”

The lady moaned as if in bodily pain. The crucifix trembled in her trembling hands, and the small sound of its quiver was louder to Clement than the whole barn.

“Courage,” he said. “Comfort is at hand.”

“From crime he fell into despair; and bent on destroying his soul, he stood one night by Tiber, resolved on suicide. He saw one watching him. It was a bravo.”

“Holy saints!”

“He begged the bravo to despatch him. He offered him all his money, to slay him body and soul. The bravo would not. Then this desperate sinner, not softened even by that refusal, flung himself into Tiber.”

“Ah!”

“And the assassin saved his life. Thou hadst chosen for the task Lodovico, husband of Teresa, whom this Gerard had saved at sea—her and her infant child.”

For a heartbeat the penitent could not breathe. Then the words burst from her like a bell’s release after a long held note.

“He lives! he lives! he lives! I am faint.”

Clement, fearing the crucifix might fall, took it from her hands. A shower of tears came, relieving her as rain relieves a parched garden. He gave her time; then continued, his voice even.

“Ay, he lives—thanks to thee and thy wickedness, guided to his eternal good by an almighty and all-merciful hand. Thou art his greatest earthly benefactor.”

“Where is he? Where? Where?”

“What is that to thee?”

“Only to see him alive. To beg him on my knees forgive me. I swear to you I will never presume again to—How could I? He knows all. Oh, shame! Father—does he know?”

“All.”

“Then never will I meet his eye; I should sink into the earth. But I would repair my crime. I would watch his life unseen. He shall rise in the world, whence I so nearly thrust him—poor soul. The Cæsare, my family, are all-powerful in Rome; and I am near their head.”

“My daughter,” said Clement coldly, “he you call Gerard needs nothing man can do for him. Saved by a miracle from double death, he hath left the world and taken refuge from sin and folly in the bosom of the Church.”

“A priest?”

“A priest, and a friar.”

“A friar?” Her breath caught. “Then you are not his confessor? Yet you know all. That gentle voice—”

She raised her head slowly and peered at him through her narrow mask, as one might peer through a slit into a bright kitchen where familiar labour goes on. The next moment she uttered a faint shriek and fell forward, laying her brow upon his bare feet.
