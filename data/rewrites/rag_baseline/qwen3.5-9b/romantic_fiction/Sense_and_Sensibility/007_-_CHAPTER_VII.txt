Barton Park sat roughly half a mile from the cottage, a substantial estate screened from the valley road by a projecting ridge. Inside, the Middletons presided over a household defined by a specific brand of opulence: Sir John derived his gratification from the sheer scale of the property, while Lady Middleton found hers in the meticulous execution of domestic rituals. Their home was perpetually crowded, a testament to their inability to entertain themselves without external validation. This constant influx of guests masked a profound intellectual poverty; their lives revolved entirely around the binary functions of hunting and child-rearing. Sir John hunted and shot, activities available only seasonally, while Lady Middleton humored her offspring year-round. Her vanity centered on the perfection of her table settings and the flawless arrangement of her rooms, a performance art she enjoyed above all else. Conversely, Sir John craved noise and volume, delighting in filling his hall until it overflowed with youth. He was a benefactor to the neighborhood's juveniles, organizing outdoor feasts in summer and hosting endless private balls in winter, regardless of the attendees' appetites.

The arrival of the Dashwoods represented a strategic acquisition for Sir John. Young, pretty, and seemingly devoid of pretension, they fit his criteria perfectly. His benevolent nature allowed him to extend genuine kindness to those he deemed unfortunate, yet settling a family of women in his manor also served his sporting interests; he preferred to exclude non-hunters from his domain unless they were decorative additions. Upon meeting them at the door, Sir John greeted the sisters with hearty sincerity, leading them to the drawing room where he reiterated his earlier lament regarding the scarcity of eligible young men. He noted that aside from himself, only one guest—a mature friend staying at the park—would be present. He expressed hope that the small party would not dampen their spirits, attributing the shortage to everyone else's prior engagements. Fortunately, Lady Middleton's mother, Mrs. Jennings, had arrived that very hour. A cheerful, rotund, and somewhat vulgar woman, she filled the silence with rapid-fire anecdotes about love and matrimony, joking that the sisters must have left their hearts back in Sussex.

Marianne bristled at the public scrutiny of her feelings, turning urgently toward Elinor to gauge her composure, a gesture that caused Elinor significant distress given the triviality of the provocation. Colonel Brandon, Sir John's companion, stood in stark contrast to the host. Silent and grave, he appeared ill-suited to friendship, much like Lady Middleton was to her husband or Mrs. Jennings to her daughter-in-law. Though perceived by the younger women as an aged bachelor past fifty-five, his features lacked conventional beauty, yet his demeanor possessed a quiet sensibility and an address that commanded respect. While the gathering offered little in terms of stimulating conversation, the collective mediocrity of the group rendered Colonel Brandon's seriousness tolerable, especially when weighed against the repulsive coldness of Lady Middleton.

The atmosphere shifted dramatically with the post-dinner entrance of the four Middleton children. Noisy and destructive, they physically overwhelmed their mother, tearing at her garments and reducing all discourse to matters of their own amusement. Later, when Marianne revealed her musical ability, the room fell into a tense anticipation. At the invitation of the assembled crowd, she performed several songs Lady Middleton had introduced upon her marriage—pieces that had presumably gathered dust on the piano since then, as the hostess had abandoned music years ago despite her mother's accounts of her former skill and her own professed love for it. Marianne sang with evident talent, receiving loud, perfunctory applause from Sir John, who interrupted every measure to boast to the others. Lady Middleton, however, remained critical, chastising him for his distraction and demanding Marianne repeat a piece she had just concluded. Throughout this display, Colonel Brandon remained the sole observer of genuine merit. He listened without the manic enthusiasm of the host or the performative indifference of the others. Marianne felt a distinct respect for him, recognizing that while his appreciation may not match the ecstatic fervor required for perfect sympathy, it was infinitely superior to the grotesque insensitivity displayed by the rest of the party. She granted him the benefit of the doubt, acknowledging that a man of fifty-five might simply have outlived the acute sensitivity necessary for such refined enjoyment.
