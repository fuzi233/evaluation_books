The golden light of late afternoon bathed the common as Maria and Harry strolled arm-in-arm, the crunch of gravel beneath their feet marking their unhurried pace.

“Tell me of those children,” Maria ventured, her voice soft, “the ones who tended you after your fall. I saw them darting about like sparrows when I visited.”

Harry’s face brightened. “The Lamberts? The finest people alive! Mrs. Lambert knew my mother years ago in Europe. The colonel—ah, a true gentleman, seasoned in service. They’ve been kindness itself. I must repay them somehow.” His hand drifted to his pocket, heavy with winnings.

Maria’s laugh was light, though her grip on his arm tightened. “Dolls for the little ones, perhaps?”

“Dolls! Hester and Theo are hardly children,” Harry protested. “Theo sings like an angel, composes her own airs. Hester reads Greek, I vow, and both play the harpsichord as if born to it.”

Maria’s smile faltered. “I scarcely noticed them. They seemed so young.”

“Young? You women are all alike! At home, if George or I praised a girl, Mother and Mountain would pick her to pieces.”

“I would never fault those who cared for you,” Maria murmured, eyes downcast. “Though I confess, I envied them.”

Harry, oblivious, patted her hand. “You’re too good, Maria.”

“No,” she sighed. “I am not good at all.”

Ahead, servants in livery clustered near the tea-garden, their laughter carrying on the breeze. Harry’s gaze followed them, restless.

“I’ve a mind to celebrate,” he declared. “Morris and March didn’t know what hit them. A few more leaps, and I’d have cleared six feet!”

Maria’s pride warred with worry. “You must beware of play, Harry. It ruined half our family—Castlewood, my brother Will, even Aunt Bernstein.”

Harry waved her off. “I’ll match March or Morris any day. Besides, what’s life without a little sport?”

The fiddlers in the pavilion struck up a lively tune, signaling the evening’s revelries. Maria’s shoulders slumped. “Aunt will be waiting.”

Harry barely nodded, already scanning the crowd.

---

The gaming tables hummed with murmured bets and clinking glasses. Bernstein held court in her corner, a fortress of cards and calculation, while Maria drifted between guests, her smiles brittle.

“Cut for us, Maria!” March called as she passed. “Warrington’s bleeding us dry.”

She hesitated. “You’re not playing deep, Harry?”

“Sixpences,” March assured, though Morris’s grimace suggested otherwise.

Harry’s hand was aflush with trumps. Maria lingered, her shadow falling across his cards.

“Double the stakes?” March suggested, casual as a man discussing the weather.

“If you like,” Harry agreed.

Maria’s breath caught. *This is how it begins.*

---

*Our Virginian is a marvel. He bested me at the jump, emptied my purse at cards, and now has half the Wells sighing after him. Even that harpy Cattarina simpers in his direction.*

*He’s won enough to buy a principality—Chesterfield’s six hundred, Morris’s pride, and my last guinea. The man is cursed with luck. Or blessed. Depending on one’s perspective.*

---

The candles burned low. Harry leaned back, gold gleaming in his palm. Across the table, March’s smile was sharp as a blade.

“Again?”

Harry grinned. “Why not?”

Maria turned away. The night stretched before them, endless and glittering—a siren’s call neither seemed inclined to resist.
