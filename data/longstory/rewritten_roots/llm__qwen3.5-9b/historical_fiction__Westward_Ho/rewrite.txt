The history of England is often told in terms of kings and battles, yet there exists another chronicle written in the blood of the common man and the dreams of the adventurous spirit. It is a tale that begins not with a crown, but with a white bird seen by Mr. Oxenham; it follows Amyas as he returns home for the first time, bearing secrets that will alter the course of his destiny. We shall witness the exploits of two gentlemen from Wales who, though they hunted with hounds, found themselves running alongside deer—a metaphor for their own elusive lives.

Love, too, has its crooked paths, and we shall see how two hearts were entangled in ways neither could foresee. The narrative takes us to Clovelly Court in days long past, through the winding combs of the far west, and into the tragic life of John Oxenham of Plymouth. From here, the story expands to include the founding of the Noble Brotherhood of the Rose, the peculiar observances of Christmas by Amyas, and the Mayor of Biddeford's bold act of baiting his hook with his own flesh.

Our heroes encounter the Pope's legate, share meals at Annery House where the bridge itself seemed to dine, and welcome back the Golden Hind. Salvation Yeo performs deeds of valor against the King of the Gubbings, while Mr. John Brimblecombe learns the true weight of an oath. The chivalrous adventures of the Good Ship *Rose* carry them to Barbadòs, where they find no men, only mysteries. They seize pearls at Margarita, face perils at La Guayra, and engage in a fierce contest between Spanish bloodhounds and English mastiffs.

Under the trees at Higuerote, they take communion amidst danger, confront the Inquisition in the Indies, and stand upon the banks of the Meta River. Amyas faces temptation from the devil himself, helps secure a golden train, and captures a great galleon. Salvation Yeo reunites with a lost little maid, and Amyas makes his third return home. The Virginia fleet is halted by royal command, Admiral John Hawkins testifies against Croakers, and the shadow of the Great Armada looms large.

In the end, Amyas throws his sword into the sea, letting the apple fall westward—ho! This is the story of those who dared to go forth when others stayed behind, a saga of courage, faith, and the relentless pursuit of liberty across the known world.

Any traveler who has wandered through the lush landscapes of North Devon knows the little white town of Bideford. It climbs gently upward from its wide tidal river, paved with golden sands, crossing the ancient, multi-arched bridge where salmon await the autumn floods. Beyond lies the pleasant upland to the west. Above the town, hills close in, cushioned with deep oak forests, through which jagged crags of fern-fringed slate protrude. Below, the terrain lowers into softly rounded knolls and fertile patches of red and green, sinking finally into the vast, hazy expanse of salt marshes and rolling sandhills where the Torridge meets the Taw. Together, they flow quietly toward the great surf of the bar and the eternal thunder of the Atlantic swell.

Pleasantly, the old town stands there beneath its soft, Italian-like sky, fanned day and night by the fresh ocean breeze. This wind keeps away the biting winter frosts and the fierce heat of the Midlands. It has stood thus for perhaps eight hundred years, since the first Grenville, a cousin of the Conqueror, returned from conquering South Wales. He gathered around him loyal Saxon serfs, free Norse rovers with their golden hair, and dark Silurian Britons from the shores of Swansea. Their mingled blood still grants the coastal people of the neighboring counties their strength, intellect, and even today, their distinctive beauty of face and form.

Yet, at the time of which I write, Bideford was more than merely a pleasant country town with a quiet quay. It was one of England's chief ports. It supplied seven ships to fight the Armada. Even more than a century later, chroniclers noted that "it sent more vessels to the northern trade than any port in England, saving (a strange juxtaposition!) London and Topsham." It was the center of a local civilization and enterprise, modest perhaps compared to the massive efforts of our own day, but who dares despise the day of small things if it proves to be the dawn of mighty ones?

England owes the foundation of her naval and commercial glory to the sea-life and labor of Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, Plymouth (then a mere village), and many other little western towns. It was the men of Devon—the Drakes, Hawkins, Gilberts, Raleighs, Grenvilles, and Oxenhams—a host of "forgotten worthies" whom we shall one day honor—that she owes her commerce, her colonies, and indeed her very existence. Had they not first crippled the ill-gotten resources of the Spaniard through their West Indian raids, and then crushed his final, huge effort in Britain's Salamis during the glorious fight of 1588, what would we be today but a papal appendage of a world tyranny as cruel as heathen Rome, and far more diabolical?

I write this book in memory of these men—their voyages, battles, faith, valor, heroic lives, and no less heroic deaths. Should I occasionally seem to warm into a style somewhat too stiff and pompous, please excuse me for the sake of my subject. It fits better to be sung than spoken, and proclaimed to all true English hearts not as a novel, but as an epic (which some man may yet undertake to write). It carries the same great message that the songs of Troy, the Persian Wars, and the trophies of Marathon and Salamis spoke to the hearts of all true Greeks of old.

One bright summer afternoon in the year of grace 1575, a tall and handsome boy lingered along the Bideford quay. Clad in a scholar's gown, carrying a satchel and slate, he watched wistfully the shipping and the sailors. Just as he passed the bottom of the High Street, he found himself opposite one of the many taverns overlooking the river. Inside an open bay window sat merchants and gentlemen discussing their afternoon drink of sack. Outside the door, a group of sailors had gathered, listening intently to someone standing in the midst of them.

Alive with interest in any news of the sea, the boy stepped up to join the sailor-lads peeping and whispering under the arms of the men. Thus, he caught the following speech, delivered in a loud, bold voice with a strong Devonshire accent and a fair sprinkling of oaths.

"If you don't believe me, go and see, or stay here and turn blue with mold. I tell you, as I am a gentleman, I saw it with these very eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo there through a window in the lower room. We measured the heap, as I am a christened man, seventy feet long, ten feet broad, and twelve feet high—silver bars! Each bar weighed between thirty and forty pounds."

"And what did Captain Drake say?"

"He said, 'There, my lads of Devon, I've brought you to the mouth of the world's treasure-house, and it's your own fault now if you don't sweep it out as empty as a stockfish.'"

"Why didn't you bring some of it home, then, Mr. Oxenham?"

"Why weren't you there to help carry it? We would have brought it away safely, and Young Drake and I had already broken the outer door. But Captain Drake went off in a dead faint. When we looked, he had a wound in his leg you could fit three fingers into, and his boots were full of blood, soaked for an hour or more. Yet his heart was set on fighting; he never knew until he collapsed. Then his brother and I carried him to the boats, him kicking and struggling, bidding us let him go on with the fight, though every step he took sank into a pool of blood. So we escaped. Tell me, ye sons of shotten herrings, wasn't it worth more to save him than the dirty silver? Silver we can get again, brave boys. There's more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave all the streets of the west country. But of such captains as Franky Drake, Heaven never makes but one at a time. If we lose him, goodbye to England's luck!"

The speaker was a tall, sturdy figure with a florid, black-bearded face and bold, restless dark eyes. Leaning against the wall with crossed legs and arms akimbo, he appeared to the schoolboy a true *magnifico*, some prince or duke at least. His dress defied all sumptuary laws of the time: a suit of crimson velvet, slightly worn; a long Spanish rapier and a pair of daggers with gaudy hilts at his side; fingers sparkling with rings; several gold chains about his neck; and large earrings in his ears, behind one of which a red rose was tucked jauntily among his glossy black curls. On his head sat a broad velvet Spanish hat, lacking a feather but fastened with a great gold clasp holding a whole Quetzal bird. Its gorgeous plumage of fretted golden-green shone like a single precious stone.

As he finished speaking, he removed the hat and looked at the bird.

"Look ye, my lads, have you ever seen such a fowl before? That is the bird the old Indian kings of Mexico allowed no one to wear but themselves. Therefore I wear it—I, John Oxenham of South Tawton—as a sign to all brave lads of Devon that, as the Spaniards are masters of the Indians, we are the masters of the Spaniards!" He replaced his hat.

A murmur of applause followed, though one voice hinted that the Spaniards were too numerous for them.

"Too many? How many men did we take Nombre de Dios with? Seventy-three, and no more when we sailed out of Plymouth Sound. Five years have passed and vanished. It is nine o'clock on a still, bright November morning, yet the bells of Bideford church ring for the daily service two hours later than usual. They do not toll soberly as is customary; rather, they break forth every five minutes into a jocular peal, tumbling head-over-heels in ecstatic joy. The streets of Bideford resemble a garden blooming in every hue, swarming with sailors, burghers, wives, and daughters, all clad in festive attire. Garlands span the thoroughfares, and tapestries adorn every window. The ships anchored in the harbor fly every conceivable flag, unleashing a tumultuous roar of cannonades of all calibers. Every stable is packed with horses, and Sir Richard Grenville's house resembles a bustling tavern, filled with feasting, drinking, unsaddling, and the frantic activity of grooms and servants.

Along the small churchyard, densely packed with women, flows the gentle blood of North Devon—tall, stately men and fair ladies worthy of an era when the English gentry led the people through personal prowess and beauty as much as through intellect and education. Leading the procession is the Countess of Bath, escorted by Sir Richard Grenville with his cap in hand, for her lord, Earl Bourchier, remains in London with the Queen. Following them are the Bassetts from Umberleigh, the Carys from Clovelly, the Fortescues of Wear, Buckland, and various other quarters, alongside the Coles of Slade, Stukelys of Affton, St. Legers of Annery, Coffins of Portledge, and even the Coplestones traveling all the way from Eggesford, thirty miles distant. Last, but certainly not least, comes Sir John Chichester of Raleigh, trailed in single file, in the old patriarchal style, by his eight daughters and three of his five renowned sons—one currently fighting valiantly in Ireland to avenge a murdered brother, destined soon to rule wisely as Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast.

At the gate, Sir John meets his cousin of Arlington, followed by a retinue of four daughters and nineteen sons. The last has not yet passed the town hall, while the first stands at the Lychgate; both laugh as they yield the way to the elder, albeit shorter, branch of that prolific family tree. Thus, they proceed into the church, seated according to their rank—or as near as possible. This arrangement provokes several sour looks, shoves, and whispers among the high-born matrons until the churchwardens and sidesmen, overwhelmed by such a distinguished assembly, hustle frantically, turning red-faced and frantic. Desperate to avoid setting half the fine ladies of North Devon against one another in deadly feud, they implore the aid of the great Sir Richard himself to identify everyone, distinguish the elder from the younger branches, determine who bears eight quarterings in their coat of arms versus four, and thereby prevent civil strife. The elderly gentlemen are safely tucked away in the corporation pews, while the youth eagerly seek vantage points to gaze upon the ladies.

Finally, silence falls. Eyes turn toward the door, and distant music rises—a braying, screaming, thunderous melody of flutes, hautboys, drums, and trumpets—that reaches the very church doors before ceasing abruptly. The churchwardens rush to the entrance, rods in hand, amidst a general whisper and rustle accompanied by glad tears and blessings from many women and some men. The wonder of the day arrives, and the rector does not begin the morning service, but rather the ancient thanksgiving for a naval victory.

What has driven old Bideford wild with this "goodly joy and pious mirth"? Why are all eyes fixed with greedy admiration upon those four weather-beaten mariners, decorated with knots and ribbons by loving hands? Even more captivated are the gazes drawn to the gigantic figure walking before them: a beardless boy possessing the frame and stature of Hercules, towering like Saul of old, a head and shoulders above the entire congregation, with golden locks cascading over his shoulders. As the five men instinctively ascend to the altar and kneel before the rails, all eyes shift to the pew where Mrs. Leigh of Burrough hides her face between her hands, her hood rustling and shaking with joyful sobs.

These are Devon men, natives of Bideford: Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly. They are the first of all English mariners to sail the globe with Francis Drake, returning now to give thanks to God.

The tale is long. To understand how this came to pass, we must rewind slightly, almost to the point where we began in the previous chapter.

More than a year after Mr. Oxenham's departure, young Amyas lived quietly, adhering to his promises, aside from occasional bursts of fierce temper common to all vigorous young males, particularly boys of strong character. His formal scholarship advanced no faster than before, yet his home education flourished healthily. He rapidly became an expert archer, horseman, and swordsman in the traditional style of buckler practice. However, fate intervened when his father traveled to the Exeter Assizes on business. As was tragically common in those days, he contracted gaol fever from the prisoners, succumbed in the very courtroom, and died within a week.

Thus, Mrs. Leigh was left to God and her own soul, tasked with raising this young lion-cub for both this life and the next. She had loved her husband fervently and holily. He had often been peevish and melancholy—a disappointed man whose estate had been impoverished by his father's folly and his own youthful ambitions. Those ambitions had led him to Court, causing him to waste his heart and purse chasing a vain shadow. He possessed almost every talent save the ability to utilize them effectively. Though a scholar, courtier, and soldier, he found himself past forty without settled employment or purpose, hindered by a certain shyness, pride, or delicate honor that prevented him from playing a winning game in the very world he coveted. Consequently, he compensated with constant grumbling.

Ultimately, by sheer good fortune, he encountered a fair young Miss Foljambe of Derbyshire, then attending Queen Elizabeth's Court. Both weary of the world's sins, though she had witnessed fewer, they delighted in one another. Although the Queen grumbled at the lady for marrying and the gentleman for worshipping anyone but Her Majesty, they received permission to vanish from the little Babylon of Whitehall and settle peacefully at Burrough. In her, he found a treasure, realizing finally what he had sought.

Mrs. Leigh was, and remained from her youth, one of those noble old English churchwomen—free of superstition and devoid of severity—who stand as the finest features of that heroic age. Yet, a melancholy shadow clung to her. Recollections of her childhood transported her back to terrifying times when being a Protestant was an awful crime. She remembered, twenty-five years prior, the burning of poor blind Joan Waste at Derby and Mistress Joyce Lewis, a lady born like herself. Sometimes, even now, her mother's bitter cries echoed in her dreams, pleading with God to spare her fiery torment or grant her the strength to endure it, as her beloved mother had done before. That mother, of good Yorkshire stock, had served in Queen Catherine's bedchamber and been the bosom friend and disciple of Anne Askew. Mrs. Leigh had sat in Smithfield, her blood curdled with horror, watching the hapless court beauty—crippled by the rack—being carried in a chair to her fiery doom beside her fellow-courtier, Mr. Lascelles. The heavens seemed to speak their wrath and grief in solemn thunder and heavy rain falling upon the crackling pyre.

A profound sadness hung over her entire life, deepening during the reign of Queen Mary. As a notorious Protestant and heretic, she had been forced to hide among the hills and caverns of the Peak, surviving only through the love of her husband's tenants and his bold declaration that, despite being a devout Catholic, he would run through the bodies of any constable, justice, priest, bishop, or cardinal daring to execute the Queen's warrant upon his wife.

She escaped, but that darkness never fully lifted. Its hem fell upon the young girl who had shared her wanderings in the lonely hills. After marriage, she gave herself utterly to God. In surrendering to God, she surrendered to her husband, her children, and the poor of Northam Town. She remained deeply respected by the Grenvilles, Fortescues, Chichesters, and other gentle families who honored her husband's talents and enjoyed his wit. She practiced austerities that often drew kindly rebukes from her husband, yet she did so without superstitious thoughts of appeasing God's wrath or seeking His pleasure through pain. Her spirit had been trained in the freest and loftiest doctrines of Luther's school. That little mystic "German Theology"—to which the Great Reformer credited more than any book save the Bible and St. Augustine—was her counselor and comforter day and night.

Amyas slept that night a weary and troubled slumber. Beside his pillow, his mother and Frank watched, seeing clearly that his mind was consumed by restless dreams.

It was no surprise. Beyond the sheer excitement of the day, the memory of John Oxenham had seized hold of his thoughts. Throughout the evening, sitting in the bay-windowed room where he had last seen the missing adventurer, Amyas had replayed every glance and gesture of the lost man, marveling at his own obsession until sleep claimed him, only to renew the fantasy in his dreams.

In his dream, he found himself sailing endlessly westward, chasing the fading sun, pursuing a tiny vessel that bore John Oxenham's identity. A terrible urgency gripped him; if he could not catch up, disaster awaited. Yet the ship refused to move. Surrounding them were the Sargasso Sea's tangled weeds, coiling like snakes to block the bow. Still, he strained against the water, forcing his mind to imagine motion, until darkness swallowed the sky. Then the moon rose, and suddenly John Oxenham's ship drifted close. Its sails were rent and flapping; tar leaked from its seams; the wood itself seemed to rot away. What hung from the mainyard? A line of hanged men. Worse still, from the yardarm directly overhead, John Oxenham's corpse stared down with grave, luminous eyes. He beckoned, pointing desperately—not ahead, but backward along their course. When Amyas turned to look back, the snow-capped peaks of the Andes glittered in the moonlight. He realized he was in the South Seas once more, with all of America separating him from home. The corpse continued to point back, its eyes filled with agonizing longing, its lips moving as if to whisper an awful secret. Startled awake by a cry of terror, Amyas found himself in the small coved chamber of dear old Burrough, the gray autumn morning already filtering in.

Feverish and unable to rest, he rose after an hour of tossing and dressed to take a bath on his beloved pebble ridge. Passing his mother's door, he paused. The dim morning light revealed the bed, untouched since the previous night. Inside, his mother, clad in a long white nightgown, knelt at her prayer desk, deeply absorbed in devotion. Without a word, he slipped in and knelt beside her. She turned, smiling, and draped an arm around him, continuing her silent prayers. Why not? They were prayers for him, and he knew it; he prayed for her, for the lost John Oxenham, and for his vanished crew.

Finally, she rose. Standing above him, she brushed the yellow locks from his brow, gazing into his face with deep affection. Between these two souls, clear as glass, there was nothing to conceal. Each understood the other perfectly. After a long, mutual gaze, she stooped to kiss his forehead, then began to turn away when a single tear fell onto his skin. Her bare feet peeked out from beneath her dress. He bent down, kissing them repeatedly, then looked up as if seeking forgiveness.

"You have such pretty feet, mother!"

With instant feminine instinct, she hid them. Once a beauty herself, though her hair was now gray and her youth long past, she remained radiant to anyone who looked beyond surface appearances.

"Your dear father used to say so thirty years ago."

He walked down to the pebble ridge, where the ocean waves, over ages, had built a rampart of gray boulders—some two miles long, curved and smoothed with such cunning it seemed crafted by human hands—to protect a fertile strip of alluvial turf from the high tides of spring and autumn. Sniffing the crisp salt air like a seasoned sailor, he stripped and plunged into the breaking surf. He dove, rolled, and tossed amidst the foam with strong arms until he heard a hail from the shore. Looking up, he saw the tall figure of his cousin, Eustace, standing atop the rampart.

Amyas felt a twinge of disappointment. Love-struck as he was, he had dreamed the entire journey of Rose Salterne and had no desire for a companion to interrupt his reverie. Yet, having not seen Eustace in three years, civility dictated he scramble out and dress while his cousin paced the grass below.

Eustace Leigh was the son of a younger brother of the Leighs of Burrough, a man who had largely severed ties with his family and country by remaining a Catholic. Though born a Papist, he had converted to Protestantism under Edward VI, only to revert under Mary. However, unlike many others who switched allegiance again under Elizabeth, Eustace possessed enough integrity to stop there. Thus, he remained a Catholic, living in seclusion in a large, rambling, shadowy house known as "Chapel," perched on the Atlantic cliffs in Moorwinstow parish, near Sir Richard Grenville's estate at Stow. The penal laws rarely bothered him; they targeted only those who made conspiracy and rebellion core tenets of their faith, or those foolish enough to demand martyrdom despite the counsel of Burleigh. Furthermore, his remote location served as a perfect sanctuary. Dozens of plots could be hatched there unheard of, and Jesuits and seminary priests moved in and out unnoticed, finding a peculiar thrill in their clandestine lives—celebrating Mass in secret chambers in the roof, playing at persecution, and preaching of hiding in caves.

Once, when the zealous local parson discovered the presence of "mass priests" and complained formally to Sir Richard, invoking the Act of the Fourteenth of Elizabeth, the knight merely rebuked him soundly as a fanatical Puritan and told him to mind his own business. Temporal authority kept a firm grip on spiritual affairs in those days, and since Mr. Thomas Leigh paid his tithes faithfully, the parson subsided, content to eat Mr. Leigh's dinners and ignore the vocation of Father Francis, who sat opposite him disguised as a layman and tutor.

Yet, the burden of conscience weighed heavily on poor Mr. Thomas Leigh regarding certain lands formerly belonging to the Abbey of Hartland. He feared losing them for holding them, yet he did not fully believe he would, nor did he surrender them—a compromise typical of many "Catholic" subjects of Mary Tudor, whose consciensces demanded return to the Church but not necessarily the restitution of stolen wealth. Like many, Mr. Leigh felt the abbey rents burning in his pocket. Following the common logic that two wrongs might make a right, he compounded his error by befriending foreign priests, listening to their schemes, and sacrificing sons here and there as offerings to the altar of superstition, sending them to Douai, Rheims, or Rome to be trained in the arts of villainy.

One such unfortunate sacrifice was Eustace Leigh, whom his father sent to Rheims as payment for his soul's sins.

He had indeed become a skilled liar. Not inherently wicked, he had been selected by the harpies at home for his "peculiar vocation"—his capacity for vague, hysterical fears of the unseen (what we now call religion) combined with a latent tendency toward roguery, traits favored by superstitious minds. Now a tall, handsome man with a high forehead, a small mouth, and a dry, set expression, Eustace constantly struggled to appear free and cheerful. He forced smiles and dimples, believing that Christian love required cheerfulness. Yet his premeditated kindness looked no more inviting than premeditated malice. Had he not been truly handsome, women who praised his sweetness would have compared his "frankness" to a skeleton dancing in chains.

Returning to England a month prior, obeying a proclamation ordering parents to recall children abroad within four months, Eustace stayed briefly with his father at Chapel before attending to private matters for the virtuous society that raised him—one of which duties had brought him to Bideford the night before.

Sitting beside Amyas on the pebbles, Eustace scanned him with gentle, cornered glances, careful not to offend either his cousin or the flies on his back. Amyas turned fully, offering a hearty smile and extending a massive hand. Eustace grasped it rapturously, leading to a vigorous handshake. What had become of the fair breaker of so many hearts, whom I have not yet introduced to my readers? She sat in the small farmhouse beside the mill, nestled deep within the green valley of Combe, halfway between Stow and Chapel. She was sulking, as much as her naturally sweet disposition would allow, at being shut out from the grand festivities in Bideford and forced to endure a "Martinmas Lent" in this remote western glen. So utterly lonely was she that, despite regarding Eustace Leigh with a mixture of aversion and Protestant suspicion, she could not bring herself to avoid chatting with him whenever he descended to the farm and its mill. He contrived to appear almost daily, ostensibly on various errands.

Her uncle and aunt initially treated these visits with stiff reserve; the aunt particularly ensured she was always present as a third party in every conversation. Yet, Mr. Leigh was a gentleman's son, and rudeness to a neighboring squire and valuable customer was unthinkable. Conversely, Rose was the daughter of a wealthy man, while they were poor cousins; quarreling with her was equally inadvisable. Furthermore, the pretty maid, aided by both wilfulness and her winning charm, invariably managed to get her own way wherever she went. She had wisely begged her aunt never to leave them alone, claiming she "could not bear the sight of Mr. Eustace, only she must have someone to talk with down here." Her aunt, considering herself a simple countrywoman compared to the sophisticated townsfolk, decided that people knew their own business best and let matters proceed as they pleased.

In the meantime, Eustace, fully aware that the difference in creed between him and Rose was the greatest obstacle to his love, carefully kept his private opinions in the background. Instead of attempting to convert the millers, he daily purchased milk or flour from them and distributed it among the elderly women of Moorwinstow, who agreed that, for a Papist, he was a remarkably godly young man. Eventually, after consulting with Campian and Parsons regarding current political plots, the trio concluded they would attend church the following Sunday. Messrs. Evan Morgans and Morgan Evans, having memorized the rubrics beforehand, conducted themselves with perfect orthodoxy, as did poor Eustace—to the great astonishment of the local gentry. Upon returning home, Eustace flattered himself that he had successfully converted the parsons, clerks, and congregation alike. Unaware of his own simplicity and cunning foolishness, he failed to notice that every good wife in the parish was whispering to her neighbor, "He turned Protestant? The devil turned monk! He's only after Mistress Salterne, the young hypocrite."

While the two Jesuits found it expedient, for the holy cause in which they were engaged, to reconcile themselves outwardly to the established powers, they remained busily plotting their overthrow in private. Since April, they had played a game of hide-and-seek across England, now waiting quietly for news from Ireland to provide their cue. Their aim was a great "rising of the West" that would sweep from her throne that stiff-necked, persecuting, excommunicated, illegitimate, and profligate usurper who falsely claimed to be the Queen of England.

They held as steadfastly to the belief in those days—as they have in later eras, with a genuine Baconian contempt for the results of sensible experience—that the heart of England truly belonged to them. They believed the British nation was on the verge of returning to the bosom of the Catholic Church and delivering Elizabeth in chains to the feet of the rightful Lord of Creation, the Old Man of the Seven Hills. This fair hope, which had skipped just ahead of them for centuries, always remaining a step further away like the spot where a rainbow touches the ground, was announced at times in language that terrified old Mr. Leigh.

One day, as Eustace entered his father's private study after his usual visit to the mill, he heard voices rising in heated dispute. Parsons was usually blustering; Mr. Leigh was peevishly deprecating; and Campian, the sweetest-natured of men, was trying to pour oil on troubled waters. Seizing the opportunity for the good of the cause, Eustace stopped outside to eavesdrop.

"My excellent sir," said Mr. Leigh, "does not your very presence here demonstrate how deeply I am affected toward the holy cause of the Catholic faith? Yet, I cannot in the meanwhile forget that I am an Englishman."

The frowning Norman keep, ruined during the Wars of the Roses, had been replaced by the rich, stately architecture of the Tudors. Altogether, the house mirrored the times: in a transitional state, faithfully representing the passage from the old Middle Ages into the new life blossoming across Europe—a springtime we pray will never see its autumn or winter.

Now comes my turn to recount the founding of that most chivalrous brotherhood of the Rose. Within a few short years, it became renowned not only in its native Devon but grew formidable across Ireland, the Netherlands, the Spanish Main, and the heart of South America. Should this chapter seem to any modern eye quixotic or fantastical, let them remember that the generation who spoke and acted thus regarding love and honor were, nonetheless, practiced and valiant soldiers, prudent politicians, and shrewd diplomats. He who penned the *Arcadia* was simultaneously one of Europe's subtlest statesmen; the poet of the *Faerie Queene* also authored serious treatises on the state of Ireland. Even if critics sneer at quoting Lilly's *Euphues*, I ask: Have they ever read it? If they have, I pity them if they fail to find within it, despite occasional tedium, a book as brave, righteous, and pious as any man could seek. No better proof exists of the nobility of the Elizabethan age than the fact that *Euphues* and the *Arcadia* were the era's two dominant romances. While Sir Walter Scott may have ridiculed the Euphuists in his *Sir Piercie Shafton*, mocking their affected politeness, we must note the anachronisms therein—such as an Euphuist knight discussing Sidney's quarrel with Lord Oxford a decade before it occurred. We deny that Lilly's work could produce such a coxcomb; his spiritual ancestors were surely Gabriel Harvey or Lord Oxford himself.

Indeed, there is a double anachronism in Scott's character, for he belongs not to Sidney's day, but to the worse times following Elizabeth's death, when folly flourished under James. Those days, perhaps, for another time. Meanwhile, let those unread of *Euphues* believe that training a son after the model of Ephoebus would save fathers money and their sons' virtue. Let us instead open our eyes to see in these old Elizabethan gallants our own ancestors, displaying with youthful wildness all the virtues that make a true Englishman. Their commercial daring, military courage, reverence for law, and solemn sense of national calling serve as examples for us today. We must confess that their chivalry is merely another garment for that beautiful tenderness and mercy which remains the twin sister of English valor. Even their fondness for Continental manners reveals that old Anglo-Norman teachability and wide-heartedness, allowing us to profit from global wisdom without losing our distinct character.

And where was Amyas on this same Christmas afternoon?

Amyas sat bareheaded in the stern of a small boat in Smerwick Bay. Sea spray whistled through his curly hair as he shouted cheerfully to his crew. "Pull, and with a will, my merry men all, and never mind shipping a sea! Cannonballs are a cargo that doesn't spoil by taking salt-water."

His mother's foreboding had proven accurate. Christmas Eve had been the last of those still, dark, steaming nights typical of early winter, but for the past twelve hours, the western gale had been roaring violently along the Irish coast.

The short daylight of the winter day was fading rapidly. Behind him lay a leaping line of billows whipped into mist by the tempest. Beside him, green, foam-fringed columns rushed up the black rocks only to crash back down in a thousand cataracts of snow. Before him stretched the deep, sheltered bay, though visibility was limited. Some four miles out at sea, a sloping roof of thick gray clouds began, stretching overhead and far inland. It cut the cliffs off at mid-height, hid the Kerry mountains, and plunged the distant firths into the blackness of night. Underneath this awful canopy of swirling mist, the storm howled inward, sweeping before it great foam-sponges and gray salt spray until the entire landscape became hazy, dim, and brown.

Let it howl! For there was more mist than ever salt spray produced, and more thunder than any sea-surge had ever echoed among the cliffs of Smerwick Bay. Along the sandhills, red sparks flashed in the evening gloom—sparks that never came from heaven. These belonged to the fort, now christened by the invaders *Fort Del Oro*, where the hated golden flag of Spain flew proudly alongside San Josepho and eight hundred enemy soldiers. Just three nights prior, Amyas, Yeo, and the finest hands of Winter's command had hoisted four culverins from the Admiral's main deck, floated them ashore, and dragged them up to the battery among the sandhills. Now, the fate of the engagement hung in the balance: would Spanish and Italian condottieri hold their own on British soil against the men of Devon?

Amyas likely gave little thought to his lonely mother at Burrough Court. Instead, his focus was fixed on the quick, bright flashes dancing across the sandhill and the fort, where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shots with deadly precision. He watched with a cool, bitter smile of triumph as the sand flew and the protective gabions crashed.

Amyas and his party had risked their lives aboard ship to secure a fresh supply of ammunition. Winter's battery was out of shot and had been firing stones for the last four hours, lacking better missiles. They ran their boat ashore through the breaking surf, landing in a cove where the beach allowed access. Once again, Yeo's eighteen-pounder roared, sending a projectile screaming away. Oh, glory! The great yellow flag of Spain, streaming defiantly in the gale, was lifted clean into the air, flagstaff and all, before pitching wildly downward, head-first, far to leeward.

A hurrah erupted from the sailors, answered by the soldiers of the opposing camp, shaking the very clouds above them. But before the echoes faded, a tall officer leaped onto the fort's parapet, clutching the fallen flag. Rearing it as best he could upon the tip of his lance, he held it firmly against the gale while the broken staff was raised anew inside the walls.

Instantly, a dozen longbows were drawn by the defenders. But Amyas shouted from behind, "Shame, lads! Stop and let the gallant gentleman have due courtesy!"

Their supper consisted of scanty biscuits, for provisions were running dangerously low. Grumbling softly, they drifted into sleep.

He settled down quietly enough at Bideford on his parole, occupying quarters far superior to those he had known for many a day. Taking things as they came, he behaved like a seasoned soldier of fortune until, after scarcely a month with Sir Richard Grenville, Old Salterne, the Mayor, arrived for supper.

Shrugging off his initial surprise, the Spaniard submitted to eating and drinking at the same table as a tradesman who sat behind a ledger, managed apprentices, and conducted business. He found Salterne to be a prudent and courteous individual, fully aware of the Spaniard's superior rank, yet holding his own ground as a wise elder—a manner the Spaniard had previously observed only among the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice.

At the conclusion of supper, Salterne invited Grenville to return the following evening, then turned to the Don with frankness. Mr. Salterne, a wise merchant eager for adventure abroad—as later proven by his efforts in settling Virginia—was equally eager to extract knowledge from guests suspected of knowing strange lands. First, he attempted to loosen the guest's tongue with generous sack; second, he posed prudent questions regarding the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, China, and the Indies. The first scheme failed, for the Spaniard was as abstemious as a monk, drinking little but water. The second met with limited success, for the Spaniard was as cunning as a fox, answering little but wind.

Into this verbal stalemate stepped the Rose of Torridge, looking as beautiful as ever. Hearing their topic, she artlessly added her own questions. Don Guzman could not refuse her, and without revealing vital commercial secrets, he provided his host and his host's daughter with a delightful evening.

Little Eros, though sometimes called the eldest of the gods, is reported by Burton in *The Anatomy of Melancholy* to be merely the child of idleness and full bellies. This slander colored Don Guzman's thoughts; being idle as a captive and well-fed thanks to Sir Richard's generous table, he sought amusement in love. Lady Grenville, being nearest, was considered first, but the Spaniard, a man of honor and mindful of his host, suppressed the idea with considerable self-praise. Regarding the sinfulness of such a pursuit, he cared little, as Southern Europeans of his era—and indeed many Englishmen later in the Stuart days—did. Having discarded Lady Grenville, he allowed Rose Salterne to enter his thoughts. There is no one acquainted with Bideford who does not know Bideford Bridge. It is the very navel, the focal point, and the soul around which the entire town has organized itself. Just as Edinburgh owes its identity to its castle, Rome to its Capitol, and Egypt to its pyramids, so Bideford is Bideford solely by virtue of its bridge. Yet, few realize the occult powers that have animated this wondrous structure for five centuries, elevating it to the status of the region's chief marvel, according to Prince and Fuller. It began as an inspired bridge, evolved into a soul-saving conduit, an alms-giving haven, an educational center, and a sentient entity. Finally, and most importantly for our purposes, it became a dinner-providing institution. Few understand how, during its construction half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried the stones downstream each night to their final resting place. Legend holds that Sir Richard Gurney, the local parson, once slept in profound perplexity, haunted by an evil spirit seemingly active in his sheepfold, only to behold a vision of an angel commanding him to build where the materials had been divinely transported—there alone, amidst the shifting sands, lay a sure foundation. Even fewer know how Bishop Grandison of Exeter proclaimed indulgences and eternal spiritual blessings to anyone who aided in bridging that perilous ford, urging the populace to "make the best of both worlds."

Fewer still know that, despite being founded upon wool, the bridge trembles at the slightest hoofbeat; that though it boasts twenty-three arches, one man named William Alford once carried four bushels of saltwater on his back across its entire span for a wager; or that the bridge is a veritable esquire, bearing its own coat of arms—a ship and bridge on a plain field—and owning lands and tenements in numerous parishes. With these assets, the miraculous bridge has founded charities, erected schools, fought legal battles, and, most notably, hosted annual feasts, maintaining for this purpose the finest wine cellar in all of Devon.

In the year 1583, all the notables of Bideford were invited to one such banquet, joined by Mr. St. Leger of Annery, a neighbor and brother to the Marshal of Munster and Lady Grenville. He was a man of immense worth and hospitality, so generous with his riches that he effectively shielded his descendants from the snares of greed. During the meal, a dispute arose between Mr. St. Leger and a trustee regarding the quality of a salmon caught below versus one caught above the bridge. Deciding that theory yielded to practice, Mr. St. Leger vowed to repay the bridge's generosity. He wagered five pounds that he could procure a salmon from the waters below Annery equal in firmness and flavor to the one they had just consumed. In a gesture of grandeur, he invited the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days hence, requesting that each guest bring a wife or daughter. Don Guzman, seated at the table, was naturally included.

Thus, a magnificent feast ensued in the great hall at Annery, scarcely rivaled since Judge Hankford entertained King Edward IV. As guests ate their fill and drank deeply, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman engaged in a silent dance of avoidance and observation. Rose, however, walked a tightrope; her father sat nearby, and directly opposite her were Messrs. William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in the Queen's Irish army currently on furlough.

For six months, Rose and the Spaniard had shared no words, though their paths had crossed frequently. Don Guzman avoided her company only within her father's house; elsewhere, he maintained a demeanor of polite indifference, offering only the stiffest salutations. Yet, whenever he appeared in her presence, he exerted himself to perfection—more witty, more eloquent, more romantic, and more filled with marvelous tales than ever before. Having failed with his initial tactic of direct pursuit, the cunning Don adopted a far more formidable strategy. He believed Rose deserved severe punishment for daring to reject a Spanish nobleman, and what finer punishment existed than the withdrawal of his attentions and the shadow of his smile? While this notion bore the seeds of vanity, it was rooted in cunning. The Spaniard understood intimately that women, like the world, tend to value a man at his own self-assessed price; the higher the demand, the higher the reward.

Now, he intended to place a high price on himself, provoking her pride, which was too accustomed to adoration to be won by simple flattery. He could have pursued another suitor, but he deemed such a crude method liable to incite indignation or despair rather than submission. Instead, he quietly demonstrated his independence from her. Poor Rose, as fate intended, immediately began to question why. What hidden treasure or reserve force rendered him independent of her, while she felt entirely dependent upon him? Did he possess a secret? How delightful to know! Some vast ambition? How sweet to share! Some mysterious knowledge? How delicious to uncover! Perhaps a capacity for love beyond the common? How exquisite to possess exclusively! He must be greater, wiser, richer-hearted, and nobler born than she. Oh, if only his wealth could supply her poverty! Thus, step by step, she was led to sue *in forma pauperis* to the very man she had spurned when he had previously sued in similar fashion. The temptation of possessing some mysterious private treasure, of serving as the priestess of a hidden sanctuary, and of thanking Heaven for not being like other women, became increasingly overwhelming for Rose, as it often is for most.

Rose was utterly weary of wearing that mask of coyness as she sat at the Annery table that day. Don Guzman observed her uneasy, downcast gaze. Thinking, somewhat arrogantly, that she had suffered sufficient punishment, he occasionally stole a glance at her. Unabashed when he saw her lower her eyes upon contact, he noted her increasing silence and abstraction, accompanied by a faint blush. He mirrored her melancholy, continuing his glances until he caught her looking at him, checking to see if he was looking at her. At that moment, he knew his prey was secure. He asked her with his eyes, "Do you forgive me?" causing her to halt her conversation, falter, drop her eyes, and lift them again a minute later in search of his gaze, prompting him to repeat the inquiry. Unable to resist, she answered with every feature and graceful curve of her neck, "And do you forgive me in turn?"

With this exchange, Don Guzman erupted like a nightingale on a branch, bursting forth with stories, jests, and sharp retorts. He instantly became the soul of the gathering and the most charming cavalier present. Poor Rose realized she was the architect of his sudden transformation, blaming herself for her actions, shuddering and blushing at her own delight, and longing desperately for the feast to end so she might retreat home to hide among sweet fantasies of a love whose reality she felt too afraid to confront.

The afternoon view from the great terrace at Annery was breathtaking. Smart ladies in gaudy attire paraded in pairs and trios before the stately house or gazed down upon the park, where ancient oaks stood sentinel over deer grazing near a broad, land-locked river shimmering like a lake under the midsummer sun. Others listened obediently to the two presiding ladies, Mrs. St. Leger the hostess and her sister-in-law, the fair Lady Grenville. Everyone chatted, laughed, admired dresses, and gossiped about husbands and servants. Only Rose Salterne remained apart, wishing to curl up in a corner to laugh or cry, unsure which.

"Our pretty Rose seems sad," Lady Grenville remarked, approaching her. "Cheer up, child! We wish you to sing for us."

Rose replied vaguely and moved mechanically. Taking the lute, she sat on a bench beneath the house while the group gathered around.

"What shall I sing?"

"Play our old song, 'Earl Haldan's Daughter,'" suggested someone.

Rose recoiled internally. It was a loud, spirited ballad ill-suited to her current thoughts; moreover, Frank had once praised it in happier days long past. Thoughts of him, of others, of her own pride and carelessness flooded her mind, making the song feel ominous. Yet, she dared not refuse, fearing suspicion where none existed, and so she began reluctantly.

Let it be confessed, to the shame of my nation, that in those days it was perilous indeed to travel from Plymouth northward along the coast of Devon. Unless one wished to detour many miles, the road led straight through the domain of a foreign and hostile potentate—a man who had repeatedly ravaged the lands and defeated the armies of Her Majesty, Queen Elizabeth. Behind his back, at least, he was known as the "King of the Gubbings." As the chronicler Fuller noted, "Secured by distance, which one of more valor durst not do to their face, for fear their fury fall upon him." Yet, despite the ingenuity of scholars like Mr. Joseph Maynard, who suggested the name derived from *gibberosos* (hunchbacks), the truth remains obscure. Some claim the term refers to fish shavings—worthless scraps that carry a stigma of shame—but the reality is far darker.

This land lies near Brent, a true Scythia within England, inhabited by pure heathens. Legend holds that two centuries prior, two unwed mothers fled there to hide, followed by lewd fellows, creating a peculiar society exempt from all civil or ecclesiastical authority. Living in hovels indistinguishable from pigsties, they multiply without marriage, speaking a dialect so coarse that the more educated find it impossible to comprehend. During the Civil Wars, no soldiers were quartered among them, lest they become trapped within. Their wealth consists solely of stolen goods, particularly sheep from the moors; searching their homes is deemed beneath a sheriff and beyond a constable's power, for these creatures possess such speed they outrun horses and such vitality they outlast men. Living in ignorance of luxury—the true extinguisher of life—they cling together like bees, avenging any offense against one member upon the entire community.

Though reports suggest they are beginning to accept baptism and return to civilization, I trust no civilized people will emulate their former barbarism. Thus ended Fuller's tale, leaving us to imagine Amyas riding out of Plymouth on a fine steed, clad in full armor, lance and sword in hand, accompanied by two great daggers or horse-pistols. Following him was Salvation Yeo and five or six North Devon men, veterans of the Irish campaign returning on furlough, armed with pikes, swords, arquebuses, and matches. Pressing onward as fast as possible through Tavistock, they aimed to reach Lydford before dark. However, delays in purchasing horses meant they did not depart until noon. It was a desolate landscape, illuminated by the wild glare of sunset: a high tableland of heath bordered on the right by the craggy heights of Dartmoor, sloping south and west toward the massive cone of Brent-Tor. From afar, faint threads of blue smoke drifted from the Gubbings' dens. More than once, the party halted, mistaking distant furze bushes and grazing ponies for the vanguard of an invading army. Poor John Braund, whose motives for entertaining such unsavory guests were likely not treason but a wife, seven children, and arrears of rent, did not thrive under the shift from Lundy's pure air to the pestilential atmosphere of Exeter Gaol. Made infamous merely two years later (if I recall correctly) by a "black assizes" nearly as deadly as the notorious one at Oxford—in which judge, jury, counsel, and bystanders, including many members of Devon's best families, sickened and died miserably within days—John Braund contracted the gaol-fever in a week. He died raving in that noisome den; his secret, if he possessed one, perished with him, leaving only vague suspicion regarding Rose Salterne's fate.

Few doubted she had fled with the Spaniard, but whither, and in what capacity? On this last subject, no mercy was shown to her by many a Bideford dame who had hated the poor girl simply for her beauty, nor by many a country lady who had "always expected that the girl would be brought to ruin by the absurd notice, beyond her station's rights, which was taken of her." Meanwhile, every young maiden aspired to fill the throne Rose had abdicated. Thus, on the whole, Bideford considered itself functioning perfectly well without poor Rose, or even better. Though she lingered in some hearts as a fair dream, the daily business and bustle soon swept that dream away, and her place knew her no more.

As for Will Cary? For a while, he was like a man distracted. He heaped upon himself superfluous reproaches for having, as he claimed, first brought Rose into disgrace and then driven her into the arms of the Spaniard. The truth is undeniable. Though many guests had suffered financially due to the expedition's failure, they had utterly forgotten that fact in the face of the awful news of Sir Humphrey's death. The feast breaks up sadly and hurriedly, while each man asks his neighbor, "What will the Queen say?"

At last, the ship halted. The cable rattled through the hawsehole. Ignoring the risk of lurking Spaniards or Caribs, an instinctive cheer erupted from every throat. Poor souls! The land breeze, which had carried them out all night, died into a glassy calm, marking the start of the tropical day.

The sick were hoisted overboard, landing boatload by boatload onto the beach to rest in the palm shadows. Within half an hour, the entire crew was scattered across the shore, save for a dozen volunteers keeping watch aboard until noon.

Fruit!* The crippled crawled from place to place, greedily plucking the violet grapes of the creeping vines, staining their mouths and blistering their lips on the prickly pears despite Yeo's warnings. If Barbados held a strange glory in the eyes of these newcomers, how much more magnificent must be the seas they now entered—calm, smiling waters untouched by the hurricanes that roared far to the north. Sky, sea, and islands formed one vast rainbow. To the sturdy, practical sailors whose minds were fixed solely on Spanish gold and pearls, the view offered little poetic appeal. All the wonder, pity, and greed of the age had concentrated itself here. Perhaps fewer than five times before had English ships ventured these mysterious seas. Yet, there were those on board who knew them too well. These were the veterans of Captain John Hawkins' expeditions, the first British mariners to attempt trade along these coasts. Interdicted by Spanish jealousy from the necessities of life, they had, in true English fashion, won their markets at the point of the sword, only to buy and sell thereafter with honest peace. Unless his reckoning was wrong, they were the mountains of Macanao, at the western end of Margarita, the Isle of Pearls. The following day saw them running along the northern side of the island, having passed undiscovered the castle the Spaniards had built at the eastern end to protect their pearl fisheries. Standing nearly six feet tall, he possessed the bold, graceful bearing of Frank or Amyas himself. Initially smiling broadly to reveal white teeth, his expression shifted instantly. Springing to the side, he shouted to his companion in Spanish, "Treachery! No Spaniard!" attempting to leap overboard, but a dozen strong men held him fast.

It took effort to subdue him; his naked limbs were slippery and his strength considerable. Amyas alternated between pleading with the crew not to harm the native and urging the Indian to remain calm. After five minutes of confusion, the stranger submitted sulkily.

"Do not bind him," Amyas instructed. "Loosen him and form a ring around him. Here is a dollar for you, my man."

The Indian's eyes gleamed as he accepted the coin.

"All I require," Amyas said, "is to know what ships are in La Guayra, and then to guide me to the Governor's house and the Custom House."

The Indian placed the coin on the deck, crossed himself, and stared Amyas in the face. "No, Senor! I am a freeman, a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria. Our forefathers swore fealty to the King of Spain before any other Indians, and he still calls us his most faithful and noble subjects. God forbid I should aid his enemies, who are my enemies too."

A murmur of approval rose from the men who understood him, with several hinting that a rope around the head or a lit match would quickly yield the desired information.

"God forbid!" Amyas declared warmly. "He is a brave and loyal man, and I shall treat him as such. Tell me, my brave fellow, how do you know we are His Catholic Majesty's enemies?"

With a shrewd smile, the Indian pointed to various objects on the ship, declaring each "Not Spanish."

"Well, and what of that?" Amyas asked.

"None but Spaniards and free Guayquerias have the right to sail these seas."

Amyas laughed. "You are a valiant piece of copper. Take your dollar and go in peace. Clear the way, men; we can learn what we need without your assistance."

The Indian paused, astonished. "Overboard with you!" Amyas commanded gently. "Do you not know when you are well off?"

"Most illustrious Senor," the Indian began in his slow, sententious tone, "I have been deceived. I heard you heretics roast and eat true Catholics—and that all your priests possess tails."

"Plague on you, sirrah!" squeaked Jack Brimblecombe. "Do I have a tail? Look here!"

"Quien sabe? Who knows?" the Indian sniffed through his nose.

"How do you know we are heretics?" Amyas pressed.

"Hmph! But in repayment for your kindness, I warn you, Illustrious Senor, not to proceed to La Guayra. Ships of war await you there. Moreover, Governor Don Guzman sailed eastward only yesterday specifically to search for you. I wonder greatly that you did not encounter him."

"To search for us! On the lookout for us!" Cary cried. "Impossible! Lies! Amyas, this is some trick to frighten us away."

"Don Guzman came out yesterday to look for us? Are you sure?" Amyas asked sharply.

"As I live, Senor, he and another ship—for which I took yours."

Amyas stamped his foot. "That was the ship we passed! Fool that I was to stay close to my enemy and let my opportunity slip! If I had fulfilled my duty, all would have gone right!"

But it was too late to regret. Besides, the Indian's tale was likely false.

"Off with you!" Amyas ordered. The Indian bounded back into his canoe, leaving the crew marveling at the stateliness and courtesy of this bold sea-cavalier.

Thus, Westward-Ho they ran beneath the mighty northern wall—the highest cliff on Earth, seven thousand feet of rock separated from the sea by a narrow strip of verdant lowland. Patches of sugar cane and clusters of coconut trees near the water's edge confirmed their tropical location, while above, everything was savage, rough, and bare as an Alpine precipice. Deep clefts allowed the southern sun to pour blazing light onto the sea margin, revealing glimpses of strange, stately trees lining the glens and a veil of perpetual mist shrouding the inner summits. White, fleecy clouds hung motionless in the burning air, enhancing the sense of vastness and solemn rest that was already overwhelming.

"Within those mountains, three thousand feet above our heads," said Drew, the Master, "lies Saint Yago de Leon, the great city founded by the Spaniards fifteen years ago."

"Is it a rich place?" Cary asked.

"Very, they say."

"And a strong place?" Amyas inquired.

"No forts, they claim. The Spaniards boast that Heaven has built such excellent walls that man needs build none."

"I doubt that," Amyas muttered. "Lads, could you climb those hills?"

"Higher than Harty Point, sir," Drew replied cautiously. "But it depends entirely on what lies behind them."

"Seven long hours in all men's sight this fight endured, until our men so feeble grew that they could fight no more. When the sun leaped up the next morning, casting its tropic light suddenly into the tropic day, Amyas paced the deck. Picture it, you who have ever lost a brother; and you, who have not, thank God that you know nothing of such agony. Amyas had revived at the sight of battle. He no longer felt his wounds or his great sorrow; even Frank's last angelic look grew dimmer as he bustled about the deck. Ere a quarter-hour had passed, his voice cried firmly and cheerfully as of old:

Jack Brimblecombe read the daily prayers and the prayers before a fight at sea. His honest voice trembled as, in the *Prayer for all Conditions of Men*, he added, despite Amyas's despair, "and especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, perhaps captive among the idolaters." So they rose.

"Now, then," said Amyas, "to breakfast. A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him—and that's always."

"And good beef and the good cause are a match for the devil," said Cary. "Come down, captain; you must eat too."

Amyas shook his head, took the tiller from the steersman, and bade him go below and fill himself. Will Cary went down and returned in five minutes with a plate of bread and beef and a great jack of ale. He coaxed them down Amyas's throat as a nurse does with a child, then scuttled below again with tears hopping down his face.

Amyas stood still, steering. His face had grown seven years older in the last night. A terrible, set calm rested upon him. Woe to the man who came across him that day!

"There are three of them, you see, my masters," he said as the crew came on deck again. "A big ship forward and two galleys astern of her. The big ship may keep; she is a race ship. If we can but recover the wind of her, we will see whether our height is not a match for her length. We must give her the slip and take the galleys first."

"I thank the Lord," said Yeo, "who has given so wise a heart to so young a general—a very David and Daniel, saving his presence, lads. And if any dare not follow him, let him be as the men of Meroz and of Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley, smite me—that boy over the head, the young monkey; why is he not down at the powder-room door?"

Yeo went about his gunnery as one who knew how to do it, possessing the most terrible mind to execute it thoroughly and the most terrible faith that it was God's work.

So all fell to. Though there was comparatively little to be done, as the ship had been kept in fighting order all night, there was "clearing of decks, lacing of nettings, making of bulwarks, fitting of waist-cloths, arming of tops, tallowing of pikes, slinging of yards, doubling of sheets and tacks"—enough to satisfy even the pedantic soul of Richard Hawkins himself. Amyas took charge of the poop, Cary of the forecastle, Yeo, as gunner, of the main-deck, while Drew, as master, settled himself in the waist. All was ready, and more than ready, before the great ship was within two miles of them.

Now, while the mastiffs of England and the bloodhounds of Spain neared each other over the rolling surges, thirsting for one another's blood, let us pause to consider the causes that enabled the English to face and conquer armaments immensely superior in size and number. It is a boast that in the whole Spanish War but one queen's ship, the *Revenge*, and one private man-of-war, Sir Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, ever struck their colors to the enemy.

What enabled Sir Richard Grenville's *Revenge*, in his last fearful fight off the Azores, to endure for twelve hours the attack of eight Spanish squadrons, two of which (three times her own burden) sank at her side? After all her masts were gone and she had been boarded three times without success, she defied to the last the whole fleet of fifty-four sail lying around her, waiting for her to sink "like dogs around the dying forest king."

What enabled young Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, though half her guns were useless through the carelessness or treachery of the gunner, to maintain for three days a running fight with two Spaniards of equal size, double the weight of metal, and ten times the number of men?

What enabled Sir George Cary's illustrious ship, the *Content*, to fight single-handed from seven in the morning till eleven at night against four great squadrons and two galleys, though her heaviest gun was but one nine-pounder and for many hours she had but thirteen men fit for service?

What enabled, in the very year of which I write, those two "valiant Turkey Merchantmen of London, the *Merchant Royal* and the *Tobie*," with their three small consorts, to cripple off Pantellaria the whole fleet of Spanish galleys sent to intercept them, and return triumphant through the Straits of Gibraltar?

Lastly, what in the fight of 1588 enabled the English fleet to capture, destroy, and scatter that Great Armada, with the loss (but not the capture) of one pinnace and one gentleman of note?

There were more causes than one. The first seemed to lie in the build of the English ships; the second in their superior gunnery and weight of metal; the third (without which the first would have been useless) in the hearts of the English men.

The English ship was much shorter than the Spanish. This, combined with the rig of those days, gave them an ease of maneuvering that utterly confused their Spanish foes. "The English ships in the fight of 1588," says Camden, "charged the enemy with marvelous agility, and having discharged their broadsides, flew forth presently into the deep and leveled their shot directly, without missing, at those great ships of the Spaniards, which were altogether heavy and unwieldy." Moreover, the Spanish fashion in the West Indies, though not in the ships of the Great Armada, was to build their men-of-war flush-decked, or "race" (*razes*), leaving those on deck exposed and open. The English fashion was to heighten the ship as much as possible at stem and stern, both by the sweep of her lines and by stockades ("close fights and cage-works") on the poop and forecastle, giving the men shelter. This was further increased by strong bulkheads ("cobridgeheads") across the main-deck below, dividing the ship into separate forts fitted with swivels ("bases, fowlers, and murderers") and loopholed for musketry and arrows.

Amyas would undoubtedly have succumbed to yellow fever, had not one overriding reason spared him. He explained this logic to Cary: he simply lacked the luxury of illness while his men were falling ill. It was a valid, sufficient excuse—a truth known too well by countless noble souls in the Crimea—as long as the adrenaline of labor remains high. Yet, it is a trap that often ensnares heroes; the moment their work ceases, the vitality drains away, and they sink into the abyss they had previously leaped over.

The following morning, facing a crisis that left him at his wits' end, Amyas convened a council of war—or, more accurately, a sanitary commission. The men were in a state of panic, teetering on the brink of mutiny. Amyas addressed them sternly, declaring that he saw no benefit in them killing him or allowing themselves to be killed by him. With that, he descended below deck to confer with his officers.

The results of the consultation were dismal. The doctor spoke only of "science"—or what amounted to nonsense—discussing humors, complexions, and animal spirits. Jack Brimblecombe offered only pulpit rhetoric, labeling the outbreak a direct visitation of God. Cary expressed pure despair, masking it with a nervous jest. Yeo displayed stoic fatalism, bolstered by Scriptural quotes. As for Drew, the master, he remained silent; his duty, he insisted, was merely to sail the ship, not to cure fevers.

Enraged, Amyas gripped his hair in his customary fashion and erupted. "Doctor! A fig for your humors and complexions! Can you cure a man's humors or alter his complexion? Can an Ethiopian change his skin, or a leopard its spots? Do not attribute your ignorance to God, sir. You claim to know the cause of this sickness, yet you know nothing. Jack Brimblecombe, cease your talk of God's visitation; this bears far more the marks of the Devil's visitation. We are doing God's work, Sir John, and He would not hinder us. Down with the devil!"

Turning to Cary, he continued, "Laughing kills the cat, but it will not cure a Christian. Yeo, if an angel were to tell me that it is God's will for us all to die like dogs in a ditch, I would accept it as God's will—but not until then. The malady lies in the air, nowhere else. I felt it coursing through me last night as I came aboard, smelling it like a sewer. If it were not in the air, explain to me why my boat's crew was struck first!"

Silence met his outburst.

"Then I shall tell you why they were struck first," Amyas declared. "When we cut through the mist, it rose only five or six feet above the stream, engulfing us, while you on board remained above it. Every man taken on board this morning slept on the main deck, and every one of them feared the fever. From this, I deduce two truths: Keep as high as you can, and fear nothing but God, and we shall yet be safe."

"But the fog reached our round-tops at sunrise this morning," Cary objected.

"I know it," Amyas replied. "Yet those of us on the half-deck were exposed to it for a shorter duration than those below, and we breathed free air. Furthermore, I suspect the evening heat draws the poison out more intensely, and as the temperature drops toward morning, the venom dissipates somehow."

How exactly it dissipated, Amyas could not say (nor could anyone else at that time); it would take nearly two centuries of fatal experience for American settlers to uncover the simple laws governing such epidemics, knowledge now held by every child. However, common sense was on Amyas's side. Yeo stood and spoke.

"As I have stated before, the Lord has often sent us a young Daniel to act as judge. I recall hearing the Spaniards say that these calentures linger only in low-lying ground, rarely rising more than a few hundred feet above the sea."

"Then let us ascend those few hundred feet," Amyas suggested.

Every man turned to look at Amyas, then at his neighbor.

"Gentlemen," Amyas proclaimed, "'Look the devil straight in the face if you wish to strike him in the right place.' We cannot launch the ship as she stands; even if we could, we cannot return empty-handed, nor can we remain here to perish of fever. We must abandon the ship and move inland."

"Inland?" echoed every voice except Yeo's.

"To those hundred feet Yeo speaks of. To the mountains. We shall stockade a camp and relocate our sick and supplies there."

The *Pelicans* and the freed slaves embraced the plan immediately, but the remainder of the crew subjected Amyas to a stormy hour. The critical question arose: Where were the hills? Within the dense mangrove thicket, visibility was limited to fifty yards.

"The hills lie no more than three miles to the southwest of you right now," Amyas assured them. "I marked every ridge as we entered."

"I suppose you intended to lead us there?"

That question ignited a fuse, and angry suspicions flared up one by one. Amyas silenced them with a counter-move.

"Fools! If I did not possess the wit to look ahead further than you do, where would you be? Are you mad as well as reckless to rise against your own captain because he possesses two strings to his bow? The men knew Amyas never uttered threats he did not intend to carry out—not that he truly intended to destroy the vessel, for he knew the threat alone was sufficient deterrent. Thus, they agreed to proceed, reassured by seeing the old *Pelican*'s men turn to the task heartily and cheerfully.

No need to burden the reader with five or six weary hours under a broiling (or rather, stewing) sun, stumbling over mangrove roots, hacking through thorny thickets, and dragging sick men and provisions up mountainous steeps amidst disappointment, fatigue, murmurs, curses, snakes, mosquitoes, false alarms of Spanish forces, and every misery save cold that flesh is heir to. Suffice it to say that by sunset that evening, they had secured a level plateau, a full thousand feet above the sea. It was backed by an inaccessible cliff forming the upper shoulder of a mighty mountain, defended below by steep, wooded slopes, requiring only the felling of a few trees to render it impregnable.

Amyas settled the sick beneath the arched roots of an enormous cottonwood tree and made a second journey to the ship to retrieve hammocks and blankets for them. Meanwhile, Yeo's wisdom and courage proved invaluable. As a pioneer, he located the small brook up which they forced their passage; he encouraged them to scale the cliffs over which it fell, correctly arguing that along its course they would inevitably find suitable ground for encampment near water. He supported Amyas whenever the weary crew begged to be dragged no farther, returning a dozen times to cheer them upward. Cary, bringing up the rear, bullied and spurred on the stragglers who sat down refusing to move, driving back more than one attempting a retreat at the point of a sword, carrying their burdens, and singing songs during halts. In everything, he approved himself as the gallant and hopeful soul he had always been. Until Amyas, beside himself with joy at discovering that the two men on whom he relied most were utterly worthy of his trust, went so far as to whisper to them both in confidence that very night:

"Cortez burned his ships when he landed. Why should not we?"

Yeo jumped upright, then sat down again, whispering, "Do you say that, Captain? 'Tis from above, then, that's certain; for it has been hanging on my mind all day."

My narrative turns now to a chapter of profound sorrow. I shall keep it brief, yet it was imperative to record these events so that future generations may understand the grim adversaries the English faced in those austere times.

Three weeks have elapsed, and the setting shifts to a dim, subterranean corridor within the city of Cartagena. Here lies a long row of stone cells. Before one such cell stands an open door, flanked by two cloaked figures. One is Eustace Leigh; the other, a confidant of the Holy Office, holds a lantern whose feeble glow illuminates a straw mattress and the slumbering form of a man. We recognize the high forehead, the pale, refined features—this is Frank.

Rescued barely alive from the brutal savagery of African captors, he has now been surrendered to the subtler cruelties of civilized Christians. He endured the rack earlier today, and now Eustace, his traitorous kinsman, has arrived. Is he here to persuade, or to trap? Even Eustace struggles to distinguish between the two. Yet, deep down, he would trade his own life to save his cousin. Has he truly ceased to value his life? Perhaps. He acts solely out of a twisted sense of duty. Now, he must fulfill that duty again: rouse the sleeper, reason with him, cajole, and threaten him into renouncing his faith while "his heart remains tender from the torture," as his masters instruct.

Yet, look at him sleep! How peacefully. Is it merely the play of the lamplight, or does a faint smile grace his lips? Eustace lifts the lamp closer, bending over the prone figure. As he leans in, he catches a whisper escaping Frank's dreaming lips—a call for his mother, and a name far holier than mortal tongue. Eustace finds no strength within him to wake the boy.

"Let him rest," Eustace whispers to his companion. "In truth, I fear my words will avail naught."

"I suspect so, sir," replies the agent. "Never have I witnessed such stubborn heresy. He dared mock our Holinesses openly."

"Ah!" exclaims Eustace. "Great indeed is the depravity of the human heart, and the power of Satan! Let us depart for the time being."

"Where is she?" asks the agent. "The elder sorceress, or the younger?"

"The younger—the—"

"Señora de Soto? Alas, poor soul! One might pity her, were she not a heretic." The man regards Eustace sharply, then adds softly, "She is currently with the notary, for the greater glory of her soul, I trust."

Eustace halts abruptly, seized by a shudder. He gathers his composure with difficulty, managing only a choked "Amen!"

"Inside there," the agent gestures casually toward a nearby door as they descend the corridor. "We may eavesdrop a moment, if you wish; but do not betray me, *señor*."

Eustace knows the man watches closely for any sign of remorse, ready to report the slightest flicker of sympathy to his superiors. Yet, a terrible curiosity overrides his terror. He lingers near the ominous threshold. His face burns with fever, his knees knock together, his ears ring, and his heart pounds against his ribs until he feels it might burst forth. Pressing himself against the wall, he hides his convulsed countenance from his companion.

From within, a man's voice is clearly audible—low, yet distinct. The notary repeats the age-old accusation of witchcraft, a charge the Inquisitors frequently levy against their victims, either to soothe their own consciences or to paint their atrocities in a lighter hue before the mob. Then, Eustace's heart sinks as he hears a woman's voice answer, sharpened by indignation and agony.

"Witchcraft against Don Guzmán? What necessity exists for such a claim, Oh God! What need?"

"You deny it, then, *señora*? We regret your plight; however—"

A confused, choking murmur rises from the victim, mixed with words that hold meaning or none.

"She has confessed!" Eustace whispers, tears streaming down his face. "Saints preserve us! She—"

A wail erupts, piercing through Eustace's ears, his mind, and his very soul! He would tear at the door to break free, but his companion drags him backward. Wail after wail follows, as the wretched man hurries away, pressing his hands to his ears in futile attempts to silence the shrieks that pursue him like avenging angels through the dreadful vaults.

He escapes into the fragrant open air, bathed in the golden moonlight of the tropics, beneath a garden that might serve as a model for Eden itself. Yet, man's hell has invaded God's heaven, and those agonizing cries seem to echo endlessly in his mind.

"Oh, misery, misery, misery!" he mutters through gritted teeth. "And I have brought her to this! I was forced to lead her there! What other choice existed? Who dares condemn me? And yet, what diabolical sin have I committed that demands such punishment? Was there no one else available? None? And yet, it may save her soul. It may drive her to repentance!"

"It may, indeed; for she is delicate and cannot withstand much. You should know as well as I, *señor*, the merciful nature of the Holy Office."

"I know it, I know it," interrupts poor Eustace, trembling violently now for his own sake. "All in love—all in love. A paternal chastisement..."

"And the evidence of heresy is manifest, alongside the strong suspicion of enchantment and the notorious reputation of the elder sorceress. Recall, *señor*, that you yourself testified she was a famous witch in England before the *señora* brought her here as her attendant."

"Naturally she was; naturally. Yes; there was no other path. And though my flesh may be weak, sir, none can prove better to the Holy Office how willing my spirit has been!"

Thus, Eustace departs. Before another sun sets, he seeks out the principal of the Jesuits. Pouring out his entire heart—or as much of it as a broken man dares confess—he begs permission to complete his novitiate and join the order, on the condition that he be immediately dispatched back to Europe or elsewhere. "Otherwise," he states frankly, "I shall go mad, even if I am not already mad."

The Jesuit, a kind-hearted man, approaches the Holy Office and arranges everything with the Inquisitors. To clear Eustace of all suspicion, he recounts the young man's past valiant service to the Church. His testimony becomes unnecessary. That very night, Eustace leaves Cartagena for Nombre, sailing the following week to some unknown destination.

I say, I know not whither. Eustace Leigh disappears from these pages forever. He may have risen to become General of his Order. He may have spent his final years in some tropical forest, "conquering souls"—along with their bodies—that of the indigenous peoples. Or perhaps he returned to England, becoming the very Ballard who was hanged and quartered three years later for his role in the Babington conspiracy. I do not know.

This book chronicles the lives of men—their virtues and sins, their triumphs and failures. But Eustace is no longer a man. He has become an instrument, a tool of the Jesuits. He moves only where commanded, performing good or evil indiscriminately based on orders. Through an act of moral suicide, he has sacrificed his soul in a desperate attempt to save it; stripped of will, conscience, and personal responsibility—to God or man—but bound solely to "The Society."

In short, as Eustace himself admits, he is "dead." Twice dead, I fear. Let the dead bury their dead. We have no further concern with Eustace Leigh.

Nearly three years had passed since that small band knelt at evening prayer beneath the giant tree of Guayra. These were years of apparent emptiness, traceable only by scattered notes and misspelled names. Across a vast expanse of eight hundred miles by four hundred, they had hunted for the Golden City. They had searched along the wooded banks of the Orinoco, beyond the foaming chaos of Maypures, and up the mighty Amazon. They had ascended into Peru itself, treading the cinchona groves of Loxa unaware of their medicinal powers, and witnessed the virgin snows of Chimborazo and the wrathful cone of Cotopaxi before volcanic streams tore down its slopes.

Foiled at the back of the Andes, they had turned eastward, plunging from alpine cliffs into "the green and misty ocean of the Montana." Slowly and painfully, they had worked their way northward along the eastern foot of the inland Cordillera. Now, they bivouacked upon one of the many feeders of the Meta, flowing down from the Suma Paz into the forest-covered plains.

Here they sat, their watch-fires glittering on the stream beneath enormous trees. Present were Amyas and Cary, Brimblecombe, Yeo, and the Indian lad who had followed them through all their wanderings—all alive and well, yet as far from Manoa, its fairy lake, and golden palaces, as ever. Again and again, rumors of its existence had drawn them into fresh directions, only to meet fresh disappointments, a hope deferred that makes the heart sick.

"Their bones are scatter'd far and wide, By mount, by stream, and sea."

Master Drew lay on the banks of the Rio Negro, dead alongside five brave companions, slain by poisoned arrows in a futile attempt to penetrate the mountain gorges of the Parima. Two more lay frozen in the Andean valleys, victims of the fierce slaty hail sweeping from the condor's eyrie; four others had perished in the Orinoco's rapids; and five or six wounded men remained behind among friendly tribes, perhaps never to be recovered. Fever, snakes, jaguars, alligators, cannibal fish, and electric eels had thinned their ranks month by month. Yet, the survivors sat beside the silent stream beneath the tropic moon. Sun-dried and lean, they were as strong and bold as ever, the quiet fire of English courage undimmed in every eye, the genial smile of English mirth fresh on every lip. They made a jest of danger and a sport of toil, as cheerily as when they sailed over the bar of Bideford in days belonging to some antenatal life. Beards grew down upon their breasts; long hair was knotted on their heads like women's to shield against the burning sun. Leggings were fashioned from the skin of the delicate Guazu-puti deer; shirts were patched with Indian cotton web; the spoils of jaguar, puma, and ape hung from their shoulders.

Their ammunition was spent; their muskets, ruined by the perpetual vapor-bath of the steaming woods, lay useless in caves near Orinoco cataracts. But their swords remained bright and terrible. They carried bows of such strength no Indian arm could bend, tipped with arrowheads forged from the remnants of their armor. Many wielded the *pocuna*, or blowgun of the Indians—a weapon more deadly, because more silent, than the firearms they had abandoned. Thus they wandered, lords of the forest and its beasts: terrible to hostile Indians, yet kindly, just, and generous to all who dealt faithfully with them. Many a smooth-chinned Carib, Ature, Solimo, or Guahiba recounted with wonder the righteousness of these bearded heroes, who proclaimed themselves deadly foes of the faithless Spaniard and spoke of a great and good queen beyond the seas, who would send warriors to deliver and avenge the oppressed Indian.

The men slept among the trees, some on the ground, others in grass hammocks slung between stems. Silence reigned, save for the heavy plunge of a tapir tearing up water-weeds for its nightly meal. Occasionally, a jaguar climbing from tree-top to tree-top after prey would rouse the clustered monkeys, who in turn aroused the birds. For ten minutes, unearthly roars, howls, shrieks, and cacklings would make the forest ring as if pandemonium had broken loose; but this soon died away. Even while it lasted, it was too common to awaken the sleepers, much less interrupt the council of war proceeding beside the watch-fire between the three adventurers and the faithful Yeo.

A hundred times they had held such councils in vain. Perhaps this one would be equally fruitless. Yet, it was more solemn than usual. The two years they had agreed to search for Manoa were long past. Unless they intended to spend the rest of their lives in that green wilderness, a new destination must be chosen.

"Well," said Will Cary, removing his cigar from his mouth, "at least we have gained something from those last Indians. It is a comfort to taste tobacco again after three weeks' fasting."

"For me," said Jack Brimblecombe, "Heaven forgive me! When I get the magical leaf between my teeth, I am tempted to sit as still as a chimney and smoke till my dying day without moving hand or foot."

"Then I shall forbid you tobacco, Master Parson," said Amyas sternly. "We must be up and away tomorrow. We have been idling here three mortal days with nothing accomplished."

"Shall we ever accomplish anything?" asked Jack. "I think the gold of Manoa is like the gold where the rainbow touches the ground—always a field beyond your reach."

Amyas fell silent, as did the others. There was no denying their hopes were nearly extinguished. In the immense circuit they had traversed, they had met with nothing but disappointment.

"There is but one more chance," Amyas said finally. "That is the range of mountains to the east of the Orinoco, where we failed the first time. The Incas may have moved on to them when they fled."

"Why not?" Cary replied. "They would place all the forests, the Llanos, and half a dozen great rivers between them and those dogs of Spaniards."

"Shall we try it once more?" Amyas asked. "This river ought to empty into the Orinoco; once there, we stand again at the very foot of the mountains. What say you, Yeo?"

"I cannot but mind, your worship," said Yeo gravely, "that when we ascended the Orinoco, the Indians told us terrible stories of those mountains—their extent, their difficulty due to the cliffs aloft and the thick forests in the valleys. Have we not already lost five good men there?"

"What care we?" retorted Amyas. "No forests can be thicker than those we have already pierced. Why, if one had but a tail like a monkey for an extra limb, one might travel a hundred miles along the tree-tops, finding it far pleasanter than trudging through brambles and being eaten by creeping things from dawn till dusk."

"But remember," said Jack, "how they warned us of the Amazons."

"What, Jack, afraid of a parcel of women?"

"Why not?" Jack countered. "I wouldn't flee from a man, as you know; but a woman—it is unnatural. They must be witches or devils. See how the Caribs feared them. They spoke of men there without necks, with eyes in their breasts. How could a Christian tackle such creatures?"

Alexander von Humboldt, in one of his more curious passages, observed a wretched group of Indians squatting dully around their fires, smeared with grease and paint, subsisting on ants and clay. With a naive candor, he remarked that were it not for science—which teaches us that such crude material constitutes the foundation of humanity and represents the state from which we all evolved—he would have been tempted to view these hapless creatures as the final, degraded remnants of a fallen and dying race. One wishes the great traveler had possessed the courage to succumb to that temptation, accepting his own reason and common sense as the true explanation of the tragic sight, rather than adhering to the dogmas of a so-called science lacking factual basis. His own good sense, it appears, aligned instinctively with Biblical doctrine: that man in a state of nature is a fallen being, doomed to death—a grim perspective, perhaps, yet one more honorable to humanity than the theory that we descended from ape-like ancestors. It is surely more hopeful to believe that the poor Otomacs or Guahibas were simply failing to meet their potential, rather than believing they were too foolish to improve their condition over millennia.

However, our voyagers, like their contemporaries, troubled their minds with no such philosophical inquiries. Accepting the Biblical narrative as written, they sided with Humboldt's personal judgment rather than his scientific conclusions—or, more accurately, they trusted Humboldt's intuition over the shallow anthropological theories popular fifty years prior. Consequently, their new hosts were viewed as immortal souls akin to themselves, "captivated by the devil at his will," currently lost in the pathless forests and destined to remain so eternally.

Facts seemed to corroborate these old-fashioned theories, though these Indians had not sunk quite as low as the Guahibas encountered earlier on the lower reaches of the river. Upon landing, the explorers witnessed a scattered village of palm-leaf sheds, from which hammocks were suspended between trees. Here and there, amidst the forest openings, patches of cassava and indigo grew, lending the settlement a neatness and comfort surpassing the regional average. Yet, ominous signs of the evil spirit were immediately apparent.

No benevolent force could have inspired the music emanating from the clearing. As Cary noted, if Apollo and Mercury ever visited America, they had played a shabby trick on the natives, providing them with inferior instruments and worse taste. On either side of the landing site stood four or five sturdy figures, each wielding a tall drum or a long earthen trumpet. These instruments, swelling along their lengths into several hollow spheres, erupted into a deafening cacophony of howls, groans, and thumps the moment the strangers stepped ashore. This sound fully justified Yeo's observation: "They are calling upon their devil, sir." Cary retorted with a semblance of logic, "They are the less likely to be disappointed, for none but Sir Urian would ever come to listen to such a noise."

"Nonsense!" Amyas declared. "We could slaughter every soul of them in half an hour, and they know it as well as we do."

More importantly, he sought to know if the tribe would provide cassava bread and allow them to rest peacefully on the island before proceeding to fight the clothed men—the Spaniards—beyond the mountains.

Finally, a triumphant burst rang out so shrilly that all ears echoed, followed by absolute silence.

The Piache, instantly restored to life, jumped upright and resumed his preaching to Amyas.

More than a fortnight had passed in grueling toil, yet nothing compared to the hardships they had previously endured. Having bid a final farewell to the verdant oceans of the eastern plains, the expedition had crossed the formidable Cordillera mountains. They had cast a longing gaze upon Santa Fe, nestled amidst lush gardens on its high plateau, only to realize it was far too fortified and populous for their ambitions. Yet, their efforts had not been wasted. Their Indian guide had uncovered intelligence: a gold train was descending from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena River.

Consequently, they had established their camp beside a treacherous, muddy rut serving as a road, hidden within a dense oak forest. The setting was deceptive; the towering oaks and fern-filled undergrowth evoked memories of Europe, save for the deep gorges yawning beneath them. From their elevated position, cooled by temperate breezes, they could peer down through a perpetual mist of tropical heat to behold the vibrant, chaotic splendor of the jungle floor.

Pitched among the tree-ferns, overlooking a steep hillside where a ruined path wound dangerously close to a sheer cliff hundreds of feet deep, the group waited. Once, during the reign of the civilized Kingdom of Cundinamarca, this route had been paved with aqueducts, bridges, and orderly post-houses. Now, Spanish misrule had reduced it to a muddy ditch where stones slipped from their places. Like the gardens and llama herds of that strange empire, all arts of civilization had crumbled in the mad scramble for gold, leaving only the magnificent cathedrals rising from the ruins of the Sun Temple—a "milder" pantheon that still demanded human sacrifice, a stark contrast to the gentle nature-worship of the Incas.

Nature had swiftly reclaimed its domain. Amyas and his crew felt as isolated as explorers in the depths of the Orinoco or the Amazon, mere miles from a significant Spanish settlement. Attempts to locate sulfur and saltpeter had failed, forcing them to rely solely on their swords and bows—a prospect that disgusted Yeo. Yet, history offered precedent; Drake had seized Nombre de Dios and its gold train with similar limited weaponry. If they could not find explosives, they would fight.

To secure their advantage, they had felled a massive tree across the road above, creating a natural blockade. Sitting among the flowers, chewing coca to quell their hunger and thirst, they pondered a mysterious roar that had haunted their nights since leaving the Meta riverbanks. It was neither jaguar nor monkey, yet it persisted. Being in a land of wonders, they dismissed the noise as secondary to the impending capture of the gold train.

It was a heartbreaking and horrific sight, yet tragically common in these remote regions where royal decrees protecting indigenous peoples were ignored. Even the wise Inquisitor Pedro de la Gasca's reforms in Peru had proven futile and delayed against such entrenched cruelty. On the frontiers, where black slaves were imported to perform labor fatal to Indians, and tribes accused of cannibalism were hunted down for "salvation," such scenes remained routine. Indeed, as Humboldt noted, conditions showed little improvement even centuries later in Jesuit missions, where military tyranny compounded monastic rule, and the Gospel was preached with fire and sword.

It was now that she entered that perilous crisis common to all savages upon first mingling with white men—a crisis that, mere years later, would hasten the extermination of the North American tribes. Had it not been for Amyas's admirable good sense and constancy, Ayacanora might have met a fate even more miserable than that of Pocahontas, the famous daughter of the Virginia king. After being welcomed into court by the pedantic King James I with the honors of a sister-sovereign, and becoming the reputed ancestress of numerous ancient families, she ultimately ended her days in wretchedness in a garret near Wapping.

The mind of the savage, crushed by witnessing the white man's superior skill, wealth, and wisdom, initially loses its self-respect. Meanwhile, his body, pampered by easily obtained luxuries rather than earning necessities through heavy toil, forfeits its self-reliance. With self-respect and self-helplessness gone, the few and fragile savage virtues vanish, leaving the path to begging, stealing, drunkenness, and idleness clear and inviting.

It seemed, indeed, that poor Ayacanora was walking swiftly down this road. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas soon appeared to be nothing more than a very naughty child. The Diana of the Meta, after satisfying her simple wonder at the great floating vessel by roaming from deck to deck and peering into every cupboard and crevice, developed a strong propensity to steal and hide every trifle that caught her fancy (too proud or too shy to ask). When Amyas forbade her to take anything without permission, she threatened to drown herself and spent the entire day sulking in her cabin. Nevertheless, she obeyed him, except regarding sweets. Perhaps she craved the vegetable foods of her native forests; certainly, the Bishop's stores of fruit and confections dwindled rapidly. Worse still, so did the sweet Spanish wine Amyas had reserved as a daily cordial for the ailing Lucy.

Upon receiving another severe lecture from Amyas regarding how mean it was to rob poor sick Lucy, Ayacanora, as usual, threatened suicide. She ran onto the deck to carry out her threat, but Amyas caught her and forgave her. This elicited a violent fit of weeping, profound penitence, and endless promises. A week later, Amyas discovered she had cheated both Satan and her conscience by tormenting the Portuguese steward into providing her with alternative wine. Fortunately for her, Amyas's warnings about alcohol proving madness had begun to manifest; she performed several foolish acts one evening and suffered a terrible headache the next morning. Consequently, the "murder" plot was exposed, and Amyas ordered the steward a sound flogging. However, Ayacanora, acting honorably, not only begged him off but offered to be whipped in his stead, confessing that the poor fellow had spoken truly when he swore she had threatened his life and that he had given her the wine only in fear for his safety.

Her own headache and Amyas's cold demeanor served as sufficient lessons. After another attempted drowning, the wilful beauty settled down for a while; remarkably, she could hardly be persuaded, to her dying day, to touch fermented liquors.

Meanwhile, poor Amyas endured countless headaches of his own, pondering how to tame a lady who, at the slightest provocation, fled to suicide. He dared not punish her, even if he possessed the heart to do so. As for setting her ashore, he harbored an instinct—not necessarily superstitious—that her strange affection for the English was sent by Heaven. He believed God had entrusted her into his charge and would demand an accounting for the soul of that fair lost lamb.

Driven nearly to his wits' end, the good simple fellow prayed repeatedly for guidance on how to handle her before she destroyed herself or someone else on the crew. It seemed best to him to have Parson Jack teach her the rudiments of Christianity, intending to baptize her properly upon reaching England.

A new difficulty immediately arose: she flatly refused to learn from Jack or anyone but Amyas himself. Since Amyas had ample reasons to decline the role of schoolmaster, Ayacanora remained untaught for a week or two, aside from the English language, which she acquired with marvelous speed.

Next, as if troubles would never cease, she developed a violent dislike for John Brimblecombe, openly mimicking his gait and voice for the amusement of the men, and for Will Cary, whom she forbade to speak to or approach. Perhaps she was jealous of his intimacy with Amyas; or perhaps, possessing the subtle instinct of a woman, she sensed he was the only other man capable of daring to woo her (though Will, to his credit, was as guiltless of such intentions as Amyas). When remonstrated with, her sole reply was that Cary was a *cacique* as well as Amyas, and there ought not to be two *caciques*. One day, she actually proposed to Amyas that he kill his supposed rival and take the ship entirely for himself, sulking for several days afterward when Amyas, amidst the crew's laughter, recounted her precious advice to its intended victim.

Furthermore, the negroes bore the brunt of her disdain. She viewed them with unspeakable repugnance, first hiding from them whenever possible, then throwing whatever she could find at them until the poor Quashies, fearing for their lives, complained to Amyas and secured temporary respite. Over the rest of the sailors, she ruled like a princess, summoning them from their labor to run errands and make toys, occasionally enforcing her commands with a sharp slap across the ear. The good fellows, particularly old Yeo, treated her like a tame leopard—petting, obeying, and jesting with her, knowing full well her claws could be unsheathed at any moment. Yet, she amused them, and even Amyas. They needed a pet, and what prettier one could they have? As for Amyas, the constant interest of her presence, coupled with the anxiety of her wilfulness, kept his mind occupied, driving away many sad forebodings regarding his meeting with his mother and the tragedy he must reveal to her. Without these burdens weighing so heavily, his worldly success and miraculous deliverance would have seemed worthless, crushing his spirit with melancholy.

Finally, as often happens, matters reached a climax.

The ship had been sailing slowly but steadily for many days under a favorable breeze. Having passed the ring of the West Indies, she was now crawling safely through the vast weed-beds of the Sargasso Sea. For the first time, it was deemed safe to relax the strict discipline maintained thus far and to "rummage" their noble prize. What they found in gold, silver, jewels, and merchandise need not concern the reader; suffice it to say, there was enough treasure, alongside the others, to make Amyas wealthy for life after paying off Cary's and the crew's shares, not to mention Mr. Salterne's third as the shipowner. In the captain's cabin, however, were discovered two chests: one filled with gorgeous Mexican feather dresses, the other with Spanish and East Indian finery destined for some *señora* in Caracas. These two chests were voted, amid the crew's acclamations, to Ayacanora as her due share of the plunder, considering her Amazonian prowess and valuable services.

The poor child seized the trifles greedily, had them carried to Lucy's cabin, and knelt gazing upon them for hours. She chose to despise the Mexican work as savage, but the Spanish dresses were a treasure. For two or three days, she appeared on the quarterdeck, sunning herself like a peacock before Amyas's eyes in Seville mantillas, Madrid hats, Indian brocade farthingales, and countless other gewgaws, wearing them with daring audacity.

The crew tittered; Amyas felt more inclined to weep. Nothing is so pathetic as a child's vanity, unless it is an adult imitating a child's vanity, or a child suffering the agony of disappointment when realizing it has been mocked rather than admired. Amyas wished to speak but feared to intervene. However, the evil brought its own remedy. The pageant proceeded, according to the performer's belief, successfully for three days. Finally, unable to contain herself, the duped girl appealed to Amyas: "Ayacanora is quite an English girl now, is she not?" Hearing a titter behind her, she glanced around, saw a dozen honest faces grinning broadly, comprehended everything instantly, darted down the companion ladder, and vanished.

Amyas, fully expecting her to jump overboard, followed as fast as he could. But she had locked herself inside with Lucy. He could hear her violent sobbing and Lucy's faint voice entreating to know what was wrong.

He knocked in vain. She refused to emerge all day, and by evening, they were forced to break down the door to prevent Lucy from starving.

Inside sat Ayacanora, her finery half-torn off and scattered across the floor in defiance, crying as though her heart would break; poor Lucy wept too, partly from fright and hunger, partly for companionship.

It was the evening of February 15th, 1587. At Burrough, Mrs. Leigh paced slowly up and down the terrace walk, her gaze fixed upon the winding river, the hazy sandhills, and the vast, western sea. She had performed this ritual every evening, in fair weather and foul, for three weary years. More than three years had passed, yet there remained no tidings of Frank or Amyas, nor of the gallant ship and the brave souls within her. The loving eyes of Bideford, Appledore, Clovelly, and Ilfracombe had grown hollow from watching and weeping for those who had sailed westward, vanishing like a dream into the infinite unknown—just as John Oxenham had done before them.

Three years of silence. Once, a flush of hope had ignited when Sir Richard, unbeknownst to Mrs. Leigh, dispatched a horseman to Plymouth upon hearing that Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle had returned from the Spanish Main. Alas, the messenger brought glorious news of the sackings of Cartagena, San Domingo, and Saint Augustine; of the relief of Raleigh's Virginian Colony. Yet, amidst the triumph, there was no word of the *Rose*, nor of those who had sailed in her.

Mrs. Leigh bowed her head in worship, murmuring, "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord!" Her hair had turned gray; her cheeks were wan; her step was feeble. She rarely ventured from home except to church or the neighboring cottages. She never uttered her sons' names, allowing not a single word to escape her lips that might betray thoughts of them. However, whenever the tide swelled high and the red flag atop the sandhills signaled water over the bar, she would pace the terrace, devouring the horizon with greedy eyes in search of a sail that never appeared.

Stately ships entered and exited the channel as of old; white sails hung off the bar for hours, days, months, and years. Yet, an inner instinct assured her that none were the vessel she sought. She knew that ship, every line of her hull, the cut of every canvas; she could have identified her from miles away amidst a whole fleet, but she never came. Mrs. Leigh bowed her head, worshipped, and moved among the poor, who regarded her as an awe-inspiring figure, one whom God had drawn near to Himself within that mysterious heaven of sorrow known so well to them. Lone women and bedridden men gazed into her steadfast eyes, finding strength there, for they understood—even though she never spoke of her own grief—that she had descended into the fiercest depths of the fiery furnace and walked there unharmed beside One whose form was akin to the Son of God. All the while, she blamed herself for her "earthly" longings, confessing nightly to Heaven the weakness she could not shake, which drew her feet to the terrace beneath the wind-clipped trees at every high tide.

That evening, however, Northam was in a ferment. The pebble ridge thundered far below, as it had years prior, but Northam was noisy enough without the rolling surge. The tower rocked with the pealing bells; the people filled the streets, shouting and singing around bonfires. They burned the Pope in effigy, toasted the Queen's health, and cried, "So perish all her enemies!" Hillsides glowed red with bonfires in every village. Far away, the bells of Bideford answered those of Northam, echoing their song from seven years ago when Amyas returned from circumnavigating the globe. Today, news had arrived that Mary, Queen of Scots, had been beheaded at Fotheringay. All England, like a dreamer shaking off a hideous nightmare, erupted in one tremendous shout of jubilation, lifting the terror and danger of seventeen anxious years forever.

"She is gone," the people whispered, "to answer at a higher tribunal than that of the Estates of England, for all the noble English blood spilled for her, and all the noble hearts she tempted into treachery, rebellion, and murder." Elizabeth's own prophecy had finally been fulfilled after years of long-suffering:

*"The daughter of debate, That discord aye doth sow, Hath reaped no gain where former rule Hath taught still peace to grow."*

Now, she could do no more evil. Murder, adultery, the unforgiving heart, and the tongue incapable of speaking truth even for its own sake had passed; perhaps they were atoned for. Even those who remembered that either she or England must perish recalled her fair face as a pitiful dream.

"Nothing is left of her now, but pure womanliness."

Even Mrs. Leigh, a staunch Protestant, breathed a prayer for mercy upon that soul, describing it as "clear as diamond, and as hard," as she had once described herself. She pondered the final scene before the fatal block—it could not have been mere acting. Mrs. Leigh had learned many priceless lessons in the last seven years; might not Mary Stuart have learned something in seventeen? Having been a courtier, she knew, as far as a chaste Englishwoman could know, of the godless profligacy of the French court in which poor Mary had been trained among the Medicis, the Guises, and Cardinal Lorraine. She shuddered and sighed, thinking, "To whom little is given, of them shall little be required!" Yet the bells continued to peal, refusing to cease.

What answered them from the deepening twilight? A flash, followed by the thunder of a gun at sea.

Mrs. Leigh halted. The flash occurred right outside the bar. It could not be a ship in distress; the wind was light and westerly, and it was a high spring tide. What could it be? Another flash, another gun. The noisy throng of Northam fell silent instantly, rushing into the churchyard overlooking the broad flats and the river.

There was a gallant ship outside the bar, running in with all sails set. She was a large vessel, nearly a thousand tons, but not of English rig. What was the meaning of it? A Spanish cruiser seeking reprisals for Drake's raid along the Cadiz shore? Unlikely. The Don favored scientific warfare with admirals, rear-admirals, vice-admirals, transports, and avisos, adhering strictly to approved methods.

Easily, on the flowing tide and fair western wind, she had slipped up the channel between the twin lines of sandhills. She was almost off Appledore now. She was no enemy; if she were a foreigner, she was a daring one, for she had never veiled her topsails—a requirement for any foreign ship within sight of an English port, lest it risk war. This was proven by the Spanish admiral who, many years prior, sailed into Plymouth Sound in peace to fetch Anne of Austria, Philip II's last wife. In his pride, he failed to veil his topsails or lower the Spanish flag. Upon this, John Hawkins, the Port Admiral, rushed forth in his famous *Jesus of Lübeck* and fired a shot between the admiral's masts. When this failed to produce the desired effect, bold Captain John ran alongside and, as his son later testified, "lacked the admiral through and through," causing the offending flag to drop. Apologies were made, though not immediately accepted by the stout guardian of her Majesty's honor. If John Hawkins acted thus against a Spanish fleet in peacetime, there were many old sea-dogs in Appledore ready to act similarly against a single ship in wartime, if they could find even an iron pot to burn powder with.

The strange sail disappeared behind the hill of Appledore, and then a cheer rose into the quiet evening air from a hundred throats. Mrs. Leigh stood still, listening. Another gun thundered among the hills, followed by another cheer.

Perhaps twenty minutes passed before the vessel hove in sight again, rounding the dark rocks of the Hubbastone as she turned up the Bideford River. During this entire time, Mrs. Leigh had stood perfectly motionless, a pale, scarcely breathing statue, her eyes fixed upon the Viking's Rock.

Finally, she rounded the stone. Music blared from the deck—drums, fifes, shawms, and trumpets, waking ringing echoes from every knoll of wood and slab of slate. As she opened fully onto Burrough House, another cheer burst from her crew, rolling up to the hills from the silver waters a mile below.

Mrs. Leigh walked swiftly toward the house and summoned her maid.

"Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!"

"No, surely? Oh, joyful sound! Praise and bless the Lord, then; praise and bless the Lord! But, madam, how did you know?"

"I heard his voice on the river; but I did not hear Mr. Frank's with him, Grace!"

"Oh, be sure, madam, where the one is, the other is. They would never part company. Both come home or neither, I warrant. Here is your hood, madam."

With Grace trailing behind, Mrs. Leigh started with rapid steps toward Bideford.

Amyas had returned home and settled quietly into domestic life. For the following twelve months, little occurred worthy of historical record. His companion, Yeo, had installed himself as *major domo*, a title with no clear duties other than trailing Amyas everywhere like a lank greyhound and spending his evenings by the fireside. True to his nature as an old sailor, Yeo kept a Bible on his knees while busily crafting countless knick-knacks—both useful and frivolous—for the entire household, particularly for Ayacanora. Every week, he would humbly offer her a toy suitable only for a child; she would pout, be scolded by Mrs. Leigh, accept the gift, and store it away forevermore. Her entire soul was consumed with the desire to become an English maiden. She spent her days chasing Mrs. Leigh, eager to learn the secrets of the kitchen, the still-room, and, above all, the art of sewing clothes for herself and eventually for everyone in Northam. First, she wished to be a good housewife like Mrs. Leigh; then, a new idea blossomed within her: the concept of giving to those who could give nothing in return.

Ayacanora had grown accustomed to the boundless hospitality of the savages, but the notion of charity toward the destitute was entirely new. Observing Mrs. Leigh spend her spare hours working for the poor and visiting their cottages, and seeing Amyas distribute money, food, and goods throughout Northam, Appledore, and Bideford after receiving public thanks in church for his safe return, she was puzzled. Her understanding of a "thank-offering" was rooted in the practices of the Indians and Spaniards—human sacrifices and the decoration of temples with skulls and bones. Yet, Amyas, a plain old-fashioned churchman, adhered strictly to the instinctive rule of giving to the Church itself. Soon, the vicar of Northam was resplendent in a new surplice, and the altar gleamed with a magnificent flagon and salver of plate taken from the great galleon (presumably lost during the Civil Wars). Ayacanora understood this sacrifice, but the alms-giving remained a mystery until Mrs. Leigh explained simply that whoever gave to the poor gave directly to the Great Spirit, for the Spirit dwelt within them—and within Ayacanora, too, if she would only be quiet and listen instead of pouting and stamping her feet. The poor child absorbed this truth readily. She worked her fingers to the bone serving the elderly women of Northam, accompanied Mrs. Leigh with a loveliness that endeared her to all, and occasionally peeked from beneath her long black lashes to see if she had won a smile from Amyas. On days she succeeded, she behaved perfectly; on days she failed, she was thoroughly naughty, testing the patience of anyone less saintly than Mrs. Leigh.

However, regarding the splendor of her attire, there were no bounds. Each Sunday, she swept into church bedizened in Spanish finery, creating such a blaze and rustle that the good vicar felt compelled to humbly remonstrate with Mrs. Leigh about the disturbance she caused to the congregation's eyes and minds. Ayacanora retorted that she paid no attention to them, nor did they need to pay attention to her. If the *Piache* (the conjurer, in plain English) desired a present, he could have all her Mexican feather-dresses; she would not wear them—they were wild Indian things, unsuitable for an English maid—but they would suit a *Piache* perfectly. Thus, she dashed upstairs, retrieved the garments, and insisted so stubbornly on dressing the vicar in them that the good man fled in haste. Nevertheless, he managed to carry off one of the finest mantles, which he cleverly repurposed into an altar-cloth. For several years thereafter, the communion at Northam was celebrated upon a blaze of emerald, azure, and crimson that had once adorned the sinful body of an Aztec prince.

While Ayacanora flaunted her colors, Amyas watched her with a mixture of amusement and simple pride in her beauty, casting glances around the church as if to declare, "Behold the fine bird I have brought home!"

Another significant trouble she caused Mrs. Leigh was her treatment of the neighborhood ladies. They flocked to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and catch a glimpse of the fair savage, but Ayacanora snubbed them all—from the vicar's wife to Lady Grenville—with such effectiveness that few dared a second visit. Mrs. Leigh remonstrated, only to be met with floods of tears. "They merely come to stare at a poor wild Indian girl," Ayacanora cried. "I would not be made a spectacle of. Once I was like a queen, and everyone obeyed me; but here, everyone looks down upon me." When Mrs. Leigh asked if she would prefer returning to the forests, the poor girl clung to her like a baby, begging not to be sent away, declaring she would sooner be a slave in the kitchen than return to the "bad people."

Month after month passed, marked by foolish storms and foolish sunshines. Yet, Ayacanora existed under the shadow of a figure characterized by neither storm nor sunshine, but a perpetual, genial calm of soft grey weather that tempered all who entered its sphere. Her outbursts grew rarer, and she became increasingly rational, though no happier, day by day.

Gradually, small hints emerged confirming her identity, at least in the eyes of Mrs. Leigh and Yeo. Once familiar with the sight of houses, she indicated she had seen similar structures before. The red cattle seemed known to her, while the sheep puzzled her until she admitted they were far too small.

"Ah, madam," remarked Yeo, seizing every clue, "it is because she has been accustomed to those great camel-sheep—the llamas—in Peru."

Ayacanora's greatest delight, however, was a horse. The mere existence of tamed animals was a daily wonder to her, but riding one was the crowning miracle. After pestering Amyas in vain for a mount (he refused to risk breaking her pretty neck), she confided in Yeo that they should steal one. Foiled in that attempt, she turned to the vicar, offering to barter all her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar, too honest to strike such a bargain, declined. Consequently, Amyas purchased her a jennet, which she mastered in a fortnight, riding like a very Gaucho.

Soon, another curious slumbering memory awoke. One evening, invited by Lady Grenville, the whole family traveled to Stow. Mrs. Leigh rode soberly on a pillion behind the groom, while Ayacanora cantered wildly around the moors like a released hound, attempting to drag Amyas into a race. That night, sleeping in the same room as Mrs. Leigh, she awoke screaming, sobbing out a long tale of how the "Old Ape of Panama"—her special abhorrence—had dragged her from her bedside into the courtyard. She claimed she had mounted a horse and ridden with an Indian over vast moors and high mountains down into a dark wood, where both the Indian and the horses vanished, leaving her suddenly transformed once more into a little savage child. The impression was so vivid that she could not be convinced it had not happened, if not that very night, then certainly on some previous occasion. Mrs. Leigh, in her pious manner, accepted this as a revelation from the Lord, telling the company the next morning that the Lord had shown the child her true identity: a girl exposed in the forests by her jealous stepfather. Neither Sir Richard nor his wife could doubt this explanation. It was probable that Don Xararte, though his home was Panama, had frequented Quito, for Yeo had seen him aboard the *Lima* at Guayaquil, one of the nearest ports. Imagine, for a moment, the spectators who last summer stood with swelling pride upon the noble port of Plymouth. They beheld its vast breakwater spanning the Sound, its arsenals and docks, its twin estuaries teeming with gallant ships. They watched the great screw-driven liners turning within their own lengths by invisible forces, or threading crowded fleets with the effortless grace of a toy skiff. Now, suppose that by some magical reversal, the nineteenth century—with all its magnificent wealth and scientific marvels—vanished entirely. Suppose they found themselves cast back three hundred years to the pleasant summer days of 1588.

Mount Edgecombe would remain, beautiful as ever. Yet, where are the docks? Where is Devonport? No vast dry-dock roofs would rise at the water's edge. Drake's Island would carry but a paltry battery, hastily erected by the man whose name it bore; Mount Wise would be merely a solitary gentleman's house amidst the fields; the citadel would be a pop-gun fortification, capable of being reduced to rubble by a third-class steamer for mere afternoon amusement. And the shipping? The floating castles of the Hamoaze would have dwindled to a handful of crawling lime-hoys. The Catwater, now packed with merchant craft, would instead be congested with the vessels destined to commence the greatest naval battle the world has ever witnessed.

To modern eyes, this squadron would seem pitifully small. Their largest vessel would not match the tonnage of a sixty-gun frigate, carrying less weight of metal than one of our modern gunboats, and unable to deploy that metal beyond a quarter of our current effective range. Would our modern tourists, arriving by rail for a brief excursion to witness the cavalry embark and returning in time for dinner, have looked down upon this modest port and its tiny fleet with a contemptuous sneer? Doubtless, they would have begun some flippant monologue regarding the progress of intellect and the triumphs of science, dismissing their benighted ancestors. Such would be the reaction of the multitude who gaze at human achievement as a raree-show to feed their silly wonder, using and enjoying it without gratitude or comprehension, much like an ox eating clover thrust into its rack without knowing how it grew.

However, if among them were those who actually *achieved* these triumphs—the thinkers and workers who, unlike the idle mob, labor themselves; who know by hard experience the struggles, self-restraints, disappointments, and staggering steps required for discovery—then their expression would not be one of pity, but of filial love. They would perceive in those outwardly paltry armaments the germs of the mightier fleets that now load the Black Sea waves. They would understand that producing such power with the limited materials and knowledge of the age demanded an intellect, energy, and spirit of invention equal to, if not surpassing, our own boasted capabilities today.

Yet, if one were a student of men rather than machines, he would find fewer nobler companies to observe than those gathered on the little terrace bowling-green behind the Pelican Inn on the afternoon of July 19th. Chatting in groups or lounging over the low wall commanding a view of the Sound and the distant shipping, sat nearly every notable man of the Plymouth fleet—the entire posse comitatus of "England's forgotten worthies." The Armada had supposedly been scattered by a storm; Lord Howard had ventured as far as the Spanish coast seeking it, but the wind had shifted south. Fearful that the Dons might slip past him, he had returned to Plymouth, uncertain whether the Armada would arrive at all.

A burly, grizzled elder, clad in greasy, sea-stained garments that contrast oddly with the massive gold chain about his neck, waddles forward as if born and living solely within a perpetual gale. The upper half of his sharp, dogged visage resembles red leather, the lower half badger's fur. As he slaps Drake on the back and shouts in a broad Devon accent, "Be you a coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or be you not?—saving your presence, my lord," the Lord High Admiral simply laughs and bids Drake go and drink his wine. For John Hawkins, Admiral of the Port, is the patriarch of Plymouth seamen; if Drake is their hero, Hawkins is their grandfather. He says and does precisely what he pleases in any company, especially given that the prospect of an Armageddon-style battle has shaken his usual crabbed reserve, overflowing him with loquacious good humor—even toward his rival Drake.

They push through the crowd, where many others await conversation. Martin Frobisher and John Davis sit on a bench smoking tobacco from long silver pipes; beside them are Fenton and Withrington, who attempted to follow Drake's global path but failed, though not through their own fault. Present also are John Drake, Sir Francis's brother and ancestor of the current Drake lineage; George, his nephew—a man not overly wise who circumnavigated the globe with Amyas; and Amyas himself, conversing with one who replies in fierce, curt sentences: Captain Barker of Bristol, brother of the ill-fated Andrew Barker who discovered John Oxenham's guns and, due to a mutiny, perished at the hands of the Spaniards in Honduras twelve years prior. Barker now commands the *Victory*, one of the Queen's finest ships, and shares grievances with the Dons just as Amyas does; thus, they growl together in a corner while the rest revel like flies upon a vine.

But who is the aged man seated against the sunny southern wall of the tavern? His long white beard flows nearly to his waist, his hands rest on his knees, and his palsied head moves slowly from side to side, catching fragments of discourse from passing captains. At his knee lies his great-granddaughter, a little maid of six, resting her curly head upon him. Having witnessed the death of old Europe and the birth-pangs of the new, he appears uneasy, peering through the groups with rheumy eyes, listening intently for a familiar voice.

Thus sang Campbell of the mariners of England, and now the prophecy hung heavy in the air as the great sea-fight commenced. It was a contest destined to decide whether Popery and despotism or Protestantism and freedom would become the law for half of Europe and the entirety of future America. It was a twelve-day epic, worthy not of dull prose but of the thunderous roll of Homer's verse. Yet, to recount it faithfully, I must rely wherever possible on the words of contemporary witnesses rather than my own invention.

"The Lord High Admiral of England, sending a pinnace named *Defiance* ahead, declared war by firing her cannons. Immediately following, he approached within musket range, unleashing a storm of thunder from his flagship, the *Ark Royal* (also known as the *Triumph*), and launched the initial assault upon what he believed to be the Spanish Admiral's vessel—though it turned out to be the ship of Alfonso de León. Shortly thereafter, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher engaged stoutly with their artillery against the rear squadron, commanded by Recalde."

The Spaniards quickly discerned the superior swiftness of the English ships. Realizing they were giving more than they took, despite Recalde's efforts, his squadron hurried forward to join the main fleet. Admiral Medina, seeing his formation scatter rapidly, gathered them into a crescent shape. The Armada pressed onward with solemn, stately purpose, resembling a massive herd of buffalo marching across a prairie, disdainfully ignoring the wolves snarling around their path. But these were no mere wolves; they were cunning hunters, swift of horse and keen of blade, intent on shaming that vast herd from the Lizard to Portland, and from Portland to Calais Roads. Even in this brief two-hour engagement, many a Spaniard began to question the boasted invincibility of their Armada.

One of the four great galliasses was already riddled with shot, her pulpits and chapels shattered, the friars within thrown into disarray. The fleet was forced to circle around her to prevent Drake and Hawkins from sinking her. In executing this maneuver, the "principal galleon of Seville," carrying Pedro de Valdez and a host of blue-blooded nobles, ran aground against her neighbor, tearing away her foremast. Despite Spanish chivalry, she was left to her fate. This did not look like victory. Yet, courage! Though Valdez was abandoned, "Our Lady," the saints, and the bull *Caena Domini*—dictated by one I dare not name here—were still with them. To doubt would be blasphemy. Meanwhile, if they fared poorly against merely a third of the Plymouth fleet, how would they withstand the forty belated ships already appearing on the horizon, whitening the blue waters between them and the Mewstone?

Thus ended the first day. Not an English ship, nor hardly a single man, was injured. The event had forever destroyed the prestige of boastful Spain in English minds and fully justified the policy Lord Howard had adopted on the advice of Raleigh and Drake: to maintain a running fight rather than clamping ships together without consideration. As Raleigh noted, had he done otherwise, he would have been lost, unlike the "great many malignant fools" who later faulted his conduct.

As it was, the day concluded with Amyas Leigh and the other Bideford ships busy for two hours hammering holes in a colossal galleon bearing the name *Sta. Catharina*. A maiden with a wheel adorned her stern, and her flag displayed the arms of the soldier-commandant, though these were shot away early in the fray, leaving Amyas unable to confirm if they belonged to Don Guzman. Still, there was ample opportunity for private vengeance. Finally recalled by the Admiral's signal, Amyas retired to his hammock and slept soundly.

However, before an hour had passed, he was awakened by Cary descending to request orders.

"We were to follow Drake's lantern, Amyas," Cary reported, "but I cannot see it, unless he has been taken aloft among the stars to become a new *Drakium Sidus*."

Amyas grumbled, turning out his lamp, but no lantern appeared. Instead, there was a sudden explosion and a great fire on a Spanish vessel, which was gradually extinguished. Consequently, the entire fleet lay-to for the night.

The next morning found them off Torbay. Amyas was hailed by a pinnace bearing a letter from Drake. Saving the somewhat arbitrary spelling common to men of those days, it read roughly as follows:

*"DEAR LAD,—I have been wool-gathering all night after five great hulks, which the Pixies transfigured overnight into galleons, and this morning again into German merchantmen. I answered him that I had no time to bargain; if he wished to die, I was the very man for him; if he wished to live, then *buena guerra*. "She sails in this squadron of Recalde," the message continued. "The Armada intended to smoke us out of Plymouth; and God's grace it was they did not try. "He has given the Lord High Admiral the dowry, at all events."

"Lord Howard is too high-hearted to stop and plunder, Papist though he is, Amyas."

Amyas answered with a growl, for he worshipped Drake and was not overly just toward Papists.

The fleet did not locate Lord Howard until nightfall; he and Lord Sheffield had held their position steadfastly all night, tracking the Spanish lanterns with only two ships. At least, there was now no doubt regarding the loyalty of English Roman Catholics. Indeed, throughout the fight, the Howards demonstrated a desperate courage that might have led less prudent men to destruction but ultimately guided them to victory. Soon, a large Spanish ship drifted by, deserted and partially burned. Some men urged leaving their posts to board her, but Amyas stoutly refused. "I have come out to fight, and not to plunder," he declared. "Let the nearest ship to her have her luck without grudging." They passed on, and the men pulled long faces as they watched the galleon snap up by their neighbor and towed off to Weymouth. There, it proved to be the ship of Miguel d'Oquenda, the Vice-Admiral, which they had seen the previous night, almost blown up by a desperate Netherland gunner who, feeling "misused," sought to settle old scores with his tyrants.

Thus ended the second day, while the Portland hills rose higher and clearer every hour. The next morning found them off the island. Would they attempt Portsmouth, though they had spared Plymouth? The wind had shifted to the north, blowing clear and cool off the white-walled downs of Weymouth Bay. The Spaniards turned to face the English, intending to stand off and on until the wind changed, hoping then to reach the Needles. At least, they would have some work to do before rounding Purbeck Isle.

The words of *The Tempest* echoed in the mind of the narrator as the reality of history settled upon the world. The great Armada was vanquished. The endless celestial war—the clash between light and darkness, Michael and Satan—had finally descended from the spiritual realm into the tangible chaos of flesh and blood. A day of judgment had arrived, dividing the sheep from the goats and testing every man's work by fire. The devil's grandiose schemes, built upon Byzantine empires, Spanish fleets, and papal authority, collapsed like a bladder pierced by a pin. While the "children of Israel" watched the Egyptian hosts perish on the shore, singing songs of deliverance, a shout of holy joy rose from England, the Netherlands, and the persecuted valleys of the Alps. This victory was the prophetic birth-paean of a new era: the dawn of North America, Australia, and the age of free commerce and science.

Queen Elizabeth herself, imitating the ancient Romans, rode into London in triumph. Drawn through the city in a chariot adorned with the captured colors of the Spaniards, she was escorted to St. Paul's Cathedral amidst a spectacle of blue cloth and banners. There, she gave thanks to God and exhorted her people to join in the celebration, declaring that the enemy had "come, seen, and fled." Like a herd of frightened deer, the surviving Spanish fleet had scattered northward toward the Orkneys, Shetlands, and the frozen wastes of Norway, only to find themselves abandoned by the very lands they sought refuge in. Cut off from supplies and driven by relentless storms, the remnant of the mighty host drifted toward the lonely Faroes and the midnight sun, their fate sealed by the Atlantic's fury.

While the survivors perished, what of Amyas and the *Vengeance*?

Upon learning that the High Admiral intended to retreat, Amyas requested permission to pursue the fleeing Spaniard. He secured provisions and ammunition from Sir John Hawkins, pledging repayment from future plunder or his own estate should he fall. Rallying his crew of Bideford men, he issued a stirring call: "Will you follow me? There will be plunder for those who love plunder; revenge for those who love revenge; and for all of us, the honor of never leaving the chase until the last Spanish flag falls from English seas." Every soul aboard vowed to follow him anywhere.

The subsequent pursuit was a harrowing odyssey. They chased the *Santa Catalina*, forcing her to flee when reinforced by the main fleet. When Medina's squadron abandoned the crippled ships, Amyas boldly thrust into the center of the Spanish formation to prevent their escape, crippling the *Santa Catalina* so severely she was forced to run south through the treacherous Minch. Along the way, tensions flared; the young hands nearly mutinied when Amyas ignored easy prizes to press the chase, and friction arose with the wild inhabitants of Skye, who initially reviled them before embracing them in a display of unexpected kinship. Yet, through it all, Amyas remained relentless. He allowed his men to loot a stranded Venetian galley to pay for their voyage but sternly forbade them from plundering Spanish wrecks off the Mull of Cantire, ensuring justice rather than greed prevailed.

Sixteen days into the chase, the scene shifted dramatically. The *Vengeance* and the stricken *Santa Catalina* found themselves trapped in the narrowing Bristol Channel. The weather turned foul; a sunless, drizzly day blanketed the sea in a leaden mist, obscuring both land and foe. Amyas paced the wet deck, his soul blackened by frustration. He cursed the lingering moments between himself and the final act of revenge he craved. His men sat sullenly, whistling for a wind that did not come, while Amyas sharpened his sword with a frenzied intensity, his body trembling with suppressed rage.

"You are not yourself," his friend Cary warned him later, noting the captain's feverish state. "You are not Amyas Leigh anymore; you are your brother's avenger." Amyas admitted as much, confessing that his throat and heart burned with a heat akin to hellfire. He prayed for nothing but the swift conclusion of his quest, willing to die instantly if it meant ending the torment.

Suddenly, the clouds parted. To the south, a beam of sunlight illuminated the tall masts of the *Santa Catalina*. She was trapped, unable to escape westward due to the wind, and likely lying-to ahead of them. Hope surged, followed immediately by dread as the wind shifted and the storm gathered force.

"The storm is coming!" Amyas declared, his voice steady despite the gathering tempest. "It will be Eastward-ho now!"

As the black line of the storm approached, the *Vengeance* caught the breeze and surged forward, driving straight toward the helpless Spanish galleon. Lightning flashed, illuminating the chaotic dance of the waves, and the thunder roared overhead. Amidst the deluge, the two ships closed the distance rapidly. Just as the squall reached its peak, a lookout screamed, "Land right ahead!"

Amyas slammed the helm hard to starboard. The *Vengeance* swung violently, sails tearing under the strain, but she held her course. Through the hail and the roar of the surf, a jagged silhouette emerged: the rocky pinnacle of Lundy Island, specifically the notorious "Shutter," a massive rock formation jutting from the sea like a black fang. Between the rock and the mainland, the *Santa Catalina* was visible, struggling desperately against the elements. Clumsy and unresponsive, the great galleon failed to maneuver, rushing blindly toward the breaking waves.

With the *Vengeance* bearing down on the doomed vessel, the stage was set for the final confrontation, framed by the terrifying beauty of the storm and the unforgiving cliffs of the Bristol Channel.

"It was the first of October. The morning was bright and still; the skies were dappled modestly from east to west with soft gray autumn clouds, as if all heaven and earth were resting after those fearful summer months of battle and storm."

Silently, as if ashamed and sad, the *Vengeance* slid over the bar, passed the sleeping sandhills, and dropped her anchor off Appledore. Her flag floated half-mast high, for the corpse of Salvation Yeo lay aboard. A small boat pulled away from the ship toward the western end of the strand. Cary and Brimblecombe helped out Amyas Leigh, leading him slowly up the hill toward his home.

A crowd had already clustered around him, offering cheers, blessings, and sobs of pity from kind-hearted women. Everyone in Appledore and Bideford knew by now what had befallen him.

"Spare me, my good friends," Amyas said softly. "I have landed here that I might go quietly home, without passing through the town and becoming a gazing-stock. Think not of me, good folks, nor talk of me; but come behind me decently, as Christian men, and follow to the grave the body of a better man than I."

As he spoke, another boat approached, carrying the body of Salvation Yeo, covered with the flag of England. The people took Amyas at his word. A man was sent ahead to Burrough to tell Mrs. Leigh that her son was coming. When the coffin was landed and lifted, Amyas and his friends took their places behind it as chief mourners, the crew following in order, while the crowd fell in behind them, gathering with every step. By the time they were halfway to Northam Town, the funeral train numbered nearly five hundred souls.

They had sent a fishing skiff the previous day to ask the sexton to dig the grave. Upon reaching the churchyard, the parson stood ready at the gate. Mrs. Leigh remained quietly at home; she had no heart to face the throng. Though her heart yearned for her son, she was content—more than content—that he should do honor to his ancient and faithful servant. Sitting in the bay-window with Ayacanora by her side, she waited until the tolling of the bell ceased. Then, opening her Prayer-book, she began to read the Burial Service.

"Ayacanora," she said gently, "they are burying old Master Yeo, who loved you, and sought you over the wide, wide world, and saved you from the teeth of the crocodile. Are you not sorry for him, child, that you look so gay today?"

Ayacanora blushed and hung her head. Thinking of nothing but Amyas, the poor child offered no answer.

When the service concluded and the parson withdrew, the people lingered, crowding round to gaze at the coffin. Amyas stood still at the head of the grave. It had been dug by his command at the west end of the church, near the foot of the tall, gray, windswept tower that watched far and wide over land and sea. Perhaps the old man liked to look at the sea, seeing ships come and go across the bar, or hearing the wind roar through the belfry on winter nights. Why not? It was merely a fancy, yet Amyas felt he too would wish to be buried in such a place; perhaps Yeo would approve.

Still, the crowd lingered, looking first at the grave and then at the blind giant standing over it. Instinctively, they sensed that something more was due. And something more did come.

Amyas drew himself up to his full height and waved his hand majestically, as one about to speak. All eyes were fastened upon him. Twice he essayed to begin, but twice the words choked upon his lips. Finally, he spoke:

"Good people all, and seamen, among whom I was bred, and to whom I come home blind this day, to dwell with you till death—here lieth the flower and pattern of all bold mariners; the truest of friends, and the most terrible of foes; unchangeable of purpose, crafty of council, and swift of execution; in triumph most sober, in failure (as God knows I have found full many a day) of endurance beyond mortal man.

Who first of all Britons helped to humble the pride of the Spaniard at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and first of all sailed upon those South Seas, which shall be hereafter, by God's grace, as free to English keels as is the bay outside. Who, having afterwards been purged from his youthful sins by strange afflictions and torments unspeakable, suffered at the hands of the Popish enemy, learned therefrom, my masters, to fear God, and to fear naught else; and having acquitted himself worthily in his place and calling as a righteous scourge of the Spaniard, and a faithful soldier of the Lord Jesus Christ, is now exalted to his reward, as Elijah was of old, in a chariot of fire unto heaven: letting fall, I trust and pray, upon you who are left behind the mantle of his valor and his godliness, that so these shores may never be without brave and pious mariners, who will count their lives as worthless in the cause of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen. Amen."

Feeling for his companions' hands, Amyas walked slowly from the churchyard, across the village street, and up the lane to the Burrough gates. The crowd made way for him in solemn silence, as for an awful being, shut up alone with all his strength, valor, and fame, in the dark prison-house of his mysterious doom.

He seemed to know perfectly when they reached the gates. Opening the lock with his own hands, he went boldly forward along the gravel path, while Cary and Brimblecombe followed him trembling. They expected some violent burst of emotion, either from him or his mother, and the two good fellows' tender hearts fluttered like a girl's.

Up to the door he went, as if he had seen it. Feeling for the entrance, he stood within and called quietly, "Mother!"

In a moment, his mother was on his bosom. Neither spoke for a while. She sobbed inwardly with tearless eyes; he stood firm and cheerful, his great arms clasped around her.

"Mother!" he said at last. "I am come home, you see, because I needs must come. Will you take me in, and look after this useless carcase? I shall not be so very troublesome, mother—shall I?"

He looked down, smiling, and kissed her brow. She answered not a word, but passed her arm gently round his waist and led him inside.

"Take care of your head, dear child; the doors are low."

Together they entered.

"Will! Jack!" called Amyas, turning round. But the two good fellows had already walked briskly off.

"I'm glad we are away," said Cary. "I should have made a baby of myself in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. How her face worked and how she kept it in!"

"Ah, well!" said Jack. "There goes a brave servant of the Queen's cut off before his work was a quarter done. Heigho! I must home now, and see my old father, and then—"

"And then home with me," said Cary. "You and I never part again! We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack; and you must not go spending your prize-money in riotous living. I must see after you, old Jack ashore, or we shall have you treating half the town in taverns for a week to come."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" said Jack, scandalized. "Come home with me, and we'll poison the parson, and my father shall give you the rectory."

So the two went off to Clovelly together that very day.

Amyas sat all alone. His mother had gone out briefly to speak to the seamen who had brought up his luggage, setting them down to eat and drink. Amyas sat in the old bay-window, where he had sat when he was a little tiny boy, reading "King Arthur," "Fox's Martyrs," and "The Cruelties of the Spaniards." He put out his hand and felt for the books; there they lay side by side, just as they had twenty years before.

The window was open, and a cool air brought in, as of old, the scents of four-season roses, rosemary, and autumn gilliflowers. On the table lay a dish of apples. He knew them by their smell—the very same old apples he used to gather when he was a boy. He put out his hand, took them, felt them over, and played with them, as if the twenty years had never been.

As he fingered them, the whole of his past life rose up before him, as in that strange dream said to flash across the imagination of a drowning man. He saw all the places he had ever seen and heard all the words ever spoken to him—until he came to that fairy island on the Meta. He heard the roar of the cataract once more, saw the green tops of the palm trees sleeping in the sunlight far above the spray, stepped amid the smooth palm trunks across the flower-fringed boulders, and leaped down to the gravel beach beside the pool.
