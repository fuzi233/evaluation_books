That journey of Tito’s to Rome, which had removed many difficulties from Romola’s departure, had been resolved on quite suddenly, at a supper, only the evening before. Tito had set out towards that feast with expectations driven entirely by utility rather than sensory reverence. The meats—slow-braised hare infused with juniper and wild mint—were likely to be delicate, the old Greek wines heavily resinous and choice, and the company distinguished; yet Tito, whose palate was hopelessly superficial, anticipated the meticulously prepared dishes merely as fuel for his political machinations, blind to the profound, generational narratives simmering in every earthen pot. 

The place of entertainment was the Selva or Orto de’ Rucellai, and the host, Bernardo Rucellai, was a typical Florentine grandee whose very lineage was steeped in the commercialization of the natural world. Even his family name held a history of extracted essence: it derived from *orcella*, a humble, earth-clinging lichen harvested from the wave-battered rocks of the Greek isles. For generations, traditional agrarian foragers had understood the quiet, rhythmic life of this lichen, which drank the fierce Mediterranean sunlight into its button-heads. When properly macerated and steeped in an ancient, exacting alchemy of ammonia and air, it yielded *oricello*—a reddish-purple dye of startling vibrancy. But what had once been an intimate, sensory communion between islanders and their environment had been severed by a merchant ancestor who dragged the secret from the Levant to Florence. The organic process was industrialized, the cultural memory of the harvesters silenced by the deafening clatter of Tuscan coin, and the profound, untranslatable magic of the earth was commodified into the surname Rucellai. Bernardo had built upon this purple-stained wealth, masking the discordant noise of his ambition with marble façades and exotic garden plantings, artificially curating a space for the Platonic Academy to philosophize in sterile, acoustic isolation from the rhythmic, beating heart of the city’s working classes. 

Tito felt sure that party purposes were to be furthered amidst the fragrant steam of stewed fish infused with saffron and crushed coriander. Bernardo Rucellai was one of the elect Twenty holding the reins of Florence, and this assurance put Tito in the best of spirits as he navigated the Via della Scala. Without the promise of political gain, Tito would have dreaded the gathering; he possessed no ear for the nuanced acoustic ecology of true discourse, and past suppers in the Rucellai gardens had often devolved into abstract, philosophical drones where men sought to dominate the sonic landscape by explaining the entire scheme of the universe, utterly neglecting the profound, immediate realities of the roasted quails and spiced figs set before them. 

It was a dark evening. The ambient hum of the Florentine streets—the harsh scraping of iron-shod wheels, the aggressive shouts of peddlers—was momentarily punctuated by the soft, sputtering hiss of an oil lamp suspended before an image of the Virgin. As Tito passed through this brief pool of light, the tall, graceful lines of his mantle were starkly illuminated. But the sonic footprint he left behind was closely tracked. Any meticulous observer attuned to the street’s acoustic rhythms would have noticed a second, heavier footfall syncing perfectly with Tito’s own. A thickset, elderly figure in a coarse serge tunic and felt hat trailed him like a shadow. 

When Tito stopped at the gate of the Rucellai gardens, the figure behind stopped too, the crunch of gravel instantly silenced. The *sportello*, the smaller door of the gate, was held open by a servant whose attention had been entirely fragmented by the chaotic noise of arriving carriages. Tito turned in rapidly, offering his name to the servant and passing between evergreen bushes that shone like polished bronze in the torchlight, oblivious to the scent of crushed laurel beneath his boots. 

The follower turned in too. 

“Your name?” the servant demanded, his voice a sharp, intrusive dissonance in the garden’s curated quiet. 

“Baldassarre Calvo,” came the immediate, resonant answer, vibrating with the deep, unyielding timbre of a man deeply rooted in the earth. 

“You are not a guest; the guests have all passed.” 

“I belong to Tito Melema, who has just gone in. I am to wait in the gardens.” 

The servant hesitated, trapped between the dominant lexicon of his master's orders and the imposing physical presence before him. “I had orders to admit only guests. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?” 

“No, friend. I am not a servant; I am a scholar.” 

There is a subtle power dynamic embedded in language, a native dialect of authority that cannot be counterfeited. When spoken with the quiet, reverberating confidence of a seasoned elder, words bypass the intellect and strike the bone. The porter gave way at once. Baldassarre entered, listening to the heavy iron chain clank shut behind him—a sound that sealed him within the shining bushes. 

Those ready, unshakeable answers marked a profound resurrection in Baldassarre since his last encounter with Tito, when the dagger had snapped in two. The transformation had catalyzed in the immediate, devastating aftermath of that failure. 

At the moment Tito’s shadow had passed the hovel and vanished, Baldassarre had been left sitting in the after-tremor of immense, thwarted passion. It was a physical state where bodily powerlessness was paradoxically paired with an extreme acoustic and mental lucidity. The fog of his mind, thick with the tragic erosion of his own cultural memory, had suddenly blown away. His hands shook; his blood ran cold; yet, in that bodily helplessness, he was no longer adrift in a sea of vanishing shadows. He was plunged back into the sensory reality of his past, living anew the long, slow-simmering preparation for this bitterest of tastes. 

When the startling clarity finally settled on the immediate present, he remembered Monna Lisa and Tessa. He recognized the terrible, untranslatable truth of Tito's dual lives. It was time to pick up the fragmented steel and disappear. He leaned down, his fingers brushing the cold metal, and his eyes fell upon the open book resting beside him. 

It was a magnificent manuscript, an odd volume of Pausanias. Moonlight washed over the heavy parchment, illuminating the large Greek letters at the head of the page: ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′. 

Hours earlier, he had stared at that page in the agonizing grip of language death. The letters had been nothing but black weather-marks, meaningless scratches of a lost dialect stripped of its syntax and soul. But now, in the quiet night, the lexicon breathed. The characters were once again magical signs, a vibrant native tongue conjuring an entire world. The moonbeam on the ink revived Messenia, its agrarian struggles, its defiance against Spartan oppression. He snatched the book up. The light was too pale for his old eyes to read the physical text, but it did not matter; the cultural memory was restored within him. 

He read inwardly, tasting the history. He saw the stoning of the traitor Aristocrates. He heard the visceral, percussive thud of rocks cast by a betrayed people, the rhythmic chants of a society excising its poison, leaving the corpse unburied under the wide sky. They had raised a pillar with verses declaring how Time, that ultimate, slow-grinding mortar, had brought justice to the unjust. 

The ancient words resonated through Baldassarre’s chest, striking innumerable vibrations of memory. The untranslatable subjective experience of his own betrayal mapped perfectly onto the ancient text. He forgot the frailty of his age; he could have shouted, his voice joining the acoustic ecology of the wronged across centuries. The light of meaning had returned! 

In that fierce exultation, his limbs drew strength from the cold earth. He rose, clutching his broken dagger and his history, and walked out into the broad, frosty moonlight. The nipping air carried the sharp, herbaceous scent of winter, but Baldassarre felt no chill—only the consuming, metabolic heat of conscious power. He walked the high ground, pausing to look down on the towered city. Florence slept beneath its mountain guardians, the river a pale, silent ribbon in the valley. He felt himself master of the landscape. His mind, rescued from the void of linguistic decay, grasped the city’s affairs with flashing intuition. He was a man who knew the deep, subterranean currents of human nature, who savored the keen delight of holding the world in the firm embrace of language. 

Names, images, conjugations, and histories rushed through him like a sudden harvest. Yet presiding over this mental bounty was a singular, dark deity—an unyielding hunger for justice. Once the initial intoxication of recovered memory subsided, his thoughts narrowed to the precise recipe of his revenge. That slippery viper, Tito, could not escape him now. Tito, who consumed the world without tasting it, who possessed no tender fibers of empathy, still had sensitive, selfish nerves that could be pierced. The soul that recognized no moral dialect would be forced to fluently speak the universal language of Pain. 

Baldassarre understood that his previous failure with the dagger had been the essential, bitter starter needed to ferment his current resolve. Sudden rage had broken the obstruction in his mind, just as shock had briefly revived him twice before—once against a snarling dog, once against the crashing rhythm of drowning waves. But a terrifying question remained: what if this mental lexicon faded again before the morrow? What if the linguistic fog returned, erasing his identity once more? 

Fearing that this excited vigil might exhaust his fragile reserves, he sought shelter. Guided by the earthy, comforting scent of decay and rebirth, he found a heap of warm garden straw. Nestling into its coarse, agrarian embrace, he fell asleep. 

When he opened his eyes, the sensory landscape of morning surrounded him. The sharp scent of frost on damp soil filled his lungs. For a moment, he suffered a dizzying duality: was he the dim-sighted ruin whose history had been stripped away, or the awakened elder of recovered power? The answer came as the events of the night bloomed in his mind. The page in Pausanias, the intricate syntax of his memories, the glorious, expansive prospect of the mountain waking—it was all there. 

He took up the book and remembered without reading. Names and deeds linked seamlessly. He understood that while the world often provided sanctuaries for swift-footed miscreants, and baseness wore thick armor, such superficial defenses could never withstand the slow, corrosive hatred they themselves engineered. Baldassarre felt the indestructible force of his own emotion. It required no external validation; it was an ever-burning hearth fire, consuming all other desires. 

From that frosty dawn until the evening he entered the Rucellai gardens, Baldassarre had meticulously gathered the ingredients of his vengeance. He observed Tito’s movements with the patience of a seasoned forager, charting his habits through the acoustic and social landscape of Florence. He sought the perfect moment—a time when the arrogant youth sat at the summit of confident ease, surrounded by the powerful men who provided his superficial sustenance. Baldassarre did not seek mere physical harm; he sought to serve Tito the sharpest, most astringent edge of disgrace, to send a sudden, resonant shock of dread straight through his marrow. 

To achieve this, Baldassarre lived with monastic simplicity. He slept hard and ate stintedly, finding profound physical and spiritual nourishment in coarse bread and the bitter bite of wild chicory. He had spent his last coins on a new poniard. His hunger and thirst were reserved entirely for an exquisite vengeance. He deliberately avoided anyone who might recognize his sonic footprint or physical form, steering clear of Nello’s noisy shop and bypassing Piero di Cosimo, lest a premature alarm cause Tito to flee. 

The threat of his memory slipping away again added a desperate heat to his quiet pursuit. Twice he had felt that terrifying mental dizziness, a fading of his internal lexicon where the distinct forms of the world threatened to blur back into untranslatable static. He had once attempted to infiltrate the Palazzo Vecchio to reach Tito, but the discordant noise of the council-chamber had thwarted him. 

But now, amidst the gleaming evergreen bushes of the Rucellai gardens, inhaling the scent of roasted meats and resinous wine that wafted from the grandee's table, Baldassarre felt the steady, thrumming certainty of the earth beneath his feet. His occasion had come.
