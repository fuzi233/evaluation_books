It was Nais who summoned me from my vigil to the harsher duties of war. The clamor of the assault reached the chamber where I tended her, and she demanded its meaning. With the brevity required by her frailty, I recounted the history of her nine-year slumber.

Her cheeks flushed with a sudden, vibrant color. "My lord is the properest man in all the world to be King," she whispered.

"I refused to engage in trade until they delivered the Queen I desired—safe and alive, upon this Mountain."

"How we poor women are made the chattels of men!" she lamented, though her tone softened. "But I seem to like this traffic well enough. You should not have let me obstruct Atlantis' good, Deucalion. Still, it is sweet to know you were weak for once, and that I caused it." She gestured vaguely. "What is that bath over there? Ah! My wits are foggy."

"You have begun the healing. Your strength will return swiftly, but it cannot be hurried. You must have patience."

"Sir, for a moment only, then I shall rest in peace. Are my looks gone? You have no mirror. I dreamed I would wake wrinkled and old."

"You are as you were, dear, the first night I saw you—the most beautiful woman in all the world."

"I am pleased you like me," she said, accepting the broth I offered. "My hair has grown, but it needs combing. Once I fancied you preferred ruddy locks to plain brown." Closing her eyes, she sank back among the cushions I had arranged, drifting into a healthy sleep with smiles playing on her lips. I covered her with the blanket and kissed her lightly, holding back my beard lest it brush her cheek, before stepping out into the corridor.

That beard had become a nuisance in recent hours. I entered a spare room, found a razor, and shaved it down to my bare chin. I also donned a clean robe, discarding my squalid rags. If a man is truly a king, he owes these things to the dignity of his office.

But if the din of the fighting was any guide, my kingdom was narrowing. Hour by hour, the battle grew fiercer and nearer. It was evident that some of the gates in the passage up the cliff's cleft—impregnable to all men—had yielded to the vehemence of Phorenice's attack. It was scarcely surprising. With her genius spurred to fury by the destruction of her fair city, the Empress would be no light adversary, and the Sacred Mountain was no longer invincible.

Stone defenses, cunningly planned and mightily built, possessed strength, but they cannot fight alone. They require men to man them, and abundance of them. In any such storm, desperate men spit at death, slingers miss their aim, archers falter, and newfangled machines like Phorenice's fire-tubes ensure that one in a thousand finds a vital spot. Thus, though the attackers lose hundreds, the defenders are not without their dead.

The slaughter proved prodigious. As fast as the stormers ascended, the Priests holding the lowest remaining gate rained great rocks upon them until the narrow stair was paved with writhing corpses. Yet Phorenice stood on a spur below, urging on the charges with insane valor. Company after company marched up to hurl themselves hopelessly against the defenses. They lacked machines to batter the massive gates; their attack was as pathetically useless as a child hammering a wall with an orange, while the terrible stones from above mowed them down remorselessly.

Company after company marched into this death-trap, and not a single man returned. Nor was it Phorenice's policy that they should. In her lust for final conquest, she intended to pour out troops until she filled the passes with the slain, creating a bridge of bodies over which she might march to a level fight. It was never her mood to count the cost. She set the objective, and it was her policy that the people of Atlantis were there to achieve it for her.

Two gates she had carried in this dreadful fashion, slaughtering the Priests behind them. Then I descended from above to take my share in the fight. There was no trumpet to announce my arrival, no herald to proclaim my quality, but the Priests, by sheer custom, picked up "Deucalion!" as a battle-cry. Some shouted that with a King to lead, no further ground would be lost.

The name carried weight. A company of doomed wretches hurrying up stopped in their charge. The word "Deucalion!" was bandied round and handed back down the line. I felt grim satisfaction; evidence that I was not completely forgotten in the land.

Shouts came from behind to urge them on, but they did not budge. Presently, a glittering officer panted up and began striking right and left among them with his sword. From the high rampart above the gate, I recognized him at once.

"It matters not what they use for their battle-cry," he shouted. "You have the orders of your divine Empress, and that is enough. You should be proud to die for her wish, you cowards. And if you do not obey, you will die afterwards under the instruments of the tormentors, very painfully. As for Deucalion, he is dead any time these nine years."

"There it seems you lie, my Lord Tatho," I shouted down.

He started, looking up.

"So you are there in real truth? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. But it is too late for composition. You are on the side of these mangy Priests, and the Empress has made an edict that they are to be rooted out. I am her most obedient servant."

"You used to be skilful of fence," I said. "If you please, stop this pitiful killing. Make yourself the champion of your side, and I will stand for mine. We will fight this quarrel in some fair place and bind our parties to abide by the result."

"It would be a grand fight between us, old friend, and it goes hard with me to balk you. But I cannot pleasure you. I am general here under Phorenice, and she has given me the strongest orders not to peril myself. Besides, though you are a great man, Deucalion, you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three."

"I am King."

Tatho laughed. "Few but yourself would say so, my lord."

"Few truly, but what there are, they are powerful. I was given the name for the first time yesterday, and as a first blow in the campaign, some mischief was done in the city. I was there myself and saw how you took it."

"You were in Atlantis!"

"I went for Nais. She is on the mountain now, and tomorrow will be my Queen. Tatho, as a priest to a priest, let me solemnly remind you of the infinite power you bite against on this Sacred Mountain. Your teaching warns you of the weapons stored in the Ark of the Mysteries. If you persist in this attack, at best you will merely lose; at worst, you will bring about a wreck over which even the High Gods will shudder."

"You cannot scare us back by words," Tatho said doggedly. "And as for magic, it will be met by magic. Phorenice has found by her own cleverness as many powers as were ever stored in the Ark."

"Yet she looked on helplessly last night when her royal pyramid was trundled into a rubbish heap. Zaemon prophesied this, and for a witness, I stood closer to her than we two stand now, and saw her."

"I will own you took her by surprise somewhat there. I do not understand these matters; I was never more than one of the Seven in the old days, and now Phorenice keeps the knowledge of her magic to herself. But it seems time is needed when one magic is to be met by another."

"Well," I said, "I know little of the business either. I leave these matters to those higher in the priesthood. Indeed, having a liking for Nais, it seems I am debarred from understanding the highest Mysteries. So I content myself with being a soldier. When the appointed day comes, I shall fall and kiss my mother the Earth for the last time. You, so I am told, have ambition for longer life."

He nodded. "Phorenice has found the Great Secret, and I am to be the first to share it with her. We shall be as Gods upon the earth, seeing that Death will be powerless to touch us. And the twin sons she has borne me will be made immortal also."

"Phorenice is headstrong. No need to shake your head and deny it. I have had some acquaintance with her. But the order has been made, and her immortality will be snatched from her rudely. Mark my words solemnly: I, Deucalion, have been appointed King of Atlantis by the High Council of the Priests, the mouthpiece of the Most High Gods. If I do not have my reign, then there will be no Atlantis left to carry either King or Empress. You know me, Tatho, for a man that never lies."

He nodded.

"Then save yourself before it is too late. You shall have your vice-royalty in Yucatan again."

"But, man, there is no Yucatan. A great horde of little hairy creatures, something less than human and something more than beasts, swept down upon our cities and ate them out. You may sneer! Others sneered when I came home, till the Empress stopped them. But you know what a train of driver ants is? You may light fires across their path, and they will march into them in blind bravery, putting them out with their bodies, and those that remain will march on in an unbroken column, devouring all that stands in their path. Those little hairy creatures were like the ants—aye, for numbers, and wooden bravery, as well as for appetite. As a result, there is no Yucatan."

"You shall have Egypt, then."

He burst at me hotly. "I would not take seven Egypts and ten Yucatans. My lord, you think more poorly of me than is kind, when you ask me to become a traitor. In your place, would you throw your Nais away to save yourself from danger?"

"That is different."

"In no degree. You have a kindness for her. I have all that and more for Phorenice, who is, besides, my wife and the mother of my children. If I have qualms—and I freely confess I know you are desperate men up there, with dreadful powers at your command—my shiverings are for them, not for myself. But I think this parley leads to nothing. Though these common soldiers here understand little of our talk, they may pick up a word here and there. I do not wish them to go on to their death, as you will see them do shortly, and carry evil reports about me to whatever Gods they chance to come before."

He saluted me with his sword and drew back. Once more the missiles began to fly, and the doomed wretches who had halted beside the steep rock walls pressed hopelessly forward. They had scaling-ladders, but no chance to plant them. They could do naught but fill the narrow way with their bodies, and to that end they had been sent, and to that end they humbly died. Our Priests, with crow and lever, wrenched great rocks from their lodgings and sent them crashing down, so that screams filled the pass and the horrid butchery was renewed.

Yet ever and again, an arrow, a sling-stone, or a fire-tube's dart found its way up from below through the defenses, and there we were with a man the less to carry on the fight. It was well enough for Phorenice to be lavish with her troops; if she wished success, there were no two ways for it. When those she levied were killed, she could readily press others into service, seeing she had the whole broad face of the country under her rule. But with us, it was different. A man down on our side was a man whose arm would bitterly be missed, one which could in no possible way be replaced.

I calculated the chances and saw clearly that if we continued the fight on the present plan, they would storm the gates one after another. By the time the uppermost gate was reached, there would be no Priest alive to defend it. Not disdaining to fashion myself on Phorenice's newer plan—that a general should sometimes plot coldly from a place of safety rather than lead the thick of the fighting—I left those standing at the gate with rough words of cheer and withdrew again up the narrow stair of the pass.

This one approach to the Sacred Mountain was vastly more difficult and dangerous in the olden days when it stood as a mere bare cleft. But a chasm had been bridged here, a shelf cut through the solid rock, and in many places the roadway was built up on piers from distant crags below to make it uniform and easy. It came to my mind that if I could destroy this path, we might gain a breathing space.

The idea seemed good, or at least no other occurred to me which would relieve our desperate situation. I looked around for means to execute it. Up and down, from the mountain to the plains below, I had traversed that narrow stair some thousands of times, knowing every stone, every turn, and every cut by heart. But I had never looked upon it with an eye to shaving off all roadway to the Sacred Mountain. So now, even in this moment of dreadful stress, I had to traverse it no less than three times afresh before deciding upon the best site for demolition.

Once the point was fixed, there was little delay. I had already sent men to the storehouses among the Priests' dwellings to fetch rams, crows, acids, hammers, and other materials needed, which stood handy behind one of the upper gates.
