The rough roads of travel hold a peculiar charm for the English soul, even for those who wander not as pilgrims of love. There is a fascination in the records of such journeys, much like the interest one finds in a fossil—antique friendship preserved in stone. Yet, since the true heart of our story beats in Holland, it is time to return there, to the ordinary lives and common incidents that have shaped human history in every age.

Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter's house to claim Margaret's promise, but he found her confined to her bed by illness. Peter, upon hearing his purpose, treated him with open affront, warning him to leave the premises. Those standing nearby were ready to dunk him in the water, for both father and daughter were beloved figures, and the entire town of Sevenbergen was in a state of hot, undiscriminating agitation, fueled by popular sympathy.

Humiliated and angry, Jorian departed in dudgeon, repenting his earlier kindness. Such penitence is not uncommon and often carries the sincerity of genuine remorse. Elsewhere, Dierich Brower, caught at "The Three Kings" attempting to buy information about Martin Wittenhaagen through drunkenness, was seized and thrown into a horsepond, threatened with further punishment if he ever returned to the burgh. Municipal jealousy soon flared; the burgomaster of Sevenbergen sent a formal letter to his counterpart in Tergou, reminding him that he had overstepped the law and inviting him to handle any future complaints against Sevenbergen's citizens.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, suppressing his rage at this rebuke, replied with feigned civility. He claimed the man he pursued was a Tergovian named Gerard who had stolen the town records. Since Gerard had fled to foreign lands, likely taking the documents with him, the matter was supposedly closed. Thus, Ghysbrecht made a virtue of necessity, though his calm was merely a veil. Baffled by the failure in Sevenbergen, he redirected his emissaries to the Tergou family to discover where Gerard had fled. To his infinite surprise, they knew nothing. This uncertainty gnawed at him; he feared Gerard was lurking nearby, waiting to make a discovery and exact a terrible revenge. From this moment, Dierich and others noted a darkening in Ghysbrecht's demeanor. He became moody and irritable, haunted by a dread that caused his eyes to cast furtive glances, as if expecting a blow from an unknown quarter. The misery he inflicted on others had failed to bring him happiness, for rarely does cruelty satisfy the soul.

The small family at Tergou, which might have resolved its differences without exiling Gerard to a distant land, retained its outward appearance but lost its inner warmth. Little Kate knew the role Cornelis and Sybrandt played in banishing Gerard. Though she never spoke of it to her mother to avoid further strife, she shuddered at their sight and blushed at their hypocritical regrets. Catherine, observing with a woman's vigilance and subtlety, said nothing but watched closely. The brothers, trying to share the family's gloom, succeeded in deceiving their father and Giles, yet the demure satisfaction beneath their actions could not escape the feminine eyes of the household.

Mistrust and suspicion now sat at the table, poor substitutes for Gerard's intelligent face, which had once brightened the entire circle. The old hosier, wounded in his pride by his son's disobedience, bore himself stiffly, refusing to mention Gerard's name. Yet beneath his Spartan exterior, Nature tugged at his heart-strings. He could not conceal one anxiety: "If I but knew where the boy is, and that his life and health are safe, my care would be small," he would sigh, followed by a deep breath. One imagines that if Gerard had walked in at that moment, there would have been many tears and embraces, and few, if any, reproaches.

The family was taken by surprise by the sudden publicity surrounding Gerard's departure. Within a week, his adventures were the talk of the town. Popular sympathy rallied warmly behind the lovers and against Gerard's cruel parents and the meddling burgomaster who had interfered in matters that did not concern him.

"Mother," Kate said softly, "it is known throughout the town that Margaret is suffering from a fever—a burning fever. Her father fears for her greatly."

"Margaret? What Margaret?" Catherine asked, feigning a treacherous calm.

"Oh, Mother! Whom else could I mean? Gerard's Margaret."

"Gerard's Margaret!" Catherine screamed. "How dare you utter such a word in this house? Never mention that hussy's name again. She has laid bare my life. She is the ruin of my poor boy, the flower of my flock. Because of her, he is not a holy priest among us but roams the world, leaving me a desolate, broken-hearted mother." She paused, seeing Kate's distress, and softened her tone. "Do not cry, my girl. I speak harshly because I bear up before you all; I must swallow my fears. But at night, I see him in my dreams, always in trouble. Sometimes torn by wild beasts, other times held by robbers with cruel knives raised to strike his pale face. Oh, Kate! When I remember that while I sit here in comfort, my poor boy may lie dead in some savage place because of that girl... her name is ratsbane to me. I tremble when I hear it."

"I will say nothing to grieve you further, Mother," Kate replied tenderly, though a sigh escaped her.

Outside, Margaret was widely spoken of and even pitied. The young men and maidens of Sevenbergen cast pitying glances at the little window where the village beauty lay "dying for love." In this familiar phrase, they underestimated her spirit and selflessness. Gerard was not dead, and she remained too loyal to doubt his constancy. Her father was dear and helpless, and but for her physical weakness, her love for Gerard would not have prevented her from fulfilling her duties, though she performed them with a drooping head and heavy heart. However, the strain of physical and mental excitement had triggered a violent fever. Only her youth and constitution saved her. Though the malady eventually left her, it did so leaving her in a terrible state of bodily weakness, where life felt like a burden.

It is in such moments that love and friendship become mortal angels, bringing comfort with their voices and healing with their hands. But this poor girl had to find a way back to life and vigor on her own. Many days she lay alone, watching heavy hours roll over her like leaden waves. In her enfeebled state, existence seemed a weight, and life a thing already gone by. She lacked the strength to strive for recovery, for Gerard was gone, and she had no one left to recover for. Often she lay motionless for hours, tears welling gently from her eyes.

One day, waking from an uneasy sleep, she found two women in her room. One was a servant; the other, distinguished by the deep fur on her collar and sleeves, was a woman of consideration. A narrow band of silvery hair spared by her coiffure revealed she was past the age when sensible women conceal their years. Both women looked kind and friendly. Margaret tried to raise herself in bed, but the old lady gently placed a hand to stop her.

"Lie still, sweetheart. We come not to put you about, but to comfort you, God willing. Cheer up a bit, and tell us, who do you think we are?"

"Nay, madam, I know you, though I have never seen you before. You are the demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard has often spoken of you and your goodness to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now," she said, sinking back as tears welled instantly.

The good-natured Reicht Heynes began to cry for company, but her mistress chided her gently. "Well, you are a pretty one for a sick-room," she said, employing innocent art to cheer the patient, with some success. An old woman who has seen life and all its troubles is a sovereign blessing beside a sorrowful young woman. She knows what to say and what to avoid, how to soothe and interest. Within an hour, Margaret's head rested on the old lady's shoulder instead of the pillow, her soft eyes dwelling there with gentle gratitude.

"Ahh! This is hair," the old lady said, running her fingers through it. "Come and look at it, Reicht!"

Reicht approached and handled it, praising it sincerely. The poor girl who owned it was not entirely immune to flattery, perhaps because she was not yet dead.

"In sooth, madam, I used to think it hideous; but he praised it, and ever since then I have been almost vain of it, saints forgive me. You know how foolish those are that love."

"They are greater fools that don't," the old lady replied sharply.

Margaret opened her lovely eyes to seek her meaning. This was only the beginning of many visits. Either Margaret Van Eyck or Reicht came nearly every day until the patient was convalescent, and she improved rapidly under their care. Reicht attributed this largely to the nourishing dishes she prepared in Peter's kitchen, but Margaret valued more the kind words and eyes that constantly reminded her she had friends to live for.

Meanwhile, Martin Wittenhaagen went straight to Rotterdam to take the bull by the horns. The bull was a biped with a crown for horns: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. Upon arriving in Rotterdam, Martin learned the court was at Ghent. He went there seeking an audience but was baffled by lackeys and pages. Undeterred, he threw himself in the sovereign's path while hunting and, contrary to all court precedents, began the conversation by roaring lustily for mercy.

"Why, where is the peril, man?" the Duke asked, looking around and laughing.

"Grace for an old soldier hunted down by burghers!" Martin cried.

Kings differ in character like other people, yet they share one trait: a mighty inclination to be affable toward men of very low estate. These subjects do not vie with them in anything, so jealousy cannot creep in; they amuse themselves with the bluntness and novelty of such men, refreshing them with a touch of nature—a rarity in courts. Philip the Good reined in his horse and granted Martin almost a private conversation. Martin reminded him of a certain battlefield where he had received an arrow intended for his sovereign. The Duke remembered the incident perfectly and was graciously pleased to view it cheerfully. He could afford to laugh, for he had not been the one hit.

Martin then recounted his Majesty's capture in the church, his imprisonment in the tower, the maneuver that freed him, and all the details of the hunt. Whether he told it better than I have, or the Duke had not heard so many good stories, it is certain that the sovereign became so engrossed that when courtiers galloped up to interrupt, he swore like a costermonger and threatened, half in jest, to cut off the next head that came between him and a good story. When Martin finished, the Duke cried out:

"St. Luke! What sport goes on in this mine earldom, ay! in my own woods, and I see it not. You base fellows have all the luck." He was indignant at the partiality of Fortune. "Lo you now! This was a man-hunt," he said. "I never had the luck to be at a man-hunt."

"My luck was none so great," Martin replied bluntly. "I was on the wrong side of the dogs' noses."

"Ah! So you were; I forgot that." Royalty was more reconciled to its lot. "What would you then?"

"A free pardon, your highness, for myself and Gerard."

"For what?"

"For prison-breaking."

"Go to; the bird will fly from the cage. 'Tis instinct. Besides, why coop a young man up for loving a young woman? These burgomasters must be void of common sense. What else?"

"For striking down the burgomaster."

"Oh, the hunted boar will turn to bay. 'Tis his right; and I hold him less than man that grudges it him. What else?"

"For killing of the bloodhounds."

The Duke's countenance fell.

"'Twas their life or mine," Martin said eagerly.

"Ay! But I can't have my bloodhounds, my beautiful bloodhounds, sacrificed to—

"No, no, no! They were not your dogs."

"Whose dogs, then?"

"The ranger's."

"Oh. Well, I am very sorry for him, but as I was saying, I can't have my old soldiers sacrificed to his bloodhounds. Thou shalt have thy free pardon."

"And poor Gerard."

"And poor Gerard too, for thy sake. And more, tell thou this burgomaster his doings mislike me: this is to set up for a king, not a burgomaster. I'll have no kings in Holland but one. Bid him be more humble; or by St. Jude I'll hang him before his own door, as I hanged the burgomaster of some town in Flanders, no, 'twas somewhere in Brabant—no matter—I hanged him, I remember that much—for oppressing poor folk."

The Duke then beckoned his chancellor, a pursy old fellow who rode like a sack, and ordered him to write out a free pardon for Martin and one Gerard.

This precious document was drawn up in form and signed the next day. Martin hastened home with it.

Margaret had left her bed a few days prior and was sitting pale and pensive by the fireside when he burst in, waving the parchment and crying, "A free pardon, girl, for Gerard as well as me! Send for him back when you will; all the burgomasters on earth daren't lay a finger on him."
