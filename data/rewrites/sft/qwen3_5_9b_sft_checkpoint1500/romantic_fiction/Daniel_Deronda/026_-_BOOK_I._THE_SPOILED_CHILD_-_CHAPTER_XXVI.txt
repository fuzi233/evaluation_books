"I bring white asses laden with the freight of Tyrian vessels—purple, gold, and balm—to bribe your will!" Daniel Deronda recited the lines aloud, his voice echoing with a theatrical intensity that belied the quiet room around him. "I'll bid them chase him forth, nor let him breathe the taint of his surmise on my secure resolve. Ay, 'tis secure! And therefore let him come to spread his freight. For firmness hath its appetite and craves the stronger lure, more strongly to resist. Would know the touch of gold to fling it off; scent wine to feel its lip the soberer; behold soft byssus, ivory, and plumes to say, 'They're fair, but I will none of them,' and flout Enticement in the very face."

His recitation was a performance of a role he had recently assumed, a mask of ironclad resolve worn to hide the churning turmoil within. Yet, as the words faded, the reality of the situation intruded upon the stage of his mind. The "secure resolve" he had just championed was already cracking under the weight of impending choices.

In the parlor below, the air was heavy with a mixture of dust and suppressed emotion. Gwendolen sat alone, the afternoon light casting long, gray shadows across the floorboards. The news from Mr. Gascoigne had settled over her like a wet woolen blanket; it was a suffocating warmth that offered no comfort. Her aunt, Mrs. Mompert, had finally scheduled an interview for the following week, a formal step that signaled the end of her sheltered existence at Offendene. The prospect of moving to Wanchester, of becoming a governess to the Mompert children, was a catastrophe disguised as duty.

Gwendolen stared at her hands, which lay limp in her lap. She had tried to convince herself, as her uncle had urged, that this was a trial of character, a chance to grow strong. "You will find an interest in varied experience," he had said, his voice smooth and reassuring. "And I have no doubt you will be a more valuable woman for having sustained such a part." But the truth gnawed at her gut. She could not pretend to believe she would like it. The idea of serving others, of being subservient to women who held her in contempt, filled her with a cold, creeping horror.

"You cannot pretend to believe that I shall like it," she had muttered when he last visited, her voice trembling with a petulance she usually kept hidden. "But I am quite aware that I am obliged to bear it."

Now, alone, the bitter tears began to rise, hot and stinging, blurring the edges of the room. Her heart rebelled against the notion that youth made suffering easier. *When,* she wondered, staring blankly at the wallpaper, *am I to have any happiness, if it does not come while I am young?* The world, once a playground of endless possibility, now seemed a barren landscape enclosed by a sterile distance. She felt a profound forlornness, a sense that she was not made for easy pre-eminence. The "spoiled child," as she sometimes felt herself, was being stripped bare, her illusions shattered by the harsh light of necessity.

She rose from the low ottoman, pacing the drawing-room restlessly. Her movements were jerky, driven by an inner restlessness. She leaned forward, resting her cheek on her palm, a single tear tracing a path down her cheek. *I have always felt that Mamma was not a happy woman,* she thought, the image of her mother surfacing unbidden. *And now I dare say I shall be more unhappy than she has been.*

The thought of her mother aged her instantly. Gwendolen imagined her mother growing old and white, herself fading into the background, two weary faces meeting in a future of shared misery. It was a vision so terrifying that it broke her composure. She sobbed, not in anger, but with a tender, crushing grief, burying her face in her hands.

The front door opened, and the sound of footsteps echoed through the hall. Gwendolen barely registered her mother entering before the woman's presence brought her crashing back to reality. Her mother, Mrs. Davilow, stood in the doorway, her face pale and tremulous, holding something wrapped in paper.

"It was nothing, Mamma," Gwendolen gasped, pressing her handkerchief hastily to her eyes, trying to regain some semblance of control. "It is all over now."

Mrs. Davilow stepped closer, withdrawing her arms gently. Gwendolen saw then that her mother held a letter. The sight sent a fresh jolt of panic through her. "What is that letter?" she asked, her voice thin with fear. "Worse news still?"

"I don't know what you will think it, dear," Mrs. Davilow said softly, clutching the envelope as if it were a live coal. "You will hardly guess where it comes from."

"Don't ask me to guess anything," Gwendolen snapped, the frustration rising in her throat like bile. As if a bruise were being pressed, she felt the need to defend her fragile emotional state.

"It is addressed to you, dear."

Gwendolen gave a slight, imperceptible toss of her head. "It comes from Diplow," Mrs. Davilow whispered, handing over the letter.

Gwendolen recognized the indistinct, sharp handwriting immediately. Her mother watched her closely, seeing the blood drain from Gwendolen's face as she read. The colors fled from her lips, leaving them pale and drawn. The words were few, formal, and chilling:

*Mr. Grandcourt presents his compliments to Miss Harleth, and begs to know whether he may be permitted to call at Offendene tomorrow after two and to see her alone. Mr. Grandcourt has just returned from Leubronn, where he had hoped to find Miss Harleth.*

Mrs. Davilow read the note, then looked at her daughter with wide, inquiring eyes. Gwendolen let the letter fall to the floor, turning away as if struck. The silence that followed was deafening.

"It must be answered, darling," Mrs. Davilow said, her voice trembling with timidity. "The man waits."

Gwendolen sank onto the settee, clasping her hands together, staring straight ahead. She looked like someone who had been startled by a gunshot and was frozen, listening to the echoes determine if more were to come. The situation had shifted violently. Moments ago, she had been resigned to a life of repulsive monotony, a path laid out for her by fate and circumstance. Now, a moment of choice had arrived.

Yet, triumph or terror? It was impossible to tell. There was a thrill in the tribute to her power, a fleeting sense that she was regaining an empire over her own life. But how to use it? The terror was immediate and visceral. Flashbacks hit her like a series of images in a book beaten open in haste: the allurements, the vacillations, the resolve to accede, the final repulsion. She saw the incisive face of the dark-eyed lady with the lovely boy, the scene of disclosure that had broken her faith in men and things. That unalterable experience made her shrink from the vision of what was coming.

Where was the good of this choice? What did she wish? Anything different? No! And yet, in the dark seed-growths of her consciousness, a new wish was forming—one so desperate it terrified her. *I wish I had never known it!* Something, anything, that would save her from the dread of letting Grandcourt come.

Minutes stretched into an eternity for Mrs. Davilow, who waited anxiously. Finally, she ventured gently, "It will be necessary for you to write, dear. Or shall I write an answer for you—which you will dictate?"

"No, Mamma," Gwendolen said, drawing a deep, shuddering breath. "But please lay me out the pen and paper."

Time. That was what she needed. Time to delay, to hesitate, to avoid the absolute decision. Was she to decline Grandcourt's visit? Close the shutters forever? Though the assurance remained that she would remain exactly where she was, trapped in her servitude. A strange, warm current of activity surged within her, stirring toward something that would be an event—an opportunity where she could look and speak with former effectiveness. The interest of the morrow was no longer at a deadlock.

"There is really no reason on earth why you should be so alarmed at the man's waiting a few minutes, Mamma," Gwendolen said, her voice dropping to a remonstrant whisper. "Servants expect nothing else than to wait. It is not to be supposed that I must write on the instant."

"No, dear," Mrs. Davilow said, her tone corrected, turning to sit down and pick up a piece of needlework that lay nearby. "He can wait another quarter of an hour, if you like."

It was simple speech, yet it felt subtly calculated. Gwendolen felt a contradictory desire to be hastened; hurry would save her from deliberate choice, from facing the abyss of what she truly wanted.

"I did not mean him to wait long enough for that needlework to be finished," she said, lifting her hands to stroke the backward curves of her hair. She rose from her seat and stood still, the room spinning slightly.

"But if you don't feel able to decide?" Mrs. Davilow asked sympathetically.
