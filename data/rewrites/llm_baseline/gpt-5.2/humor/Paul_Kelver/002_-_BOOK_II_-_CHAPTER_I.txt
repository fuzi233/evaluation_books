Fate, having a taste for practical jokes, arranged that I should be born in May. June, everybody knows, is the month in which babies ought to make their debut if they have any regard for comfort and reputation; in June the world has a kind word ready, the air smells of cut hay, and the very sun seems to say, “Come in, my boy; we’ve been expecting you.” But May—May is the month that looks you over, sniffs, and says, “Oh. You.”

Mrs. Fursey—my nurse and the chief spokeswoman of Providence in our household—always insisted my arrival was premature because I had been impatient to begin. She was a thin, knobbly woman, full of angles and convictions, with a thimble that clicked like a beetle’s jaw and a mind that kept superstitions as other people keep jam: tightly corked, carefully hoarded, and brought out on special occasions to spread upon everything.

The special occasion for my coming, as she explained it, was that news had reached the house two hours earlier of the ruin of my father’s mines by inundation. My poor mother, in attempting to reach the bell during the ensuing agitation, fell against a cheval-glass and broke it—thus, Mrs. Fursey assured me, sealing the first seven years of my existence with disaster, because Welsh blood, like Welsh cakes, holds a great deal in it that no amount of kneading will ever get out.

“And I must confess,” she added, sighing, “it do look as though there might be truth in the saying, after all.”

This was a new tone. Hitherto her system had been to persuade me I was an exceptionally lucky child. For example: I could retire to bed at six o’clock—while less favoured boys were allowed to remain up until eight or nine, growing old and haggard on the carpet. Also, at the first sniffle of a cold, it became my privilege to lunch on linseed gruel and sup on brimstone and treacle—the treacle being, as I discovered, a mere decoy sent forward to draw the innocent into range, while the brimstone did the actual fighting. Other boys, she said, were left alone when they were ill. Poor, neglected creatures—how I envied them in my wilder moments.

So at her sudden hint that luck might not be my natural atmosphere, I stared at her in honest alarm.

“Then ain’t I a lucky little boy?” I asked.

The good woman saw she had slipped a stitch.

“Oh, you—you’re lucky enough,” she said hurriedly. “I was thinking of your poor mother.”

“Isn’t mamma lucky?”

“Well, she hasn’t been too lucky since you came.”

“Wasn’t it lucky, her having me?”

“I can’t say it was, at that particular time.”

“Didn’t she want me?”

Mrs. Fursey belonged to the school of thought that holds childhood should enter a room apologising, remain apologising, and leave apologising.

“Well,” she said, “I daresay she could have done without you.”

I remember the scene as clearly as if it were still sizzling in the grate. I sat on a low chair by the nursery fire, one knee hugged in my locked hands, while Mrs. Fursey’s needle scratched and clicked against her thimble with the steady industry of an insect. Outside, I could hear the house itself—the minute domestic orchestra: the far clink of a pan-lid, the whisper of coals settling, a sparrow’s sudden quarrel in the ivy. All sound seemed to lean in, attentive.

And into my small soul knocked, for the first time, the large question.

Without moving, I asked:

“Then why did she take me in?”

The thimble stopped. Silence fell so sharply I could hear the fire give a little sigh.

“Took you in! What’s the child talking about? Who’s took you in?”

“Why, mamma. If she didn’t want me, why did she take me in?”

Even while I asked it—dignified, resentful, certain I had cornered all grown-up logic—I felt an odd gratitude that she had. My imagination leapt to the terrible alternative: myself at the bedroom window, refused like a parcel without a label. I saw the stork—large, solemn, officious—holding me out by the neck as Tom Pinfold held out a fish by the tail, only to have Anna sniff once and shut the kitchen door in his face. Would the stork, too, have gone away scratching his head with one of his long compass-legs, muttering, “Well, I never,” and carrying me elsewhere?

And how had he carried me? In the garden I had watched a blackbird with a worm, and the worm, though perhaps safe enough, always looked most unhappy—writhing with a sort of polite protest. Had I wriggled in like fashion? Had I made myself disagreeable? And where would the stork have taken me then? Perhaps to Mrs. Fursey’s cottage; it stood nearest. But I knew Mrs. Fursey would not have taken me in; she never took anything in unless it was lint. Next lived Mr. Chumdley the cobbler, a lame man in a half-underground shop, hammering boots with hands as black as treacle before it’s been sweetened. He looked like a poor-spirited ogre. I should have hated being his little boy. Perhaps nobody would have taken me. I grew pensive at the thought of being rejected by the whole village—an unwanted morsel, like a crust nobody quite trusts.

This produced a new question, which I asked in a low voice, lest the answer should be as chilly as the thought.

“Nurse, where did I come from?”

“Why, I’ve told you often. The stork brought you.”

“Yes, I know. But where did the stork get me from?”

Mrs. Fursey paused a long time. Perhaps she feared conceit. But even she saw that a boy cannot be left in mid-air.

“Oh,” she said at last, “from Heaven.”

“But I thought Heaven was a place where you went to,” I objected, “not where you comed from.” I remember my “comed” because at that time my irregular verbs were a standing scandal to my mother. “Goned,” too, was a favourite; I had forged it myself, with the pride of an inventor.

Mrs. Fursey made a point of ignoring my grammar. She had been told not to meddle in that department; my mother held that conflicting opinions only muddled a child—an observation that proved, in practice, a good deal wider than verbs.

“You came from Heaven,” repeated Mrs. Fursey, “and you’ll go to Heaven—if you’re good.”

“Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?”

“So they say,” she answered, in a tone suggesting she would not stake her thimble on it.

“And did you come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?”

“Of course I did. Where do you think I came from?”

At once, Heaven suffered a serious loss of prestige. I had already wondered why everybody I knew was going there—so I was always assured, though I noticed no particular eagerness in the preparations. Now, linked as it was with Mrs. Fursey’s origin, Heaven seemed less a radiant country and more a sort of depot that issued nurses.

But her statement stirred up another sorrow in me, sharp as vinegar.

“Didn’t they want me in Heaven then, either?” I asked. “Weren’t they fond of me up there?”

The wretchedness in my voice must have penetrated even Mrs. Fursey’s well-padded certainty.

“Oh, they liked you well enough, I daresay,” she said, softening a little. “I like you—but I like to get rid of you sometimes.”

There was no disputing that. Even then I suspected that my famous six o’clock bedtime occasionally crept back toward half-past five with the stealth of a cat.

Her comfort did not comfort me. The facts seemed plain: God did not want me; He was glad to get rid of me. My mother did not want me; she could have done without me. Nobody wanted me. Why was I here?

And then, sudden as the opening and shutting of a door into a lit room, there came into me the sense that Something—somewhere—must have need of me, or I could not be. Something I belonged to, and that belonged to me; Something as much part of me as I of it. I could not have named it, but it warmed my chest like a swallowed spark. Years later, the son of a Portuguese Jew would give words to what I had felt. At that moment I only knew, very distinctly, that I was alive, and that I was I.

The next instant the door shut, and I was again merely a puzzled boy by the nursery fire, faced with life and Mrs. Fursey.

A recollection—half overheard, half invented—bounced into my head.

“Nurse, why haven’t we got a husband?”

Mrs. Fursey stared as if I had suggested we keep one in the coal-scuttle.

“What maggot has the child got into its head now?” she demanded. “Who hasn’t got a husband?”

“Why, mamma.”

“Don’t talk nonsense, Master Paul. You know your mamma has got a husband.”

“No, she ain’t.”

“And don’t contradict. Your mamma’s husband is your papa, who lives in London.”

“What’s the good of him!”

“You wicked child, you! Where’s your commandments? Your father is in London working hard to earn money to keep you in idleness, and you sit there and say, ‘What’s the good of him!’ I’d be ashamed to be such an ungrateful little brat.”

I had not meant ingratitude. I was only repeating words I had heard the day before between my mother and my aunt—those two, who could quarrel as naturally as kettles can boil.

My aunt had said, with her cheerful venom: “There she goes, moping again. Drat me if ever I saw such a thing to mope as a woman.”

My aunt was qualified to speak. She grumbled all day about everything, but did it with a bright face—like a person singing a dirge to a dance tune.

My mother had been standing, hands clasped behind her (a favourite pose), looking out through the high French window into the garden. It must have been spring; I remember crocuses—white and yellow—stuck in the grass like little careful flames.

“I want a husband,” my mother had answered in a voice so childishly ridiculous I had looked up from my fairy story, half expecting her to have shrunk into a little girl. “I hate not having a husband.”

“Help us and save us,” said my aunt. “How many more does a girl want? She’s got one.”

“What’s the good of him all that way off,” my mother pouted. “I want him here where I can get at him.”

I had heard of this father of mine often enough—he existed, apparently, somewhere in London, and to him we owed the blessings of bread, butter, and respectability. But my childish mind, trying to fit information to experience, had made him into something rather like a hymn: always spoken of, never seen, impressive but intangible. I agreed with my mother that such a being, however revered, was no substitute for the flesh-and-blood fathers other boys possessed—the big masculine thing that hoisted one pig-a-back round the garden or took one to sail boats.

“You don’t understand me, nurse,” I explained carefully; “what I mean is a husband you can get at.”

“Well, and you’ll ‘get at him,’ poor gentleman, one of these days,” said Mrs. Fursey. “When he’s ready for you he’ll send for you, and then you’ll go to him in London.”

I saw she still did not understand, but I suspected that spelling it out would make her drop her thimble in horror. So I asked, very plainly:

“How do you get to London; do you have to die first?”

“I do think,” said Mrs. Fursey, in the tone of resigned despair, “that, without exception, you are the silliest little boy I ever came across. I’ve no patience with you.”

“I am very sorry, nurse,” I began. “I thought—”

“Then,” she interrupted, speaking with the voice of many generations, “you shouldn’t think. London,” she continued—because her experience told her that peace is best purchased with information—“is a big town, and you go there in a train. Some time—soon now—your father will write to your mother that everything is ready. Then you and your mother and your aunt will leave this place and go to London, and I shall be rid of you.”

“And shan’t we come back here ever any more?”

“Never again.”

“Then I’ll never play in the garden again, never go down to the pebble-ridge to tea, or to Jacob’s tower?”

“Never again.” She seemed to savour the phrase; it came out like something from a prayer-book: solemn, final, and faintly enjoyable.

“And I’ll never see Anna, or Tom Pinfold, or old Yeo, or Pincher, or you, ever any more?”

“Never any more. You’ll go away and begin an entirely new life. And I do hope, Master Paul,” she added, warming to moral instruction, “it may be a better one. That you will make up your mind to—”

Whatever improvement she wished me to make up my mind to, I did not hear. The fire hissed softly as a sap-log broke; outside, the house gave its usual domestic sounds—yet suddenly they seemed like the noises of a place one is leaving for ever: the clink of crockery that would go on clinking without me, the footstep in the passage that would learn to forget my name. My whole world, with its garden and pebble-ridge and tower and people and dog, was being taken up like a cloth after tea and shaken into nothing.

One went away, leaving pleasant places, never to return. One left one’s play and one’s friends and the familiar noises of one’s own rooms, and stepped into a strange land. Life was indeed odd; and to understand it one must sit very still and stare into the fire while grown-ups talked nonsense overhead.

That night, when my mother came to kiss me good-night, I turned my face to the wall and pretended to be asleep, as children do when they are offended with the universe. But when I felt her soft curls brush my cheek, my pride melted like butter near a warm plate. I caught her neck in my arms and pulled her face down close to mine.

“I suppose you couldn’t send me back now, could you?” I whispered. “You see, you’ve had me so long.”

“Send you back?”

“Yes. I’d be too big for the stork to carry now, wouldn’t I?”

My mother knelt by the bed till our faces were level. Her eyes—always too large for her childish face—held mine, and the fear that had been worrying me fell away as if it had been only a bad dream.

“Who has been talking foolishly to a foolish little boy?” she asked, keeping my arms clasped round her.

“Oh, nurse and I were discussing things,” I said, “and she said you could have done without me.” Now it sounded absurd; clearly it had been only Mrs. Fursey’s way of seasoning conversation.

My mother drew me closer.

“And what made her think that?”

“Well,” I admitted, “I came at a very awkward time, didn’t I; when you had a lot of other troubles.”

My mother laughed—then grew grave, as she often did, like sunshine suddenly shaded by a cloud.

“I did not know you thought about such things,” she said. “We must be more together, you and I, Paul, and you shall tell me all you think, because nurse does not quite understand you. It is true there was trouble; it came just at that time. But I could not have done without you. I was very unhappy, and you were sent to comfort me and help me to bear it.”

This was better. It made me feel less like an unwanted parcel.

“Then it was lucky, your having me?” I asked.

Again she laughed; again that earnest look came, as if she were about to say something that mattered more than fairy tales.

“Will you remember what I am going to say?” she asked.

I wriggled up to sit, solemn with importance.

“I’ll try,” I said. “But I ain’t got a very good memory, have I?”

“Not very,” she smiled. “But if you think about it a good deal it will not leave you. When you are a good boy, and later on, when you are a good man, then I am the luckiest little mother in all the world. And every time you fail, that means bad luck for me. You will remember that after I’m gone, when you are a big man, won’t you, Paul?”

So, with two earnest childish faces close together in the lamplight, I promised. I smile now at the scene; yet I think—though I have little to boast of—there might have been even less had I forgotten altogether.

From that day my mother grows in my memory, and Mrs. Fursey, with all her promontories, recedes. We had sunny mornings in the neglected garden, where leaves made a soft shifting music above our heads and the air smelled of damp earth and crushed green things; twilight evenings in the window seat, half hidden behind dark red curtains, where we talked in whispers (why we whispered I never knew) of good men and noble women, ogres and fairies, saints and demons. The house had its own soundscape then, a kindly one: bees blundering among blossoms, a distant cart on the lane, the small applause of sparrows in the hedge—no shriek of machinery, no iron roar of progress. Our thoughts, like our days, were allowed to have quiet.

Possibly our education lacked method. My mother’s mind was a pantry into which everything delightful had been put—legends, poems, history, nursery tales—without labels, and when she cooked for me she seasoned freely, so that chronology became a stew where kings, heroes, and nursery villains bobbed up together. Aphrodite rose from the sea ready armed to meet King Canute, who sat throned upon the sand and bade her come no nearer lest she wet his feet. In a forest glade I saw King Rufus fall to a poisoned arrow shot by Robin Hood; but sweet Queen Eleanor sucked the poison from his wound, and so he lived. Oliver Cromwell, having killed King Charles, married his widow and was stabbed by Hamlet. Ulysses, in the Argo, discovered America. Romulus and Remus slew the wolf and rescued Little Red Riding Hood. Good King Arthur, for letting the cakes burn, was murdered by his uncle in the Tower of London. Prometheus, bound to the Rock, was saved by good St. George. Paris gave the apple to William Tell.

What did it matter? The world was full. It needed only rearranging—like a cupboard after a feast.

Sometimes of an afternoon we climbed the steep winding path through the woods, past awful precipices that seemed made for ghosts to practise on, by grassy swards where fairies danced o’ nights, by briar and bracken sheltering caves in which, I was sure, fearsome creatures lived waiting for someone to ask the wrong question. High above the creeping sea we reached the open plateau where old Jacob’s ruined tower stood. “Jacob’s Folly” they called it, and some “The Devil’s Tower,” for legend said old Jacob met his master there in windy weather to wave false wrecking lights to troubled ships. Who Jacob had been, or why he built the thing, I never learned; only that the fishermen hated his name, and swore that on stormy nights strange lights still flickered behind the ivy-curtained windows.

But by day no place was more inviting. The short moss-grass before its broken door was springy underfoot; lichen freckled its stones like flour on a baker’s board. From the top platform you could see distant mountains faint as spectres, and silent ships appearing and vanishing; below lay farm lands and the grave sweet river, winding through them with a soundless patience.

Smaller and poorer the world has grown since then. Now behind those hills lie smoky towns and dingy villages; but then they screened a country of wonder where princesses dwelt in castles and cities shone with gold. Now the ocean is a matter of six days and a Custom House. Then, had one set sail upon it, one would have travelled far beyond moonlight, beyond the gate of clouds, to the magic land of the blood-red shore, t’other side o’ the sun. I never dreamt in those days that a world could be made so mean by measuring it.

On the topmost platform ran a wooden seat inside the parapet, and there, hand in hand, sheltered from the wind that always worried the tower, my mother would people earth and air with myth and legend. Perhaps unwisely; I do not know. They were beautiful fancies—or she made them so, turning them always toward love and pity, as do the tales that live. Often, looking into my eyes, she would remember herself and add, hastily respectable: “But that, you know, is only an old superstition, and of course there are no such things nowadays.” Yet sometimes, overtaken by shadows on the homeward path, we would hurry quickly through the dim woods holding tightly to each other’s hands, and the forest’s noises—owl, twig-snap, the rush of unseen wings—would make even her whisper.

Spring fattened into summer; summer thinned into autumn. Then one morning my aunt and I, sitting at breakfast, saw my mother through the open window come skipping up the garden path. She held a letter open in her hand and waved it above her head, singing:

“Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, then comes Wednesday morning.”

She caught me up and began dancing with me round the room.

My aunt, steadily eating bread and butter, observed as if addressing the ceiling: “Just like ’em all. Goes mad with joy. What for? Because she’s going to leave a decent house to live in a poky hole in the East End of London, and keep one servant.”

My aunt regarded the second person as a grammatical luxury; she spoke not to people but about them, flinging opinions like dish-water. It allowed the victim, if self-respecting, to pretend it was impersonal philosophy. Unfortunately my mother had never learned that art.

“It’s not a poky hole,” she retorted. “It’s an old-fashioned house, near the river.”

“Plaistow marshes!” snorted my aunt. “Calls it the river!”

“So it is the river,” said my mother. “The river is the other side of the marshes.”

“Let’s hope it will always stop there,” said my aunt.

“And it’s got a garden,” my mother went on, refusing to taste the poison. “Which is quite an unusual feature in a London house. And it isn’t the East End; it is a rising suburb. And you won’t make me miserable because I am too happy.”

“Drat the woman!” said my aunt. “Why can’t she sit down and give us our tea before it’s all cold?”

“You are a disagreeable thing!” cried my mother.

“Not half milk,” said my aunt, untroubled. My aunt’s peace was the peace of a person who can always find something wrong and therefore is never surprised.

For three days my mother packed and sang; and a dozen times a day unpacked and laughed, hunting for things that were always at the bottom of the very last box she looked into. Anna, waiting for a certain undergarment of my aunt’s which shall remain nameless out of respect for the dead and for laundry, offered practical wisdom.

“If I were you, ma’am,” said Anna, “I’d look into the last box you’re going to look into first.”

The garment was eventually found in the first box—meaning the one my mother had meant to search first, but, following Anna’s suggestion, had nobly reserved for the last. This caused my mother to be quite short with Anna on the subject of wasted time.

By Tuesday afternoon everything stood ready. We were to start early Wednesday morning.

That evening, missing my mother indoors, I went to the garden and found her, as I expected, on her favourite seat under the great lime tree. The leaves above her made a soft, whispering roof, and the whole place smelled of green sweetness. Yet to my surprise there were tears in her eyes.

“But I thought you were glad we were going,” I said.

“So I am,” she answered, wiping her eyes only to make room for more.

“Then why are you crying?”

“Because I’m sorry to leave here.”

Grown-up people with their contradictory ways were a continual puzzle to me then. I am not sure I understand them even now—myself included.
