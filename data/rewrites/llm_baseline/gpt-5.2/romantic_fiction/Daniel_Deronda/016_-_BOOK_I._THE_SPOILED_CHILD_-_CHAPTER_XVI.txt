Men, like seasoned soups, keep two histories at once: the surface you can skim with a spoon, bright with fat and garnish; and the depth that holds its long-simmered bones, its bitter herbs, its secret salt. If you would truly account for a human action, you must do what the careful cook does—follow the hidden stages: the first heat that wakes scent from cold flesh, the slow bubbling where what was separate begins to belong together, the sudden boil-over of feeling that seems itself an act, like a cry flung out so fiercely it changes the air that hears it.

For Daniel Deronda, one instant—no larger than a mouthful and yet impossible to swallow—became the chief epoch of his early life. It came in July, when the sun had the frank warmth of ripe fruit, and the last petals of great pink roses were letting go, one by one, onto a lawn within a grassy court. Three sides were enclosed by a Gothic cloister whose stone held coolness as a pantry holds the night. The place had its own small acoustic: a hush salted with birdsong, the dry tick of petals landing, the far, softened rumor of a household moving behind thick walls—sounds that never struck like iron, never rasped like London wheels, but soothed the mind into a belief that the world was ordered.

There lay a boy of thirteen stretched prone where the grass was in shadow, his curly head propped on his arms above an open book, while his tutor—an able young Scotchman who served as secretary to Sir Hugo Mallinger—sat under shelter on a camp-stool with his own reading. Daniel’s book was Sismondi’s history of the Italian republics. He loved history with an appetite like hunger: he wanted to know how time had been filled since the Flood, what men did in the dull stretches when no one seems to happen, and by what recipes nations kept themselves alive.

All at once, in the unguarded, boyish tone that can sound as pure as a treble note sung in a still room, he let his left arm drop and said, without looking away from the page,

“Mr. Fraser, how was it that the popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?”

Mr. Fraser, roused unwillingly from his political economy as from a lukewarm bowl, answered in that clear-cut Scotch chant which can make blunt truth ring twice as hard.

“Their own children were called nephews.”

“Why?” Daniel asked.

“It was for the propriety of the thing,” said Mr. Fraser, giving the last words their emphatic edge, impatient to return to his book. “Because, as you know, priests don’t marry, and the children were illegitimate.”

He had already turned his eyes back to his page when Daniel—stung as if the rose had suddenly shown a thorn—started upright with his back to the tutor and sat perfectly still.

He had always called Sir Hugo Mallinger his uncle. Once, when it had occurred to him to ask of father and mother, Sir Hugo had said, with that easy cheerfulness which made even sorrow sound like a story long finished, “You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you.” Daniel, trying to see back into that earliest twilight, could catch only a dim sense: too many kisses, a cloudy softness like scented drapery close to his face, and then his fingers snagging on something hard that hurt him into crying. After that, all memory belonged to the world he lived in now.

And that world had been so complete, so kindly set, that until this moment he had not hungered for any other. Sir Hugo was indulgent and bright—a fine man in the noon of life. Diplow, Sir Hugo’s place, was one of the finest in England: historical, romantic, and yet as habitable as a well-run kitchen where everything has its hook and shelf. The house was an architectural outgrowth from an abbey; remnants of the monastic trunk remained, as if old stone still remembered the murmur of Latin, even when the living tongues had changed. The Mallingers had their grant of Monk’s Topping from Henry the Eighth, and before that had held neighboring lands of King’s Topping, tracing themselves back—so it was said—to a Hugues le Malingre who came in with the Conqueror. The portraits in the gallery above the cloisters looked down in rows upon Daniel as he walked: men in armor with pointed beards and arched brows; pinched ladies in hoops and ruffs whose faces seemed all law and little flesh; grave gentlemen in black velvet and stuffed hips; fair frightened women holding boys by the hand; later, smiling politicians swelling in magnificent perruques; ladies painted like prize animals, rosebud mouths and heavy lids; then Kneller’s revised faces, and Reynolds and Romney smoothing types into refinement, until the line ended at Sir Hugo and his younger brother Henleigh—who had married Miss Grandcourt, taken her name and estates, and made that junction of old families which now gave promise to their son, Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt.

Sir Thomas Lawrence, in Sir Hugo’s youthful portrait, had done justice to his agreeable alacrity, and more than justice to his nose, which he obligingly lengthened. The Mallinger nose reappeared in refined regularity in Sir Hugo’s younger brother, and in the nephew Grandcourt. But in Daniel Deronda there was no reflex of those family faces. He was handsomer than any of them; at thirteen his face might have served any painter wanting the most memorable of boys—one of those countenances that make you believe mankind has done nobly and might do more nobly yet, and that make you shudder freshly at the thought of grossness and basely wrought griefs, as if such things were smoke that might drift into a clean room and taint it.

But on this July grass, among the rose-petals, Daniel was meeting griefs at close quarters. An idea had entered him as swiftly as a sharp smell can enter a kitchen and change every breath. He sat with his back to Mr. Fraser, and his face went through rapid inward transitions. The deep blush that had risen at the first shock subsided; but his features held that look of subdued activity that comes when the mind is suddenly compelled to re-measure familiar facts.

He had been kept apart from other boys. His ignorance and his knowledge were oddly mingled, as often happens in bright children who read more than they talk. He had Shakespeare and histories in him; he knew how men born out of wedlock were held unfortunate, as if they carried an invisible stain that required them to become heroes merely to stand where their lawful brothers stood by right. But he had never let that knowledge touch his own life. It had been a story about other people, like Pericles or Columbus, a way of filling blank places before fame began.

Until now.

Now, with the magic of quick comparison, there darted into him the possibility that the secret of his own birth lay here: that the uncle he loved might in truth be his father. The thought came not as a reasoned conclusion but as a taste that will not leave the mouth. With it came other imaginings—dim, dreaded revelations carried by a veiled guest entering the tender chambers of his life and refusing to be sent away.

What had become of his mother? Had he been taken from her? Was there wrong—yes, wrong—done him? The ardor that usually ran into the worlds of books rushed upon his own history and spent its pictorial energy there. Sir Hugo, in a moment, took on a double aspect: still beloved, still the center of morning happiness with his watch-chain and seals and the rightness of his voice; but also possibly a man who held secrets, a man who had done him injury. And to speak of it, to ask, seemed like uttering something that would fall through his imagination like flakes of fire.

Those who have known impassioned childhood know that dread: shame connected with parents is felt as if it were a brand laid on the child’s own skin. Daniel could not at once reflect that he might be trembling at a fiction of his own. Feeling and the fear of betraying it collided within him until at last the pressure found relief in big, slow tears, falling without restraint.

Then Mr. Fraser’s voice reached him, as practical as the clink of a spoon on china.

“Daniel, do you see that you are sitting on the bent pages of your book?”

Daniel moved the book without turning. He held it before him a moment, then rose and walked away into the open grounds where he could dry his tears unseen.

Once the first shock had passed, he could tell himself there was no certainty—only conjecture. He had often invented motives for dead men to fill blank places; perhaps he was doing it now with himself. Yet certain facts returned with obstinate reality, like fragments of a broken bridge that still show where the arches must have been. At other times he would recoil from his own conjectures as from an offense, as if he were prying meanly after what he was not meant to know; for there was scarcely a delicacy of feeling the boy was not capable of.

But in sum, a secret impression had come to him, giving him something like a new sense. The idea that others might know what they would not mention planted in him a premature reserve. His ear became newly awake to words that before would have passed him like harmless kitchen clatter. Trifles that imagination could fasten to his suspicion began to gather feeling round them, as a plain broth takes on richness when some strong essence is added.

One such incident, a month later, sank deep.

Daniel had a thrilling boy-voice—one of those voices that seem to bring an idyllic heaven and earth before the mind—together with a fine musical instinct. Early, he had found accompaniments for himself at the piano while he sang from memory; later he had had some teaching. Sir Hugo delighted in him and liked to ask for his singing before guests.

One rainy morning, after Daniel had been singing “Sweet Echo” for a small party of gentlemen detained indoors, Sir Hugo—passing from a smiling remark to his neighbor—called out,

“Come here, Dan!”

Daniel came forward with unusual reluctance. He wore an embroidered holland blouse that set off the rich coloring of his head and throat. There was a resistant gravity about his mouth and eyes as he stood under admiration, and that very resistance made his beauty more striking, like fine fruit that is not yet to be plucked.

Sir Hugo spoke as if in play, but play can have a blade.

“What do you say to being a great singer? Should you like to be adored by the world and take the house by storm, like Mario and Tamberlik?”

Daniel reddened at once. There was a perceptible pause—no more than the moment before a knife comes down—then he answered with angry decision,

“No; I should hate it!”

“Well, well, well!” Sir Hugo said, surprised but kindly, intending to soothe. Yet Daniel turned quickly away, left the room, and went to his own chamber. He flung himself on the broad window-sill, a favorite perch when there was nothing demanded of him.

From there he watched the rain slacken, and gleams split the clouds to light a great reach of parkland. Old oaks stood apart like elders in a field, each with its own authority. Beyond them the bordering wood was pierced by a green glade meeting the eastern sky. The scene was part of his home—part of the dignified ease he had taken as naturally as bread at breakfast. His ardent clinging nature had claimed it all with affection. He knew much of what it was to be a gentleman by inheritance, not by thinking about himself but by living in a world where certain sounds—the quiet orders, the respectful footfall, the absence of harsh mechanical din—said, without words, You belong.

It had never occurred to him that he might be shut out from such a lot. True, some people choose poverty like a bride, preferring scoured deal and whitewash to splendor; some run away from privilege to bind themselves to hard-handed labor. But Daniel’s tastes matched his nurture: he did not rebel against habitual ease; he loved it, as one loves the familiar loaf, the known orchard, the pantry whose scents mean safety. And now he had been stung by the idea that Sir Hugo—perhaps his father—could imagine a career for him utterly unlike his own: to be dressed up and made to sing before fine people who would care for him only as they care for a clever toy, applauded and then put away.

He had been to the opera with Sir Hugo, who indulged the boy’s ear. The image of taking the house by storm was vivid; he could almost hear the roar that sweeps over a singer like wind over corn. But with that sound came another—a more cruel undertone—like coins clinking, like wheels, like the grind of commerce where art becomes wares. The thought of being set up for admiration in that way made his stomach turn as if at something too sweet, too perfumed, meant to hide what was coarse beneath.

And Sir Hugo’s question felt to Daniel like proof: something about his birth cast him out from the class of gentlemen. Would it ever be spoken? Would a day come when Sir Hugo would tell him everything? He shrank from it; his imagination preferred ignorance. If his father—he used strong words in his inward hurt—if his father had done wrong, he wished it might never be spoken. It was cutting enough to think such knowledge might live in other minds.

Was it in Mr. Fraser’s? Probably not, or he would not have spoken so freely about the pope’s nephews. Yet Daniel, like older people, fancied everyone’s consciousness must be as busy as his on what was vital to him. Did Turvey the valet know? Old Mrs. French the housekeeper? Banks the bailiff, with whom Daniel had ridden on his pony over the farms where cows chewed and whey steamed faintly sour-sweet in the dairy? And then memory pricked him with a small sharpness: years ago, drinking Mrs. Banks’s whey—cool, thin, and alive with the clean tang of milk turned—he had heard Banks say to his wife with a wink and a cunning laugh, “He features the mother, eh?” At the time little Daniel had only thought Banks made a silly face, like common farming men laughing at what was not laughable; he had resented being winked at as if he did not understand. Now that trifling moment became information to be weighed.

How could he be like his mother and not like his father? His mother must have been a Mallinger if Sir Hugo were his uncle. But no—his father might have been Sir Hugo’s brother and changed his name, as Henleigh Mallinger had taken Grandcourt. But then why had Sir Hugo never spoken of a brother Deronda, as he spoke of brother Grandcourt?

Before, Daniel had not cared about the family tree, except for the ancestor who killed three Saracens in one encounter. Now his mind turned to a cabinet of estate-maps in the library, where he had once seen an illuminated parchment hanging out, which Sir Hugo had called the family tree. The phrase had seemed odd then. Now he longed to examine that parchment, to trace names as if they were ingredients in a dish whose true flavor depended on what had been added and what omitted. He imagined the cabinet always locked. He even felt the urge to try it.

Then he checked himself. He might be seen. And he would not bring himself near even a silent admission of the sore opened in him.

It is in such experiences—while elders debate whether education is science or literature—that the main lines of character are often set. If Daniel had been less ardently affectionate, his reserve and the sense that others held something against him might have hardened into proud antagonism. But his inborn lovingness kept level with resentment. He was fond of almost every creature in his habitual world, teasing them now and then as happy children do—everyone except that he did not tease Sir Hugo, whom he called “Nunc,” as Sir Hugo had taught him; the baronet was not strait-laced and let his dignity take care of itself.

Daniel loved him with that deep-rooted filial love that makes children happier merely to be in the same room with father or mother, even while doing nothing together. Sir Hugo’s handwriting, his way of smoking, his tone in speaking to dogs and horses—each had a charm and rightness that mixed with the happiness of morning and breakfast. Sir Hugo’s politics—he had always been a Whig—made Tories and Radicals equally opponents of what seemed to Daniel the truest and best. Sir Hugo’s writings, whether travels in brilliant style, or articles on everything, or pamphlets on political crises, were all consecrated in the boy’s belief: what was his had a rightness that could test other men’s knowledge.

And so the bitterness of suspecting something not quite right in this object of complete love was like tasting spoilage in what you have always trusted as wholesome. Children demand their heroes fleckless; the first discovery of a possible stain can feel like the ground shifting.

Some time after Daniel’s renewed agitation, it appeared that Sir Hugo’s question about singing had been only a playful experiment. He sent for Daniel into the library. When the boy entered, Sir Hugo looked up from his writing and threw himself sideways in his arm-chair.

“Ah, Dan!” he said, drawing one of the old embroidered stools close. “Come and sit down here.”

Daniel obeyed. Sir Hugo laid a gentle hand on his shoulder and looked at him affectionately.

“What is it, my boy? Have you heard anything that has put you out of spirits lately?”

Daniel was determined not to let tears come, but his throat closed; he could not speak.

“All changes are painful when people have been happy, you know,” Sir Hugo went on, lifting his hand to rub the boy’s dark curls. “You can’t be educated exactly as I wish you to be without our parting. And I think you will find a great deal to like at school.”

This was not what Daniel expected, and the relief came like a draught of fresh air after smoke. He found voice.

“Am I to go to school?”

“Yes. I mean you to go to Eton. I wish you to have the education of an English gentleman; and for that it is necessary you should go to a public school in preparation for the university. Cambridge I mean you to go to; it was my own university.”

Daniel’s color came and went.

“What do you say, Sirrah?” Sir Hugo asked, smiling.

“I should like to be a gentleman,” Daniel said, with firm distinctness, “and go to school, if that is what a gentleman’s son must do.”

Sir Hugo watched him a moment in silence, thinking he understood now why the lad had bristled at the idea of being a singer. Then he said, tenderly,

“And so you won’t mind about leaving your old Nunc?”

“Yes, I shall,” Daniel said, clasping Sir Hugo’s caressing arm with both hands as if holding to something warm and certain. “But sha’n’t I come home and be with you in the holidays?”

“Oh yes, generally,” said Sir Hugo. “But now I mean you to go at once to a new tutor, to break the change for you before you go to Eton.”

After this, Daniel’s spirits rose again. He was meant to be a gentleman; perhaps—by some unguessed arrangement—his conjectures were wrong. His very keenness taught him to find comfort in ignorance: while he had been constructing possibilities, it became plain there must be possibilities of which he knew nothing. He stopped brooding. Youthful joy and the spirit of adventure are not easily quenched; and in the interval before leaving, he sang about the house, danced among the old servants, made them parting gifts, and repeated his injunctions to the groom about the care of the black pony, as if love could be packed into instructions and carried forward intact.

“Do you think I shall know much less than the other boys, Mr. Fraser?” Daniel asked. He was always inclined to think strangers would be surprised by his ignorance.

“There are dunces to be found everywhere,” Fraser replied, judiciously. “You’ll not be the biggest; but you’ve not the makings of a Porson in you, or a Leibnitz either.”

“I don’t want to be a Porson or a Leibnitz,” Daniel said. “I would rather be a greater leader, like Pericles or Washington.”

“Ay, ay,” said Fraser, “you’ve a notion they did with little parsing, and less algebra.” But in truth he thought his pupil remarkable, able to take up anything easily, if only he cared.

Things went well enough for Daniel in his new world—except in one point that left a lasting taste. A boy he was at once inclined to love as a close friend talked to him much of his own home and parents, and seemed to expect the same openness in return. At once Daniel shrank into reserve, as if a door had been quietly shut. That experience stayed with him, checking his natural bent toward intimate friendship. Everyone—even his tutor—called him reserved, though he was good-humored, unassuming, quick at study and sport; no one found his reserve disagreeable.

And certainly his face had much to do with that favorable judgment. In him, the beauty of closed lips told no falsehood.
