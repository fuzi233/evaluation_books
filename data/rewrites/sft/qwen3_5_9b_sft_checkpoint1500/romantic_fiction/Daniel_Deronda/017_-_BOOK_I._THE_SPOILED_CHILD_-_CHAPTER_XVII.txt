"This is true the poet sings,
That a sorrow's crown of sorrow
Is remembering happier things."
—TENNYSON: *In Memoriam*.

On a fine evening near the end of July, Daniel Deronda rowed alone upon the Thames. It had been nearly a year since his return to England, a journey undertaken with the vague promise that his education was concluded and he was ready to assume his station in society. Though Sir Hugo had wished him to study law as a shield against idleness, the endeavor had served only to anchor his indecision more deeply. Now, surrounded by the Mallingers in town, his ancient passion for boating had resurfaced with renewed vigor. The river offered a solitude unavailable anywhere else, and whenever Sir Hugo did not require him, it became his primary sanctuary to row until the stars emerged.

Yet, Deronda was not lost in sentimentality. Rather, he harbored a contemplative mood, prevalent among thoughtful young men of his era, characterized by a profound questioning of whether participation in the world's struggles was truly worthwhile. Specifically, he questioned the value of joining a battle where the labor of inquiry was sustained by a mere fraction of capital amassed by others through prior struggle. Sir Hugo, a man of impeccable Whig sensibilities, found Deronda's hesitation perplexing; how could a youth who stood in such stark contrast to the sickly and sentimental be hamstrung by "spectral illusions"? Especially since Deronda rejected authorship, the profession supposedly designed to monetize idle musings.

Clad in a dark-blue shirt and skull-cap, with closely cropped curls and a mouth framed by abundant, soft waves of beard, Deronda carried only disguised traces of the seraphic boy who once trailed clouds of glory. To the uninitiated eye, he appeared merely as a handsome young man, though those who knew Gwendolen might recall the peculiar, "dreadful" gaze she attributed to him—a look that was, in truth, a mild and penetrating scrutiny. His voice, occasionally heard in subdued fragments of song, was a high baritone rather than the rare tenor nature sometimes sacrifices for beauty. One needed only to observe his lithe, powerful frame and the firm gravity of his countenance to understand that he possessed a strength grounded in earth, not ethereal flight.

His hands told a similar story: long, flexible, and firmly grasping, evoking the ideal of refinement combined with force as depicted by Titian. There was a striking resemblance between his face and hands—the uniform pale-brown skin, the perpendicular brow, and the calmly piercing eyes. He was no longer seraphic; he was thoroughly terrestrial and manly, a type capable of recognizing dignity even in poverty. Such figures appear intermittently among the average conditions of life, like a workman whistling over a measurement, yet the profound meanings of such faces often lie in the immediate impressions they evoke.

Deronda rowed beneath Kew Bridge, unaware that his appearance was destined to alter the course of an encounter. He vigorously rejected the notion, hinted at by others, that his looks naturally commanded attention. Compliments regarding his appearance resonated angrily within him, stemming from a complex history he could not yet articulate. For years, his reflection in the glass had haunted him with the unsettling realization that he resembled someone about whose character and fate he constantly wondered but dared not inquire.

As the hour approached seven, the river near Kew Bridge was no longer a solitude. People sauntered along the towing path, and boats plied the water. Deronda had rowed quickly to bypass this area, but upon seeing a great barge approaching, he guided his boat aside, resting his oar within yards of the bank. Unconsciously, he continued the low-toned chant that had haunted his throat since leaving Putney—the gondolier's song from *Otello*, setting Dante's immortal words to music: *"Nessun maggior dolore / Che ricordarsi del tempo felice / Nella miseria"* ("No greater sorrow / Than to remember happy times / In misery").

As he paused, the melodic wail of *"nella miseria"* drifted softly onto the water's edge. A few bystanders had stopped to watch the barge pass, and likely noted the young gentleman in the boat, but the sound held significance only to one ear. Turning his head toward the riverbank, Deronda spotted a figure that seemed an embodiment of the misery he voiced. It was a girl, hardly eighteen, of slender build and delicate features. Her dark curls were tucked behind her ears beneath a large black hat, and a long woolen cloak draped her shoulders. Her hands hung clasped before her, her eyes fixed on the river with an immovable, statue-like despair.

Her gaze arrested him completely, silencing his song. As his voice cut off abruptly, she shifted slightly, glancing around with a frightened look before meeting his face. Her expression was akin to that of a fawn before it bolts—no blush, no panic, only a gentle timidity that could not stop her from holding his gaze for a moment longer. To Deronda, she seemed half-conscious of her surroundings, perhaps driven by hunger or bewilderment. A surge of interest and compassion overwhelmed him, but instantly, she turned and walked toward a bench under a nearby tree.

He had no right to linger. Poorly dressed, melancholy women were common sights; however, her delicate beauty and picturesque lines made her image exceptionally vivid in his mind. He began to row away, soon far up the river, yet the pale specter of the unhappy girl refused to fade. He speculated endlessly on the tragedy hidden behind her loneliness, smiled at his own prejudice that interesting faces must harbor interesting stories, and justified his sorrow for her plight.

"I should not have forgotten the look of misery if she had been ugly and vulgar," he murmured to himself. Yet, the attractiveness of the image ensured its persistence. It was clear to him as an onyx cameo: the brown-black drapery, the white face with minute features and long-lashed dark eyes. His mind wandered over the unseen tragedies playing out in the world, those hidden away like wounds in a copse where helpless creatures drag wounded wings and stain the moss with blood. Recently, his solitary excursions had been dominated by uncertainties regarding his own future, but these doubts, vast in scope, connected with all of life and history. Consequently, the new image of helpless sorrow blended seamlessly with his reasons for shrinking from the world's routines—the apologies for wrongdoing and the treatment of opinions as mere professional tools. Why should he not pull at any thread in the hopelessly entangled scheme?

Using his oars sparingly, content to drift with the tide and be returned by it, Deronda indulged in a solemn passivity born of lengthening shadows and mellow light. Thinking and desiring melted together imperceptibly, arguments transforming into passionate visions. By the time he returned past Richmond Bridge, the sun was nearing its setting. The approach of his favorite hour—the deepening stillness and darkening masses of trees and buildings caught between the double glow of sky and river—prompted him to linger.

Seeking a perfectly solitary spot to moor his boat, he chose a bend opposite Kew Gardens, where the water reflected the sky's glory while he remained in shadow. Lying on his back with his head propped on cushions, he watched the sunset and the emergence of the stars, answering God's call. He lay with his hands behind his head, propelling himself at a level with the boat's edge, able to see all around but unseen by anyone nearby. For a long while, he gazed fixedly ahead, forgetting everything else in a speculative identification with the landscape, imagining how far he might shift his center of consciousness until his personality existed outside him as purely as the scenery.

Then, sensing movement on the opposite bank, bordered by willow bushes, he turned his glance. A darting presentiment seized him. Soon, he could see the small face illuminated by dying sunlight. Fearing to startle her, he watched with motionless attention. She looked around, seemingly seeking security in the apparent solitude, hiding her hat among the willows, and immediately removing her woolen cloak. She seated herself and deliberately dipped the cloak into the water, holding it there briefly before pulling it out with evident effort, rising as she did so.

Deronda realized she intended to wrap the wet cloak around herself like a drowning shroud. There was no time to hesitate. Rising and seizing his oar, he moved swiftly across. Fortunately, her position lay slightly below him. Overcome with terror at the sight of the boatman, she sank back onto the brink, clutching the cloak half-out of the water. She crouched, covering her face, perhaps hoping the boatman was merely passing by accident.

Soon, however, he was close enough to steady his boat against the bank. Speaking very gently, he called out, "Don't be afraid. You are unhappy. Pray, trust me. Tell me what I can do to help you."

She raised her head and looked up at him. The light now fell upon his face, and she recognized him. Silence stretched between them for several moments, renewing their former gaze. Finally, in a low, sweet voice with an accent suggesting foreignness, she said, "I saw you before." After a pause, she added dreamily, "*nella miseria*."

Deronda, failing to grasp the connection, assumed her mind was weakened by distress and hunger. "It was you, singing?" she asked hesitantly—"*Nessun maggior dolore*." The mere words, uttered in her sweet undertones, seemed to impart a melody to his ear.
