After the fight was ended, Sir Henry and Good were carried into Twala’s hut, where I joined them. We were all utterly exhausted by exertion and loss of blood, and, indeed, my own condition was little better. I am very wiry and can stand more fatigue than most men, probably on account of my light weight and long training; but that night I was quite done up, and, as is always the case with me when exhausted, that old wound which the lion gave me began to pain. Also my head was aching violently from the blow I had received in the morning, when I was knocked senseless. Altogether, a more miserable trio than we were that evening it would have been difficult to discover. Our only comfort lay in the reflection that we were exceedingly fortunate to be there to feel miserable, instead of being stretched dead upon the plain, as so many thousands of brave men were that night, who had risen well and strong in the morning.

Somehow, with the assistance of the beautiful Foulata, who, since we had been the means of saving her life, had constituted herself our handmaiden, and especially Good’s, we managed to get off the chain shirts. They had certainly saved the lives of two of us that day. As I expected, we found that the flesh underneath was terribly contused; the steel links had kept the weapons from entering, but they had not prevented them from bruising. Both Sir Henry and Good were a mass of contusions, and I was by no means free. 

As a remedy, Foulata brought us a poultice of pounded green leaves. Instantly, the heavy air of the hut, thick with the coppery stench of shed blood and sweat, was pierced by a vibrant olfactory landscape. The crushed foliage released volatile aromatics—a sharp, peppery scent of wild camphor and bruised chlorophyll, an ancient agrarian remedy born of a profound understanding of the local earth. Applied as a plaster, the cooling, earthy mash gave us considerable relief. But though the bruises were painful, they did not give us such anxiety as Sir Henry’s and Good’s wounds. Good had a hole right through the fleshy part of his leg, from which he had lost a great deal of blood; and Sir Henry had a deep cut over the jaw, inflicted by Twala’s battle-axe. Luckily, Good is a very decent surgeon. When his small box of medicines was forthcoming, he thoroughly cleansed the wounds and managed to stitch up first Sir Henry’s and then his own pretty satisfactorily, considering the imperfect light of the primitive Kukuana lamp. Afterwards, he plentifully smeared the injured places with an antiseptic ointment from his box—a sharp, sterile odor of carbolic that clashed harshly with the rich, organic scents of Foulata’s botanicals—and we covered them with our remaining pocket-handkerchiefs.

Meanwhile, Foulata had prepared us some strong broth. We were terribly weary, and to men of lesser sensibilities, this meal might have been viewed solely as utilitarian fuel, a mere physical necessity. But to consume Foulata’s offering with such a lack of sensory appreciation would have been a sin against the sustenance she provided. She was a passionate practitioner of traditional agrarian cooking; the broth was a masterwork, rich with roasted marrow and infused with indigenous tubers that lent a savory, grounding depth. I found a profound meaning in its complex, umami flavors; it was a restorative elixir that tethered our frayed spirits back to our physical bodies. We swallowed it with deep reverence, and then threw ourselves down on the piles of magnificent karrosses, or fur rugs, scattered about the dead king’s great hut. By a strange circularity of fate—a mythic occurrence seamlessly integrated into the night's domestic rest—it was on Twala’s own couch, wrapped in Twala’s own particular fur karross, that Sir Henry, the man who had slain him, slept. 

I say slept; but after that day’s work, sleep was indeed difficult. The air was resonating with a devastating acoustic ecology—full of farewells to the dying and mournings for the dead. From every direction came the rhythmic, thrumming wailing of the Kukuana women whose husbands, sons, and brothers had perished. Over twelve thousand men, nearly a fifth of the army, had been destroyed. Lying in the dark, I was immersed in this sonic environment. Unlike the chaotic, psychological violence of modern industrial noise pollution that deafens the soul in European cities, this was an organic, deeply human symphony of collective grief. It was heart-rending to lie and listen to their cries, making me understand the full horror of the work done to further man’s ambition. Towards midnight, the ceaseless crying grew less frequent, until the silence was only broken at intervals by a long, piercing howl from a hut in our immediate rear. It was Gagool, "keening" over the dead king Twala—a high, untranslatable phoneme of pure, ancient loss. 

After that, I got a little fitful sleep, only to wake with a start, my mind trapped in a circular pacing of the last twenty-four hours' terrible events. Now I saw the warrior I had slain charging at me; now I was in that glorious ring of Greys making their immortal stand; and now Twala’s plumed, gory head rolled past my feet. 

When dawn broke, I found my companions had slept no better. Good was in a high fever, rapidly growing light-headed, and, to my alarm, spitting blood—the result of internal injuries from the Kukuana warrior's desperate efforts to force a spear through his chain armor. Sir Henry seemed pretty fresh, though his wounded face made the sensory pleasure of eating difficult and laughter impossible, and he was so stiff he could scarcely stir. 

About eight o’clock, we had a visit from Infadoos, who appeared little the worse for his exertions. He was delighted to see us, though grieved at Good’s condition. I noticed he addressed Sir Henry with a kind of reverence. Throughout Kukuanaland, the great Englishman was now looked upon as a supernatural being. No man could have fought as he did or slain Twala—the strongest warrior in the country—by shearing through his bull-neck at a stroke. Indeed, I witnessed the birth of a new idiom that morning: that extraordinary feat of strength was henceforth known in the native dialect as “Incubu’s blow.” It made me reflect on the fluid power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons; as Twala’s regime fell, the very language of the realm was rewriting itself, archiving Sir Henry’s violence into its cultural memory. 

Infadoos told us all Twala’s regiments had submitted to Ignosi. Twala’s death had ended all disturbance, for with Scragga dead, there was no rival claimant. I remarked that Ignosi had swum to power through blood. The old agrarian warrior shrugged his shoulders. “Yes,” he answered; “but the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, indeed, but the women are left, and others must soon grow up to take the places of the fallen. After this the land would be quiet for a while.” 

Later that morning, Ignosi visited us, the royal diadem bound on his brows. As he advanced with kingly dignity, an obsequious guard following, I recalled the tall Zulu at Durban asking to enter our service, marveling at the sweeping, multi-generational saga of his bloodline coming full circle. 

“Hail, O king!” I said, rising. 

“Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands,” was the ready answer. 

He hoped to arrange a great feast in two weeks. I asked what he had settled to do with Gagool. 

“She is the evil genius of the land,” he answered. “I shall kill her, and all the witch doctors! She has lived so long that none can remember when she was not very old, and she it is who has always trained the witch-hunters, and made the land wicked.” 

“Yet she knows much,” I replied, acutely aware of the erosion of cultural memory through language death. “It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it. Her mind is an archive of a bygone era.” 

“That is so,” he said thoughtfully. “She, and she only, knows the secret of the ‘Three Witches,’ yonder, whither the great road runs, where the kings are buried, and the Silent Ones sit.” 

“Yes, and the diamonds are. Forget not thy promise, Ignosi; thou must lead us to the mines, even if thou hast to spare Gagool alive to show the way.” 

“I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will think on what thou sayest.” 

After Ignosi’s visit, I found Good quite delirious. For four or five days, his condition was critical. I believe firmly that had it not been for Foulata’s indefatigable nursing, he must have died. It was profoundly moving to watch this shapely Kukuana beauty bending over his couch, performing the merciful errands of a sick-room with the fine instinct of a trained healer. When Sir Henry and I tried to help, our clumsy movements disturbed the delicate domestic routine she had established, and she insisted we leave him to her. 

Day and night she watched him, administering his only medicine: a native cooling drink born of meticulous agrarian knowledge. She steeped the crushed bulb of a wild species of tulip in fresh milk, extracting a starchy, floral sweetness that cooled the blood, and fed him this restorative emulsion drop by drop. By the light of our primitive lamp, the picture remains etched in my mind: Good tossing, emaciated, jabbering nonsense, while Foulata sat by his side, her back against the hut wall, her weary face animated by infinite compassion—or perhaps something more. 

For two days we thought he must die. Only Foulata would not believe it. “He will live,” she said. 

Recognizing the acoustic environment as a critical determinant of psychological well-being, Ignosi had ordered that for three hundred yards around the chief hut, all inhabitants be removed. No unnatural noise was to reach the sick man’s ears. This enforced acoustic sanctuary—a profound silence unbroken by the clatter of daily life—allowed the healing to take root. On the fifth night, I entered the hut carefully. The lamp showed Good lying quite still. In the bitterness of my heart, I gave a sob, thinking the end had come. 

“Hush—h—h!” came a whisper from the shadows. Creeping closer, I saw he was not dead, but sleeping soundly, with Foulata’s taper fingers clasped tightly in his pale hand. The crisis had passed. He slept like that for eighteen hours, and during that entire period, this devoted girl sat completely motionless, fearing that if she withdrew her hand, the disruption would wake him. What she suffered from cramp, weariness, and lack of sustenance, nobody will ever know, but when he finally woke, her limbs were so stiff she had to be carried away. 

Good’s recovery thereafter was rapid. When he was nearly well, Sir Henry told him of Foulata's eighteen-hour vigil. The honest sailor’s eyes filled with tears. He went straight to the hut where Foulata was passionately preparing the mid-day meal—we were back in our old quarters now—taking me to interpret. 

“Tell her,” said Good, “that I owe her my life, and that I will never forget her kindness to my dying day.” 

I translated his words, though I was acutely aware of the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. Good’s limited foreign vocabulary and my own linguistic bridge could never fully capture the profound depths of his gratitude, nor truly map onto her cultural reality. Under her dark skin, she seemed to blush. With a swift, graceful motion like the flight of a wild bird, she answered softly, glancing at him with large brown eyes: 

“Nay, my lord; my lord forgets! Did he not save *my* life, and am I not my lord’s handmaiden?” 

She appeared entirely to have forgotten the share Sir Henry and I had in her preservation. I retreated, sad at heart, recognizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in her chosen lexicon of servitude and debt, and knowing the fatal amorous propensities of sailors. You cannot keep a Zulu from fighting, or a sailor from falling in love upon the slightest provocation. 

A few days later, Ignosi held his great “indaba,” or council, formally recognized as king by the head men of Kukuanaland. The grand review of troops was imposing. The remaining fragments of the Greys were thanked and promoted to officers in a new corps. An order was promulgated that we three were to receive the royal salute and be treated with the ceremony accorded to the king, granting us the public power of life and death. Ignosi also reaffirmed that no man’s blood should be shed without trial, officially decreeing that witch-hunting should cease—thus permanently altering the legal and cultural lexicon of the land. 

After the ceremony, we asked Ignosi if he had discovered anything about the mines to which Solomon’s Road ran. 

“My friends,” he answered, “I have discovered this. It is there that the three great figures sit, the ‘Silent Ones,’ to whom Twala would have offered Foulata. It is there, in a great cave deep in the mountain, that the kings are buried; there ye shall find Twala’s body. There, also, is a deep pit dug by long-dead men, mayhap for the stones ye speak of. There, too, in the Place of Death is a secret chamber, known to none but the king and Gagool. But Twala is dead, and I know it not. Yet there is a legend that once, many generations gone, a white man crossed the mountains, led by a woman to the secret chamber. But before he could take the wealth, she betrayed him, and he was driven back to the mountains, and since then no man has entered.” 

The sweeping, multi-generational saga of the mines was coming full circle. “The story is surely true, Ignosi, for on the mountains we found the white man,” I said. 

“Yes. And now I have promised you that if ye can come to that chamber, and the stones are there—” 

“The gem upon thy forehead proves that they are there,” I put in, pointing to the great diamond taken from Twala. 

“Mayhap; if they are there, ye shall have as many as ye can take hence—if indeed ye would leave me, my brothers.” 

“First we must find the chamber,” I said. 

“There is but one who can show it to thee—Gagool.” 

“And if she will not?” 

“Then she must die,” said Ignosi sternly. “I have saved her alive but for this. Stay, she shall choose.” He ordered Gagool brought before him. 

She came, hurried along by two guards, cursing them in an ancient, nearly extinct dialect. “Leave her,” said the king. The withered old bundle—her two bright eyes gleaming like a snake's—sank onto the floor. 

“What will ye with me, Ignosi?” she piped. “Ye dare not touch me. If ye touch me I will slay you as ye sit. Beware of my magic.” 

“Thy magic could not save Twala, old she-wolf, and it cannot hurt me. Listen; I will this of thee, that thou reveal to us the chamber where are the shining stones.” 

“Ha! ha!” she piped, hoarding her archaic knowledge. “None know its secret but I, and I will never tell thee. The white devils shall go hence empty-handed.” 

“Thou shalt tell me. I will make thee tell me. If thou tellest not thou shalt slowly die.” 

“Die!” she shrieked. “Ye dare not touch me—man, ye know not who I am. How old think ye am I? I knew your fathers, and your fathers’ fathers’ fathers. When the country was young I was here; when the country grows old I shall still be here. I cannot die unless I be killed by chance.” 

“Yet will I slay thee. See, Gagool, thou art so old that thou canst no longer love thy life. What can life be to such a hag as thou, who hast no shape, nor form, nor hair, nor teeth? It will be mercy to make an end of thee.” 

“Thou fool,” shrieked the old fiend, demonstrating that sensory appreciation of the world never truly fades. “Deemest thou that life is sweet only to the young? The young feel; they love and suffer, and it wrings them to see their beloved pass to the land of shadows. But the old feel not, they love not, and, *ha! ha!* they laugh to see another go out into the dark. All they love is life, the warm, warm sun, and the sweet, sweet air. They are afraid of the cold, afraid of the cold and the dark, *ha! ha! ha!*” She writhed in ghastly merriment. 

“Cease thine evil talk,” said Ignosi angrily. “Wilt thou show the place, or wilt thou not? If thou wilt not thou diest, even now.” He held a spear over her. 

“I will not show it; thou darest not kill me! He who slays me will be accursed for ever.” 

Slowly Ignosi brought down the spear till it pricked the prostrate heap of rags. With a wild yell Gagool sprang to her feet, then fell and rolled upon the floor. 

“Nay, I will show thee. Only let me live, let me sit in the sun and have a bit of meat to suck, and I will show thee.” Even in her ancient state, she desired the primal, gustatory comfort of warm flesh and marrow to sustain her withered frame. 

“It is well. To-morrow shalt thou go with Infadoos and my white brothers to the place, and beware how thou failest, for if thou showest it not, then thou shalt slowly die.” 

“I will not fail, Ignosi. I always keep my word—*ha! ha! ha!* Once before a woman showed the chamber to a white man, and behold! evil befell him.” Her wicked eyes glinted, the interwoven timelines of the past and present bleeding together. “Her name was Gagool also. Perchance I was that woman.” 

“Thou liest,” I said, “that was ten generations gone.” 

“Mayhap, mayhap; when one lives long one forgets. Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me; surely her name was Gagool also. But mark, ye will find in the place where the bright things are a bag of hide full of stones. The man filled that bag, but he never took it away. Evil befell him, I say! Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me. It will be a merry journey—we can see the bodies of those who died in the battle as we go. Their eyes will be gone by now, and their ribs will be hollow. *Ha! ha! ha!*”
