On the fourth night, I slipped into sleep—and this time, I truly awoke.

I opened my eyes. Darkness surrounded me, thick and absolute, yet I knew without doubt that I lay within the house of death. Every moment since I had closed my eyes had been a dream—now, at last, I was awake.

"At last!" I whispered to my heart, and it leapt in joy.

I turned my gaze—and there she stood: Lona. She waited by my side, her presence a beacon in the void. I had never lost her—not really. Only for a brief while had I lost sight of her. How foolishly I had mourned!

The darkness held no power over her. Her eyes blazed with the same light as the Mother’s, and that radiance poured from her face, from her very being. Even her shroud—the garment of death—was transformed. It shimmered like snow, glowing with an inner fire, tenfold brighter now that her soul had risen into fullness. She had slept as a girl; she awakened as a woman, matured in beauty, radiant with the essence of life itself.

I pulled her close, and in that embrace, I felt certain: I lived.

"I woke first," she said, smiling with wonder.

"You did," I replied, "and you woke me."

"I only looked at you… and waited," she murmured.

Then, from the shadows, a candle floated toward us, gliding on unseen currents. Moments later, Adam, Eve, and Mara appeared beside us.

They greeted us quietly, with soft smiles—used to such awakenings, as if they were born anew each dawn.

"I hope your darkness was pleasant?" the Mother asked.

"Not very," I admitted. "But waking from it is heavenly."

"Only just begun," she replied. "You are scarcely awake yet."

"He is clothed upon with Death," Adam said, his voice warm and knowing. "And that is the radiant robe of Life."

He embraced Lona—his child—then placed an arm around me. He paused, studying the princess with quiet intent, then gently patted the head of the leopardess.

"I think we shall meet again soon," he said, looking first at Lona, then at me.

"Must we die again?" I asked.

"No," Adam answered, his smile mirroring the Mother’s. "You have died into life. You will not die again. You must only remain dead—once you’ve died, all dying is done. Now, you have only to live. And live fully, with every blessed strength you possess. The more you live, the stronger you become."

"But won’t I grow weary?" I wondered aloud. "What if I fail to live with all my might?"

"The will is enough," the Mother said. "Strength follows. Pure life knows no fatigue. Our life is fed by the great Life. Those who refuse to die—die many times, constantly, deeper each time, never ending. But here—there is only upward motion, love, and joy."

She smiled, and her glance seemed to say: *We understand. We are mother and son. No farewell can ever pass between us.*

Mara kissed my forehead, laughing softly. "I told you, brother—everything would be well. Next time you offer comfort, say: 'What will be well, is already well.'"

She sighed—a small sound, but heavy with meaning. I thought it carried a silent truth: *They will not believe you.*

"And now," she added, her smile like her mother’s, "you know me."

"I do," I said. "You are the voice crying in the wilderness before the Baptist came. You are the shepherd whose wolves bring the lost sheep home before the night deepens."

"My work will one day end," she said. "Then I shall rejoice with the Great Shepherd who sent me."

"All night long," Adam said, "the morning draws near."

"What is that flapping of wings?" I asked.

"The Shadow hovers," Adam replied. "There is one here he claims as his own. But once ours—she can never be his again."

I turned to look at my parents’ beds, wanting to kiss them before we left. But their places were empty—only the Little Ones remained, having claimed the space with fearless affection.

For a moment, the old terror returned—the dream of desolation, the emptiness. I stepped aside.

"What troubles you?" Lona asked.

"Their empty beds frightened me," I confessed.

"They rose long ago," Adam said. "They kissed you before they went. They whispered, 'Come soon.'"

"And I could neither feel nor hear them," I murmured.

"How could you? You were far away—in your cold, old house. You thought the dread place had reclaimed you. But now—go find them. Your parents, my child," he added, turning to Lona, "must come to find you."

Our hour had come.

Lona approached the couch where the woman who had slain her lay. She bent down and kissed her gently—then nestled into her father’s arms.

"That kiss will draw her home," Adam said.

"Who were her parents?" Lona asked.

"My father," Adam replied, "is hers too."

She turned and placed her hand in mine. I knelt, humbly thanking the three for helping me die.

Lona knelt beside me. Together, they breathed upon us—life, warmth, blessing.

"Hark!" Adam said. "I hear the sun."

I listened. The sun was coming—not slowly, but in a rush, like a thousand times ten thousand distant wings beating the air. A roaring wave of molten fire, millions of miles away, surged toward us. His approach was a crescendo—a symphony of a hundred harmonies rising in one grand chord.

Adam, Eve, and Mara exchanged glances and smiled. That smile lifted into the sky like a three-petaled flower—the family’s morning offering.

From their lips, their faces, their bodies, light spread outward, shining through their garments.

Before I could speak—"Lo, they change!"—Adam and Eve stood before me as angels of resurrection. Mara stood beside them, like the Magdalene at the tomb.

Adam’s face flashed like lightning. Eve held a napkin that scattered sparks of brilliance across the room.

A wind began to moan—deep, pulsing gusts.

"You hear his wings now," Adam said—and I understood he meant not the wings of dawn, but something older, darker.

"It is the great Shadow stirring to depart," he continued. "Wretched creature—he carries himself within him, and cannot rest."

"But is there not something deeper within him?" I asked.

"Without substance," Adam replied, "a shadow cannot exist. Nor without light behind the substance."

He listened, then called out with joy: "Hark! The golden cock!"

Silent for eons, perched on the clock of the universe, the golden cock had finally stirred. Now, at last, he flapped his wings—and soon, he would crow. And men would hear him, again and again, until the eternal day broke.

I listened. Far off, in the heart of endless silence, I heard the clear, jubilant cry—the golden throat shouting defiance at death, at dark. It sang infinite hope. It sang calm. It was the longing of creation finding voice at last—the cry of chaos yearning to become kingdom.

Then—great flapping.

"The black bat has flown!" Mara said.

"Amen, golden cock, bird of God!" Adam cried—and the words echoed through the silent house, rising into the heavens.

At that Amen—like doves lifting from broken pottery on silver wings—the Little Ones sprang up from their beds, kneeling in unison.

"Crow! Crow again, golden cock!" they shouted, as if they had seen and heard him in their dreams.

Each turned to their sleeping companion, gazed with loving eyes, kissed them gently, then leapt from the bed.

Those who had lain beside my parents stared blankly at the empty spaces—sad, confused—for a moment. Then they slid slowly to the floor, falling into each other’s arms. At last, through each other’s eyes, they confirmed: *We are alive. We are awake.*

Suddenly seeing Lona, they rushed to her, radiant with joy.

Odu, spotting the leopardess at the princess’s feet, bounded forward. He threw an arm over her great, sleeping head, fondling and kissing her.

"Wake up, wake apart, darling!" he cried. "It's time to wake!"

But the leopardess did not stir.

"She’s slept herself cold!" Odu said, turning to Mara with wide, pleading eyes.

"She waits for the princess to wake," Mara said gently.

Odu looked at the princess—and saw two of his companions still asleep beside her.

He flew at them.

"Wake up! Wake up!" he shouted, pushing and pulling, frantic.

But they would not move.

Soon, his joy faded. He turned to me, eyes misty.

"They won’t wake," he whispered. "And why are they so cold?"

"They wait for the princess too," I said.

He reached out, laid his hand on her face.

"She’s cold too!" he cried. "What is happening?"

He looked around in horror.

Adam approached.

"Her awakening is not yet ripe," he said. "She is busy forgetting. When she forgets enough to remember—then she will wake."

"Remember?" Odu asked.

"Yes—but not too much at once."

"But the golden cock has crowned!" the child argued, rushing back to his friends. "Peter! Peter! Crispy! Wake up! We’re all awake—except you two! The golden cock has crowed so loud! The sun is coming! Oh, why won’t you wake?"

But Peter did not wake. Neither did Crispy.

Odu wept—openly, helplessly.

"Let them sleep, darling," Adam said softly. "Would you want the princess to wake and find no one? They are happy. So is the leopardess."

Odu wiped his tears—though they were new, and strange, and would soon vanish forever.

We followed Eve to the cottage. There, she offered no bread, no wine—only radiant silence, urging us onward.

So, without word or farewell, we stepped outside.

The horse and elephants waited at the door.

But we were too joyful to mount. Instead, we walked—and they followed.
