It came to pass, two days following the events previously recounted, that Gerard, while traversing one of Rome's most sordid thoroughfares, was caught in the grip of a violent thunderstorm. Seeking shelter, he entered a humble tavern. Ordering a measure of wine, and with the deluge persisting without abatement, he soon succumbed to a stupor, dozing heavily with his head resting upon his folded arms and his elbows propped upon the rough wooden table.

As time wore on, the establishment grew crowded, and the raucous clamor of the coarse patrons roused him from his slumber. It was then that his attention was drawn to two figures seated nearby, engaged in hushed conversation. One was clearly a pardoner; the other, though dressed neatly and modestly enough to resemble a respectable tradesman, betrayed his true nature by the manner in which he had pulled his cap low over his brow, obscuring every feature save his beard. Such a gesture marked him as one who deliberately avoids the gaze of upright citizens and the scrutiny of the law. These two were negotiating a transaction within the black market of sin. Under a celestial contract common to that era, the crime for which forgiveness was sought had not yet been committed.

The man with the lowered cap grumbled about the exorbitant cost of pardons. "If prices rise further," he lamented, "we poor souls shall be barred from Heaven entirely." The pardoner dismissed this fear with a flat denial. "Indulgences have never been more affordable for prudent stewards of their coin," he retorted. His companion asked whom these bargains typically favored. "Why, those who sin openly, like rational beings," the pardoner replied. "But should you be so obstinate as to select a transgression the Pope has explicitly condemned, look to your own conscience, not mine!"

To demonstrate that sins of various magnitudes were accessible even to the lowest strata of society (save perhaps the absolute dregs), the pardoner unrolled a parchment and recited the fee schedule. It was a peculiar list, too dark for print in this volume, yet readily available in any grand library. To alter its contents would be to distort history. Suffice it to say that the price for the murder of a layman was far less than many offenses my readers might consider trivial. This fact sealed the deal; with minor concessions from both sides, the agreement was finalized. Money changed hands, and the aspiring soul was absolved in advance for the future killing of a civilian. Yet, the term "assassination" was never spoken aloud between them.

All this murmured business filled Gerard's ears, though he remained buried beneath his arms, listening with a vacant stare. Only when the conspirators rose and departed did he lift his head slightly, fixing a glare upon the retreating figure of the buyer.

"If Margaret lived," he muttered, his voice thick with drunken despair, "I would seize you by the throat and crush you, you cowardly murderer! But she is dead—dead, dead. Let the whole world perish; it matters nothing to me now. So let us all die together; I shall be among the first."

Upon returning home, Gerard found a man wearing a slouched hat pacing briskly back and forth on the opposite side of the street. "There goes that cur again," Gerard thought. Yet, in his shattered mental state, the sight left no mark upon his consciousness whatsoever.
