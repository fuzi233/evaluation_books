As the last wisps of dawn dissipated above the rolling hills of Flatland, the sun's ascent cast a warm, golden glow upon the landscape, like the gentle smile of a benevolent deity. In this tranquil scene, the once-vibrant tapestry of art and culture was beginning to fray, worn by the relentless march of progress. The revered Art of Sight Recognition, once the cornerstone of Flatland's esteemed University, now lay dormant, its practitioners dismissed as relics of a bygone era. The studies of Geometry, Statics, and Kinetics, those foundational disciplines that had once defined the very fabric of society, were similarly neglected, their relevance lost on a world that had grown enamored with the mystical allure of Colour.

In the Elementary Schools, the inferior Art of Feeling was next to fall, its decline hastened by the Isosceles classes, who, emboldened by their newfound equality with the Polygons, refused to contribute to the education system, citing the outdated nature of these "monopolizing and aristocratic Arts." As the years passed, the Soldiers and Artisans, now raised to an equality with the Polygons, grew increasingly vocal in their demands for an end to the privileged status of Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling. They argued that Colour, this "second Nature," had rendered all distinctions obsolete, and that the law should reflect this new reality, recognizing all individuals and classes as equal and entitled to equal rights.

The Revolution's leaders, a cabal of cunning and ambitious figures, sensed an opportunity to consolidate their power and advanced their demands. They proposed a Universal Colour Bill, which would assign distinct colours to each class, with Priests and Women to be painted red and green, respectively. The plan was deceptively clever, born not from the mind of an Isosceles, but from an Irregular Circle, a master strategist who had managed to evade the usual fate of his kind.

On one hand, the proposal would win the support of the Women, who would be granted the same colours as the Priests, ensuring that in certain positions, they would appear indistinguishable from their male counterparts, and thus deserving of the same respect and deference. But the plan's true intention was more insidious, a subtle ploy to blur the lines between classes and create a society where the distinction between Priest and Woman was all but erased.
