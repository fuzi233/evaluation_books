The horror that would come to be known as the Dunwich terror began its final descent in the waning days of summer, 1928. Dr. Armitage, who had long feared such an event, found himself among the first to witness its grotesque overture.

Word had reached him of Wilbur Whateley’s desperate pilgrimage to Cambridge—his frenzied attempts to obtain the forbidden *Necronomicon* from the depths of Widener Library. Armitage had taken precautions, issuing stern warnings to all custodians of that accursed tome. Wilbur, it was said, had been a pitiful sight—his agitation palpable, torn between his hunger for the book and a frantic need to return home, as if some unseen clock were ticking toward catastrophe.

Then, in the dead of night on August 3rd, the inevitable came to pass.

Armitage was roused from sleep by the savage, unearthly howls of the college watchdog—a deep, guttural snarling that rose and fell in unnatural waves. And then—another sound. A scream. Not human, not animal, but something *other*, something that clawed at the mind and sent the sleeping citizens of Arkham bolting upright in their beds, their hearts hammering with a dread they could not name.

Armitage threw on his clothes and ran.

The campus was already stirring. The shrill wail of a burglar alarm pierced the night, and above it, the fading echoes of that terrible scream. A black, gaping maw of a window stood open in the moonlight. Whatever had entered was now inside. The dog’s barks had dissolved into a low, shuddering whine, thick with something like fear.

Armitage knew—*knew*—that whatever lay within was not meant for mortal eyes. He pushed back the gathering crowd with a sharp command, unlocked the vestibule door, and motioned for two colleagues—Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan—to follow.

The stench hit them like a physical blow—a reek of corruption and something worse, something *alien*. The dog’s whine had steadied into a watchful drone, but outside, the whippoorwills had begun their infernal chorus, their piping synchronized like a dirge for the dying.

The three men moved toward the genealogical reading room, the source of the sound. No one dared touch the light at first. Then Armitage, steeling himself, flipped the switch.

One of them screamed.

Professor Rice would later claim he had blacked out for an instant, though his legs never buckled. The thing sprawled before them defied reason. Nearly nine feet in length, it lay twisted in a pool of viscous, greenish-yellow slime, its clothing—and patches of its skin—torn away by the dog’s jaws. It was not yet dead. Its chest heaved in time with the whippoorwills’ chant, its limbs twitching in silent spasms.

Fragments of clothing and leather littered the floor. A revolver lay near the desk, unfired. An empty canvas sack had been tossed by the window.

But none of that mattered.

The *thing* commanded all attention.

To say it was indescribable would be a lie—but to describe it was to invite madness. Its upper half bore a ghastly mockery of humanity: manlike hands, a head with the unmistakable Whateley features—goatish and chinless. But below the waist, it was an abomination.

Its torso was a grotesque patchwork—leathery, scaled hide like some primordial reptile, furred in coarse black strands. From its abdomen hung a writhing mass of tentacles, their mouths glistening red and wet. Its hips bore pinkish, ciliated sockets—rudimentary eyes. And where a tail should have been, a pulsating appendage, ringed in purple, twitched obscenely.

Its breath came in ragged gasps, its flesh shifting colors unnaturally—greens deepening, sickly yellows pulsing beneath the surface. No blood flowed—only that foul ichor, seeping across the floor.

Then, as if sensing their presence, it began to mumble.

No words of this earth. At first, just sounds—guttural, impossible. Then, near the end, broken fragments of the *Necronomicon*’s black liturgy.

The words dissolved into silence as the whippoorwills’ shrieks reached a fever pitch.

Then—a final, shuddering breath.

The dog howled.

The thing’s face twisted, its great black eyes sinking inward. Outside, the whippoorwills fell abruptly silent. A great rustling filled the air as unseen things took flight, fleeing in terror from what they had sought to feast upon.

The dog, suddenly panicked, bolted through the window.

Armitage shouted for the doors to be barred. He drew the curtains tight, sealing away the horror within.

By the time the medical examiner arrived, there was little left to see.

Only a whitish, viscous smear on the floorboards.

And the lingering certainty that Wilbur Whateley had never been human.

Not truly.

Not in the ways that mattered.
