The maternal grandfather of our Virginians, Colonel Esmond, had spent a significant portion of his long retirement in the New World composing memoirs of his early life. In these volumes, Madame de Bernstein—then known as Mrs. Beatrice Esmond—played a considerable role. George Warrington, having read his grandfather's manuscript countless times, knew his kinswoman intimately long before he ever laid eyes upon her. He knew the lady of half a century prior: young, beautiful, and wilful. Now, he observed her in the decline of her days. When cheeks fade and eyes grow dim, is it sorrow or delight for a woman who is no longer a beauty to recall the bloom of her youth? When the heart withers, does it not ache to remember how it once beat with warmth? When spirits grow languid, do we not long for the buoyant hope and eager enjoyment of other days? Thus fall the buds of prime, the roses of beauty, the florid harvests of summer—leaving only naked branches to shiver in the winter.

"And that was a beauty once!" George thought as his aunt, adorned in rouge and diamonds, entered from her evening rout. "And that ruin was once a splendid palace. Crowds of lovers have sighed before those decrepit feet and been bewildered by the brightness of those eyes." His mind drifted to a firework display at Williamsburg on the King's birthday, recalling the skeleton-wheel and the sockets of exploded Roman candles. The dazzling brilliance of Aunt Beatrice's early career flashed before him as he reflected on his grandsire's journals. Honest Harry had witnessed those times too, yet Harry, not being a scholar, had not scrutinized the manuscript with such intensity. Moreover, unlike his melancholy senior, Harry possessed little inclination for moralizing.

Mr. Warrington deemed it unnecessary to inform his aunt of his intimate knowledge of her early history and thus remained silent. After their meal, she pointed her cane toward her escritoire, instructing her attendant to retrieve a letter lying beneath the inkstand. George recognized the superscription immediately; it was the correspondence he had carried from home.

"It appears by this letter," the old lady remarked, fixing a sharp gaze upon her nephew, "that since your return, differences have arisen between you and my sister."

"Indeed?" George replied. "I was unaware that Madam Esmond had alluded to them."

The Baroness adjusted a large pair of spectacles over eyes that once kindled passions unknown, glanced over the letter, and handed it to George. He read aloud:

"RICHMOND, VIRGINIA, December 26th, 1756.

"HONOURED MADAM! AND SISTER!—I acknowledge receipt of your ladyship's favor via the Rose packet of October 23rd. I answer at a season meant for goodwill and peace, yet one wherein Heaven decrees we must still bear our share of earthly sorrow. My reply shall be delivered by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us miraculously from the Valley of the Shadow of Death—as our previous letters informed my poor Henry—and who desires, with my consent, to visit Europe, though he has been among us but briefly.

"I grieve to think that my dearest Harry appeared at home—in England—under false pretenses, presented to His Majesty, our family, and his own as his father's heir, while my dear son George was still alive, though dead to us. Ah, madam! During the eighteen months of his captivity, what anguish his mother's and brother's hearts endured! My Harry's is the tenderest of any man's now living. In the joy of seeing Mr. Esmond Warrington restored to life, he will forget the worldly misfortune that befalls him. He will return to comparative poverty without a pang. The most generous and obedient of human beings, of sons, he will gladly yield to his elder brother that inheritance which was his own but for the accident of birth and the providential return of my son George.

"Your beneficent intentions toward dearest Harry will be more than ever welcome, now he is reduced to a younger brother's slender portion! Many years ago, an advantageous opportunity arose to provide for him in this province; by now, he would have owned a noble estate and negroes, capable of making a figure here, had my wishes been complied with and his father's small portion, currently yielding little interest in British funds, been invested in this excellent purchase. But legal formalities and, I grieve to admit, my elder son's scruples prevailed, causing me to lose this admirable opportunity. Harry will find his savings carefully accumulated—may he live long to enjoy them! May Heaven bless you, dear sister, for whatever you may add to his modest store! As I gather from your letter that the sum allowed him has proven insufficient for his expenses in fine company—and the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, one who nearly succeeded his lordship, surely claims equality with any nobleman in Great Britain—I entrust a sum I had always intended for the poor child's establishment to my eldest son. To do him justice, he possesses a most sincere regard for his brother, and I charge him to invest it for Harry's best advantage."

"It took him out of prison yesterday, madam," George interjected at this juncture. "I believe that was the best use to which we could put it."

"Nay, sir, I know nothing of the sort! Why not keep it to buy a pair of colors for him, or assist toward another estate and some negroes if he has a fancy for home?" cried the old lady. "Besides, I had a fancy to pay that debt myself."

"I hope you will allow his brother to handle that. I request leave to act as my brother's banker in this matter, considering I have borrowed so much from my mother, to be repaid to my dear Harry."

"Do you say so, sir? Give me a glass of wine! You are an extravagant fellow! Read on; you will see your mother thinks so. I drink to your health, nephew George! 'Tis good Burgundy. Your grandfather never loved Burgundy; he preferred claret, the little he drank."

George continued reading:

"This remittance will, I trust, amply cover any expenses incurred by dearest Harry owing to the mistake regarding his position. I wish I could trust his elder brother's prudence as confidently as I trust my Harry's! Yet I fear that even in captivity, Mr. Esmond W. has learned little of the humility becoming all Christians, which I have ever endeavored to teach my children. Should you chance to show him these lines when, by Heaven's blessing on those who go down to the sea in ships, the Great Ocean divides us, he will know that a fond mother's blessing and prayers follow both her children. There is no act I have ever done, no desire I have ever expressed—however little he may have been inclined to obey it—but hath been dictated by the fondest wishes for my dearest boys' welfare."

"There is a scratch with a penknife and a great blot upon the letter, as if water had fallen on it," George's aunt observed kindly. "Your mother writes well, George. I suppose you and she had a difference?"

"Yes, ma'am, many," the young man answered sadly. "The last concerned a question of money—a ransom I promised to the old lieutenant of the fort who aided my escape. I told you he had a mistress, a poor Indian woman who helped me and was kind to me. Six weeks after my arrival home, the poor thing appeared at Richmond, having found her way through the woods by much the same track I followed, bringing me the token Museau promised to send when he connived at my flight. A commanding officer and considerable reinforcement had arrived at Duquesne. Charges of peculation, though his messenger could not express herself clearly, had been brought against Museau. He was arrested, attempted escape, but, less fortunate than myself, was shot on the rampart. He sent the Indian woman to me with my grandfather's watch and a line scrawled in his prison on his deathbed, begging me to send *ce que je scavais* to a notary at Havre de Grace in France to be transmitted to his relatives at Caen in Normandy. My friend Silverheels, the hunter, assisted my poor Indian companion on her journey. I know not how she would have escaped scalping otherwise. But at home, they received her sternly, hardly granting her a welcome. I won't detail the suspicions they harbored regarding her and me. The poor wretch fell to drinking whenever she could find means. I ordered food and shelter, yet she became the jest of our negroes and the subject of scandal and gossip among the old fools in our little town. Our Governor, fortunately, was a man of sense; I made interest with him and procured a pass to send her back to her people. Her grief at parting with me only confirmed the suspicions against her. A fellow preached against me from the pulpit, I believe; I had to treat another with a cane. And I had a violent dispute with Madam Esmond—a difference not yet healed—because I insisted on paying the heirs Museau designated the money I had promised for my deliverance. You see that scandal flourishes at the borders of the wilderness and in the New World as well as the Old."

"I have suffered from it myself, my dear!" said Madame Bernstein demurely. "Fill thy glass, child! A little dash of cherry-brandy! 'Twill do thee all the good in the world."

"As for my poor Harry's marriage," the letter continued, "though I know too well, from sad experience, the dangers to which youth is subject, and would keep my boy, at any price, from them, though I should wish him to marry a person of rank as becomes his birth, yet my Lady Maria Esmond is out of the question. Her age is almost the same as mine; and I know my brother Castlewood left his daughters with the very smallest portions. My Harry is so obedient that I know a desire from me will suffice to cause him to give up this imprudent match. Some foolish people once supposed that I myself once considered a second union with a person of rank very different from ours. No! I knew what was due to my children. As succeeding to this estate after me, Mr. Esmond W. is amply provided for. Let my task now be to save for his less fortunate younger brother: and, as I do not love to live quite alone, let him return without delay to his fond and loving mother.

"The report your ladyship gave of my Harry fills my heart with warmest gratitude. He is all indeed a mother may wish. A year in Europe will have given him a polish and refinement he could not acquire in our homely Virginia. Mr. Stack, one of our invaluable ministers in Richmond, has a letter from Mr. Ward—my darling's tutor of early days—who knows my Lady Warrington and her excellent family, and says that my Harry has lived much with his cousins of late. I am grateful to think that my boy has the privilege of being with his good aunt. May he follow her counsels and listen to those around him who will guide him on the way of his best welfare! Adieu, dear madam and sister! For your kindness to my boy, accept the grateful thanks of a mother's heart. Though we have been divided hitherto, may these kindly ties draw us nearer and nearer. I am thankful that you should speak of my dearest father so. He was, indeed, one of the best of men! He, too, thanks you, I know, for the love you have borne to one of his children; and his daughter subscribes herself—with sincere thanks, your ladyship's most dutiful and grateful sister and servant, RACHEL ESMOND WN.

"P.S.—I have communicated with my Lady Maria; but there will be no need to tell her and dear Harry that his mother or your ladyship hope to be able to increase his small fortune. The match is altogether unsuitable."

"As far as regards myself, madam," George said, laying down the paper, "my mother's letter conveys no news to me. I always knew that Harry was the favorite son with Madam Esmond, as he deserves indeed to be. He has a hundred good qualities which I have not the good fortune to possess. He has better looks—"

"Nay, that is not your fault," the old lady said slyly, looking at him. "And, but that he is fair and you are brown, one might almost pass for the other."

Mr. George bowed, and a faint blush tinged his pale cheek.

"His disposition is bright, and mine is dark," he continued. "Harry is cheerful, and I am otherwise, perhaps. He knows how to make himself beloved by everyone, and it has been my lot to find but few friends."

"My sister and you have pretty little quarrels. There were such in old days in our family," the Baroness said. "And if Madam Esmond takes after our mother—"

"My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth," George interjected.

"Eh! That is a common character for people when they are dead!" cried the Baroness. "And Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like—at least your grandfather thought so. But let me tell you, sir, that angels are sometimes not very commodious to live with. It may be they are too good to live with us sinners, and the air down below here doesn't agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she never could forgive me for being otherwise. Ah, mon Dieu! how she used to oppress me with those angelical airs!"

George cast down his eyes, thinking of his own melancholy youth. He did not care to submit more of his family secrets to the cynical inquisition of this old worldling, who seemed, however, to understand him despite his reticence.

"I quite comprehend you, sir, though you hold your tongue," the Baroness continued. "A sermon in the morning; a sermon at night; and two or three on a Sunday. That is what people call being good. Every pleasure cried fie upon; all us worldly people excommunicated; a ball an abomination of desolation; a play a forbidden pastime; and a game of cards perdition! What a life! Mon Dieu, what a life!"
