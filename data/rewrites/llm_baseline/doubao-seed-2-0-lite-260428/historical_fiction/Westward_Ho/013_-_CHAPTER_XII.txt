No one who has ever wandered the lanes of Bideford can miss its famous stone bridge: it is the beating heart, the very anchor of the town, just as Edinburgh is defined by its castle, Rome by its Capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids. Bideford is not truly Bideford without that long, weathered span arching across the Torridge. But few outsiders know the strange, old magic that has moved and guarded the bridge for five hundred years. According to local legends recorded by Prince and Fuller, it is the greatest wonder of all Devon: first a bridge that won souls, fed the poor, paid for schoolmasters, seemed almost alive, and last — far from least — threw legendary annual feasts.

Few know that when builders first tried to lay the bridge foundations half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried every stone down the river each night, until the parish parson, Sir Richard Gurney, went to bed distraught certain a devil was haunting his sheepfold, and saw an angel in a vision. The angel told him to build the bridge exactly where the stones had landed: that was the only spot in miles with solid ground beneath the wide, shifting sands of the ford. Few know that Bishop Grandison of Exeter issued proclamations across his whole diocese promising indulgences, blessings, and a share in all eternal spiritual rewards to anyone who gave money to build the crossing, so travellers might save their souls and their bodies at once, and make the best of both worlds.

Even the local folk can’t all tell you that though the bridge’s foundations are said to rest on bales of wool, it shakes at the lightest step of a trotting horse; or that a man named William Alford, a modern Milo, once wagered he could carry four bushels of salt all the way across its twenty-three arches, and won. It is a legal esquire, no less, with its own official coat of arms: a ship and a bridge on a plain silver field, and it owns lands and cottages across half a dozen local parishes. Over centuries that miraculous bridge founded charities, built schoolrooms, fought lawsuits in the courts, and saved for its famous yearly dinners the best stocked wine cellar in all of Devon.

It was to one of those 1583 feasts that every notable citizen of Bideford was invited, plus Mr. St. Leger of nearby Annery House — brother to the Marshal of Munster and to Lady Grenville, a famously generous, hospitable gentleman who gave away so much of his wealth to neighbours during his life that he spared his children all risk of the corrupting temptations that come with excess riches.

Halfway through the dinner, a heated argument broke out between St. Leger and one of the bridge trustees: was a salmon caught below the bridge in the river better or worse than one caught upstream? The only way to settle such a weighty question was with a practical test, St. Leger declared. He vowed that since the bridge had treated him to an excellent dinner, he would return the favour. He bet five pounds that he could pull a salmon out of the pool below Annery that was just as firm, just as flaky, as the Appledore salmon they had just finished eating — and on the strength of that joyful impulse, he invited every single guest at the table to come dine at Annery House three days later, each bringing a wife or daughter along. Don Guzman, the Spanish nobleman staying in town, got an invitation too.

The feast in Annery’s great hall was one the house had not seen since old Judge Hankford hosted King Edward IV there generations before. Plates were piled high, wine flowed free, and all the while, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman pretended not to see each other across the tables, even as they watched each other more closely than they had watched anyone else all night. Rose had good reason to be careful with her glances: her father sat right beside her, and directly across from her were William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, two lieutenants in the Queen’s Irish army who had only just come home on furlough a few days prior.

Rose and the Spaniard had not exchanged a single word in six months, though they had crossed paths dozens of times. The Don never avoided her in public, save for stepping clear of her father’s house: he only followed the unspoken rule she had set when she spurned him, greeting her with the most formal, distant courtesy when he arrived or departed, and acting as if he did not notice her at all the rest of the time. But he took every chance to shine in her presence: wittier, more eloquent, full of wilder, more romantic tales of far-off lands than he had ever been before. His first tactic of open wooing had failed, so he was trying a far more dangerous one. He would punish her first for daring to refuse a Spanish nobleman, and what better punishment than to withdraw the honour of his attentions entirely? It was both conceited and clever: the Don knew better than most that people, women especially, will value a man exactly as highly as he values himself, and that the more dignity he claimed for his own worth, the more eager others would be to grant it.

He would not make the crude, obvious mistake of flirting with another woman to make her jealous — that would only spark rage, or disgust, or hopelessness, and never make her turn to him. Instead, he would quietly, unobtrusively prove he could live perfectly well without her. Rose, spoiled as she was by months of admirers fawning at her feet, found herself soon wondering exactly what made him so self-sufficient, when she could no longer pretend she was unaffected by him. Did he harbour some great secret ambition? What a thrill it would be to share it. Did he hold some hidden, rare knowledge? What a joy to learn it. Did he love deeper than any ordinary English gentleman she knew? It would be heaven to be the only one to claim that love. Step by step, she found herself drifting toward the man she had once turned away, ready to beg his favour as he had once begged hers. That temptation, to hold a private, secret treasure no other woman in the world could access, to be the only initiate of his hidden life, was fast wearing down all her resolve. The Spaniard knew well that women by nature far more often crave to worship than to be worshipped, to surrender than to rule — that all the coy, sharp scorn they hide behind is just a mask they soon tire of wearing themselves.

That day at the Annery feast, Rose was utterly tired of her mask of cold indifference. Don Guzman saw it in her downcast, restless face, and thinking he had punished her long enough, began stealing soft glances her way across the table. He did not look away when their eyes met and she dropped her gaze, cheeks flushing bright, and he noticed her growing quieter, more distracted by the minute. At last, he caught her peeking up at him to see if he was looking at her, and he knew his prize was his. He asked her, only with his eyes, *Do you forgive me?* She froze mid-conversation with the lady next to her, stammered, dropped her gaze, then lifted it again a minute later to meet his, answering as clear as day with every line of her soft face and every tilt of her neck, *And do you forgive me too?*

At once Don Guzman burst into giddy, sparkling good humour, churning out tales, jests, and sharp repartee until he was the undisputed soul of the whole party, the most charming gentleman in the room. Rose knew she was the cause of his sudden change of mood, and she berated herself for her weakness, even as her chest flooded with a giddy, shameful delight. She longed for the feast to end so she could hurry home, lock herself away, and indulge in all the soft, impossible daydreams she dared not face as reality.

That midsummer afternoon, the great stone terrace of Annery was a perfect sight: fine ladies in their bright, embroidered gowns strolled up and down in pairs and trios before the grand old house, or leaned over the balustrade to look out at the park below — the ancient gnarled oaks, the herds of grazing deer, and the wide, still, landlocked stretch of the Torridge spreading like a lake far beneath them, glinting gold in the hot sun. They chattered and laughed, compared dress trims, gossiped about neighbours’ husbands and maidservants, while the two ladies of the house — Mrs. St. Leger the hostess, and her sister-in-law, the fair Lady Grenville — presided graciously over the group. Only Rose kept apart, hovering at the edge of the group, half wanting to laugh and half wanting to cry, she could not tell which.

“Our pretty Rose looks sad,” Lady Grenville said, stepping up beside her. “Cheer up, child! We’re all waiting for you to come play for us.”

Rose mumbled some reply she barely heard, and followed her mechanically. She picked up a lute sitting on a stone bench beneath the house, and the rest of the ladies gathered around her.

“What should I sing?”
“Give us your favourite old ballad, *Earl Haldan’s Daughter*.”

Rose shrank back. It was a loud, rousing, swashbuckling song that did not fit her mood at all — and Frank had praised it, once, in the happy, careless days long past. The song felt suddenly like a bad omen, but she dared not refuse for fear of drawing unwanted attention, so she began to sing, forced:
It was Earl Haldan's daughter,
She look'd across the sea;
She look'd across the water,
And long and loud laugh'd she;
'The locks of six princesses
Must be my marriage-fee,
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Who comes a wooing me?'

It was Earl Haldan's daughter,
She walk'd along the sand;
When she was aware of a knight so fair,
Come sailing to the land.
His sails were all of velvet,
His mast of beaten gold,
And 'hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat,
Who saileth here so bold?'

'The locks of five princesses
I won beyond the sea;
I shore their golden tresses,
To fringe a cloak for thee.
One handful yet is wanting,
But one of all the tale;
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Furl up thy velvet sail!'

'He leapt into the water,
That rover young and bold;
He gript Earl Haldan's daughter,
He shore her locks of gold;
'Go weep, go weep, proud maiden,
The tale is full to-day.
Now hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Sail Westward-ho, and away!'

As the last note faded, a measured voice with a soft foreign accent thrilled through her.
“In the East, they say the nightingale sings to the rose; Devon is luckier still, for it has the nightingale and the rose wrapped up in one lady.”

“We have no nightingales in Devon, Don Guzman,” Lady Grenville laughed. “But our little forest thrushes sing as sweetly as you hear, enough to delight any ear. What brings you away from the gentlemen inside so early?”
“These letters, that were just handed to me,” he said. “They call me home to Spain, and I could not bear to waste a single minute of the lovely company I must soon leave behind.”

“To Spain?” half a dozen voices exclaimed at once — the Don was a favourite with every soul in Bideford.
“Yes, and from there on to the Indies. My ransom has finally arrived, and with it a new post. I have been named Governor of La Guayra, in Caracas. Congratulate me on my promotion.”

A mist clouded over Rose’s eyes. The Spaniard’s voice sounded sharp, flippant. Did he truly care for her at all? Even if he did, was it all utterly hopeless? Her cheeks burned bright enough, she was sure, that everyone could see her distress. Desperate to draw attention away from herself, she blurted out the first foolish question that popped into her head.
“Where is La Guayra?”

“Halfway round the world, on the coast of the Spanish Main. It is the loveliest spot on earth, and the governor’s house stands in a palm forest at the foot of a mountain eight thousand feet high. I would only need a wife there to live in paradise.”

“I have no doubt you will persuade some fair Seville lady to come with you,” Lady Grenville said.
“Thank you, gracious madam. But the truth is, since I have had the joy of knowing English ladies, I have come to think they are the only women on earth worth wooing.”
“What a kind compliment! But I fear none of our free English maidens would ever submit to being chaperoned everywhere by a duenna. Eh, Rose? How would you like to be locked up all day by an ugly old woman with a horn on her forehead?”

Rose blushed so scarlet that Lady Grenville saw her secret in an instant, and would have turned the conversation away — but before she could speak, a merchant’s wife blundered in making a commonplace joke about how jealous Spanish husbands were, and another tittered out a coarse, ill-judged remark about the West Indies planters and their fair slaves.

“Ladies,” Don Guzman said, flushing with indignation, “believe me, all of that is nothing but ignorant slander. If we Spaniards are more jealous than other peoples, it is only because we love far more passionately. If some of our men abroad act recklessly, it is only because they have no wives at their sides — women who keep them pure, as the amethyst stone keeps the man who wears it sober and true. I could tell you tales of Spanish husbands in the Indies as loyal and constant as any story a romance writer ever dreamed of.”

“Tell us one then!” the ladies cried at once.
“I fear it would be far too long, madam.”
“The longer the better, senor. What better way to spend an hour, while the gentlemen inside finish their wine?”

Storytelling was a beloved pastime in those days, when books were far rarer than they are now, and everyone knew the Don was a master spinner of tales. All the ladies crowded around him, servants dragged out chairs and benches, and Don Guzman sat himself down at Lady Grenville’s feet, and began to speak, with quiet, proud humility.

“Fair, noble ladies, you have no doubt heard of Sebastian Cabot, who sailed forth forty-five years ago with a commission from my old master, the Emperor Charles the Fifth, to find the legendary golden lands of Tarshish, Ophir, and Cipango. When his ships ran short of provisions, he turned back at the mouth of the great South American river he named Rio de la Plata, sailed upstream a ways, and discovered the fair land of Paraguay. But few of you know that at the mouth of the Rio Terceiro, he built a fort that men still call Cabot’s Tower — and no one tells the strange, tragic tale that has made that tower a sacred spot for every true lover in Spain.

“When Cabot sailed home to Spain the next year, he left a garrison of one hundred and twenty men under the command of Nuno de Lara, Ruiz Moschera, and Sebastian da Hurtado — old friends and fellow soldiers of my invincible grandfather Don Ferdinando da Soto. With them was a treasure more precious than any gold Spain had ever owned: Lucia Miranda, Hurtado’s wife, famous across the Emperor’s court for her wisdom, modesty, and unrivalled beauty. She gave up all the pomp and luxury of palace life to marry a poor adventurer, and sailed across the world with him to face every hardship of the new lands. Mangora, the cacique of the local Timbuez Indians, who had struck up a false friendship with de Lara, saw her once, and lusted after her the moment he laid eyes on her. He plotted with all the savage cunning he had to seize her by force, for he knew he would never win her consent.

“Miranda saw his passion quickly enough, told her husband, and kept far out of his sight. Mangora kept pressing Hurtado to come visit his village, and bring his wife along with him. Hurtado, suspecting a trap but afraid to offend the cacique, politely excused himself on the grounds of his soldier’s duties. Mangora, mad with frustrated desire, began plotting to kill Hurtado.

“Weeks went by, until food ran scarce at the tower, and Hurtado and Ruiz Moschera took fifty soldiers with them up the river on a foraging mission. Mangora saw his chance, and struck at once.

“Ladies, I have spoken to men who saw Cabot’s Tower with their own eyes: it stands on a knoll where the two rivers meet, and a wide, dreary marsh full of tall grass and thick bushes stretches inland from its walls. The perfect hiding spot for four thousand Indian warriors. Mangora left his whole army hidden in that marsh, and walked boldly up to the tower with thirty of his own men, loaded down with sacks of grain, fruit, game, and every delicacy his forests could provide.

“Smiling all the while, he told de Lara how sorry he was that the Spaniards were going hungry, and begged him to accept the food he had brought. As he expected, de Lara invited him inside to share a cask of Spanish wine.

“Mangora and his thirty bandits walked right into the tower, and the feast of songs and drinking lasted far into the night. Mangora looked around for Miranda again and again, and finally had the gall to demand she be brought out — but she had locked herself in her chamber, claiming she was ill.

“It was a useless plea. No sooner had the drunken, unwary Spaniards fallen asleep, leaving Mangora and his men still inside the walls, than the garrison woke to the screams of burning thatch, the explosion of their gunpowder magazine, and the roar of four thousand warriors pouring in from the marsh.

“I will not sicken your gentle ears with the gory details of the slaughter. In a few terrible minutes, every unarmed, bewildered Spaniard was dead. The only survivors were Miranda, and four other women with their small infants, all bound and led away in triumph through the forest toward the Indian village.
