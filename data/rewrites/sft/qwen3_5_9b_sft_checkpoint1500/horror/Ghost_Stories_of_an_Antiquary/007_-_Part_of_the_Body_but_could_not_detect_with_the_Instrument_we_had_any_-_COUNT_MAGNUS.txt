The documents from which I have woven this narrative arrived in my possession through obscure channels, a mystery I shall not unravel here. However, to contextualize the fragments that follow, I must first establish the nature of their origin. They comprise, in part, a series of journals intended for a travelogue—a genre popular in the mid-nineteenth century, akin to Horace Marryat's *Journal of a Residence in Jutland*. Typically, such volumes chronicled expeditions to remote Continental regions, adorned with woodcuts, offering practical advice on accommodations, and featuring dialogues with locals. They were, above all, chatty.

Yet, as these papers evolved, they shed their intended format to become a singular, continuous record of personal experience, maintained until the eve of its conclusion. The author was Mr. Wraxall. Based solely on his writings, I infer he was a man past middle age, financially secure but profoundly isolated. He possessed no fixed home in England, drifting instead between hotels and boarding houses, haunted by a dream of settlement that never materialized. A fire at the Pantechnicon in the early seventies likely consumed vital records of his past, as he mentions property stored there. He had previously published an anonymous or pseudonymous account of a holiday in Brittany, but his current obsession was a new project concerning Scandinavia.

Struck by the historical potential of the region, particularly the great Swedish families, Wraxall sought letters of introduction and departed for Sweden in the summer of 1863. While his time in Stockholm was brief, a local scholar directed him to the archives of the proprietors of an ancient manor, Råbäck (pronounced roughly "Roebeck"), in Vestergötland. This estate, built around 1600 by a branch of the De la Gardie house, stands as a red-brick and stone monument, indistinguishable from its Dutch or German counterparts of the era. Though the De la Gardies treated him kindly, preferring independence, Wraxall lodged at a nearby village inn, requiring a daily trek of less than a mile to the manor.

The landscape surrounding Råbäck is defined by a park of mature timber, a walled garden, and a fringing woodland bordering a small lake. Beyond lies the demesne wall, leading to a steep knoll crowned by a peculiar church. To the English eye, it is stark: low nave and aisles, galleries, and a western organ painted gaily with silver pipes. Its ceiling bears a seventeenth-century "Last Judgment" of lurid flames, burning cities, and grinning demons. Distinctive, however, is an addition at the eastern end of the north aisle: a mausoleum erected by the builder for himself and his kin. An octagonal structure with oval windows, a domed copper roof painted black, and a pumpkin-like spire, it sits starkly white against the gloom, accessible only via its own northern portal.

Upon arriving at Råbäck, Wraxall explored the church, noting the grandeur of the interior but failing to enter the mausoleum. Through the keyhole, he glimpsed fine marble effigies, copper sarcophagi, and rich armorial bearings, fueling his desire to investigate. The family papers he examined confirmed his hopes, revealing a history of the De la Gardies marked by strength and severity. The first Count Magnus appeared as a formidable leader who suppressed peasant uprisings with brutal efficiency, executing ring-leaders and inflicting harsh punishments. A portrait of Count Magnus hung in the hall, leaving a profound impression not on Wraxall's aesthetic sensibilities, but on his sense of power; the count was described as phenomenally ugly.

During his first evening, Wraxall walked back from the manor in the twilight and noted the sexton standing on the steps of the mausoleum. The following day, he questioned the innkeeper regarding local legends. The landlord, evasive and reluctant, hinted at the "Black Pilgrimage," suggesting Count Magnus had returned from it with something or someone. When pressed, the landlord recounted a chilling tale from ninety-two years prior: two men ventured into the forbidden woods behind Råbäck for a "free hunt." Instead of silence, they heard a scream that twisted the soul, followed by a laugh that was not human, and finally, the sound of a heavy door shutting. By dawn, the men were found dead—or so they believed. One, Hans Thorbjørn, pushed at invisible obstacles until he collapsed and died shortly after; the other, Anders Björnsson, was a beautiful man whose face had been stripped from his skull, leaving the bone exposed. As the funeral procession sang the psalm, Anders's eyes opened wide beneath the bier's covering, causing the mourners to flee and hastily bury him.

Driven by this lore, Wraxall visited the sexton, who confirmed the existence of the "Black Pilgrimage" and mentioned Chorazin, a ruined village where, according to local superstition, the Antichrist was destined to be born. Intrigued, Wraxall delved deeper into the estate's library. Among accounts of the first Count Magnus, he discovered a sixteenth-century tract containing a fragment titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis* ("Book of the Black Pilgrimage"). The Latin text spoke of obtaining a faithful messenger and seeing the blood of enemies by visiting the City of Chorazin and saluting a prince "of the air."

Fascinated and unsettled, Wraxall found himself returning to the churchyard in a trance-like state, muttering, "Are you awake, Count Magnus?" He located the key to the mausoleum hanging near the pulpit and entered. Inside, the central chamber held three copper sarcophagi. Two bore large crucifixes; the third, belonging to Count Magnus, featured an elaborate engraving depicting a battle, an execution, and a chase scene. In the latter, a man ran with outstretched hands, pursued by a strange, monstrous form that defied anatomical logic, resembling a devil-fish's tentacle rather than an arm. Wraxall interpreted this as an allegory for a fiend hunting a soul. He noted that one of the three massive steel padlocks securing the sarcophagus had been detached and lay on the floor.

Unable to resist, Wraxall removed the two loose locks, attempting vainly to replace them. He refrained from opening the lid, fearing the consequences, though he admitted his fascination with the "ferocious and grim" noble. That night, as he prepared to leave, a final, terrifying event transpired. Standing over the sarcophagus, he felt a blow on his foot and heard a clatter as the third padlock fell. Before he could fully rise, the sound of creaking metal echoed, and the lid shifted upward. Terrified, he fled the building, unable even to turn the key back into the lock. Whether the noise continued or if he saw something more, he could not recall.

Wraxall departed Råbäck the next day, appearing physically safe but mentally shattered. His journal entries became disjointed, filled with frantic observations of strangers on canal boats—a pastor, a commercial traveler, and a recurring figure in a long black cloak and broad hat. Upon landing at Harwich, he resolved to escape his tormentors. Taking a closed fly, he raced across the countryside to the village of Belchamp St Paul on a moonlit August night. At a crossroads, two figures in dark cloaks—one wearing a hat, the other a hood—stood motionless. The horse shied violently, and Wraxall, sinking back in despair, realized he had seen them before.

At his lodging in Belchamp St Paul, Wraxall wrote his final, fragmented notes, plagued by the question, "What has he done?" He awaited visitors who never specified, locked his door, and cried out to God, knowing that doctors would deem him mad. Local memory recalled a strange gentleman who arrived in August years prior and was found dead the following morning. The jury at the inquest fainted, refusing to speak, and the verdict was "visitation of God." The villagers subsequently abandoned the area. Years later, the house came into my hands as part of a legacy. Having stood empty since 1863, I demolished it, discovering the aforementioned papers hidden in a forgotten cupboard beneath the window of the master bedroom.
