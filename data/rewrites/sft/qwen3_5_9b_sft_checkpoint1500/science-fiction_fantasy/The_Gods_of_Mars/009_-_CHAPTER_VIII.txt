The realization struck me with the clarity of a sudden dawn: the black pirate captain had held me captive not merely to recount a tale, but because he had anticipated salvation. His gaze, fleeting yet piercing, betrayed the imminent arrival of aid. Had that single look remained unspoken, the hovering battleship above us would have launched its boarding party moments ago, swarming our deck like locusts and extinguishing any flicker of hope I harbored for escape.

Yet, experience in aerial combat served me well. With practiced ease, I reversed the engines and dove the vessel in a sheer plummet of a hundred feet, dropping beneath the shadow of the giant ship. Above, I watched the dangling figures of the marines swing from the hull as the battleship raced overhead. Seizing the opening, I surged upward at a sharp angle, pushing the throttle to its limit. Like a projectile fired from a crossbow, my sleek craft shot its steel nose straight toward the whirring propellers of the behemoth above. A mere brush would disable the machine for hours, buying me time.

Just as I aimed, the sun breached the horizon, revealing a hundred grim, black visages peering over the stern of the enemy vessel. A roar of fury erupted from their throats as they spotted us. Their shouts for action came too late to avoid the collision. With a thunderous crash, we slammed into the massive stern, wedging our prow into the gap torn in their armor.

In an instant, I reversed my engines, but the momentum carried me briefly into the breach before I tore free. Those few seconds were enough for the black warriors to swarm my deck. There was no resistance offered; the sheer numerical superiority overwhelmed us. Just as blades threatened me, a command from Xodar halted the frenzy. "Secure them," he ordered, "but do not injure them."

Though several pirates had already freed Xodar, he personally attended to my disarmament, ensuring I was tightly bound. He believed the ropes secure, failing to account for the fact that I was not a native Martian. What he mistook for iron bands were merely puny strands that would have snapped like cotton string against a true Barsoonian grip. The same fate befell the girl, and soon we were tethered together, hoisted onto the disabled battleship.

Over a thousand black men manned the great engine of destruction, crowding the decks with a frenetic energy tempered only by rigid discipline. They pressed forward, eyes fixed on their captives. The girl's beauty drew forth brutal comments and vulgar jests from these self-proclaimed supermen, exposing their profound lack of refinement compared to the red men of Barsoom. My own appearance—a close-cropped black mane and thern-complexion skin—attracted similar scrutiny. When Xodar recounted my fighting prowess and strange origins to his nobles, they surrounded me with eager questions. The harness and metal remnants of a fallen thern convinced them that I was an enemy of their ancient foes, elevating my status in their estimation.

These blacks were strikingly handsome and physically imposing. Their officers dazzled in resplendent trappings, adorned with harnesses encrusted in gold, platinum, silver, and precious stones that completely obscured the leather beneath. One commander's harness was a solid mass of diamonds, blazing against his ebony skin with peculiar intensity. The scene was a spectacle of barbaric opulence: polished skeel wood decking, cabins of sorapus grain inlaid with intricate jewels, burnished gold rails, and gleaming gunmetal.

Phaidor and I were dragged below, thrown into a cramped compartment with a single porthole. As our escorts left, they barred the door. Outside, we heard the grinding work on the broken propellers, and through the glass, the vessel drifted lazily southward. Silence fell between us, heavy with unspoken thoughts. I worried for Tars Tarkas and Thuvia; if they evaded capture, they would inevitably fall into the hands of the red men or the greens, facing swift and terrible death as fugitives from the Valley Dor.

I longed to have joined them. I could have shown the intelligent red men the truth behind the cruel superstitions plaguing them. Tardos Mors would have believed me; Dejah Thoris would have believed me. But here, trapped with the enemy, I faced a different peril: if I ever escaped these blacks, I might fall into the hands of hostile red or green warriors, meeting a harsh end. Yet, the likelihood of escaping the blacks seemed vanishingly small.

Linked by a short rope, we sat on a low sorapus bench beneath the porthole. The walls and floor were constructed of carborundum aluminum, a lightweight, impenetrable material favored by Martian fighters. After a time, my gaze shifted to Phaidor. She regarded me with an expression I had rarely seen, her beauty radiant in the gloom. Suddenly, her white lids lowered, and a delicate flush tinged her cheeks. Embarrassed at being caught staring, I teased her gently about studying the "lower orders." She looked up with a nervous, relieved laugh, admitting her fascination with my profile.

"Do you know where we are going?" she asked, breaking the silence.

"To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine," I replied dryly.

"I am heading toward a fate worse than that," she murmured, shuddering.

"Explain," I urged.

"No thern damsel," she said softly, "has ever returned from the black pirates to speak of their experiences. The fact that they never take male prisoners suggests the fate of the girls they steal exceeds even death."

"Is it not just retribution?" I asked, unable to suppress the inquiry.

"What do you mean?" she countered.

"Do not the therns themselves lure the poor souls down the River of Mystery? Was not Thuvia a slave for fifteen years? Is it not just that you suffer as you have caused others to suffer?"

"You misunderstand," she replied, her tone shifting to gentle conviction. "We therns are a holy race. It is an honor for a lesser creature to serve us. We occasionally save those foolish enough to drift down the river, sparing them from the plant men and the apes."

"But do you not perpetuate a cruel deception among the outer world?" I argued. "Why foster such a wicked lie?"

"All life on Barsoom exists solely to support the race of therns," she insisted. "Without their labor and sustenance, how could we survive? Would a thern demean himself by manual toil?"

"Does this mean you eat human flesh?" I asked, horror dawning in my voice.

Her pitying gaze softened my anger. "Truly, we consume the flesh of the lower orders. Do you not?"

"The flesh of beasts, yes," I admitted, "but never of man."

"As man may eat the flesh of beasts, so may gods eat the flesh of man," she declared. "The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom."

Disgust rippled through me. "An unbeliever," she noted gently, "yet perhaps, if we escape and return to the courts of Matai Shang, you will see the error of your ways. And—" she hesitated, her voice trembling slightly, "perhaps we shall find a way to make you... one of us."

Her eyes dropped, a faint color suffusing her cheeks. I failed to grasp her meaning, nor did I for a long time. Dejah Thoris often called me a simpleton, and perhaps she was right. I confessed that I feared I could not reciprocate the hospitality of her father, for my first duty as a thern would be to guard the mouth of the River Iss to rescue the deluded pilgrims, and dedicate my life to destroying the plant men and the great white apes.

Her reaction was one of genuine horror. "No!" she cried, "Do not utter such sacrilegious thoughts! If they discover you harbor them, they will put you to death. Even I..." Her face flushed again, and she stammered, "...even I could not save you."

I said nothing. Clearly, she was as steeped in superstition as the Martians of the outer world, worshipping not a hope of love and peace, but the hideous plant men and apes as the abodes of their dead.

Suddenly, the door opened and Xodar entered. He smiled kindly, devoid of malice. "Since escape is impossible," he said, "there is no need to confine you below. I will release your bonds, and you may join us on deck. You will witness something fascinating, and since you will never return to the outer world, it does no harm to let you see it. You will behold what the First Born and their slaves alone know: the subterranean entrance to the Holy Land, the real heaven of Barsoom."

"It will teach this daughter of the therns a valuable lesson," he added, "for she will see the Temple of Issus, and perhaps Issus herself will embrace her."

Phaidor's head lifted in defiance. "What blasphemy, you dog of a pirate!" she shrieked. "If Issus sees you, she will wipe out your entire breed!"

"You have much to learn, thern," Xodar replied with an ugly smile, "and I do not envy you the method by which you will learn it."
