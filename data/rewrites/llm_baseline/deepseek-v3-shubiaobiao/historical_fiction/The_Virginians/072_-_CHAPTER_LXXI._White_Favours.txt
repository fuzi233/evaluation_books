The disagreement between George and his cousin Will had driven George to avoid Bloomsbury Square, for Will’s presence there had grown intolerable. Bound by their pledge to keep the peace, Will had taken to taunting George with such relentless spite that it was all George could do not to throttle him on the spot.

Lydia, ever artless, had developed a peculiar habit of praising Theo in George’s presence. Only weeks ago, she had been consumed with jealousy over his affections for another; now, she took every opportunity to declare how perfectly suited he and Theo were.

“How fortunate that dreadful duel was prevented!” she remarked one afternoon, her voice dripping with sweetness. “Those constables arrived just in time, did they not? And poor Mr. Esmond—such a temper! But I’m sure Miss Theo must be grateful. She would have been quite heartbroken if anything had happened to you.”

Such remarks, delivered with wide-eyed innocence before company, did little to endear her to George. He found himself excusing himself from dinners, inventing engagements—anything to avoid the spectacle of Will’s gloating or Lydia’s barbed compliments.

When the truth of the duel’s near-occurrence reached Dean Street, the Lambert household was in an uproar.

“You meant to kill a man, and you sat in our parlor saying nothing of it?” Theo exclaimed, aghast.

“He insulted my brother and me,” George defended. “Would you have had me swallow the lie that we were cowards?”

General Lambert listened in grim silence, his disapproval palpable.

“You’ve always said dueling was wicked, Papa,” Theo pressed.

“To refuse a challenge takes courage,” the General admitted reluctantly.

“A courage I’m not sure I possess,” muttered Jack Lambert, his expression dark. “If any man called me a coward, I’d forget every sermon I’ve ever preached.”

“There! You see?” George said triumphantly.

Jack met his gaze. “I cannot condemn you, Mr. Warrington.”

“*Mr. Warrington*?” George echoed, stung.

“Would you have him break God’s law?” Theo demanded of her brother.

“I am a soldier’s son,” Jack replied tersely. “And no murder was done.”

Hetty, pale at this turn in the conversation, was swiftly ushered out by Theo. George, sensing some unspoken accusation, rose abruptly.

“Do you mean to say,” he demanded, “that you believe I shrank from this fight?”

The General’s stern face softened. “I told Jack it was a lie.”

“A lie? What lie?” George’s temper flared.

Jack flushed. “Miss Lydia implied you had… avoided the duel by less honorable means.”

George’s blood ran hot. “That little viper!”

“Do not speak of her so!” Jack snapped, his own anger rising. “She is innocence itself! If she was mistaken, it was out of relief that bloodshed was averted.”

“Mistaken?” George laughed bitterly. “She slandered me deliberately. And you believed her?”

Jack looked wretched. “I—I may have been swayed by jealousy. I confess, I feared she favored you.”

The admission hung between them. George, stunned, realized the truth: Jack was in love with Lydia.

The General, re-entering, found them at an impasse. “Made your peace, lads?”

“Not quite,” George muttered.

Jack, red-faced, excused himself shortly after dinner. George, too, left early—ostensibly on business, but in truth, he meant to confront Lydia.

At Bloomsbury Square, however, he found Jack already there, pacing before the door. The servant informed them that the household had departed for Hampshire—to Castlewood, no less.

“Gone?” Jack’s voice cracked. “With *him*?”

George’s jaw tightened. Will had clearly wasted no time.

Aunt Lambert arrived in her sedan chair, only to learn her errand was pointless. Jack, in a black fury, stormed off.

George, left seething, spent the evening in a fruitless search for Will. The next morning, he penned two letters—one to Will, calling him a liar to his face, and another to Van den Bosch, warning him against slander.

Days later, a reply arrived—not from Will, but from Castlewood himself.

*Dear Cousin,* the letter began, with infuriating civility. The Earl announced his impending marriage to Lydia, dismissed Will’s temper as a passing fit, and extended hollow invitations for reconciliation.

George read it with disgust. Castlewood, nearing sixty, had secured himself a wealthy young bride, and Will had been neatly sidelined. The London papers soon confirmed it: the Earl of Castlewood had wed Miss Van den Bosch of Virginia, her fortune trumpeted for all to see.

The game, it seemed, had been played—and George had lost a round. But the match was far from over.
