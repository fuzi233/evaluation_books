The Roman Emperor, in the legend, put to death ten learned Israelites to avenge the sale of Joseph by his brethren. History remembers the cruelty, yet there have always been enough of his kin—whose piety consists in punishing those who can perceive the justice of grudges but fail to grasp the logic of gratitude. One cannot convince the stronger feeling that it lacks the stronger reason, nor bend the heart that holds no love to admit there is fertile ground for it. As language itself reveals, *love* precedes *lovable*; the impulse must exist before the object can be named.

When Deronda presented his letter at the banking-house on Schuster Strasse in Mainz, requesting Joseph Kalonymos, he was ushered into an inner chamber. Seated at a table scattered with open correspondence was the white-bearded man he had glimpsed the previous year within the Frankfurt synagogue. He wore the same battered felt hat, and beside him rested a packed portmanteau draped with a wrap and overcoat. Upon Deronda's entrance, Kalonymos rose but held his distance, offering no handshake. His gaze, small and penetrating, glittered like black gems set against the yellowish hue of his face and snow-white hair.

"Good!" Kalonymos declared in German. "Now it is you who seek me, young man."

"Yes," Deronda replied, his voice steady despite the tremor in his spirit. "I seek you with gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather's. I owe you a debt for the trouble you have taken on my behalf." He spoke the liberal German tongue with ease, a dialect that embraced strange accents into its maternal bosom.

Kalonymos extended his hand, his grip cordial. "So you are no longer angry at being something more than an Englishman?"

"On the contrary," Deronda said, relief washing over him. "I thank you heartily for saving me from the ignorance of my parentage, and for safeguarding the chest my grandfather left in trust."

"Sit," Kalonymos urged, his tone shifting to a quick, urgent whisper as he resumed his seat and gestured to a chair nearby. Deliberately removing his hat, he revealed a head thick with white hair, stroking and clutching his beard as he examined Deronda's young face with intense scrutiny.

The moment struck Deronda with the force of an electric shock. In the presence of a man still bound by zealous friendship to the grandfather whose hopes had yearned for him before birth—who, though dead, seemed to speak through the written memorials that Milton described as containing "a potency of life... as active as that soul whose progeny they are"—Deronda felt he was touching the living chain of his own ancestry. He endured Kalonymos's searching gaze with a mixture of delighted awe and solemn reverence, akin to witnessing the commemoration of ancient deeds that still profoundly shape the present. Such sensitivity might seem impossible to men of duller fiber, whose affections refuse to diffuse through the vast travel of imagination; yet it existed, stubborn and real, granting Deronda's face an expression of profound satisfaction that pleased the observer.

In Hebrew, Kalonymos quoted from the liturgy: "As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter." After a pause, he continued, "Young man, I rejoice that I was not yet set off again on my travels, and that you have come in time for me to see the image of my friend as he was in his youth—no longer perverted from the fellowship of your people, no longer shrinking in proud wrath from the touch of him who claimed you as a Jew. You come with thankfulness to claim the kindred and heritage that wicked contrivance would have stolen from you. You come with a willing soul to declare, 'I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.' Is it not so?"

"Assuredly it is," Deronda affirmed.

"But let me say," Kalonymos interjected, "that I should never have treated a Jew with incivility simply because he was a Jew. You can understand why I shrank from telling a stranger, 'I know nothing of my mother.'"

"A sin! A sin!" Kalonymos cried, raising a hand and closing his eyes in visceral disgust. "A robbery of our people—as when our youths and maidens were reared for the Roman Edom. But it is frustrated. I have frustrated it. When Daniel Charisi—may his Rock and his Redeemer guard him!—was a stripling and I a lad little above his shoulder, we made a solemn vow always to be friends. He said, 'Let us bind ourselves with duty, as if we were sons of the same mother.' That was his bent from first to last—to fortify his soul with bonds. He once said, 'Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law; and law is the nature of the Eternal.' So we bound ourselves. Though we drifted apart in later life, the bond remained unbroken. When he died, they sought to rob him; but they could not rob him of me. I rescued that remainder of him which he prized and preserved for his offspring. And I have restored to him the offspring they had stolen."

Kalonymos left the room briefly and returned with a clerk bearing the chest. He set it on the floor, removed a leather covering, and departed again. It was not large, yet weighted by ornamental bracers and handles of gilt iron. The wood was exquisitely incised with Arabic lettering.

"So!" Kalonymos said, returning to his seat. "And here is the curious key," he added, retrieving it from a small leather pouch. "Keep it carefully. I trust you are methodic and wary." He bestowed upon Deronda the monitory, slightly suspicious glance that age often directs when entrusting precious objects to youth.

"I shall be more careful of this than of any other property," Deronda smiled, slipping the key into his breast pocket. "I have never before possessed anything that signifies so much cherished hope and effort. And I shall never forget that this effort was partly yours."

"Do you have time to tell me more of my grandfather? Or am I trespassing by staying longer?"

"Stay yet a while," Kalonymos said, glancing at his watch. "In an hour and eighteen minutes I depart for Trieste, and soon my sons will demand my attention. Shall I introduce you to them, so they may show hospitality to my friend's grandson? They dwell here in ease and luxury, though I prefer the life of a wanderer."

"I shall be glad if you will commend me to their acquaintance for a future opportunity," Deronda replied. "Pressing claims call me to England—friends who desperately need my presence. I have been kept away too long by unforeseen circumstances. But to know more of you and your family would be motive enough to draw me back to Mainz."

"Good! You will hardly find me, for I am beyond three-score years and ten, a wanderer carrying my shroud with me. But my sons and their children dwell here in wealth and unity. The days have changed since Karl the Great brought my ancestors from Italy to impart a tincture of knowledge to our rough German brethren. My generation and ours fought hard for it. Our youth fell on evil days, but we won. We accumulate wealth in safety, and the learning of all Germany is fed and fattened by Jewish brains—even if they do not always retain their Jewish hearts. Have you been left entirely ignorant of your people's life, young man?"

"No," Deronda admitted. "Recently, before I suspected my parentage, I was led to study everything pertaining to their history with more interest than any other subject. It turns out I have been preparing myself to understand my grandfather."

He was anxious lest time be wasted before this circuitous conversation could return to the core of his longing. Age rarely distinguishes between what it needs to express and what youth requires to know; distance seems to level the objects of memory. Yet Kalonymos, though keenly active, possessed an inkstand in the wrong place that would have hindered his imagination from reaching Beirut; he had long united restless travel with punctilious observation. Deronda's final sentence achieved its purpose.

"So—you would perhaps have become such a man as he if your education had not hindered? For you resemble him in features—yet not altogether, young man. He had an iron will in his face; it braced everyone around him. Even quite young, he bore a deep, upright line in his brow. I see none of that in you."

"Daniel Charisi used to say, 'Better a wrong will than a wavering; better a steadfast enemy than an uncertain friend; better a false belief than no belief at all,'" Kalonymos recited. "What he despised most was indifference. He had longer reasons than I can give you."

"Yet his knowledge was not narrow?" Deronda asked, subtly referencing the common excuse for indecision—that it stems from knowing too much.

"Narrow? No," Kalonymos shook his head, a compassionate smile playing on his lips. "From his childhood upward, he drank in learning as easily as a plant sucks up water. But he early turned to medicine and theories concerning life and health. He traveled to many lands, spending much of his substance in seeing and knowing. What he insisted upon was that the strength and wealth of mankind depended on the balance of separateness and communication. He was bitterly opposed to our people losing themselves among the Gentiles. 'It's no better,' he would say, 'than many sorts of grain going back from their variety into sameness.'
