Jerome remained as stern and somber as his nature dictated, yet a profound softening had taken root in his regard for Gerard. Throughout his tenure in England, the absence of his younger brother had gnawed at him with a ferocity he had scarcely anticipated, leaving him to wonder constantly of Gerard's whereabouts. The tidings from Gouda initially bolstered his esteem; particularly the report that Gerard had stood firm against the Pope and the Minorites, defiantly resisting what Jerome termed the "infernal heresy of the immaculate conception." However, this admiration curdled into revulsion when a novice monk, eyes glistening with unshed tears, revealed the true cause of Gerard's wasting sickness. The image of a man dying for a woman shattered Jerome's composure, reigniting his contempt. Determined to extinguish this perceived scandal, Jerome besieged Clement's cell twice daily, deploying every ounce of his former influence and eloquence to urge the friar to abandon this unspiritual despondency. He pleaded that Gerard was robbing the Church of its greatest asset—its piety and its voice—at a moment of supreme crisis.

Gerard listened, nodding in approval of Jerome's logic, admiring the intensity of his rebuke, yet he confessed his own fragility. Visibly, he began to fade, drifting toward the land of the faithful. On one occasion, Jerome declared that he had heard Gerard's entire story and listened with a mixture of sorrow and pride. "Yet now," Jerome argued, his voice tight with disapproval, "you undo everything. This is the triumph of earthly passion. Had you simply yielded and repented, it would have been better than resisting while she lived, only to succumb utterly now, body and soul alike."

"Dear Jerome," Clement replied, his tone so sweet it drained the sting from Jerome's reproof, "perhaps you misunderstand me. There is no passion here, only a deep affection for which I shall not blush in this life, nor in the next. Consider, Jerome: does the poor dog that perishes of grief beside his master's grave commit a sin of passion? Neither do I. Passion would have saved my life but cost me my soul. She was my guardian angel; she upheld me in my duties and charities. Her countenance fortified me in the pulpit; her lips soothed me amidst ingratitude. She wove herself into every thread of goodness in my existence. After leaning upon her so long, I could not stand alone. And, dear Jerome, believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God's will to release me. When they cast the earth upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my bosom—a fracture that cannot mend. I heard it, and I felt it. From that hour, Jerome, no food I consumed held any flavor. With my eyes bandaged, I could no longer distinguish bread from flesh by taste alone."

"Holy saints!" Jerome exclaimed involuntarily.

"And from that same hour," Clement continued, his voice trembling with a serene resignation, "my deep dejection lifted, and I smiled again. I smile often. Why? I read it thus: He in whose hands lie the issues of life and death granted me that final minute the great summons. A cord of life snapped within me. He is merciful. I would have lived unhappily; but He said, 'No; enough is done, enough is suffered. Poor, feeble, loving servant, thy shortcomings are forgiven, thy sorrows hasten thine end; come thou to thy rest!' I come, Lord, I come!"

Jerome groaned, the weight of loss pressing down upon him. "The Church has ever relied upon her holy yet feeble servants," he murmured. "Now I would trade ten years of my life to save yours. But I perceive it cannot be. Die in peace."

Thus, within a few short days, Gerard lay dying in a state of such holy tranquility that more than one aged saint seemed present to gather his final words. That evening, he had seen Giles, begging him to ensure poor Jack did not starve, to oversee that Little Gerard's trustees fulfilled their obligations, and to convey kisses to his parents and dispatch Denys to his friends in Burgundy—"Poor thing," he had noted, "he will feel so strange here without his comrade." Following this, he shared a private audience with Jerome alone. What transpired between them remains obscure, yet the aftermath spoke volumes: Jerome emerged from the door with his hands clasped across his breast, his noble head bowed low, sighing heavily as he walked away.

The two monks who kept vigil until matins recounted that throughout the night, Gerard periodically erupted in pious ejaculations, praises, and thanksgivings. Once, however, they reported that he wandered, convinced he saw her walking in verdant meadows alongside other spirits clad in white, beckoning him forward. The monks smiled and waved back, though later admitted it might have been mere fancy. Just before dawn, they claimed to hear three light taps against the wall, spaced slowly and deliberately. The dying man heard them and whispered, "I come, love, I come."

It is certain that Gerard uttered these words and prepared for his departure. He summoned all the monks keeping watch. They gathered, hovering like gentle spirits around him, offering holy comfort. Some knelt in silent prayer, faces pressed to the floor; others tenderly supported his head. When one mentioned his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard silenced him. Addressing them all, he declared, "My dear brethren, take heed: he who dies here so happy holds not these new-fangled doctrines of human merit. Oh, what a wretched hour it would have been for me had I believed them! Nay, I hold, with the Apostles and their successors in the Church, the ancient Fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, nor piety, nor the works wrought in holiness of heart, but by faith alone."

A profound silence fell, heavy with significance. "If you please, sweep the floor," the dying Christian commanded, his voice suddenly restored with supernatural clarity and force. Instantly, the monks obeyed, moved by awe and curiosity. "Make a great cross with wood ashes." They scattered the ashes upon the floor, forming a massive crucifix. "Now lay me down upon it, for thus shall I die." They gently lifted him from his bed and placed him upon the ash-cross.

"Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?" someone asked softly.

"Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?" he replied. He lay silent, eyes raised in ecstatic vision. Presently, speaking half to them, half to himself, he murmured with a subdued, concentrated rapture, "Oh... I feel it buoyant. It lifts me, floating in the sky whence my merits had sunk me like lead."

Daybreak revealed his face cast upward in silent rapture, his hands joined in prayer, reminiscent of Margaret's. As the clock struck the hour of her death, he spoke his final word in this world: "Jesu!" And with that syllable, he fell asleep.

They laid him out for burial. Beneath his linens, they discovered a shirt woven of horsehair. "Ah!" cried the young monks, "Behold a saint!" Underneath the haircloth, they found a long, thick tress of auburn hair. Gasps filled the room, horror mingling with confusion, sparking a babel of voices—some condemning, some excusing. Into the fray stepped Jerome. Hearing the dispute, he turned to Basil, an ardent young monk crying out the loudest accusations of scandal.

"Basil," Jerome said firmly, "is she alive or dead that owns this hair?"

"How may I know, Father?" Basil stammered.

"Then, for all you know, it may be the relic of a saint?"

"Certes, it may be," Basil replied skeptically.

"You have then broken our Rule, which states: 'Put no evil construction on any act done by a brother which can be construed innocently.' Who are you to judge such a man as this was? Go to your cell and remain there for a week as penance." Jerome seized the lock of hair and carried it away. When the coffin was to be sealed, he cleared the cell and placed the tress upon the dead man's bosom. "There, Clement," he whispered to the still face. Setting himself a penance for the act, he nailed the coffin shut himself.

The following day, Gerard was interred in the Gouda churchyard. The monks followed him in procession from the convent. Jerome, clearly executing the deceased's wishes, read the funeral service. The grave was deep, lined with a lead coffin at the bottom. Poor Gerard, light as a feather due to his emaciation, was lowered and placed beside it. After the rites, Jerome addressed the crowd of parishioners who had gathered for a final farewell. Speaking of the departed's virtues, loud wails and weeping erupted, tears falling upon the coffin like rain.

When the monks returned to the refectory, Jerome assembled them and spoke thus: "We have today laid a saint in the earth. The convent will keep his trentals, but we shall feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; his labors are ended, and he has entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast and do penance; for to my shame I confess, I was unjust to him, knowing not his worth until it was too late.
