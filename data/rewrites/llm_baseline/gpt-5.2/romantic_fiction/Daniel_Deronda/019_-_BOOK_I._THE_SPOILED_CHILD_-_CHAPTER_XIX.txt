CHAPTER XIX.

“I pity the man who can travel from Dan to Beersheba, and say, ‘’Tis all barren’: and so it is: and so is all the world to him who will not cultivate the fruits it offers.”—STERNE: _Sentimental Journey_.

No one would have called Deronda romantic without doing violence to the quiet composure he wore as carefully as a well-cut coat. Yet beneath it there burned a heat that answered to ordinary life as a kitchen fire answers to plain ingredients—making fragrance out of what looked like mere necessity. Some minds pass through the world as if everything were tasteless, and then complain there is no savor; but poetry, like good broth, is not rare—it is only lost on those whose senses have been dulled by habit, by hurry, by the ceaseless clatter of surfaces.

He felt it that night with a vividness that would not let him lie down inside himself. London, even at its latest hours, has its own soundscape: the far, iron-shod roll of wheels along the main streets; the occasional shout that rises and breaks like a gull’s cry; a door slammed too hard; a watchman’s measured step; and, in the spaces between, that uneasy urban hush which is never truly silence, only a thinner veil over the city’s restless breathing. Deronda lay listening, but it was not the external noises that kept him awake. His mind replayed, with the clean sharpness of remembered taste, the moments since he had first discerned Mirah on the river-brink.

The Thames, too, has its voice—lap and suck against the stones, the sly whispering along timbers, the sudden hollow slap under an oar. He heard it again as if the water still ran under his bed. And with that voice came her own: not loud, not showy, but carrying an accent like a spice you cannot mistake once you have known it. He took up a book to force his thoughts into the narrow channel of printed lines; the words were only a mesh, and through it his inward sight kept seeing her—her look of resolved despair, the wet chill of the river air clinging about her, the tremor in her speech that made every sentence feel as if it had cost her breath.

For him, the finding of Mirah had the kind of stirring inevitableness that ancient stories ascribe to fateful meetings—only it had happened in a very modern way, among common garments and common dangers, with no witnesses but the river and the city. Yet the mythic and the domestic can share the same room, as easily as a microscope can sit on the same table with a loaf newly cut. What drives them out is not science or railways, but the deadening clang of minds that cannot be moved by awe, tenderness, or fellowship—minds that hear the world’s music as mere noise.

Mirah’s search for her mother gripped him with a peculiar force, because some chord in his own life answered to it. The first movement of his sympathy was practical: if certain persons were indeed alive somewhere in London, there were ways—quiet, methodical, almost experimental—of setting the right machinery in motion until a hidden fact surfaced. There are men who can trace a family as a food-historian traces an ingredient through markets and mouths: by small evidence, by the residue of habit, by the scent of a dialect clinging to a name. It was possible.

But his sympathy did not come alone; it brought with it a dread he knew too well—the dread that to find what one longs for may be to meet calamity. In the boat she had said her mother and brother were good. Yet goodness, he knew, can be a thing preserved in the amber of a child’s memory—pure because the child was pure, and because longing will sweeten what it cannot verify. Ten or twelve years are time enough for harsh changes. A pot left unattended does not keep its flavor; it scorches, turns, breeds bitterness. Time does its work on souls as on provisions.

And there were other forebodings, less fair and more humiliating to his pride in justice. Deronda’s nature was strongly inclined to take the side of those who bore inherited disadvantages; scorn flung at a man for the mere name of his people would always rouse him to indignant pity. Yet he had never been drawn, in any close and daily way, toward existing Jews. What he knew of them was chiefly a collection of facts and rumors—some true, some swollen by prejudice—which had gathered in his mind like rancid tales passed from mouth to mouth until they seem to have the authority of experience. Of the learned and accomplished among them, he had assumed—without having cared enough to examine—that they must have dropped their religion, wished to be merged and absorbed, and so to escape the old mark.

He hated, in theory, the severance of past from present; he had often protested against that cheap forgetting which refuses to see history moving in living bodies. Yet in practice he had been like many who protest nobly and inquire little. He had been content with generalities: a long-oppressed race must have its virtues and its vices—endurance, acuteness, clannishness, suspicion, and whatever else the world’s cruelty has kneaded into human nature when it keeps it always under the hand.

Now, because Mirah’s yearning had made the question immediate—because it was no longer an “idea” but a life that might be broken—his mind, to his own discomfort, began to crowd itself with disagreeable images of what the finding might mean. It was as if he stood at the door of some kitchen whose air might, at the first opening, either welcome him with the warm clean smell of bread, or strike him sick with the sourness of neglect. He could not yet know. Perhaps, through Mrs. Meyrick, he might gather more exact hints from Mirah’s own lips—names, places, scraps of remembered speech. Her voice and accent were themselves clues, belonging to no fashionable smoothing-out of utterance, but to a native cadence carrying history inside it—something that could not be translated away without loss. And her very refinement, her delicacy of manner, the purity that seemed to clothe her like a consecrating garment, made him shrink the more from associating her—either in imagination or in fact—with anything he dreaded as coarse, contaminating, or shameless.

Fine words can be used like perfumes to hide what is unpleasant; but the mind, when it is afraid, thinks in naked pictures. Deronda’s thoughts would not remain decorous. They went on, swift and involuntary, showing him scenes as if they were thrust upon his sight. He saw himself led—by some official guide with indifferent eyes—into a dingy street where the air was thick with stale damp and cheap fried food, where drains and soot had their own bitter taste on the tongue. He entered through a dim doorway and found a hawk-eyed woman, rough-haired, unwashed, bargaining with hard rapidity, pinching a hungry girl’s last poor finery between fingers that had no gentleness in them. Or else—in some quarter only the more hideous for being smarter, where bright shop-lights made vice look respectable—he found himself under the too-close breath of a young man talkative and familiar, eager to show acquaintance with “gentlemen’s tastes,” speaking in a slippery lingua franca of commerce, that dominant lexicon which smooths over shame and calls it business. He imagined that voice—oily, incessant—filling the air like a machine that cannot be silenced; and he felt, with physical repugnance, how such sounds can invade the mind, scraping at it until one’s better feelings are worn down.

It would be easy, perhaps, to blame him for the unlovely drift of these images. Yet they sprang less from any natural delight in insult than from the governance of dread; and if Mirah’s parents had been Christian, the chief difference might have been only that his forebodings would have fed on a wider acquaintance. The habit of connecting unknown parentage with peril was already rooted in him. His own life had made the link seem reasonable; his own withheld knowledge had taught him how a family history can be like a language half-lost—words missing, meanings guessed at, the whole story liable to be distorted by the louder voices that dominate a household and a society.

But the practical question pressed: what was to be done with Mirah now? She needed shelter—shelter of the body and of the spirit; protection not as alms, but as a human right owed to her by the mere fact that she was vulnerable and alone. In him, every instinct of chivalry rose up, not as a flourish but as a duty: the sooner and the more firmly he could engage the interest of others besides himself, the more safely he would fulfill her claim upon him. He had no right to make her dependent on him alone, though he might have the means. Indeed, the depth of the impression she had made on him sharpened his resolve that she should understand herself to be entirely independent of him—free from any chain that gratitude might fasten, free from any suspicion that could poison what he meant to keep clean.

And there was another anxiety, more inward. Vague visions of the future—visions he tried to dismiss as mere fantastic vapor—still left their influence behind like an after-taste: he felt an urgent need that those who watched him closely should know, from the first, the true history of his relation to Mirah. He had learned to hate secrecy in the great ties and obligations of life—hated it the more because he himself was ensnared in a secrecy he could not simply cast off. He had made a vow that, since the truths which disgrace mortals are not all of their own making, the truth should never be made a disgrace to another by his act. The vow was like a steady tone in his ear, a sustaining note he feared to lose amid the city’s discord. He was not without terror lest, by some weakness, he should break it and slide into that apologetic philosophy which explains the world into meaning that nothing better exists than one’s own conduct.

Once, in the thick of the night, he resolved that he would tell the whole of his adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger at breakfast the next morning—plainly, at once, so that no shadow could grow by concealment. But then another possibility checked him: his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick’s might reveal something quite new—some detail from Mirah, some name, some remembered phrase in her own tongue, something that would change what he ought to say and how. He wavered, weighing openness against premature disclosure, until at last he went to sleep on one conclusion: he would wait until that visit had been made.
