Her bearing would have moved any eyes not clogged with a tale already told. She stood as a creature startled in its own nest: breast fluttering like a trapped linnet, red and white chasing one another across her cheeks, one palm braced against the stone as though the wall alone could keep her from falling. Yet the look she lifted—those purple eyes, brimmed with love and reverent fear and the soft, domestic remembering of a thousand small kindnesses—was the strangest thing that had entered that cave of penance.

“Aha!” cried the hermit, and the cry rang thin and sharp in the hollow. “Thou art come at last in flesh and blood; come as thou camest to holy Anthony. But I am ware of thee. I am armed.”

He snatched up his little crucifix and thrust it towards her; in the other hand the candle shook so that the flame fretted and licked at the air. Both hands trembled—hunger, vigil, and that long, starved solitude that makes a sound of every breath. “Exorcizo te.”

“Ah, no!” Her hands flew up, two pretty, pleading palms, as if to ward off a blow. “Alas, work me no ill! It is Margaret.”

“Liar!” he shouted. The word cracked like dry wood. “Margaret was fair, but not so supernatural fair as thou. Thou didst shrink at that sacred name, thou subtle hypocrite. In Nomine Dei exorcizo vos.”

“Ah, Jesu!” Margaret gasped, the terror in her voice not feigned, but born of the old dread that still lived in stone and candle-smoke. “Curse me not! I will go home. I thought I might come. For very manhood be-Latin me not! Oh, Gerard—is it thus you and I meet after all, after all?”

She crouched as if the floor would take her into it, sobbing with that mingled fear of heaven and fear of man that the age breeds in women. He—so soaked in dread of Satan that every tenderness had begun to look like a snare—might still have doubted whether Nature itself could be an art of Hell. But her spontaneous cry of his name, the very sound of it as she had once spoken it at a hearth, broke the phantom as a hand breaks a cobweb. His face changed; the glare went out like a wick pinched.

He gave a cry that was half repentance, half pain, and bent to lift her. Yet fasts had made him weak, and emotion weaker still; he shook above her and could not draw her up.

“What,” he panted, “is it thou? And have I met thee with hard words? Alas!”

For a little while neither could speak. Their breaths were loud in the cave; outside, the small stream kept its cold whispering, and the night insects made their thin, tireless music, as if the world, untroubled, continued its kitchen-work of grinding and pouring.

“I heed it not much,” Margaret said at last, brave against her tears. “You took me for another—for a devil; oh! oh! oh!”

“Forgive me, sweet soul!” His voice broke upon the words. When he found speech again, it came in fragments. “I have been much beset by the evil one since I came here.”

Margaret shuddered and glanced round at the dank walls, the straw, the soot on stone. Even the air smelled of old tallow and wet earth—no clean bread-smell, no wood-smoke seasoned with herbs. “Like enow. Then, oh, take my hand and let me lead thee from this foul place.”

He stared at her as if she had offered him a knife by the handle. “What, desert my cell and go into the world again? Is it for that thou hast come to me?” There was sadness in it, and reproach.

“Ay, Gerard. I am come to take thee to thy pretty vicarage. Art vicar of Gouda, thanks to Heaven and thy good brother Giles; and mother and I have made it so neat for thee.” Her words hurried now, as if she could warm him by naming warmth. “’Tis well enow in winter, I promise thee. But bide a bit till the hawthorn bloom, and anon thy walls put on their kirtle of brave roses, and sweet woodbine. Have we forgotten thee, and the foolish things thou lovest? And, dear Gerard, thy mother is waiting; and ’tis late for her to be out of her bed. Prithee, prithee, come! And the moment we are out of this foul hole I’ll show thee a treasure thou hast gotten and knowest nought on’t—or sure hadst never fled from us so.”

She stopped short, stricken by the look that came into his face. He had drawn himself up into a knot of fear, his eyes wide, his mouth tense, suspicion and longing wrestling like two dogs in one leash.

“Alas! what is to do?” she cried. “What have I ignorantly said, to be regarded thus?”

“Unhappy girl,” he said solemnly, though his whole body quivered, “would you have me risk my soul and yours for a miserable vicarage and the flowers that grow on it? But this is not thy doing: the bowelless fiend sends thee, poor simple girl, to me with this bait. But, oh cunning fiend, I will unmask thee even to this thine instrument, and she shall see thee, and abhor thee as I do. Margaret, my lost love—why am I here? Because I love thee.”

“Oh! no, Gerard, you love me not—or you would not have hidden from me; there was no need.”

“Let there be no deceit between us twain that have loved so true; and after this night shall meet no more on earth.”

“Now God forbid!” she cried.

“I love thee, and thou hast not forgotten me—or thou hadst married ere this, and hadst not been the one to find me, buried here from sight of man.” He spoke as if each sentence cost him blood. “I am a priest, a monk. What but folly or sin can come of you and me living neighbours, and feeding a passion innocent once, but now—so Heaven wills it—impious and unholy? No. Though my heart break I must be firm. ’Tis I that am the man; ’tis I that am the priest. You and I must meet no more, till I am schooled by solitude, and thou art wedded to another.”

“I consent to my doom but not to thine. I would ten times liever die; yet I will marry—ay, wed misery itself—sooner than let thee lie in this foul dismal place, with yon sweet manse awaiting for thee.”

At each word she spoke, two things stood clearer to him than the candle’s jittering light: duty, and the agony it demanded.

“My beloved,” he said, with tenderness and a stubbornness that was like a tightened cord, “I bless thee for giving me one more sight of thy sweet face; and may God forgive thee and bless thee for destroying in a minute the holy peace it hath taken six months of solitude to build. No matter. A year of penance will, Dei gratia, restore me to my calm. My poor Margaret, I seem cruel: yet I am kind. ’Tis best we part—ay, this moment.”

“Part, Gerard? Never. We have seen what comes of parting.” She stepped closer, her voice thick with hurt, yet driven. “Part? Why, you have not heard half my story—no, nor the tithe. ’Tis not for thy mere comfort I take thee to Gouda manse. Hear me!”

“I may not. Thy very voice is a temptation with its music—memory’s delight.”

“But I say you shall hear me, Gerard, for forth this place I go not unheard.”

“Then must we part by other means,” he said sadly.

“Alack! what other means? Wouldst put me to thine own door, being the stronger?”

“Nay, Margaret. Well thou knowest I would suffer many deaths rather than put force on thee; thy sweet body is dearer to me than my own; but a million times dearer to me are our immortal souls, both thine and mine. I have withstood this direst temptation of all long enow. Now I must fly it. Farewell! farewell!”

He turned, made for the door, opened it, and had half gone out when she sprang and caught him by the arm.

“Nay, then another must speak for me. I thought to reward thee for yielding to me; but unkind that thou art, I need his help, I find. Turn then this way one moment.”

“Nay, nay.”

“But I say ay! And then turn thy back on us an thou canst.”

She loosened her grasp a little, thinking he would not refuse so small a boon. He saw the smallest space and took it as a drowning man takes air.

“Fly, Clement, fly!” he almost shrieked; and zeal, that terrible strength, lent him his old limbs for a breath. He tore himself away, ran a few steps, leaped the little stream, and went along it with the wildness of one pursued by invisible claws. Then, finding she did not follow, he stopped and looked back.

Margaret lay prone, hands flung out. Without meaning it, he had thrown her down upon stone. The sight wrung a groan from him; he took one step toward her—and then, as if punishment must be taken at once to keep his soul from turning, he flung himself into the icy water instead.

“There,” he cried, teeth chattering on the words, “kill my body—but save my soul!”

The cold rose like knives. It took his breath, seized his throat. He stood, up to the neck, as if the stream were liquid winter. Margaret, still on the ground, sent up one long moan and rose to her knees. In the candle’s distant glow and the moon’s pale help he saw her face, whitened now, her eyes shining like wet violets.

And in the still night, over the hush of water and the far, soft rustle of leaves, he heard her words clearly, as if each syllable were dropped into a bowl of metal.

“Oh, God! Thou that knowest all, Thou seest how I am used. Forgive me then! For I will not live another day.”

She sprang up and fled past his hiding-place, not like a woman but like some wounded creature that has no thought left but speed and pain; and she shrieked as she ran, each cry tearing the air:

“CRUEL!—CRUEL!—CRUEL!—CRUEL!”

What a world of anguish can be poured into one syllable. Wounded love, wounded pride, despair, and the first shadow of madness—all were in it. It froze his heart more than the stream froze his bones. He had won, in his own stern account, a victory; and it tasted like ashes. For a moment, in that bitter triumph, a savage thought rose—almost a curse against the Church, against religion itself, for exacting such brutality from flesh-and-blood man.

At last he crawled out, half dead, and staggered back to his den.

“I am safe here,” he groaned. “She will never come near me again—unmanly, ungrateful wretch that I am.”

He flung his wasted, shivering body down upon the floor, with a secret hope it might never rise. But presently the hour-glass, patient as ever, showed him midnight had gone. He rose, slow and shaking, stripped off his wet garments, and with every movement moaned—not at his own pain, but hers, because in hurting her he had bruised the very place he loved.

He took the old hermit’s cilice of bristles and drew it on. The sharp points bit and scored; the sting was clean, like nettles, and for a moment it helped—pain answering pain. Over that he buckled the breastplate he had never dared to wear, doubting himself worthy of a tried soldier’s armour. Now he must take every aid, even if the aid were only the hard reminder of vows.

He knelt and prayed—humbly, doggedly—for release that very night from the burden of the flesh. Then he lit all his candles, until the cave’s black corners drew back and the smoke-smell thickened. He began to recite his psalter. Each word seemed a lump of lead dragged up from a leaden heart and dropped with a dull weight to the ground; and between the verses he moaned, not loud, but from the marrow.

In the midst of this, he noticed a little bundle in the corner he had not seen before in the weaker light; and at one end of it something shone like gold spun into silk. He went nearer; and the instant he saw what lay there, he lifted both hands as if in rapture.

“It is a seraph,” he whispered. “A lovely seraph. Heaven hath witnessed my bitter trial, and approves my cruelty; and this flower of the skies is sent to cheer me, fainting under my burden.”

He fell on his knees and stared as a famished man stares at bread: at the golden hair, the tender skin, the cheeks like a ripe peach. “Let me feast my sad eyes on thee ere thou leavest me for thine ever-blessed abode, and my cell darkens again at thy parting, as it did at hers.”

The lovely visitor stirred. Two eyes opened, sky-coloured and astonished; and seeing a wild figure kneeling close, armour glinting in candlelight, the child’s mouth puckered and he cried out, piteously, with the whole hunger of a little life:

“MUMMA! MUM-MA!”

Tears ran down his cheeks. The spell of angel-vision broke at once, yet not unhappily. Clement—who had not looked on a human face for six months and more—was perhaps the more just in his estimate of childish beauty. In truth, this fair northern child, with long hair like flax in sun, looked more like any angel men have ever truly wanted than the painted cherubs of churches.

The innocent distress went through the hermit’s heart. He was fond of children, and the monastic life, for all its harshness, often keeps a soft place for them as a hedge keeps a quiet nest. He used all his gentleness, all his small art of coaxing and making peace. He spoke low—low enough that the cave itself seemed to listen rather than echo. The child, wonderfully quick for his years, gulped down his sobs; wonder slid in where fear had been. With great wet eyes he studied the strange man who looked savage and yet spoke kindly; who wore metal and yet did not strike, but soothed.

Clement himself was at a loss how this little human flower had come blooming into his stony dark. He remembered the door left wide. A shameful thought came: that some unhappy creature, high or low, had taken the open hermitage as a place to leave a child and never return. At that, his bowels yearned so over the poor abandoned cherub that tears of tenderness stood in his eyes.

Yet even in the mother’s crime he saw, with that monkish habit of reading Providence in all things, the strange divine goodness that had guided such heartlessness to comfort His servant’s breaking heart.

“Now bless thee, bless thee, bless thee, sweet innocent,” he murmured. “I would not change thee for e’en a cherub in heaven.”

“At’s pooty,” the child replied, as infants do, ignoring all that did not touch his curiosity.

“What is pretty here, my love, besides thee?”

“Ookum-gars,” said the boy, pointing to the breastplate.

Clement half smiled despite himself. The sound of metal had caught the child as surely as a bright trencher catches a hungry eye. Hector’s babe had screamed at a glittering helm; this medieval little one was charmed by a polished cuirass, and his griefs eased by its shine.

“There are prettier things here than that,” Clement said. “There are little birds; lovest thou birds?”

“Nay. Ay. En um ittle, ery ittle? Not ike torks. Hate torks—um bigger an baby.”

Between broken words he confided that storks, with their great flapping wings, frightened him—darkened his small world with their shadow and clatter.

“Ay, but my birds are very little, and good, and oh, so pretty!”

“Den I ikes ’m,” said the child, dictating terms like a prince. Then his face puckered again. “I ont my mammy.”

“Alas, sweet dove! I doubt I shall have to fill her place as best I may. Hast thou no daddy as well as mammy, sweet one?”

At or within a breath of that question, another presence—unseen—caught every word as if it were bread to the starving. For the night had not ended for Margaret.

When she fled past Gerard, she was near mad, in that bleak state where love turns on itself like milk gone sour, and the mind whispers of throwing away what it cannot bear. She ran to Reicht Heynes, pale and trembling, flung her arms round the girl’s neck, and could only moan, “Oh, Reicht! oh, Reicht!”

Reicht kissed her and began to whimper; and the great mastiff beside them gave one long, human-sounding whine. Even his blunt sense knew sorrow was abroad.

“Oh, Reicht,” Margaret got out at last, choking, “see how he has served me!” She showed her hands, scraped and bleeding where she had fallen. “He threw me down—he was so eager to fly from me. He took me for a devil; he said I came to tempt him. Am I the woman to tempt a man? you know me, Reicht.”

“Nay, in sooth, sweet Mistress Margaret—the last i’ the world.”

“And he would not look at my child. I’ll fling myself and him into the Rotter this night.”

“Oh, fie! fie! eh, my sweet woman, speak not so. Is any man that breathes worth your child’s life?”

“My child!” Margaret started as if struck. “Where is he? Why, Reicht, I have left him behind. Oh, shame! Is it possible I can love him to that degree as to forget my child? Ah, I am rightly served for it.”

She sat down, and faithful Reicht beside her, and they sobbed in one another’s arms. After a while the sobbing dried into something harder.

“Let us go home,” Margaret said, dogged.

“Ay, but the bairn?”

“Oh! he is well where he is.” Bitterness made her reckless. “My heart is turned against my very child. He cares nought for him; wouldn’t see him, nor hear speak of him. And I took him there so proud, and made his hair so nice—I did—and put his new frock and cowl on him. Nay, turn about: it’s his child as well as mine; let him keep it awhile. Mayhap that will learn him to think more of its mother—and his own.”

“High words off an empty stomach,” said Reicht, practical as bread. “Time will show. Come you home.”

They took two steps, and Time showed quicker than it levels abbeys. Margaret stopped. She could neither go nor turn, but stood like a post in the road.

“Reicht,” she said, her voice suddenly small, “what else have I on earth? I cannot.”

“Whoever said you could?” Reicht answered, with a tenderness hidden under plain speech. “Think you I paid attention? Words are woman’s breath. Come back for him without more ado; ’tis time we were in our beds—much more he.”

Reicht led, and Margaret followed readily enough in that direction. Yet as they drew near the hermitage she halted again, dread and pride tangled.

“Reicht, go you and ask him, will he give me back my boy—for I could not bear the sight of him.”

“Alas, mistress, this do seem a sorry ending after all that hath been betwixt you twain. Bethink thee now: doth thine heart whisper no excuse for him? Dost verily hate him for whom thou hast waited so long? Oh, weary world!”

“Hate him, Reicht?” Margaret’s bitterness cracked and showed the love beneath, raw and bleeding. “I would not harm a hair of his head for all that is in nature; but look on him I cannot. I have taken a horror of him. Oh! when I think of all I have suffered for him—and what I came here this night to do for him—and brought my own darling to kiss him and call him father…” Her voice broke, and her mind, in its pain, ran backward in a circle to another hurt. “Ah, Luke, my poor chap, my wound showeth me thine. I have thought too little of thy pangs, whose true affection I despised; and now my own is despised. Reicht, if the poor lad was here now, he would have a good chance.”

“Well, he is not far off,” said Reicht; but she said it without alacrity, as if that nearness was a danger.

“Speak not to me of any man,” Margaret said fiercely. “I hate them all.”

“For the sake of one?”

“Flout me not. But prithee go forward and get me what is my own—my sole joy in the world. Thou knowest I am on thorns till I have him to my bosom again.”

Reicht went forward. Margaret sat by the roadside, covered her face with her apron, and rocked herself as her people did when grief would not be still. She was so deep in that inward storm she did not hear Reicht running back; she started when the young woman laid a hand upon her shoulder.

“Mistress Margaret,” said Reicht quietly, “take a fool’s advice that loves ye. Go softly to yon cave, wi’ all the ears and eyes your mother ever gave you.”

“Why? Reicht?” Margaret stammered.

“I thought the cave was afire, ’twas so light inside; and there were voices.”

“Voices?”

“Ay, not one, but twain, and all unlike—a man’s and a little child’s—talking as pleasant as you and me. I am no great hand at a keyhole for my part; ’tis paltry work. But if so be voices were a-talking in yon cave, and them that owned those voices were so near to me as those are to thee, I’d go on all fours like a fox, and I’d crawl on my belly like a serpent, ere I’d lose one word that passes atwixt those twain.”

“Whisht, Reicht! Bless thee! Bide thou here. Buss me—pray for me!”

Almost ere the words were out, Margaret flew toward the hermitage as noiselessly as a lapwing. Near it she crouched, and the old instinct of hiding—learned in childhood games and in womanhood’s necessities—made her movements supple and silent. She reached the door, found a chink, and listened.

More than once it cost her a struggle not to burst in; but defeat had taught her caution. She held herself like a drawn bow, resolute to let the moment ripen. After a while, slowly, she lifted her head to the widest crack and looked, listening with her whole soul.

Inside, the child—asked whether he had no daddy—shook his head at first, refusing. Then, pressed gently, he seemed to remember what some foolish tongue had told him, and said, in his broken speech, “Dad-da ill man; run away and left poor mum-ma.”

Margaret, outside, winced as if the words had struck her cheek. It was new to her as to Clement.

Clement burst out, all heat and indignation, the kind that is as much tenderness as anger. “The villain! the monster! He must be born without bowels to desert thee, sweet one. Ah! he little knows the joy he has turned his back on. Well, my little dove, I must be father and mother to thee, since the one runs away and t’other abandons thee to my care. Now to-morrow I shall ask the good people that bring me my food to fetch some nice eggs and milk for thee as well; for bread is good enough for poor old good-for-nothing me, but not for thee. And I shall teach thee to read.”

“I can yead, I can yead.”

“Ay, verily—so young? All the better; we will read good books together, and I shall show thee the way to heaven.” His voice softened, as if he were stirring something gentle in a pot, careful not to scorch it. “Heaven is a beautiful place—a thousand times fairer and better than earth—and there be little cherubs like thyself in white, glad to welcome thee and love thee. Wouldst like to go to heaven one day?”

“Ay, along wi’-my-mammy.”

“What, not without her then?”

“Nay. I ont my mammy. Where is my mammy?”

Oh, what it cost poor Margaret not to fling the door wide and gather him up, to feel the weight of him, warm and living, and smell his hair that she had smoothed and kissed.

“Well, fret not, sweetheart,” Clement said. “Mayhap she will come when thou art asleep. Wilt thou be good now and sleep?”

“I not eepy. Ikes to talk.”

“Well, talk we then. Tell me thy pretty name.”

“Baby.” The child stared in amazement at Clement’s ignorance.

“Hast none other?”

“Nay.”

“What shall I do to pleasure thee, baby? Shall I tell thee a story?”

“I ikes tories,” said the boy, clapping his hands.

“Or sing thee a song?”

“I ikes tongs,” and excitement shook him.

“Choose then, a song or a story.”

“Ting I a tong. Nay, tell I a tory. Nay, ting I a tong. Nay—” The corners of his little mouth turned down; he had half a mind to cry at the injustice of choice. Then his face cleared with sudden invention. “Ting I a tory,” he pronounced.

“Sing thee a story?” Clement laughed softly—softly, as if laughter were a fragile vessel in that place. “Well, after all, why not? And wilt thou sit o’ my knee and hear it?”

“Yea.”

“Then I must e’en doff this breastplate; ’tis too hard for thy soft cheek.” He unbuckled it with care; the metal gave a small clink that the cave caught and held. “And now I must doff this bristly cilice; it would prick thy tender skin, perhaps make it bleed—as they have me, I see.” He drew it off and shivered, not only from cold but from the sudden absence of pain. “And now I put on my best pelisse, in honour of thy worshipful visit. See how soft and warm it is; bless the good soul that sent it. And now I sit me down; so. And I take thee on my left knee, and put my arm under thy little head; so. And then the psaltery, and play a little tune—so, not too loud.”

“I ikes dat.”

“I am right glad on’t. Now list the story.”

He began to chant—a child’s tale set to a kind of recitative, with a little moral refrain returning now and then like a familiar scent in a kitchen. The boy listened with rapture.

“I ikes oo,” he said. “Ot is oo? Is oo a man?”

“Ay, little heart—and a great sinner to boot.”

“I ikes great tingers. Ting one other tory.”

He chanted a second. The child’s gaze never left his face, as if the voice were food.

“I ubbs oo,” the boy cried suddenly, impetuous as a spark. “Ot caft is oo?”

“I am a hermit, love.”

“I ubbs vermins. Ting other one.”

During the third, Nature lifted her leaden sceptre over the youthful eyelids. “I is not eepy,” he whined faintly, and surrendered.

Clement laid down the psaltery as one lays down a knife beside sleeping bread, and began to rock his new treasure in his arms. He crooned over him a lullaby well known in Tergou, the very tune his own mother had used, as natural to him as kneading. The child sank deep into sleep upon his arm.

Clement stopped singing and gazed on him with infinite tenderness and a sadness that went behind words. For in that moment, as the boy’s breath made its little regular music, he could not help thinking what might have been—what family sounds might have filled a house—had there not been a piece of paper with a lie in it. He sighed, and the sigh was like wind through bare branches.

The next moment, moonlight burst into the cell, and with it, and in it, swift as it, Margaret Brandt was down at his knee, her hand timorous upon his shoulder.

“GERARD, YOU DO NOT REJECT US—you cannot.”
