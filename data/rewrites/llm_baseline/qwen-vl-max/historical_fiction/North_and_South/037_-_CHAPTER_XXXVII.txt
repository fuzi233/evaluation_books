“A spade! a rake! a hoe!
A pickaxe or a bill!
A hook to reap, or a scythe to mow,
A flail or what ye will—
And here’s a ready hand
To ply the needful tool,
And skilled enough, by lessons rough,
In Labour’s rugged school.”

The next day, Higgins’s door was locked when Margaret and her father called on Mrs. Boucher. A neighbour, eager to be helpful, informed them that he had indeed left home earlier that morning. But before departing, he had stopped by to see the widow—though no one could say what business had taken him away.

It was a visit that brought little comfort to Mrs. Boucher. She saw herself as a victim of cruel fate, unjustly abandoned by her husband’s suicide. There was truth in her sorrow—enough to make it hard to argue against—but her grief was tangled with self-absorption. Her thoughts turned inward, fixed upon her own suffering, and even her children seemed burdens rather than blessings, though she loved them fiercely in a way that felt raw and instinctive.

Margaret tried to connect with the children, to draw them into conversation. They were more honest mourners than their mother. Each child remembered something kind Daddy had done—how he’d let them stay up late, how he’d shared his food, how he’d tucked them in with a gentle word. One whispered, trembling, “Is yon thing upstairs really him? It doesna look like him. I’m feared on it, and I never was feared o’ daddy.”

Margaret’s heart ached. The mother had dragged the children to see their dead father, disfigured by water and time—mixing horror with grief, turning natural sorrow into something grotesque. She gently steered the children toward action: what they could do for their mother now, what their father would have wanted them to do. They responded—not with words, but with quiet effort, helping clean the messy room, each trying to carry out her suggestion.

But Mr. Hale struggled. He spoke of duty, virtue, divine mercy—abstract ideals too distant for a woman whose mind was numb with pain and resentment. She couldn’t imagine her husband’s despair; she only felt its consequences. She refused to accept that he had been driven to desperation. Instead, she blamed everyone else—the masters, especially Mr. Thornton, whose mill had been attacked; the Union, represented by Higgins; the children themselves, so many and so hungry. All of them formed an army of enemies who had forced her husband into ruin.

Margaret listened, growing weary. When they returned home, she found herself unable to lift her father’s spirits.

“It is the town life,” she said quietly. “People are stirred by constant motion, noise, confinement. These houses, shut tight and dark, wear down the soul. In the country, people live outside—even in winter. Children play in fields, breathe fresh air. There’s space to think, to rest.”

“But people must live in towns,” replied her father. “And in the country, some grow stagnant—fatalists, almost, who see no future beyond the next meal.”

“Yes,” she agreed. “Each life brings its own trials. Town dwellers struggle with impatience, overwhelmed by the rush of every moment. Country folk may fall into dullness, content with mere survival, without hope or plan. Both fail to imagine the future—townsfolk because the present is too loud, country folk because they’ve forgotten there *is* a future.”

“And so both end in the same place,” he murmured. “Yet this poor woman—what can we do for her?”

“Nothing, perhaps,” Margaret said softly. “But we cannot stop trying. Even if our efforts seem useless—we must go on. Oh, Papa… it’s such a hard world.”

“So it is,” he answered. “But we’ve been happy, even through our sorrow. Remember Frederick’s visit?”

“Yes,” she said, brightening. “It was lovely—brief, secret, forbidden.”

Then she stopped. A wave of shame washed over her. She had lied to protect herself. And she despised cowardice above all things. To lie—especially to someone like Mr. Thornton—was a betrayal of everything she believed in. She thought of him now, wondering how he might judge her. Would it have mattered less if it had been anyone else? She imagined telling Aunt Shaw, Edith, Captain Lennox, Frederick. The thought of Frederick knowing—of him seeing her weakness—hurt deeply. But worse still was the fear of facing Mr. Thornton again. Not just shame, but humiliation. She could not bear the idea of meeting his eyes, knowing he saw her as dishonest.

And yet—she longed to see him. To know where she stood. To face the truth.

Her cheeks burned as she recalled their early conversations. How proudly she had spoken of trade, condemning it for deceit—passing off inferior goods, pretending wealth one didn’t possess. She remembered his calm reply: that in the long run, dishonourable acts always failed. That success built on lies was folly. That honesty was not merely a moral ideal, but a practical necessity.

She had challenged him once—asking if buying cheap and selling dear wasn’t itself a failure of justice. She used the word *chivalric*. Her father corrected her: “Christian.” And she had sat back, feeling a flicker of superiority.

No more. Now, she would feel humbled. Disgraced. And she knew it.

When the doorbell rang, her heart leapt. Then sank. Again and again. She waited—hoping, dreading. Her father expected Mr. Thornton. He had looked forward to their promised meeting, to continuing their talk about society, religion, labor, and the meaning of justice. He had been restless all evening, certain the visitor would come.

“I quite expected to see Mr. Thornton,” he said. “Perhaps the messenger forgot a note last night. Has anything arrived today?”

“I’ll check, Papa,” Margaret said. Then—“Stay! There’s a ring!”

She sat down quickly, pretending to work. A step sounded on the stairs—only one. Dixon.

“Sarvant, sir!” Dixon announced. “He wants to see you—or Miss Hale. Or maybe Miss Hale first, then you. He’s in a queer state.”

“Let him come up,” said Mr. Hale. “We’ll both be here. He can choose.”

“Oh, very well,” Dixon sighed. “I don’t care to hear what he has to say. If you could see his shoes, you’d say the kitchen was better suited.”

“He can wipe them,” said Mr. Hale.

Dixon went to fetch him. But when Higgins reached the bottom stair, he paused. He took off his muddy boots, walked up barefoot, and entered silently.

“Sarvant, sir,” he said, smoothing his hair. “If hoo’l excuse me (looking at Margaret) for being i’ my stockings—I’ve been tramping all day. Streets aren’t clean.”

Margaret noticed his fatigue, his unusual quietness. He seemed unsure of himself.

Mr. Hale, always kind to those who hesitated, offered tea. “You’re tired, I’m sure. Margaret, dear, can’t you hasten it?”

She did—though it meant taking over from Dixon, who was still grieving for her former mistress and now irritable. But Martha, like others drawn to Margaret, willingly helped. Soon, even Dixon softened.

“Why master and you must always bring the lower classes up-stairs since we came to Milton, I cannot understand,” she muttered. “At Helstone, folk weren’t brought higher than the kitchen. I’ve told a few they might count it an honour just to be there.”

Higgins found it easier to speak alone. After Margaret left, he closed the door, stepped close to Mr. Hale.

“Master,” he began, “yo’d not guess what I’ve been doing today. Especially if yo’d heard me yesterday. I’ve been seeking work. I have.”

He paused. “I said to myself, I’d keep a civil tongue in my head, no matter what. I’d set my teeth into my tongue before speaking in haste. For that man’s sake—yo’ understand.”

“No,” said Mr. Hale, baffled. “Who is ‘that man’?”

“That chap as lies theer,” Higgins said, jerking his thumb. “Him as drowned himself. Poor chap! I never thought he’d let the water take him so still. Boucher, yo’ know.”

“Yes,” said Mr Hale. “Go on.”

“For his sake. Yet not for his sake—he’s gone, wherever he is. But for the wife’s sake. For the bits o’ childer.”

“God bless you,” Mr. Hale breathed, rising. Then, calming himself: “What do you mean? Tell me plainly.”

“I’ve told yo’,” Higgins said, surprised by his emotion. “I wouldn’t ask for work for myself. But them’s left as a charge on me. I reckon I should’ve guided Boucher better. I led him off the road—and so I must answer for it.”

Mr. Hale seized his hand, gripping it tightly. Higgins looked embarrassed, ashamed.

“Theer, theer,” he stammered. “There’s ne’er a man among us who wouldn’t do the same—ay, better. But I’ve got no work. None. I told Hamper I’d be good for his mill—better than any. But he wouldn’t have me. Nor any other. I’m a poor black feckless sheep. Childer may clem for ought I can do—unless, parson, yo’d help me?”

“Help you?” Mr. Hale asked. “How? I would do anything—but what can I do?”

“Miss there”—Margaret had returned—“has often talked o’ the South. The ways down there. I dunnot know how far it is, but I’ve been thinking—if I could get ’em down theer, where food’s cheap and wages good, and folks rich and poor, master and man, friendly like—yo’ could help me find work. I’m not forty-five. I’ve strength in me, measter.”

“What kind of work could you do?” asked Mr. Hale.

“Well, I reckon I could spade a bit—”

“And for that,” Margaret interrupted, stepping forward, “you might earn nine shillings a week—maybe ten. Food’s much the same. You might have a garden.”

“The childer could work at that,” he said.

“I’m sick o’ Milton anyways,” he added. “Milton is sick o’ me.”

“You must not go south,” Margaret said firmly. “You could not endure it. You’d be out in all weather. It would kill you with rheumatism. The work—so hard, so monotonous—would break you down. The food is different. You’re used to butcher’s meat once a day. Pay for that out of ten shillings? Keep those children? I owe it to you—to tell you plainly. You’d hate the dullness. Those who live there all their lives are used to it—they soak in stagnant waters. They labour endlessly in vast, steaming fields—never speaking, heads bent low. The work robs their minds. Their imaginations die. They don’t care to talk after work—just want food and sleep. You could never stir them into companionship—something you have in towns as naturally as air. And you, of all men, could not stand such isolation. What is peace to them would be torment to you. Don’t think of it, Nicholas. Besides—you could never afford to move your family. That’s one good thing.”

“I’ve reckoned that,” he said. “One house would do. Furniture from the other would help. Men there have families—six or seven children. God help ’em.”

He fell silent, suddenly convinced. The dream dissolved.

“God help ’em,” he repeated. “North and South—each has its own troubles. Where wages are high, work is scarce. Where work is steady, wages starve. The world’s in confusion—beyond understanding. It needs fixing. But who’s to fix it, if all we see is what we see?”

Mr. Hale cut bread and butter. Margaret was glad—he kept the conversation light. Higgins ate mechanically, barely aware. Then, slowly, he pushed his chair back.

Suddenly, Margaret said—her voice trembling slightly—“Higgins, have you been to Marlborough Mills to seek work?”

“Thornton’s?” he asked.

“Ay, I’ve been at Thornton’s.”

“And what did he say?”

“Such a chap as me isn’t likely to see the master. Th’ overlooker bid me go and be d——d.”

“I wish you had seen Mr. Thornton,” said Mr. Hale. “He might not have given you work—but he wouldn’t have spoken like that.”

“As to language,” Higgins said, “I’m welly used to it. Doesn’t matter. But the fact—that I were na wanted—no more than anywhere else—that’s what hurt.”

“I wish you had seen Mr. Thornton,” Margaret repeated.

“Would you go again?” she asked. “It’s a lot to ask—but would you try tomorrow? I’d be so glad if you would.”

“I’m afraid it would be no use,” said Mr. Hale softly.

“It would be better if I spoke to him,” he added.

Margaret looked at Higgins. His grave, soft eyes were hard to resist.

He sighed deeply. “It would tax my pride. If it were for myself, I could bear a deal. I’d sooner knock him down than beg. I’d sooner be flogged. But yo’re not a common wench—asking pardon. Yo’r ways are different. I’ll e’en make a wry face and go at it tomorrow. Dunna yo’ think he’ll do it. That man has it in him to be burnt at the stake afore he’ll give in. I’ll do it for yo’r sake, Miss Hale. First time in my life I’ve given way to a woman. Neither my wife nor Bess could ever say that.”

“All the more do I thank you,” Margaret smiled.

“Though I don’t believe you,” she added. “I believe you’ve given way to wife and daughter as much as most men.”

“And as to Mr. Thornton,” said Mr. Hale, “I’ll give you a note. I think I may venture to say it will ensure you a hearing.”

“Thank yo’ kindly, sir,” Higgins said. “But I’d as lief stand on my own bottom. I dunnot stomach the notion of having favour curried for me by one who doesn’t know the ins and outs. Meddling ’twixt master and man is liker meddling ’twixt husband and wife than aught else. It takes wisdom to do good. I’ll stand guard at the lodge door—from six in the morning till I get speech. But I’d liefer sweep th’ streets, if paupers hadn’t taken that work. Dunna yo’ hope, miss. There’ll be more chance o’ getting milk out of a flint. I wish yo’ a very good night, and many thanks.”

“You’ll find your shoes by the kitchen fire,” Margaret said. “I took them to dry.”

He turned, looked at her steadily, brushed his hand across his eyes, and left.

“How proud that man is!” said her father, annoyed by Higgins’s refusal of help.

“He is,” Margaret said. “But what grand makings of a man there are in him—pride and all.”

“It’s amusing to see how he respects the part of Mr. Thornton’s character that’s like his own.”

“There’s granite in all these northern people, papa, is there not?”

“There was none in poor Boucher,” he said. “None in his wife.”

“I should guess by their tones they had Irish blood. I wonder what success he’ll have tomorrow. If he and Mr. Thornton could speak as man to man—if Higgins would forget Mr. Thornton was a master, and speak to him as he does to us—and if Mr. Thornton would listen with his human heart, not his master’s ears—”

“You are beginning to do Mr. Thornton justice at last, Margaret,” said her father, pinching her ear.

Margaret felt a strange choking in her chest. She couldn’t speak.

“Oh,” she thought, “if only I were a man. Then I could go to him, force him to express his disapproval, and tell him honestly that I deserved it. It seems cruel to lose him as a friend—just when I began to value him. How tender he was with dear mamma! If only for her sake, I wish he would come. Then I would know how much I am abased in his eyes.”
