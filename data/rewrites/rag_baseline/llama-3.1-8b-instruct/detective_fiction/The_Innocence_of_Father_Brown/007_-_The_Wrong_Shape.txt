The village of Clapham lay shrouded in the soft, golden light of late afternoon, its quiet streets lined with the warm tones of old stone and the gentle scent of blooming flowers. The air was heavy with the promise of Whitsuntide, a time of celebration and renewal, as if the very atmosphere itself was alive with anticipation. Amidst this tranquil backdrop, a lone figure emerged from the front door of a house that seemed frozen in time – a house that defied the conventions of its surroundings, its white and pale green façade radiating an aura of exoticism, as if plucked from the sun-kissed streets of India.

This was the residence of the enigmatic Leonard Quinton, a poet whose works were known for their vivid tapestry of colours and imagery. His home, with its sweeping veranda and sun-blinds, was an extension of his art, a space where the boundaries between reality and fantasy blurred. The house was an unusual sight, its long, low profile seeming to stretch out along the street like a languid cat, punctuated by the occasional glimpse of a brightly coloured flower or the delicate curve of a ceramic vase.

As the front door swung open, a faint scent of jasmine wafted out, mingling with the cool breeze carrying the whispers of the passing villagers. Father Brown, the affable priest of St. Mungo, stepped out onto the porch, his pipe clenched between his teeth as he exchanged a few words with his friend, the tall and elegant Flambeau. The Frenchman's slender frame seemed almost out of place amidst the sturdy, rustic charm of the English countryside, yet there was an air of familiarity about him, as if he had always belonged here, just as much as the house itself.

Those who passed by the house that day, on the Thursday before Whit-Sunday, would have noticed the peculiar arrangement of the interior, a layout that defied the conventional expectations of a typical English home. The house was built in the shape of a T, with a long, two-story cross-piece facing the street and a shorter, single-story tail-piece stretching out at the back. The front door led into a spacious hall, which in turn gave way to a series of interconnected rooms, each one a world unto itself.
