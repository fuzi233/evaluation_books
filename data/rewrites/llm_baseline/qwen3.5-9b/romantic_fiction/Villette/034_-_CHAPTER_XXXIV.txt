On a Thursday afternoon, Madame Beck summoned me, asking if I had any duties preventing me from venturing into town to handle a few small errands for her. Since I was free, I readily placed myself at her disposal. She handed me a list of materials needed for the students' projects—wools, silks, embroidery threads—and I prepared accordingly, dressing suitably for the ominous, sultry weather. As I reached for the latch of the street door, Madame's voice recalled me to the dining room.

"Pardon me, Miss Lucy!" she exclaimed, feigning the sudden inspiration of an impromptu thought. "I have just remembered one final task for you, provided your good nature doesn't feel overburdened?"

Naturally, I assured her otherwise with vigorous agreement. Madame then darted into the small salon and returned carrying a delicate basket overflowing with exquisite hothouse fruits—rosy, perfect, and inviting—nestled among dark green, wax-like leaves and pale yellow blossoms of some exotic plant.

"There," she said, "it isn't heavy, and won't mar your neat attire like a mundane household chore. Please deliver this basket to Madame Walravens with my congratulations on her birthday. She resides in the Old Town, Number 3, Rue des Mages. I suspect the walk will be lengthy, but you have the entire afternoon; do not rush. If you aren't back for dinner, I'll have a meal saved, or Goton, who adores you, will toss up something special for your enjoyment. You shall not be forgotten, my dear Miss Lucy. And oh, please!" (she called me back once more) "insist on seeing Madame Walravens personally and handing the basket directly to her. She is a very particular person. Adieu! Au revoir!"

Finally, I departed. The shopping proved tedious; selecting and matching silks and wools is always a laborious affair. Eventually, I completed the list: patterns for slippers, bell ropes, and bags were chosen; slides and tassels for purses were picked. The entire "scramble" was finished; only the delivery of the fruit and my felicitations remained.

I actually enjoyed the prospect of a long stroll deep into the grim, ancient Basse-Ville. The mood wasn't worsened by the evening sky, which settled into a mass of black-blue metal, glowing red-hot at the edges.

I feared a strong wind, for storms demand physical exertion—a struggle against nature that I always find painful. However, driving rain, heavy snow, or dark deluges require only resignation—the quiet surrender of one's clothes and body to be soaked. In return, they sweep a great capital clean, carving a silent path through broad, majestic streets. They petrify a living city as if by Eastern enchantment, transforming Villette into Tadmor. So, let the rains fall and the floods rise—but first, I must shed this basket of fruit.

An unknown clock from an unseen tower tolled the quarter-past-five (Jean Baptiste's voice was now too faint to hear). Upon reaching the street and house Madame Beck described, I realized it was hardly a street but rather part of a square. It was quiet; grass grew between the broad grey flagstones. The houses were large and ancient, backed by trees suggesting private gardens. An air of antiquity brooded over this district; commerce had been banished. Once, wealthy men and grandeur had held court here. The church, with its dark, half-ruinous turrets overlooking the square, was the venerable, formerly opulent shrine of the Magi. But wealth and greatness had long since spread their gilded wings and flown away, leaving these ancient nests perhaps to house poverty temporarily, or to stand cold and empty, crumbling untended through winter.

Crossing this deserted "place," where raindrops the size of five-franc pieces slowly darkened the pavement, I saw no sign of life save for an infirm old priest passing by, bent and leaning on a staff—the embodiment of age and decay. He emerged from the very house to which I was directed. When I paused before the door, which had just closed after him, and rang the bell, he turned to look at me. He did not quickly avert his gaze; perhaps he thought my basket of summer fruit and my lack of the dignity usually associated with age made me an incongruous figure in such a setting. Had a young, ruddy-faced maid opened the door, I might have felt similarly out of place. But finding myself confronted by a very old woman dressed in an antique peasant costume—a hideous yet costly cap with long flaps of native lace, a cloth petticoat and jacket, and wooden clogs resembling tiny boats—it all seemed fitting, soothingly in character.

Her facial expression, however, was less comforting than her attire. She was the most cantankerous creature I had ever seen, barely replying to my inquiries about Madame Walravens. She likely would have snatched the fruit basket from my hand had not the old priest, hobbling forward, intervened. He checked her and listened to my message himself.

His apparent deafness made understanding my instructions difficult. I had to emphasize that I must see Madame Walravens and personally entrust the fruit to her. Finally, he grasped the necessity of fulfilling my orders literally. Speaking to the aged woman not in French, but in the indigenous language of Labassecour, he persuaded her to let me pass the inhospitable threshold. Escorting me upstairs, he ushered me into a sort of salon and left me there.

The room was spacious, featuring a magnificent old ceiling and nearly cathedral-like stained-glass windows. Yet, it was desolate, and under the looming storm, it looked strangely oppressive. Inside, a smaller room opened; however, the blind covering its single window was drawn tight. Through the deep gloom, few details of the furniture were visible. I amused myself trying to discern them, particularly drawn to the outline of a picture on the wall.

Suddenly, the picture seemed to dissolve. To my bewilderment, it shook, sank, and rolled backward into nothingness. Its disappearance revealed an arched opening leading to a winding stone passage and a mysterious spiral staircase. Both were of cold, uncarpeted, unpainted stone. Down this dungeon-like stair descended a *tap, tap* sound, like a stick striking steps. Soon, a shadow fell upon the stairs, followed by a substance.

Was this actual substance, this apparition approaching me? This obstruction partially darkening the arch?

It drew nearer, and I saw it clearly. I began to understand where I was. Indeed, this old square deserved its name as the Quarter of the Magi; indeed, the three towers overlooking it rightly claimed three mystical sages of a dead, dark art as their godfathers. Here, hoarfrost enchantment prevailed. A spell had opened a portal to elf-land—that cell-like room, that vanishing picture, that arch and stone passage, and stone stair were all parts of a fairy tale. Even clearer than these scenic details stood the central figure: Cunegonde, the sorceress! Malevola, the evil fairy. What form did she take?

She might have been three feet tall, yet she possessed no defined shape. Her skinny hands rested together, pressing the golden knob of an ivory, wand-like staff. Her face was large and set, positioned before her chest rather than upon her shoulders; she seemed to have no neck. One would say there were a hundred years in her features, and perhaps more in her eyes—her malignant, unfriendly eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. With what severe, dull displeasure she regarded me!

This being wore a brocade gown dyed a brilliant blue, as intense as the gentianella flower, covered in a large pattern of satin foliage. Over it draped a costly shawl, gorgeously bordered and so vast for her stature that its multi-colored fringe swept the floor. But her defining feature was her jewelry: long, clear earrings blazing with a lustre that could not be fake; rings on her skeletal hands with thick gold hoops and stones of purple, green, and blood-red. Hunchbacked, dwarfish, and doting, she was adorned like a barbarian queen.

"What do you want of me?" she rasped, with a voice possessing the roughness of male rather than female old age. Indeed, a silver beard bristled her chin.

"I bring a gift and a message," I replied.

"That is all?" she demanded.

"It is all," I said.

"Truly, it was well worth the trip," she answered. "Return to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy fruit whenever I wish, and regarding her congratulations, I mock them!" With that, the courteous lady turned her back.

Just as she turned, a peal of thunder shattered the air, and a flash of lightning blazed broadly across the salon and boudoir. The tale of magic proceeded with the proper accompaniment of the elements. The wanderer, lured into the enchanted castle, heard the spell-wakened tempest rising outside.

What, in all this, was I to think of Madame Beck? She possessed strange acquaintances; she offered messages and gifts at a unique shrine, and the bearing of the uncouth thing she worshipped seemed inauspicious. There went that sullen Sidonia, tottering and trembling like embodied paralysis, tapping her ivory staff on the mosaic floor and muttering venomously as she vanished.
