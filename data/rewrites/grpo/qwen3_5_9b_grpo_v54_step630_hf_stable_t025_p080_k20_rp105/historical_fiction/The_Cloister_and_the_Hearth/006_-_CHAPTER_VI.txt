"Look into your own heart and write!" cried Herr Cant, and the cuckoos of the earth seemed to echo his command. Delve into the deepest currents of the Rhine or the thickest waters of the Thames, and you shall find the truth painted upon the bottom. Cast a bucket into a well of self-deception, and whatever rises must be immortal verity, must it not? Yet, no son of Adam ever truly reads his own heart unless he has first studied the hearts of others for years, acquiring a sagacity that allows him merely to decipher his own soul rather than read it fluently. Halfway to Sevenbergen, Gerard cast his gaze inward and asked his heart why he journeyed there. The reply came without hesitation: "We go out of curiosity to know why she spurned us, to prove to her that our hearts remain unbroken, and that we are content with our future honors and benefice, needing neither her nor any ally of her fickle sex."

Soon, Gerard located Peter Brandt's cottage. A girl sat in the doorway, busily working with her needle, while a stalwart figure leaned against a long bow, conversing with her. At the sight of him, Gerard felt a sudden, inexplicable pang. The man, however, proved to be past fifty, an old soldier whose shooting skills Gerard remembered with admiration. A moment later, the youth stood before them. Margaret looked up, dropped her work, and let out a faint cry, her face flushing white and then red in rapid succession. Yet, these signs of emotion vanished swiftly, replaced by a chill indifference that would have been absent had she not betrayed such agitation.

"What! Is it you, Master Gerard? What on earth brings you here?" she asked.

"I was passing by and saw you; so I thought I would pay my respects and inquire after your father."

"My father is well. He will be here shortly."

"Then I may as well wait for his arrival."

"As you wish. Good Martin, go into the village and tell my father a friend has come."

"And not of yours?"

"My father's friends are mine."

"That is doubtful. It was not like a friend to promise to wait, only to vanish the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret, you little know how I searched the town for you; how, without you, nothing was pleasant to me."

"These are idle words. If you had truly desired my father's company or mine, you would have returned. I had a bed prepared for you at my cousin's, and he would have made much of you; perhaps I might have done so too. I was in the humor that day. You will not catch me in the same mind again, neither you nor any young man, I warrant."

"Margaret, I returned the moment the Countess released me, but you were not there."

"Nay, you did not, or you would have seen Hans Cloterman at our table; we left him to escort you."

"I saw no one there, only a drunken man who had just fallen."

"At our table? How was he dressed?"

"I paid little heed; he wore sad-colored garb."

At this, Margaret's face gradually warmed. Presently, assuming an air of incredulity and severity, she posed many shrewd questions, to which Gerard answered most loyally. Finally, the clouds cleared, and they deduced the source of the misunderstanding. Then came a revulsion of tenderness, all the more powerful because they had wronged one another, followed by mutual confessions. Neither had been happy since their parting; neither ever would have been so, but for this fortunate reunion.

Gerard found a manuscript of the Vulgate lying open on the table and pounced upon it like a hawk. Manuscripts were his delight, but before he could reach it, two white hands quickly covered the page, and a red face appeared above them.

"Nay, take away your hands, Margaret, that I may see where you are reading. I will read there too at home, so that my soul may meet yours in the sacred page. Will you not? Nay, then I must kiss them away." He kissed them so often that, overcome with shame, they were forced to withdraw. Lo! the sacred book lay open to the verse: "An apple of gold in a setting of silver."

"There, now," she said. "I had been hunting for it for so long, and found it but even now—and to be caught!" With a touch of inconsistency, she pointed it out to Gerard with her white finger.

"Ay," he replied, "but today it is all hidden beneath that great cap."

"It is a comely cap, I am told by some."

"Perhaps; but what it hides is beautiful."

"It is not; it is hideous."

"Well, it was beautiful at Rotterdam."

"Ay, everything was beautiful that day," she sighed softly.

Then Peter entered, welcoming Gerard cordially and inviting him to supper. Margaret disappeared, and Gerard enjoyed a learned conversation with Peter. Soon, Margaret reappeared, her hair arranged in a silver net, casting a glance that was half-arch, half-coy. She glided about them, spreading supper, and beamed with gaiety and happiness. In the cool evening, Gerard coaxed her out; she objected but came. He urged her onto the road to Tergou; she declined but came. They strolled up and down, hand in hand. When he must depart, they pledged each other never to quarrel or misunderstand one another again, sealing the promise with a long, loving kiss. Gerard went home on wings.

From that day forward, Gerard spent most of his evenings with Margaret. Their attachment deepened on both sides until the hours they shared were the hours they truly lived; the rest of time they merely endured. At the outset of this profound bond, all went smoothly. Obstacles existed, yet they seemed distant and small through the eyes of hope, youth, and love. The feelings and passions of so many others that this attachment might thwart gave no warning smoke to reveal their volcanic nature. The course of true love ran smoothly and placidly until it had drawn these two young hearts into its current forever.
