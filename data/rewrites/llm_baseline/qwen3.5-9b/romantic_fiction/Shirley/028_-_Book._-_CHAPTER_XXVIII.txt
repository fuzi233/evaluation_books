That evening, Shirley managed to get along quite well with Sir Philip. By the following morning, she descended to breakfast in one of her finest moods.

"Who will accompany me for a walk?" she asked immediately after eating. "Isabella and Gertrude, will you?"

Such an invitation from Miss Keeldar to her female cousins was so rare that the girls hesitated before accepting. However, upon receiving their mother's approval, they quickly gathered their bonnets, and the trio set off.

These three young women generally preferred not to spend much time together. Miss Keeldar enjoyed the company of very few ladies; indeed, she found genuine warmth only in the societies of Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. While she was invariably civil, kind, and attentive toward her cousins, she rarely had much to say to them. On this particular sunny morning, however, she somehow managed to entertain the Misses Sympson. Sticking to her usual habit of discussing only ordinary topics, she imbued them with an extraordinary interest; the sparkle of her spirit danced across her phrases.

What caused such joy? The source must have been entirely within herself. The day itself was not bright—it was dim, a pale, waning autumn afternoon. The paths through the brown woods were damp; the air was heavy, and the sky was overcast. Yet, it seemed that within Shirley's heart lived all the light and azure of Italy, while all its fervor laughed in her gray English eyes.

Necessary instructions for her foreman, John, held her back as her cousins approached Fieldhead on their return. Perhaps twenty minutes passed between her departure from them and her re-entry into the house. During this interval, she had spoken to John, then lingered in the lane at the gate. A summons to lunch called her inside. She excused herself from the meal and went upstairs.

"Isn't Shirley coming to lunch?" Isabella asked. "She said she was hungry."

An hour later, finding her still in her chamber, one of the cousins went to seek her. There she found Shirley sitting at the foot of the bed, her head resting on her hand. She looked quite pale, deeply thoughtful, and almost sad.

"Aren't you ill?" someone asked.

"A little sick," replied Miss Keeldar.

There was certainly a noticeable change in her compared to two hours prior. Yet, this transformation—accounted for by those three words alone, originating from wherever it did, and accomplished in a mere ten minutes—passed like a light summer cloud. When she rejoined her friends at dinner, she talked as usual. She stayed with them throughout the evening. When questioned again about her health, she declared herself perfectly recovered. It had been a mere passing faintness, a momentary sensation not worth mentioning; yet, everyone felt there was a difference in Shirley.

The next day—and the day after, and the week after, and the fortnight after—that new and peculiar shadow lingered on Miss Keeldar's countenance. A strange quietude settled over her expression, her movements, and even her voice. The alteration wasn't severe enough to invite constant questioning, yet it was undeniably there and refused to fade. It hung over her like a cloud that no breeze could stir or disperse. Soon it became clear that noticing this change annoyed her. At first, she shrank from remarks; if pursued further, she would, with her characteristic hauteur, repel them.

"Are you ill?" someone would ask.
"I _am not_," she would reply decisively.

"Does something weigh on your mind? Has something affected your spirits?"
She would scornfully ridicule the notion. "What do they mean by 'spirits'? I have no spirits, black or white, blue or gray, to be affected."

"Something must be the matter—you are so altered."
"She supposes she has a right to alter at her leisure. She knows she is plainer. If it suits her to grow ugly, why should others fret about it?"

"There must be a cause for the change. What is it?"
She would peremptorily demand to be left alone.

Then, she would make every effort to appear cheerful, seeming indignant at herself for failing to succeed perfectly. When alone, brief, self-reproaching epithets would burst from her lips. "Fool! Coward!" she would call herself. "Poltroon! If you must tremble, tremble in secret! Quail where no eye sees you!"

"How dare you," she would ask herself, "show your weakness and betray your imbecile anxieties? Shake them off; rise above them. If you cannot do this, hide them."

And so she hid them. She once again became resolutely lively in company. When weariness forced her to relax, she sought solitude—not the gloomy isolation of her room (for she refused to mope shut away between four walls), but the wilder solitude found outdoors, which she could chase while mounted on Zoë, her mare. She took long rides lasting half a day. Her uncle disapproved but dared not remonstrate. It was never pleasant to face Shirley's anger, even when she was healthy and gay; but now, with her face appearing thin and her large eye looking hollow, there was something in the darkening of that face and the kindling of that eye that was both touching and alarming.

To all comparative strangers who, unaware of the changes in her spirits, commented on the alteration in her appearance, she gave one reply:
"I am perfectly well; I have no ailment."

Indeed, she must have possessed robust health to endure the exposure to the weather she now encountered. Whether wet or fair, calm or stormy, she took her daily ride over Stilbro' Moor, Tartar keeping pace beside her with his wolf-like gallop, long and untiring.

Two or three times, the prying eyes of gossipers—those eyes that are everywhere, in closets and on hilltops—observed that instead of turning toward Rushedge, the top ridge of Stilbro' Moor, she rode straight ahead all the way to the town. Scouts were eager to note her destination. It was ascertained that she dismounted at the door of Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor related to the vicar of Nunnely. This gentleman and his ancestors had served as agents for the Keeldar family for generations. Some claimed Miss Keeldar had become entangled in business speculations concerning Hollow's Mill—that she had lost money and was forced to mortgage her land. Others conjectured she was about to marry and that settlements were being prepared.

Mr. Moore and Henry Sympson were together in the schoolroom. The tutor was waiting for a lesson the pupil seemed busy preparing.

"Henry, hasten. The afternoon is progressing."
"Is it, sir?"
"Certainly. Are you nearly ready with that lesson?"
"No."
"Not _nearly_ ready?"
"I have not construed a single line."

Mr. Moore looked up. The boy's tone was peculiar.
"The task presents no difficulties, Henry; or if it does, bring them to me. We will work together."
"Mr. Moore, I cannot do any work."
"My boy, you are ill."
"Sir, I am not worse in bodily health than usual, but my heart is full."

"Close the book. Come here, Harry. Come to the fireside."
Harry limped forward. His tutor placed him in a chair; his lips quivered, his eyes brimmed with tears. He laid his crutch on the floor, bowed his head, and wept.

"This distress is not caused by physical pain, you say, Harry? You have a grief; tell it to me."
"Sir, I have such a grief as I have never known before. I wish it could be relieved somehow; I can hardly bear it."
"Who knows but that, if we discuss it, we may relieve it? What is the cause? Whom does it concern?"
"The cause, sir, is Shirley; it concerns Shirley."
"Does it? You think she has changed?"
"All who know her think she has changed—even you, Mr. Moore."
"Not seriously—no. I see no alteration but what a favorable turn might repair in a few weeks; besides, her own word must carry weight: she says she is well."
