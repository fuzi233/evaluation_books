I settled against the rough bark of a sentinel tree, watching as the children dispersed in scattered pairs and small clusters toward their arboreal nests. Their exhaustion was absolute, rendering them content to surrender to sleep, only to rise renewed with the dawn. Though weariness weighed upon me as well, I remained awake, my vigilance sharp. Lona had not bid me goodnight, and I was certain she would return.

The moment I beheld her again, a profound recognition seized me—a resemblance to the Princess that left no room for doubt she was the daughter Adam had described. Yet, in Lona, the dazzling radiance of Lilith was tempered by an innocent childlikeness, deepened further by the serene gravity of motherhood. *"She is likely occupied,"* I murmured to myself, *"with the child of the woman I encountered fleeing!"*—a woman who, Lona had confessed, was barely fulfilling her maternal duties.

Her arrival was inevitable. She sat beside me, and after a pause filled with silent affection, manifested primarily through the gentle stroking of my face and hands, she recounted every event since my departure. As we spoke, the moon ascended, casting intermittent, trembling beams of silver light through the canopy onto her visage. Her expression was etched with thought and a burden of care, yet this very concern redeemed and glorified her features. How such a child could issue from such a mother—or rather, from such a union of three souls—was difficult to comprehend, yet the harmony was undeniable. She captivated me with qualities that mirrored my own innermost self; I loved her as one destined to achieve perfection, forever remaining a child in spirit. I knew then that my love for her was absolute, and that the hope of reunion had been my sole solace. Every word she uttered pierced directly to my heart, refining it like pure truth.

She explained that following my exit from the Orchard Valley, the Giants' belief in the tangible existence of their neighbors grew, fueling a corresponding hostility. Occasionally, the Little Ones witnessed them trampling furiously, reacting to mere phantasms of their presence while the giants themselves stood nearby, mocking their futile rage. Soon, however, this animosity evolved into pragmatic destruction: the Giants began felling the very trees that sustained the Little Ones' livelihood. This crisis drove the Mother of them all to devise a countermeasure. Dispatching her sharpest scouts to eavesdrop at night, she discovered the Giants believed I was concealed nearby, planning to strike them down in their sleep once I regained strength. Concluding that the only path to halt the devastation was to convince the Giants they had been driven out, she formulated a strategy. The Little Ones must migrate into the forest—beyond the Giants' reach, yet close enough to harvest their own timber by night. The primary obstacle was the forest floor's lack of undergrowth, offering no concealment.

Yet, she reasoned, where birds existed, the Little Ones could find refuge. Possessing an innate sympathy for all modes of life, they could emulate the wildest creatures. Why not abandon the low brushwood for the lofty foliage? Why not construct nests where hollows were impossible? All that birds achieved, the Little Ones could replicate—save for flight.

She addressed the assembly, and they received her proposal with enthusiastic approval. Already adept climbers and keen observers of avian nest-building, they viewed the forest trees with admiration. These giants of flora reached higher toward the sky, their branches spreading wide, some even drooping downward as if issuing an invitation. Perhaps, nestled in the crown of the tallest, they might locate the bird that incubated eggs until maturity, then released the hatchlings. Thus, they resolved to construct sleep-houses aloft, invisible to the Giants, who rarely lifted their dull heads to scan the canopy. Convincing themselves the Little Ones had fled the country, the Giants neglected their trees and nearly ceased believing in their diminutive neighbors.

Lona queried whether I had noticed many of the children maturing. I admitted ignorance but acknowledged the likelihood. She confirmed their physical growth and emphasized that the intellectual expansion evident in their minds served as significant mitigation for the alarm their ascent had initially caused.

As twilight faded and the moon illuminated the clearing, they descended to gather sufficient fruit for the following day. The Giants avoided the twilight, viewing it as a form of darkness they detested; indeed, they would have extinguished the moon had they possessed the power. However, the Little Ones quickly discerned that fruit harvested under lunar light spoiled by noon. A new dilemma emerged: rather than feigning abandonment, might it be wiser to compel the malicious Giants to vacate the territory?

Through prior exploration, they had established acquaintanceship with the forest's fauna, learning the strengths, wisdom, and docility of various species. Leveraging this knowledge, they approached the animals with playful affection, swiftly transforming strangers into allies. Addressing horses and elephants as "Brother" or "Sister," they eventually assigned individual names to each. Bears followed shortly thereafter, and one day a young boy cried out, "Ah, Sister Serpent!" to a snake that had bitten him during rough play. Caterpillars elicited indifference unless observed undergoing metamorphosis; once transformed into butterflies, the entire group hailed them as "Sister Butterfly," employing a term akin to *repentance*, treating the transformation as a sacred event.

On a moonlit eve, while gathering provisions, they encountered a woman seated on the ground cradling an infant—the very woman I had met en route to Bulika. Mistaking her for a Giantess who had abducted one of theirs (for they regarded all infants as communal property), they attacked her in a multitude, delivering blows that were childish yet bewilderingly effective. Desperate to flee, she was halted by a boy who pinned her by the feet. Regaining her composure, she recognized her assailants as the very hosts she sought, and immediately surrendered the child. Lona intervened, carrying the infant to her bosom.

While the woman observed that the children struck without harming the babe, the Little Ones noted her tender embrace and kisses, mirroring their own desires. Concluding she was a Giantess allied with the benevolent kind, they presented her with fruit and showered her with attentive kindness.

Perplexed regarding her destination, the woman dared not return to the city, fearing the Princess would identify the culprit responsible for disabling her leopardess. Delighted by the Little People's friendship, she decided to stay temporarily. Caring for her infant posed no trouble, and she hoped to eventually reunite with her wealthy husband, adorned in gems and rarely cruel.

Here, I must supplement Lona's account with conjecture derived from the traditions of Bulika's inhabitants. They knew the Princess lived in terror of an infant destined to bring about her destruction. Unaware of the horrific methods she employed to preserve her youth and beauty—and noting her deteriorating physical state required increased reliance on these measures—they interpreted her heightened hostility toward children as a harbinger of impending doom. Although no one anticipated an active assault, this belief nurtured latent hopes for change.

Seizing upon this, the woman conceived a plan to accelerate the prophecy's fulfillment or leverage the myth for her own restoration. What probability existed that the fate foretold resided with these remarkably brave children, whereas the Bulikans were cowards in abject terror of animals? If she could ignite in the Little Ones the ambition to conquer the city, she could exploit the ensuing chaos to slip unnoticed past the retreating army, return to her home, and lie in hiding to observe the outcome. Should the children successfully expel the Giants, she intended to propose this grander objective while they were still intoxicated by victory. Though naturally unsuited for warfare—rarely quarreling, never fighting, loving all living things, and hating both inflicting and suffering pain—they were impressionable and capable of mastering exercises within their strength. Immediately, she instructed the younger ones in stone-throwing. Soon, they were engrossed in the new game, honing their skills with remarkable aptitude.

The first practical application of this training occurred during my rescue. While gathering fruit, they discovered me asleep, returned to their stronghold, convened a council, and arrived the following day accompanied by elephants and horses. Overwhelming the few Giants guarding me, they carried me off. Jubilant over their triumph, the younger boys boasted childishly, the older ones displaying less ostentation, while the girls, though their eyes flashed with intensity, remained unusually taciturn. The woman of Bulika undoubtedly felt emboldened.

Our conversation continued throughout the night, focusing primarily on the children's development and its implications. Familiar with Lona's uncanny ability to perceive truth, I was now astounded by her practical wisdom. Had I been more youthful in spirit, I might have questioned less.

Long before dawn, a subtle rustling and scrambling disturbed the silence. Rising on my elbow, I observed numerous Little Ones descending from their nests. They vanished into the underbrush, and within moments, tranquility returned.

"What are they doing?" I asked.

"They believe," Lona replied, "that, however foolish they may seem, the Giants will scour the woods. Hence, they have departed to collect stones to repel them.
