To truly grasp the architecture of Hollow's Cottage, one must first understand the absolute poverty of its universe at the hour of dawn. It was a microscopic empire of three, subjugated by a man who, though born of French lineage in Antwerp, had been conditioned to call himself "Master." He was a square-built, brutal artifact of a world that refused to acknowledge his origins, rising even earlier than usual to assert his dominion over the waking hours. He awoke his man, Joe Scott, not with a shout, but with the melodic intrusion of a French song sung during his toilet—a sonic assertion of superiority that demanded immediate compliance.

"Ye're not custen dahn, then, maister?" Joe cried, his voice rough with sleep.

"Not a stiver, *mon garçon*—which means, my lad. Get up, and we'll take a turn through the mill before the hands come in, and I'll explain my future plans. We'll have the machinery yet, Joseph. You never heard of Bruce, perhaps?"

"And th' arrand (spider)? Yes, but I hev. I've read th' history o' Scotland, and happen knaw as mich on't as ye; and I understand ye to mean to say ye'll persevere."

"I do."

The dialogue that followed was a collision of worlds, a linguistic battlefield where identity was contested as fiercely as territory. Joe, folding up his temporary bed, pressed the question of nationality, probing the fissures in his master's self-conception. Moore rejected the labels of France, Holland, and Flanders with a mixture of indignation and intellectual arrogance, insisting on his status as an *Anversois*, a man defined by the city of his birth rather than the blood of his parents or the soil of his upbringing. Yet, as he dissected the physical traits of the Flemish—the clumsy nose, the receding forehead, the pale blue eyes—he inadvertently revealed the very features he claimed to scorn, trapped between the cosmopolitan pretensions of the merchant and the gritty reality of the industrial north.

Joe, the overseer, was a creature of a different order entirely. He was a manufacturing lad sharpened by the necessity of survival, a man who understood that trade required wit and that mechanics were forced to think. In his eyes, the "greasy chaps" smelling of oil and the dyers with blue-black skins possessed a depth of understanding regarding laws and governance that far exceeded the "soft uns" like Christopher Sykes or the hectoring Irish curate. He viewed himself as intelligent, a product of a harsh environment that forged clarity from chaos.

"You think yourself a clever fellow, I know, Scott," Moore replied, his tone shifting from defensive to paternalistic condescension.

"Ay! I'm fairish... but there's thousands i' Yorkshire that's as good as me, and a two-three that's better."

In this exchange, the ideological chasm between the two men widened. Moore, representing the bourgeois ideal, dismissed Joe's pride as the arrogance of a "prig," a conceited noodle mistaken for a man of science simply because he could distinguish cheese from chalk or find scraps of chemistry in a dyeing vat. He articulated a worldview steeped in the confidence of the established order: that the working class were not martyrs, that the government was not fundamentally flawed, and that virtue was not confined to cottages while vice inhabited slate roofs. He insisted that human nature was a constant, a blend of vice and virtue regardless of station, citing villains among the rich, the poor, and the moderately competent alike.

"The clock is going to strike six. Away with you, Joe, and ring the mill bell."

As the clock approached the hour, the atmosphere outside shifted. It was the middle of February, a season of suspended animation. Dawn did not break with warmth but with a pale, colorless ray that penetrated the brown obscurity of the night like a weak probe. The sun, a white disc clear and tintless, looked almost as chill as ice as it peeped over the dark crest of a hill. The air was raw, the wind stirring the mass of night-cloud to reveal a stratum of paler vapour beyond the silver-gleaming ring of the horizon. The earth was sodden, pools and rivulets swollen from the relentless rains, reflecting a landscape weary of winter's severity.

Inside, the mill-windows glowed with artificial light, the bell ringing loud to summon the workforce. Children ran in, hurried and bundled against the inclement air, their faces flushed with the exertion of surviving storms and frost. Mr. Moore stood at the entrance, watching them pass, counting them with the precision of an accountant auditing a ledger. To those who arrived late, he delivered a sharp reprimand, a command reinforced by Joe Scott when the lingerers reached the work-rooms. There was no savagery in their tones, only rigidity. They were not monsters, yet they were unforgiving. Moore made the tardy worker pay his penny down immediately, informing him that the next offense would cost him twopence. These were rules, necessary in the machinery of industry, enforced with a tyranny that bordered on cruelty, though the novelists conscience recoiled from depicting the degradation of such figures. Here, the punishment was financial, not corporal; the overseer had once flogged his own son, but he was too phlegmatic, too reasonable, to make violence the standard method of correction.

Mr. Moore haunted his domain until the sickly dawn strengthened into day. The sun rose, changing the livid edge of the cloud above the hill to silver, casting a solemn gaze down the narrow dale. By eight o'clock, the mill lights were extinguished, and the signal for breakfast was given. The children retreated to their tin cans of coffee and small baskets of bread, a meager sustenance for bodies pushed to the limit.

Finally, Mr. Moore quitted the mill-yard and bent his steps toward his dwelling-house. It lay a short distance away, separated from the factory by a hedge and a high bank that lent it an air of seclusion. It was a small, whitewashed place with a green porch, where scanty brown stalks hinted at the promise of summer creepers. The lawn was bleached, the grass on the bank and under the hedge devoid of verdancy, save for the first timid shoots of snowdrop or crocus peeping green as emerald from the earth. Three trees, gracefully grouped, rose beside the cottage, looking well and imposing despite their lack of height. It was a snug nest for contentment, yet one within which the wings of action and ambition could not long remain folded.

Yet, the air of modest comfort possessed no particular attraction for its owner. Instead of entering the house, Moore fetched a spade from a little shed and began to work in the garden. For about a quarter of an hour, he dug on uninterrupted, a solitary figure tilling the soil as if preparing for a battle. Then, a window opened, and a female voice called to him in French.

"Eh, bien! Tu ne déjeûnes pas ce matin?"

The answer, and the rest of the conversation, was in French, a language that marked the boundary between the public sphere of the mill and the private sanctuary of the home. Moore paused, throwing down his spade, and entered the house. The narrow passage led to a small parlour where a breakfast of coffee, bread, butter, and stewed pears awaited. Presiding over these viands was the lady who had spoken from the window.

She seemed a little older than Mr. Moore, perhaps thirty-five, tall and proportionately stout. She had very black hair, currently twisted up in curl-papers, a high color in her cheeks, a small nose, and a pair of little black eyes. The lower part of her face was large in proportion to the upper; her forehead was small and rather corrugated. She wore a fretful though not ill-natured expression, an appearance that invited one to be half provoked and half amused. The strangest point was her dress—a stuff petticoat and a striped cotton camisole. The petticoat was short, displaying well a pair of feet and ankles which left much to be desired in the article of symmetry.

To the casual observer, she might appear a remarkable slattern, but Hortense Moore, Mr. Moore's sister, was in fact a very orderly, economical person. This attire was her morning costume, the uniform in which she had always "gone her household ways" in her own country. She did not choose to adopt English fashions because she was obliged to live in England; she adhered to her old Belgian modes, quite satisfied that there was a merit in so doing.

Mademoiselle had an excellent opinion of herself—an opinion not wholly undeserved, for she possessed some good and sterling qualities. Yet, she over-estimated the kind and degree of these qualities, quite leaving out of account sundry little defects which accompanied them. One could never persuade her that she was prejudiced and narrow-minded, too susceptible on the subject of her own dignity and importance, and too apt to take offence about trifles; yet all this was true. However, where her claims to distinction were not opposed, and where her prejudices were not offended, she could be kind and friendly enough. To her two brothers, the sole remaining representatives of their decayed family, she was very much attached. Their persons were almost sacred in her eyes. Of Louis, however, she knew less than of Robert.
