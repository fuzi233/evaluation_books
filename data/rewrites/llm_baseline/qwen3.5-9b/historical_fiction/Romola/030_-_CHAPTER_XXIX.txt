"The old fellow has vanished; he headed toward Arezzo the next morning. I suppose he didn't care for the scent of the French after spending time as their prisoner. I stopped by the hospital to ask if those monkish cooks had managed to determine whether he was in his right mind. They told me he showed no signs of madness—merely ignored questions entirely, appearing as though he were tending a vineyard twenty miles away. He was a mysterious old tiger. I would have loved to learn more about him."

These words were spoken by Piero di Cosimo within Nello's shop on the twenty-fourth of November, exactly one week following the arrival of the French forces. A gathering of six or seven souls had assembled at the unusually late hour of three in the afternoon. It was a day when all of Florence buzzed with anticipation regarding a decisive political turning point. Every public resting place was packed, and every shopkeeper without a spouse or deputy to manage the premises stood at his doorway, thumbs hooked in his belt. Meanwhile, the streets were dotted with artisans pausing or drifting lazily like floating debris, poised to surge forward with impetuosity should any spark catch their attention.

Nello had been strumming his lute while half-sitting on a board propped against the shop window, keeping a vigilant eye on the piazza. "Ah," he declared, setting down the instrument with emphasis, "for a gold florin, I would not have missed that spectacle of the French soldiers waddling in their broad shoes after their fleeing prisoners! That is the penalty for leaving my shop to shave magnificent chins. It is always thus: whenever I depart from this navel of the world, something significant happens in my piazza."

"Yes, you should have been there," Piero remarked in his cutting tone, "to witness your favorite Greek trembling as though Satanasso himself had seized him. I delight in seeing your perpetually smiling gentlemen caught in a sudden gale, forced to reveal their linings despite their best efforts. What color do you imagine a man's liver turns when he resembles a bleached deer the moment a chance stranger grabs him?"

"Piero, keep your vinegar reserved as a condiment for your own eggs!" Nello retorted. "What fault does my *bel erudito* bear for looking startled when he felt a pair of claws upon him and spotted an unchained madman at his side? Your scholar is not akin to those beastly Swiss and Germans, whose heads are suited only for battering-rams and whose appetites are so voracious they consider swallowing a cannonball before breakfast acceptable. We Florentines value qualities in a man beyond that vulgar commodity known as bravery, which is acquired by hiring dullards at a price per dozen. Tell you what: once men realized they possessed greater intellect than oxen, they set the oxen to pull plows for them. And when we Florentines discovered we held superior minds to other nations, we commissioned them to fight for us."

"Treason, Nello!" a voice cried from the inner room. "That contradicts the doctrine of the State. Florence is currently grinding its weapons, and the latest authenticated vision proclaimed by the Friar depicts Mars standing atop the Palazzo Vecchio, his arm resting on the shoulder of Saint John the Baptist, who offers him a piece of honeycomb."

"It is well, Francesco," Nello replied calmly. "Florence possesses a few thick-skulled individuals suitable for bombarding Pisa; meanwhile, the finer spirits remain at home to perform the thinking and the shaving. As for our Piero here, if he prizes valor so highly, let him take his largest brush as a weapon and his palette as a shield, challenging the widest-mouthed Swiss he encounters in the Prato to single combat."

"Go on, Nello," growled Piero. "Your tongue rolls on as usual, like a mill when the Arno is swollen—regardless of whether there is grain to grind."

"Excellent grain, I assure you. It would be as logical to expect a weather-beaten painter like you to enjoy having a javelin thrust into you as it would be to anticipate a man whose wit has been honed by the classics enjoying having his handsome face torn by a wild beast."

"There you go again, assuming people will insert their legs into a sack merely because you term it 'hosen,'" Piero countered. "Who suggested a wild beast, or an unarmed man charging into battle? Combat is a trade, and it is not mine. I would be a fool to pursue danger, yet I could confront it if it sought me out."

"How is it you fear the thunder so greatly, then, my Piero?" Nello pressed, determined to expose the accuser. "One might assume you understand why a trivial matter shakes one man while leaving others unmoved—you who hide among the rats the moment a storm approaches."

"That stems from a particular sensitivity to loud noises; it has nothing to do with my courage or my conscience."

"Well, perhaps Tito Melema shares a similar sensitivity to being unexpectedly seized by prisoners escaping from French soldiers. Humans are born with antipathies; I personally cannot abide the smell of mint. Tito was born with an aversion to elderly prisoners who stumble and clutch. Ecco!"

A general laugh erupted at Nello's defense, revealing clearly that Piero's dislike for Tito was not shared by the assembly. The painter, wearing an indecipherable grimace, pulled tow from his bag and stuffed his ears in indignant contempt, while Nello continued triumphantly.

"No, my Piero, I cannot afford to have my *bel erudito* disparaged; nor can Florence, with her scholars molting off her at the tender age of forty. Our Phoenix Pico has ascended straight to Paradise, as the Friar informs us; and the incomparable Poliziano departed not two months ago—to well, let us hope he has not joined the eminent scholars in the Malebolge."

"By the way," interjected Francesco Cei, "have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has surpassed the Friar in prophecy? Two years ago she foretold Pico would die during the season of lilies. He passed in November. 'Not at all the time of lilies,' scoffed the critics. 'Go to!' replies Camilla; 'I meant the lilies of France, and they seem close enough beneath your noses.' I say, 'Euge, Camilla!' Should the Friar prove that any of his visions have been fulfilled as accurately, I shall declare myself a Piagnone tomorrow."

"You are somewhat too flippant regarding the Friar, Francesco," observed Pietro Cennini, the scholar. "In these recent weeks, we owe him dearly for preaching peace, quietude, and the abandonment of factional strife. Those of small discernment would gladly see the populace slip the Friar's leash just now. And if the Most Christian King remains obstinate regarding the treaty today, refusing to sign terms fair and honorable to Florence, Fra Girolamo is the man we must trust to bring him to reason."

"You speak truth, Messer Pietro," Nello agreed. "The Friar is one of the firmest nails Florence has to hang upon—at least, that is the opinion of the most respectable chins I have the honor of shaving. Yet, young Messer Niccolò remarked earlier this morning—and doubtless Francesco implies the same—there exists a wonderful elasticity in interpreting visions, comparable to stretching Dido's bull-hide. It appears a dream may signify whatever follows it. As our Franco Sacchetti notes, a woman dreams overnight of a serpent biting her, smashes a drinking cup the next day, and cries out, 'Behold, I thought something would occur—it is plain now what the serpent meant.'"

"But the Friar's visions are not of that sort," stated Cronaca. "He declares not only what will happen—that the Church will be scourged and renewed, and the heathens converted—but insists it shall happen swiftly. He is no slippery pretender providing loopholes for himself; he is—"

"What is this? What is this?" Nello exclaimed, leaping from the board and pressing his head out the door. "People are streaming into the piazza, shouting. Something must have occurred in the Via Larga. Aha!" he burst forth with delighted astonishment, stepping outside laughing and waving his cap.

The remainder of the company hastened to the threshold. News from the Via Larga was precisely what they awaited. Yet, they were not slightly surprised by the manner of its arrival. Seated upon a bench, seemingly snatched from the street and hoisted above the shoulders of the mob, sat Tito Melema, smiling in amused resignation at the compulsion he faced. His cap had slipped from his head, dangling by the *becchetto* loosely wound around his neck. Upon spotting the group at Nello's door, he raised his fingers in a gesture of beckoning recognition. Within the next minute, he had leaped from the bench onto a cart laden with bales stationed in the broad space between the Baptistery and the steps of the Duomo, while the crowd swarmed around him with the noisy eagerness of poultry awaiting feed.

Silence fell when he began to speak in his clear, mellow voice.

"Citizens of Florence! I possess no warrant to deliver this news save your own will. Yet the tidings are good, and will harm none in hearing. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty honorable to Florence. But you owe this to one of your citizens, a man who spoke words worthy of the ancient Romans—you owe it to Piero Capponi!"
