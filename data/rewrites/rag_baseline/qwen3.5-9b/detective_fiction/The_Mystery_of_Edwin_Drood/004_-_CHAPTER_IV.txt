The air in the auctioneer's office was thick with the scent of port wine and the dust of things sold. Mr. Thomas Sapsea sat before a fire that burned with the polite indifference of an early luxury, his posture a rigid assertion of his own importance. On the table beside him, a bottle of port waited alongside a clock that measured time and a weather-glass that defied the elements. Both instruments served a single, internal logic: to uphold the auctioneer against the chaos of the world outside.

Sapsea held a scrap of manuscript. He read it aloud, his voice rolling over the syllables with the gravity of a sermon, pausing only where the rhythm demanded a breath. The name "Ethelinda" hung in the silence, a ghost of a wife whose memory he curated with religious fervor.

"The world," Sapsea declared, pacing the room with thumbs hooked in his waistcoat, "is a place for the knowing. And I know it."

His guest, Mr. Jasper, watched him. Jasper was a man of the city, cooped within its narrow bounds, yet he listened to Sapsea's claims of global familiarity with a mixture of amusement and pity. Sapsea spoke of clocks from Paris and tea from Pekin, claiming to identify them by the mere touch of a finger, as if geography were a texture one could feel through fabric. It was a performance of omniscience, a shield against the vulnerability of being merely a man in a small town.

"You are a man of taste," Sapsea told Jasper, pouring wine with a flourish that suggested he was bestowing a blessing rather than serving a drink. "And I propose a toast to the coming of the French. May we meet them at Dover!"

Jasper smiled, a thin expression that did not reach his eyes. "We were to speak of the late Mrs. Sapsea."

The atmosphere shifted, becoming heavier, more sepulchral. Sapsea filled the glasses, his movements stiffening. He spoke of Miss Brobity, the woman who had once admired his style until she died of the inability to match his intellect. He recounted her final words—"O Thou!"—and the hollow victory of his solitude. He poured wine into the void, asking himself if a husband closer to her level might have saved her liver, or perhaps his own sanity.

"Now," Sapsea resumed, holding up the manuscript again, "let us judge the inscription. Read it."

Jasper took the paper. The ink was fresh, the words carved in the language of the grave:

*ETHIELINDA, Reverential Wife of MR. THOMAS SAPSEA, AUCTIONEER, VALUER, ESTATE AGENT, &c., OF THIS CITY. Whose Knowledge of the World, Though somewhat extensive, Never brought him acquainted with A SPIRIT More capable of LOOKING UP TO HIM. STRANGER, PAUSE And ask thyself the Question, CANST THOU DO LIKEWISE? If Not, WITH A BLUSH RETIRE.*

"It is admirable," Jasper said, returning the paper. "Striking, characteristic, and complete."

Sapsea inclined his head, accepting the praise as a due reward. Just then, the maid announced the arrival of Durdles.

Durdles entered, a figure composed of stone and shadow. He wore a suit of coarse flannel, a yellow neckerchief, and boots the color of the graves he knew better than anyone else. He was the chartered libertine of Cloisterham, a man who slept in cathedral crypts and spoke of the dead as if they were old acquaintances waiting to greet him. He carried a two-foot rule in his pocket and a hammer in his hand, tools for measuring the dead and breaking the living.

Sapsea handed him the manuscript. Durdles took it unfeelingly, pulling the rule from his pocket. He did not read the words; he measured the spacing between them, alloying the poetry with the grit of stone-dust.

"This is for the monument?" Durdles asked.

"The Inscription. Yes."

"It'll come in to an eighth of an inch," Durdles replied, his voice flat and resonant. "Your servant, Mr. Jasper. Hope I see you well."

"How are you, Durdles?"

"I've got a touch of the Tombatism on me, Mr. Jasper, but that I must expect."

"Tombatism?" Sapsea snapped, nettled by the casual reception of his labor. "You mean rheumatism."

"No, I don't," Durdles corrected, turning his gaze toward the window where the cold air seemed to seep in. "I mean the Tombatism. It's another sort from rheumatism. Mr. Jasper knows what Durdles means. You get among them tombs afore it's well light on a winter morning, and keep on walking in the same all the days of your life, and you'll know what Durdles means."

"It is a bitter cold place," Jasper assented, shivering despite the fire.

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the windows of the auctioneer's office. Inside, the clock ticked on, counting down the seconds of a life lived in the shadow of monuments that would soon rise to claim the dead.
