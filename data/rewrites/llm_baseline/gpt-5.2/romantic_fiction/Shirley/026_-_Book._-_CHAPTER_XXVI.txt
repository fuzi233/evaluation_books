By the time the carriage rolled back from Fieldhead to Briarfield, Caroline had come up out of her illness as bread rises after the first anxious watching—still delicate, still needing warmth, but with life again in the centre of her. The news of her convalescence had reached Shirley by post; and Shirley, once home, let scarcely an hour stand between her and the rectory door.

Rain was falling—not in gusts, but in a steady, fine descent that made every late blossom hold its perfume closer, and sent the wet smell of turned earth and bruised box edging into the house. The garden wicket swung; the sound cut through the quiet like the clean click of a latch in a still kitchen. A moment after, Shirley’s figure crossed the window, and then she was in the room.

When she was deeply moved, she did not pour herself out in words. Strong feeling, with her, went first to the arms and the eyes, and only afterwards—if at all—to the tongue. She gathered Caroline up, held her as if to make sure she was truly there, and gave one swift look that seemed to take account of every change in the thin face; then one kiss.

“You are better.”

A minute later, as if her heart had spoken behind her teeth and now must be obeyed: “I see you are safe now; but take care. God grant your health may be called on to sustain no more shocks!”

Then, as though she had pressed down the weight within her, she began talking—quickly, brightly—of her journey, of roads and inns and relatives. Yet all the while, her glance slipped back and back to Caroline, measuring the frailty that still clung about her like a light shawl. Solicitude shone in it—trouble too, and a kind of amazement, as if she could not reconcile Caroline’s presence with the peril she had passed.

Suddenly that glance turned and fixed on Mrs. Pryor. It was not an idle look; it pierced, it searched.

“When will my governess return to me?” Shirley asked.

Caroline, at that, drew in breath—then turned to her mother with a look that asked leave. Mrs. Pryor’s permission was given without speech; a small gesture, quiet and resigned.

So Shirley was told. The secret—so long guarded, so long suffered—was spoken in her hearing. Mrs. Pryor, Caroline’s mother: the bond confessed at last.

Shirley’s comment came coolly, as if she were passing judgment on a point already settled in her mind. “Very good—very good! But it is no news to me.”

Caroline stared. “What! did you know?”

“I guessed long since the whole business. I have heard somewhat of Mrs. Pryor’s history—not from herself, but from others. With every detail of Mr. James Helstone’s career and character I was acquainted. An afternoon’s sitting and conversation with Miss Mann had rendered me familiar therewith; also he is one of Mrs. Yorke’s warning examples—one of the blood-red lights she hangs out to scare young ladies from matrimony.” Shirley’s voice sharpened a little, as if amused and offended together. “I believe I should have been sceptical about the truth of the portrait traced by such fingers—both these ladies take a dark pleasure in offering to view the dark side of life—but I questioned Mr. Yorke on the subject, and he said, ‘Shirley, my woman, if you want to know aught about yond’ James Helstone, I can only say he was a man-tiger. He was handsome, dissolute, soft, treacherous, courteous, cruel—’” Her eye flicked to Caroline’s face. “Don’t cry, Cary; we’ll say no more about it.”

“I am not crying, Shirley; or if I am, it is nothing. Go on; you are no friend if you withhold from me the truth. I hate that false plan of disguising, mutilating the truth.”

“Fortunately I have said pretty nearly all that I have to say,” Shirley returned; “except that your uncle himself confirmed Mr. Yorke’s words; for he too scorns a lie, and deals in none of those conventional subterfuges that are shabbier than lies.”

“But papa is dead; they should let him alone now.”

“They should; and we will let him alone.” And then, as if she could not help laying her hand, with a strange tenderness, on the very thing Caroline feared, she added: “Cry away, Cary; it will do you good. It is wrong to check natural tears. Besides, I choose to please myself by sharing an idea that at this moment beams in your mother’s eye while she looks at you. Every drop blots out a sin. Weep! your tears have the virtue which the rivers of Damascus lacked. Like Jordan, they can cleanse a leprous memory.”

Then she turned to Mrs. Pryor with that frank, fearless directness which could be kindness when it pleased, and ruthlessness when it must.

“Madam,” she said, “did you think I could be daily in the habit of seeing you and your daughter together—marking your marvellous similarity in many points, observing (pardon me) your irrepressible emotions in the presence and still more in the absence of your child—and not form my own conjectures? I formed them, and they are literally correct. I shall begin to think myself shrewd.”

“And you said nothing?” Caroline asked, when she had mastered herself again. Her voice had steadied, though it was still low, as if tears had salted it.

“Nothing. I had no warrant to breathe a word on the subject. My business it was not; I abstained from making it such.”

“You guessed so deep a secret, and did not hint that you guessed it?”

“Is that so difficult?”

“It is not like you.”

“How do you know?”

“You are not reserved; you are frankly communicative.”

“I may be communicative, yet know where to stop.” Shirley’s smile came, sudden and half veiled. “In showing my treasure I may withhold a gem or two—a curious, unbought graven stone—an amulet of whose mystic glitter I rarely permit even myself a glimpse. Good-day.”

That speech—light, strange, and final—left Caroline with a new understanding of her friend, as if a door had swung open on a chamber she had not known existed.

Soon Caroline could bear the change of scene and the small stir of company; as soon as she could, Shirley claimed her. Day by day she sued for Caroline’s presence at Fieldhead with an eagerness that was almost hunger. Whether she had grown weary of her honoured relatives she did not say; she only held Caroline fast, as if one warm, familiar soul in the house made all the difference between being at home and being merely lodged.

The Sympsons were church people—methodical as a parish calendar, punctual as the tolling bell that ordered their weeks. The rector’s niece was received with courtesy, of course; with them, courtesy was a rule like saying grace. Mr. Sympson was a man of spotless respectability, worrying temper, pious principles, and worldly views. His lady was truly good—patient, kind, well-bred—but her mind had been reared on a narrow diet. It was as though someone had set her a table of bitter herbs and then commanded her to call it feast: excellent principles pressed into a stiff crust of bigotry—wholesome, perhaps, but hard to digest—and no seasoning of imagination, no free savour of doubt or laughter. She bore it without complaint, as she bore everything.

The daughters were patterns—tall, with Roman noses, and with lives ruled as if by an invisible metronome. Their education had been faultless; all they did was well done. They had read history and solid books, and possessed principles and opinions that seemed to them beyond mending. They had learned by heart a schoolroom code for young ladies—laws of language, demeanour, propriety—and they never deviated. In others they watched for deviation with a secret, whispered horror. The Abomination of Desolation, to them, was no mystery; they had found it and named it: Originality. Where they saw its trace—in look, in word, in deed; where they heard fresh, expressive language instead of the hackneyed phrases they trusted—they recoiled, as if something wild had cried in the fields at night. Being unintelligible to them, it must be bad; therefore it must be denounced and chained.

Henry Sympson, their only son and youngest child, was fifteen. He kept mostly with his tutor; and when he escaped him, he sought Shirley. He was little, lame, and pale; his large eyes, set in a wan orbit, were often dim, yet capable of sudden blaze when feeling lit them from within. His mother loved him with a tender, almost solemn partiality, believing his peculiarities to be marks of election—a new Samuel, called from birth. Mr. Sympson and the Misses Sympson, not understanding him, left him alone. Shirley made him her pet; he made Shirley his playmate.

And in the midst of this family circle—or rather, always just outside it—moved the tutor: Louis Moore.

He was a satellite of the house of Sympson—connected, yet apart; always attending, always distant. Each member of that correct household treated him with proper dignity. The father was austerely civil and sometimes irritable; the mother was attentive, but formal; the daughters regarded him as an abstraction, not a man. They were learned, so was he—but not for them; they were accomplished, he had talents too, and these were imperceptible to their senses. His spirited sketches were blanks to their eyes. Original observations fell unheard on their ears. Their propriety was unassailable.

Caroline, watching, was astonished to discover that her cousin had no sympathizing friend at Fieldhead; that to Miss Keeldar he was as much a mere teacher, as little a gentleman, as little a man, as to the Misses Sympson. Shirley was not openly haughty; but she never noticed him. He came and went; spoke or was silent; and she rarely acknowledged his existence.

As for Louis Moore, he carried himself like one long used to such life, and determined to bear it for a time. His faculties seemed walled up—captive, and quiet in their captivity. He never laughed; he seldom smiled; he complained of nothing. He fulfilled his duties scrupulously. His pupil loved him; from the rest he asked only civility—and, it seemed, would accept nothing more in that house. When Caroline made gentle overtures of friendship, he did not encourage them; he avoided rather than sought her.

Only one living creature besides his pale, crippled scholar did he fondle at Fieldhead: the great ruffianly Tartar. Sullen and impracticable to others, the dog showed a singular partiality for the taciturn tutor. Sometimes, when Moore—summoned to a meal—entered a room and sat down unwelcomed, Tartar would rise from his lair at Shirley’s feet and betake himself to Louis’s side, as if choosing the hand that understood him. Once—only once—Shirley noticed the desertion. She held out her white hand, spoke softly, tried to coax him back. Tartar looked, slavered, sighed, but ignored her invitation and coolly settled himself by Louis. Louis drew the big black-muzzled head to his knee, patted him, and smiled one small smile to himself.

An acute observer might have remarked that, later the same evening, after Tartar had resumed his allegiance to Shirley and lay again by her footstool, the audacious tutor by one word and gesture fascinated him back. The dog pricked his ears at the word, started erect at the gesture, and came with head lovingly depressed to receive the expected caress. As it was given, that significant, quiet smile rippled again across Louis Moore’s face.

One day, when Caroline and Shirley sat alone in the summer-house—green gloom all around, and the smell of wet leaves coming up as if from a cool cellar—Caroline said, as if lightly, “Shirley, did you know that my cousin Louis was tutor in your uncle’s family before the Sympsons came down here?”

Shirley’s answer did not spring out as her answers usually did. It came after a pause. “Yes—of course; I knew it well.”

“I thought you must have been aware of the circumstance.”

“Well! what then?”

“It puzzles me to guess how it chanced that you never mentioned it to me.”

“Why should it puzzle you?”

“It seems odd. I cannot account for it. You talk a great deal—you talk freely. How was that circumstance never touched on?”

“Because it never was,” said Shirley, and laughed.

“You are a singular being! I thought I knew you quite well; I begin to find myself mistaken. You were silent as the grave about Mrs. Pryor, and now again here is another secret. But why you made it a secret is the mystery to me.”

“I never made it a secret; I had no reason for so doing. If you had asked me who Henry’s tutor was, I would have told you. Besides, I thought you knew.”

Caroline hesitated, then spoke what had been troubling her. “I am puzzled about more things than one in this matter. You don’t like poor Louis. Why? Are you impatient at what you perhaps consider his servile position? Do you wish that Robert’s brother were more highly placed?”

“Robert’s brother, indeed!” Shirley’s exclamation came sharp—scornful; and with a movement of proud impatience she snatched a rose from a branch that pushed its way in through the lattice. The rose, wet and late, gave off that faint, bruised sweetness autumn roses have, half perfume, half decay.

“Yes,” Caroline repeated, mild but firm. “Robert’s brother. He is thus closely related to Gérard Moore of the Hollow, though nature has not given him features so handsome or an air so noble as his kinsman; but his blood is as good, and he is as much a gentleman were he free.”

“Wise, humble, pious Caroline!” Shirley cried, with irony. “Men and angels, hear her! We should not despise plain features, nor a laborious yet honest occupation, should we?” She pointed through an opening in the creepers. “Look at the subject of your panegyric. He is there in the garden.”

Louis Moore was indeed coming slowly down the walk.

“He is not ugly, Shirley,” Caroline pleaded. “He is not ignoble. He is sad; silence seals his mind. But I believe him to be intelligent; and be certain, if he had not something very commendable in his disposition, Mr. Hall would never seek his society as he does.”

Shirley laughed—once, twice—each time with a slightly sarcastic sound. “Well, well. On the plea of the man being Cyril Hall’s friend and Robert Moore’s brother, we’ll just tolerate his existence; won’t we, Cary? You believe him to be intelligent, do you? Not quite an idiot—eh? Something commendable in his disposition!—id est, not an absolute ruffian. Good! Your representations have weight with me; and to prove that they have, should he come this way I will speak to him.”

He approached the summer-house. Unaware it was occupied, he sat down on the step. Tartar, now his customary companion, followed and lay across his feet, a warm, heavy presence.

“Old boy!” Louis said, pulling the tawny ear—or rather the torn remains of it, chewed in a hundred battles—“the autumn sun shines as pleasantly on us as on the fairest and richest. This garden is none of ours, but we enjoy its greenness and perfume, don’t we?”

He sat silent then, still caressing the dog. In the trees around, a faint twitter began—thin, quick notes threading through the damp hush. Something fluttered down as light as leaves. Little birds landed on the sward at a shy distance, hopping as if expectant.

“The small brown elves actually remember that I fed them the other day,” Louis soliloquized. “They want some more biscuit. To-day I forgot to save a fragment. Eager little sprites, I have not a crumb for you.”

He put his hand in his pocket and drew it out empty.

“A want easily supplied,” whispered Shirley, who had been listening.

From her reticule—never, it seemed, without some domestic provision, as if she carried a larder’s corner with her—she took a morsel of sweet-cake. It was the kind made in country houses for visitors and children: close-crumbed, faintly spiced, smelling of butter and sugar rather than of any grand confectioner’s art. She broke it carefully, crumbling it fine between her fingers, and bent over Louis’s shoulder to drop the crumbs into his hand.

“There,” she said. “There is a providence for the improvident.”

Louis, not in the least discomposed, scattered the crumbs over the grass with a calm, practised motion; the birds darted nearer, their small claws whispering in the wet. “This September afternoon is pleasant,” he observed.

“Even for you?”

“As pleasant for me as for any monarch.”

“You take a sort of harsh, solitary triumph in drawing pleasure out of the elements and the inanimate and lower animate creation.”

“Solitary, but not harsh. With animals I feel I am Adam’s son, the heir of him to whom dominion was given over ‘every living thing that moveth upon the earth.’ Your dog likes and follows me. When I go into that yard, the pigeons from your dovecot flutter at my feet. Your mare in the stable knows me as well as it knows you, and obeys me better.”

“And my roses smell sweet to you, and my trees give you shade.”

“And,” Louis continued, his voice quiet but edged with something that held pride and hurt in one, “no caprice can withdraw these pleasures from me; they are mine.”

Then he rose and walked away. Tartar followed him as if bound in affection and duty. Shirley remained standing on the summer-house step, looking after him; her face was pale, as if pride had been wounded inwardly.

“You see,” Caroline remarked, almost apologetically, “his feelings are so often hurt it makes him morose.”

“You see,” Shirley retorted, with sudden ire, “he is a topic on which you and I shall quarrel if we discuss it often; so drop it henceforward and for ever.”

Caroline said no more, but thought: he has more than once spoken thus, and it has made Shirley distant to him. Yet she wondered Shirley could not make allowance for character and circumstance—wondered that the general modesty, manliness, sincerity in Louis’s nature did not plead for him. Shirley was not often so inconsiderate, so irritable.

Two testimonies soon strengthened Caroline’s favourable view. William Farren, whose cottage Louis had visited with Mr. Hall, pronounced him “a real gentleman;” there was not such another in Briarfield. William “could do aught for that man.” And then—“to see how t’ bairns liked him, and how t’ wife took to him first minute she saw him. He never went into a house but t’ childer wor about him directly. Them little things wor like as if they’d a keener sense nor grown-up folks i’ finding our folk’s natures.”

Mr. Hall, asked what he thought of Louis Moore, replied promptly that he was the best fellow he had met with since leaving Cambridge.

“But he is so grave,” Caroline objected.

“Grave! the finest company in the world! Full of odd, quiet, out-of-the-way humour. Never enjoyed an excursion so much in my life as the one I took with him to the Lakes. His understanding and tastes are so superior, it does a man good to be within their influence; and as to his temper and nature, I call them fine.”

“At Fieldhead he looks gloomy, and, I believe, has the character of being misanthropical.”

“Oh! I fancy he is rather out of place there—in a false position. The Sympsons are most estimable people, but not the folks to comprehend him. They think a great deal about form and ceremony, which are quite out of Louis’s way.”

“I don’t think Miss Keeldar likes him.”

“She doesn’t know him—she doesn’t know him; otherwise she has sense enough to do justice to his merits.”

Well, Caroline thought, perhaps she doesn’t know him. That simple explanation served for a little while—until it could not.

One day Caroline happened to be in the schoolroom with Henry Sympson, whose affectionate, amiable temper had quickly recommended him. The boy was busy with some mechanical contrivance; his lameness made him fond of work done sitting—things made with hand and thought, when the body could not run. He began ransacking his tutor’s desk for wax or twine. Moore was absent; Mr. Hall had called for him to take a long walk. Henry searched compartment after compartment, growing impatient; at last he opened an inner drawer and came upon—not twine or beeswax—but a small bundle of marble-coloured cahiers, tied with tape.

Henry looked at them and made a face. “What rubbish Mr. Moore stores up in his desk!” he said. “I hope he won’t keep my old exercises so carefully.”

“What is it?” Caroline asked.

“Old copy-books.” And he tossed the neat packet to her.

The outside was so careful—tape smooth, covers clean—that curiosity stirred. She felt almost as if the very tidiness invited respect. “If they are only copy-books, I suppose I may open them?”

“Oh yes, quite freely. Mr. Moore’s desk is half mine—for he lets me keep all sorts of things in it—and I give you leave.”

She untied the tape. The books proved to be French compositions, written in a hand peculiar but compact, wonderfully clean and clear—each line laid down as if with a steady, exacting ear for order, as though the words themselves had to keep time. Caroline recognized the writing; yet she scarcely needed that evidence, for at the close of each theme a name was signed.

It was the name that astonished her: “Shirley Keeldar, Sympson Grove, ——shire,” and dates four years back.

Caroline retied the bundle and held it, meditating. A half shame came upon her, as if she had opened not mere schoolwork but a chamber in someone else’s memory.

“They are Shirley’s, you see,” Henry said carelessly.

“Did you give them to Mr. Moore? She wrote them with Mrs. Pryor, I suppose?”

“She wrote them in my schoolroom at Sympson Grove, when she lived with us there. Mr. Moore taught her French; it is his native language.”

“I know. Was she a good pupil, Henry?”

“She was a wild, laughing thing, but pleasant to have in the room. She made lesson-time charming. She learned fast—you could hardly tell when or how. French was nothing to her. She spoke it quick, quick—as quick as Mr. Moore himself.”

“Was she obedient? Did she give trouble?”

“She gave plenty of trouble, in a way. She was giddy, but I liked her. I’m desperately fond of Shirley.”

“Desperately fond—you small simpleton! You don’t know what you say.”

“I am desperately fond of her. She is the light of my eyes. I said so to Mr. Moore last night.”

“He would reprove you for speaking with exaggeration.”

“He didn’t. He never reproves and reproves, as girls’ governesses do. He was reading, and he only smiled into his book, and said that if Miss Keeldar was no more than that, she was less than he took her to be; for I was but a dim-eyed, short-sighted little chap.” Henry’s voice sank, and his quick pride had a plaintive undertone. “I’m afraid I am a poor unfortunate, Miss Caroline Helstone. I am a cripple, you know.”

“Never mind, Henry; you are a very nice little fellow; and if God has not given you health and strength, He has given you a good disposition and an excellent heart and brain.”

“I shall be despised. I sometimes think both Shirley and you despise me.”

“Listen, Henry. Generally, I don’t like schoolboys. I have a great horror of them. They seem to me little ruffians, who take an unnatural delight in killing and tormenting birds, and insects, and kittens, and whatever is weaker than themselves. But you are so different I am quite fond of you. You have almost as much sense as a man (far more, God wot,” she added inwardly, bitterly and softly, “than many men); “you are fond of reading, and you can talk sensibly about what you read.”

“I am fond of reading. I know I have sense, and I know I have feeling.”

At that moment Miss Keeldar entered.

“Henry,” she said, “I have brought your lunch here. I shall prepare it for you myself.”

She set upon the table a glass of new milk—so fresh it seemed still to carry the cool, animal sweetness of the byre—beside a plate of something that looked not unlike leather, and a utensil resembling a toasting-fork.

“What are you two about,” she continued, “ransacking Mr. Moore’s desk?”

“Looking at your old copy-books,” Caroline replied.

“My old copy-books?”

“French exercise-books. Look here! They must be held precious; they are kept carefully.”

She held out the tied bundle. Shirley snatched it up as if the touch of it stung. “Did not know one was in existence,” she said. “I thought the whole lot had long since lit the kitchen fire, or curled the maid’s hair at Sympson Grove.” Then, turning sharply on Henry: “What made you keep them?”

“It is not my doing. I should not have thought of it. It never entered my head to suppose copy-books of value. Mr. Moore put them by in the inner drawer of his desk. Perhaps he forgot them.”

“C’est cela. He forgot them, no doubt,” Shirley echoed; and then, smoothing the covers with a hand that tried to look indifferent, she added, with a complacency that seemed to mask something else: “They are extremely well written.”
