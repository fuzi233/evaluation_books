CHAPTER L. After a brief but firm resistance—just enough to preserve her reputation for propriety, yet not so strong as to appear unkind—Mrs. Ferrars allowed Edward back into her presence and declared him once more her son. Her family had been in constant flux lately. For many years, she had two sons; then, when Edward was deemed lost and disgraced, she lost one. Robert’s own downfall left her without any son for nearly two weeks. Now, with Edward restored, she had one again. Yet despite this reconciliation, Edward remained uneasy. He feared that revealing his engagement to Miss Dashwood might provoke another fatal turn in his mother’s temper—and perhaps hasten his end as swiftly as before. So he disclosed the news with careful hesitation, expecting outrage. But to his surprise, Mrs. Ferrars listened with an unnerving calm.

She did, at first, try to dissuade him—using every argument at her disposal. She reminded him of Miss Morton, whose rank and fortune far surpassed those of Elinor: a nobleman’s daughter with thirty thousand pounds, while Elinor was merely the daughter of a private gentleman with no more than three. But when she saw that Edward acknowledged the truth of her words yet remained resolute, she wisely decided—based on past experience—to yield. After a delay long enough to maintain her dignity and avoid any appearance of favoritism, she finally gave her consent to the marriage.

Next came the question of financial support. Here, it became clear that though Edward was now her only surviving son, he was still not her favorite. Robert had been assured of a thousand pounds a year; Edward, however, was expected to take holy orders for the sake of two hundred and fifty pounds at most. No additional sum was promised—beyond the ten thousand pounds already given with Fanny. Still, this amount was more than Edward and Elinor had hoped for, and they accepted it gratefully. Mrs. Ferrars herself seemed astonished by her own restraint, offering only half-hearted excuses for not giving more.

With their income secured, all that remained was to wait for the completion of the Parsonage at Delaford—where Colonel Brandon, eager to make the house suitable for Elinor, had taken upon himself to oversee extensive improvements. As usual, delays plagued the workmen, and promises were broken. But just as always, Elinor could not resist the temptation to marry before everything was perfectly ready. The wedding took place early in autumn at Barton church, under the eyes of friends and family.

The first month of their married life was spent at the Mansion-house, where they could personally supervise the finishing touches on their new home. They chose wallpapers, planned shrubberies, and even designed a sweeping driveway. Mrs. Jennings’s predictions—though tangled and haphazard—proved mostly true. By Michaelmas, she visited them at the Parsonage and declared, with genuine conviction, that she had never seen a happier couple.

Indeed, Edward and Elinor had little to desire. Their only wishes were for Colonel Brandon and Marianne to marry, and for better grazing land for their cows. They were welcomed by nearly all their relations and friends. Mrs. Ferrars came to inspect the happiness she had reluctantly permitted—though she treated them with only polite affection, never granting them real favor. That privilege belonged to Robert and Lucy, whose cunning and humility had won her over.

Lucy’s conduct throughout the affair was a masterclass in self-interest. At first, Robert had approached her simply to break off Edward’s engagement—believing it would be easy. But Lucy, instead of yielding, drew him deeper into her web. Each visit bred new doubts, each conversation required another meeting. Soon, they spoke less of Edward and more of Robert—whose charm and ambition captivated her completely. Before long, Robert had replaced his brother entirely in her affections.

Proud of his triumph, Robert married Lucy privately, defying his mother’s authority. They enjoyed several months of blissful seclusion at Dawlish, where Lucy reconnected with old acquaintances and Robert sketched grand plans for cottages. When they returned to town, Lucy urged Robert to beg forgiveness from Mrs. Ferrars—a simple act that worked wonders. At first, the pardon applied only to Robert. Lucy, having owed no duty to Mrs. Ferrars, remained unforgiven for weeks. But through persistent humility, constant apologies, and exaggerated gratitude, she gradually earned her attention—and eventually, her favor.

Soon, Lucy became indispensable to Mrs. Ferrars, as essential as Robert or Fanny. While Edward was never truly forgiven for once intending to marry her, and Elinor was regarded as an outsider despite her superior station, Lucy was openly cherished as a beloved child.

They settled in London, supported generously by Mrs. Ferrars, and lived in harmony with the Dashwoods—except for the simmering jealousy between Fanny and Lucy, and the occasional quarrels between Robert and his wife. Otherwise, peace reigned.

Edward’s fall from grace and Robert’s rise were mysterious to many. But the results justified the arrangement: Robert showed no signs of regret over his modest inheritance, nor did Edward seem discontented. On the contrary, Edward fulfilled his duties faithfully, loved his wife deeply, and lived with quiet joy. His contentment was evident in every aspect of his life.

Elinor’s marriage kept her closely tied to her family. Her mother and sisters spent more than half their time at Delaford. Mrs. Dashwood made frequent visits not only for pleasure, but also out of hope—her greatest wish being to unite Marianne with Colonel Brandon. It was now her dearest aim. Though she treasured her daughter’s company, she longed even more to see Marianne settled at the mansion-house. Edward and Elin: both felt the depth of Colonel Brandon’s grief and their own debt to him. And so, by mutual agreement, Marianne was to be his reward.

Surrounded by such affection, guided by such understanding, what could Marianne do? She was born for an extraordinary destiny. She was meant to discover the flaws in her own beliefs, to act against her deepest convictions, and to love someone else—not out of passion, but out of duty and compassion.

At nineteen, she found herself surrendering to new feelings, embracing new responsibilities, stepping into a new home. She became a wife, a mistress of a household, and a patroness of a village. Colonel Brandon, in turn, found in her the comfort he had long sought. His sorrows were eased by her regard; her presence brought light back into his life. And Marianne, in loving him fully, found her own fulfillment in making him happy.

Marianne never loved halfway. Her heart, once devoted entirely to Willoughby, now gave itself completely to Colonel Brandon.

Willoughby, upon hearing of her marriage, felt a pang—but soon after, he received the final blow: Mrs. Smith forgave him, citing his marriage to a woman of character as the reason. This revelation taught him that had he treated Marianne with honor, he might have had both happiness and wealth. His remorse was sincere. He envied Colonel Brandon, and grieved for Marianne. But he did not remain broken. He did not flee society or dwell in perpetual sorrow. He lived. He hunted, bred horses, raised dogs, and found joy in the pleasures of life. He remained fond of Marianne, always regarding her as the ideal woman—no other beauty ever measured up in his eyes.

Mrs. Dashwood, wise and practical, stayed at the cottage rather than move to Delaford. Fortunately for Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, Margaret had grown old enough to dance—and was now considered quite eligible for romance.

Between Barton and Delaford, there was a steady flow of affection and companionship—natural to close-knit families. Among the many blessings of Elinor and Marianne’s lives, let it not be forgotten that though they were sisters, living almost within sight of one another, they lived together in perfect harmony—without conflict between themselves, or discord between their husbands.
