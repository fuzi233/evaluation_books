In Which the Printer's Devil Knocks at the Door

Arthur Pendennis, amidst the humdrum delights of his bachelor existence, suddenly sensed a shadow lengthening over his feast. His coin was running dangerously thin. A substantial chunk had vanished into the realm of the club subscription, followed closely by the heavy price tags on his new furniture. Now, standing before the final five-pound note in his wallet, he faced a terrifying silence: there was no replacement currency on the horizon. For a boy raised to believe he was a prince—or at the very least a cherished infant fed by a loving mother—such a fiscal cliff was a shock akin to falling from a tree.

His companion, Warrington, remained blissfully ignorant of the depth of this abyss. To Warrington, Pen appeared a creature of pure aristocracy: an only child, the heir to a country estate and the whims of a dandy Uncle who dined daily with high society. Pen possessed gold chains and a dressing case worthy of a Duke. Yet, his economy was not that of a prudent merchant, but of a spoiled child. He could not be bothered to tip a waiter twopence; he would hail a cab regardless of rain or wind, invariably paying double the fare; he despised "cleaned gloves" and the concept of saving pennies. If he had been born to a fortune of ten thousand a year, he likely would have been indistinguishable from his current self, a man who would happily hand his last shilling to a beggar with a pitiful face or a pair of crying children. It was a sumptuous nature, perhaps, that refused to look twice at a ledger, driven by a generous heart and a vanity that relished the praise of even the lowest cab driver.

Pen had not deemed it necessary to confess his financial ruin to Warrington. While his friend knew of his wild college days, the specifics of his mother's dwindling resources and his own catastrophic spending habits had been kept in the dark.

The truth finally spilled out as Pen stared grimly at the change for the last five-pound note lying on the pub tray beside Warrington's pot of ale.

"It is the last rose of summer," Pen lamented, his voice heavy with theatrical despair. "Its blooming companions have fled long ago; and behold, the last flower of the garland has dropped its leaves!" He recounted the tale of his mother's failing funds, his own follies, and Laura's boundless generosity. As he spoke, Warrington smoked his pipe, listening with intense gravity.

"Poor impecuniosity will do you good," Warrington declared, tapping ash onto the floor. "For an honest man, nothing is more wholesome than a state of tick. It is an alterative and a tonic. It keeps your moral constitution in a perpetual state of alertness. Think of a rider facing a fence, or a swordsman staring down an opponent; he is forced to look the obstacle squarely in the eye and brace himself to strike or parry. A pinch of necessity brings out your pluck if you have any, and nerves you to wrestle with Fortune. You'll soon discover what you can do without when you've no money to buy it. You won't need new gloves, varnished boots, eau de Cologne, or cabs. You've been bred a molly-coddle, Pen, and spoiled by the ladies. A single man with health and brains who cannot find a livelihood in this world doesn't deserve to breathe its air. Pay your last halfpenny and leap over Waterloo Bridge. Steal a leg of mutton, get transported, and leave the country—you're not fit to live in it."

"You have certainly spoken," Pen admitted, "but how is one to live? There is beef and bread aplenty in England, but it costs money or work. And who will hire my labor? What trade can I perform?"

Warrington erupted in laughter. "Suppose we advertise in the *Times*," he suggested, "seeking an usher's post at a classical and commercial academy. 'A gentleman, B.A. of St. Boniface College, who was plucked for his degree,'"—Pen groaned, "Wishes to impart lessons in classics and mathematics, plus the rudiments of French. He can cut hair, supervise the younger pupils, and play the piano second chair to the Principal's daughters. Address A.P., Lamb Court, Temple.'"

"Go on," Pen muttered, though his curiosity piqued.

"Men adapt to all sorts of vocations," Warrington continued, amused. "Consider Bloundell, a professional blackleg who tours the Continent, picking up young fellows of fashion and fleecing them. Then there is Bob O'Toole, my school chum, who now drives the Ballynafad mail, ferrying honest Jack Finucane's correspondence. I know a doctor's son—forgive the offense—who argued with his guardian over finances. When he reached his last five-pound note, what did he do? He let his mustaches grow, migrated to a provincial town, introduced himself as Professor Spineto, chiropodist to the Emperor of All the Russians, and by performing a miraculous operation on the local newspaper editor, established a reputable practice for three years. He has since reconciled with his family and inherited his father's gallopots."

"Hang the gallopots!" Pen cried. "I cannot drive a coach, trim corns, or cheat at cards. That is all you propose."

"Yes," Warrington insisted, "there is our own correspondent. Every man has his secrets. Before you revealed your finances, I believed you a man of fortune; your airs and demeanor suggested it. Clearly, you cannot draw further from your mother. You must settle accounts with that trump card of a girl, Laura. Here is to Laura! And you must carry a hod rather than ask for a shilling from home."

"But how does one earn?" Pen asked, desperate.

"How do I live, pray?" Warrington countered. "On my younger brother's allowance, Pendennis? I have secrets of my own, my boy." His expression sobered. "I squandered that allowance five years ago. If I had taken my own life a little sooner, it might have been better. I have played off my own bat ever since. I require little money. When my purse is empty, I return to work to fill it, then lounge like a serpent or an Indian until I have digested the wealth. Observe," Warrington added, displaying a long, gaunt purse containing but a few sovereigns. "I begin to feel hollow."

"And how do you replenish it?" Pen pressed.

"I write," Warrington whispered, blushing slightly. "I prefer not to announce it openly. I dislike having it said that George Warrington writes for bread. However, I contribute to the *Law Reviews*; examine these articles." He flipped through several sheets. "Occasionally, I write for a newspaper where a friend serves as editor." Leading Pen to the club later, he requested a file of the *Dawn*, pointing silently to a few pieces. Pen read them with delight, recognizing immediately the strong thoughts, curt periods, biting satire, and scholarly depth of his friend's prose.

"I am not up to this," Pen confessed, admiring his friend's talents. "I know little of politics or history, and my Latin is merely a smattering. I cannot soar on wings like yours."

"But you can on your own, my boy, which are lighter and perhaps soar higher," Warrington replied kindly. "Those little scraps and verses I've seen display a rare natural gift. Need not blush, you conceited young jackanapes. You've harbored this thought for a decade. You possess the sacred flame—a touch of real poetic fire, I suspect. Our oil lamps are mere candles compared to it. You are a poet, Pen, my boy." Stretching out his broad hand, he clapped Pen on the shoulder.

Arthur was so overwhelmed by the compliment that tears welled in his eyes. "How kind you are, Warrington!" he exclaimed.

"I like you, old boy," Warrington said. "I was terribly lonely in chambers, craving someone. Your honest face somehow pleased me. I liked the way you laughed at Lowton—that poor, good little snob. And, frankly, the reason I cannot tell—it is simply that I am alone in the world, sir, and I needed someone to keep me company." A glimmer of profound kindness and melancholy passed through Warrington's dark eyes.

Pen was too elated by his newfound identity to notice his friend's sorrow. "Thank you, Warrington," he said, "for your friendship and your words. I have often suspected I was a poet. I will be one—I am one, as you say, though the world may disagree." He hesitated. "Was it *Ariadne in Naxos* you favored? I wrote it at eighteen."

Warrington roared with laughter. "Young goose! Of all the miserable, weak rubbish I've ever attempted, *Ariadne in Naxos* is the most mawkish and disgusting. The Prize Poem is so pompous and feeble I'm surprised it didn't win the medal. Do you imagine you are a serious poet, preparing to supplant Milton and Aeschylus? Do you intend to become a Pindar, absurd little tom-tit, believing you possess the strength and pinions of the Theban eagle soaring supreme through the azure fields? No, my boy, I think you can manage a magazine article and compose a decent stanza; that is my verdict
