Sunlight came slanting in through my attic window and landed, with a sort of insolent cheerfulness, on the wreckage of my respectability. I sat up and listened.

The streets were already in full throat—carts, cries, the hard iron scuffle of London getting on with itself without the least regard for my private catastrophe. I stretched for my watch. No watch. I turned my head, which appeared to have been replaced in the night by something larger, heavier, and—judging by the throbbing—partly made of mallet.

My clothes lay about as if they had tried to escape and been overtaken. One boot stood upright on the chair by the fireplace, like a moral lesson. The other had gone into hiding, as though ashamed to be associated with me.

A peal of church chimes came through the open air—clean, orderly sound, the sort of sound that makes you feel you ought to be clean and orderly too. I counted. Eleven.

I sprang out of bed, and sat down at once on the floor.

The room was still politely turning, not the reckless whirl of last night’s nonsense, but a weary rotation, as if it had danced all night and now, out of sheer habit, could not quite remember how to be still. I crawled to the washstand and, after two false dives, got my head into the basin. Cold water has a way of talking to you plainly.

Then, by degrees, I drew on my trousers, reached the easy-chair, and began to reconstruct the universe from the only point available: the present, which was disgusting, and moving backward toward the cause, which I suspected would be worse.

I felt very ill. Something had disagreed with me.

“That cigar,” I whispered, weakly and with a strong desire to be unjust to an innocent object. “I ought never to have ventured upon it. And that little room with all those people. And I have been working hard. I must take more exercise.”

It gave me a momentary satisfaction—like a man finding a coin in the street while his house burns—to discover that, cowardly though I might be, I was not wholly to be taken in by my own excuses.

“Nonsense,” I told myself abruptly. “Don’t be an idiot. You were drunk.”

“Not drunk,” I pleaded; “don’t say drunk. It’s such a coarse word. Some people can’t stand sweet champagne. It affected my liver. Do make it a question of liver.”

“Drunk,” I persisted with the brutality of a better self that has no pity. “Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk—drunk as any ’Arry after a Bank Holiday.”

“It is the first time,” I murmured.

“It was your first opportunity,” I answered.

“Never again,” I promised.

“The stock phrase,” I replied.

“How old are you?”

“Nineteen.”

“So you haven’t even the excuse of youth. How do you know it won’t grow upon you—that, having thus begun your downward career, you won’t sink and sink, and end as a confirmed sot?”

My big head fell into my hands. Temperance tales read on pious Sunday afternoons marched back upon me in grim procession. I saw myself, with an imagination that was never moderate in any direction, hurrying toward a drunkard’s grave: now struggling heroically, now yielding weakly, the craving thickening like fog. In the mist I saw my father’s white face, my mother’s sad eyes; Barbara’s mouth, lovely enough to scorn one to death; Hal’s tremendous contempt, which would have made weakness feel not only ashamed but badly mannered.

“It shall be never again!” I cried aloud. “By God, it shall!” (At nineteen one swears as if heaven itself were a witness in a police-court.) “I will leave this house at once,” I continued, addressing my room as though it had lured me to ruin; “I will get away from its unwholesome atmosphere. I will wipe it out of my mind, and all connected with it. I will make a fresh start. I will—”

Something, which had been lurking like a debt at the back of my brain, stepped forward and sat down in the middle of my resolution: the flabby, smiling figure of Miss Rosina Sellars.

“What is she doing here?” I demanded of myself. “What right has she—”

“The right of your affianced bride,” my other half supplied, with a grin that I would have struck if it had possessed a face.

“Did I really go as far as that?”

“We will not go into details,” I answered; “I do not wish to dwell upon them. That was the result.”

“I was not quite myself,” I protested. “I didn’t know what I was doing.”

“As a rule, we don’t when we do foolish things. But we abide by the consequences all the same. Unfortunately it happened in the presence of witnesses, and she is not the sort of lady to be easily got rid of. You will marry her and settle down with her in two small rooms. Her people will be your people. You will come to know them better before many days are passed. Among them she is regarded as ‘the lady,’ from which you may judge of them. A nice commencement of your career, is it not? A nice mess you’ve made of it!”

“What am I to do?” I asked.

“Upon my word, I don’t know,” I replied.

I passed a wretched day. I would not face Mrs. Peedles, nor even the slavey, as if the very sight of another human being would make my disgrace sound louder. I kept to my room with the door locked, and toward dusk—feeling a shade less as if my head were being used as a drum—I slipped out and ate a meal that was intended to be penitential: tea without sugar and a kippered herring at a coffee-house near by. The kipper—hot, sharp, honest, all salt and smoke—was the only thing that seemed to have any moral position in the world. A gentleman sat opposite and ordered hot buttered toast. The smell of it—warm, rich, irresponsible—made me feel that mankind was not taking my downfall seriously enough, and I fled.

At eight Minikin called round from the office to ask what had happened. I confessed, appealing to shame to make it seem smaller than it was.

“Thought as much,” he said. “Seems to have been an A1 from the look of you.”

“I’m glad it’s happened, now it’s over,” I assured him. “It will be a lesson I shall never forget.”

“I know,” said Minikin. “Nothing like a fair and square drunk for making you feel real good; better than a sermon.”

In my trouble I wanted advice. Minikin, though younger than I, had the calm, worldly air of a person who had been born with his sleeves rolled up.

“That’s not the worst,” I admitted. “What do you think I’ve done?”

“Killed a policeman?” suggested Minikin.

“Got myself engaged.”

“No one like you quiet fellows for going it when you do begin,” he observed. “Nice girl?”

“I don’t know,” I said. “I only know I don’t want her. How can I get out of it?”

Minikin took out his left eye and began polishing it on his handkerchief—a habit he had when thought was required, as other men rub their chins. He seemed to consult the glass orb for counsel.

“Take-her-own-part sort of a girl?”

I intimated that he had drawn Miss Rosina Sellars to the life.

“Know how much you’re earning?”

“She knows I live up here in this attic and do my own cooking,” I answered.

Minikin glanced round the room. “Must be fond of you.”

“She thinks I’m clever,” I explained, “and that I shall make my way.”

“And she’s willing to wait?”

I nodded.

“Well, I should let her wait,” said Minikin, replacing his eye. “There’s plenty of time before you.”

“But she’s a barmaid,” I groaned, “and she’ll expect me to walk out with her, to take her out on Sundays, to go and see her friends. I can’t do it. Besides, she’s right: I mean to get on. Then she’ll stick to me. It’s awful.”

“How did it happen?” asked Minikin.

“I don’t know. I didn’t know I’d done it till it was over.”

“Anybody present?”

“Half-a-dozen,” I said, as if that number were a sentence.

The door opened and Jarman walked in; he never knocked anywhere, as if doors were a superstition. Instead of his usual noisy greeting, he crossed in silence and shook my hand with the solemnity of a man offering condolences over a funeral.

“Friend of yours?” he asked, indicating Minikin.

I introduced them.

“Proud to meet you,” said Jarman.

“Glad to hear it,” said Minikin. “Don’t look as if you’d got much else to be stuck up about.”

“Don’t mind him,” I told Jarman. “He was born like it.”

“Wonderful gift,” replied Jarman. “D’you know what I should do if I ’ad it? ’Ire myself out to break up evening parties. Ever thought of it seriously?”

Minikin said he would consider the idea.

“You’d make your fortune going round the suburbs,” Jarman assured him. Then he turned his attention back to my ruins. “Pity you weren’t ’ere last night,” he added. “Might ’ave saved our young friend a deal of trouble. Has ’e told you the news?”

I said Minikin knew everything.

“Now you’ve got a good, steady eye,” said Jarman to Minikin, who, in his manner, had fixed him with the polished orb; “’ow d’you think ’e is looking?”

“As well as can be expected under the circumstances,” returned Minikin.

“Does ’e know the circumstances?” asked Jarman. “Has ’e seen the girl?”

I confessed I had not yet enjoyed that privilege.

“Then ’e don’t know the worst,” said Jarman. “A hundred and sixty pounds of ’er, and still growing! Bit of a load for ’im, ain’t it?”

“Some of ’em do have luck,” said Minikin.

Jarman leaned forward and examined Minikin’s new acquaintance with interest.

“That’s a fine ’ead of yours,” he remarked. “All your own? No offence,” he added, without giving Minikin time to retaliate. “I was merely thinking there must be room for a lot of sense in it. Now, what do you, as a practical man, advise ’im: dose of poison, or Waterloo Bridge and a brick?”

“I suppose there’s no doubt,” I put in, “that we are actually engaged?”

“Not a blooming shadow,” said Jarman cheerfully, “so far as she’s concerned.”

“I shall tell her plainly,” I said, clutching at honesty as if it were a life-buoy, “that I was drunk.”

“And ’ow are you going to convince ’er of it?” asked Jarman. “You think your telling ’er you loved ’er proves it. So it would to anybody else—but not to ’er. Besides, if every girl is going to give up ’er catch just because the fellow ’adn’t all ’is wits about ’im at the time—well, what do you think?” He appealed to Minikin.

Minikin agreed that if such a principle prevailed, girls might as well shut up shop.

Jarman, having “got even” with Minikin, became almost cordial and drew his chair nearer, engaging him in a confidential argument from which I was excluded as completely as if I had been a teapot.

“You see,” explained Jarman, “this ain’t an ordinary case. This chap’s going to be the future Poet Laureate. Now when the Prince of Wales invites ’im to dine at Marlborough ’ouse, ’e don’t want to go there tacked on to a girl that carries aitches with ’er in a bag, and don’t know which end of the spoon out of which to drink ’er soup.”

“It makes a difference,” agreed Minikin.

“What we’ve got to do,” said Jarman, “is to get ’im out of it. And upon my sivvy, blessed if I see ’ow to do it!”

“She fancies him?” asked Minikin.

“What she fancies,” said Jarman, “is that nature intended ’er to be a lady. And it’s no use pointing out the mistake, because she ain’t got sense enough to see it.”

“No good talking straight to her,” suggested Minikin—“telling her it can never be?”

“That’s our difficulty,” replied Jarman; “it can be. This chap—” (I listened as a prisoner listens to counsel, interested and helpless) “don’t know enough to come in out of the rain. ’E’s just the sort of chap this sort of thing does ’appen to.”

“But he don’t want her,” urged Minikin. “He says he don’t want her.”

“Yes, to you and me,” said Jarman. “And of course ’e don’t. I’m not saying ’e’s a natural-born idiot. But let ’er come along and do a snivel—tell ’im ’e’s breaking ’er ’eart, and appeal to ’im to be’ave as a gentleman—and what do you think will be the result?”

Minikin admitted the problem had points.

“Of course, if ’twas you or me, we’d just tell ’er to put ’erself away somewhere where the moth couldn’t get at ’er and wait till we sent round for ’er, and there’d be an end. But with ’im it’s different.”

“He is a bit of a soft,” said Minikin.

“’Tain’t ’is fault,” explained Jarman; “’twas the way ’e was brought up. ’E fancies girls are like in plays—going about saying ‘Un’and me!’ ‘Let me pass!’ Maybe some of ’em are. But this ain’t one of ’em.”

“How did it happen?” asked Minikin again.

“’Ow does it ’appen nine times out of ten?” returned Jarman. “’E was a bit misty, and she was wide awake. ’E gets a bit spoony and—well, you know.”

“Artful things, girls,” said Minikin.

“Can’t blame ’em,” Jarman replied, magnanimous as a judge. “It’s their business. Got to dispose of themselves somehow. Oughtn’t to be binding without a written order dated the next morning; that’d make it all right.”

“Couldn’t prove a prior engagement?” Minikin suggested.

“She’d want to see the girl first,” said Jarman. “Only natural.”

“Couldn’t get a girl?” urged Minikin.

“Who could you trust?” asked Jarman, cautious. “Besides, there ain’t time. She’s letting ’im rest to-day; to-morrow evening she’ll be down on ’im.”

“Don’t see anything for it,” Minikin decided, “but for him to do a bunk.”

“Not a bad idea,” mused Jarman. “Only where’s ’e to bunk to?”

“Needn’t go far,” said Minikin.

“She’d find ’im and follow ’im,” said Jarman. “She can look after ’erself, mind you. Don’t go doing ’er any injustice.”

“He could change his name,” suggested Minikin.

“’Ow could ’e get a crib? No character, no references.”

Then Jarman sprang up as if the ceiling had whispered to him.

“I’ve got it—the stage!”

“Can he act?” asked Minikin.

“Can do anything,” retorted Jarman, “that don’t want too much sense. That’s ’is sanctuary, the stage. No questions asked, no character wanted. Lord! why didn’t I think of it before?”

“Wants a bit of getting on to, doesn’t it?” said Minikin.

“Depends where you want to get,” replied Jarman. For the first time he turned directly to me. “Can you sing?” he demanded.

“A little,” I said. “Not in public.”

“Sing something now,” ordered Jarman. “Let’s ’ear you. Wait a minute!”

He was out of the room before I could protest. I heard him pause on the landing and knock at the door of the fair Rosina’s room. In a moment he returned.

“It’s all right,” he said. “She’s not in yet. Now sing for all you’re worth. Remember, it’s for life and freedom.”

I sang “Sally in Our Alley,” but without much of the gentlemanly tremor that song requires. With every mention of the lady’s name, my imagination obligingly supplied Miss Sellars, abundant and undeniable, and the romance died in my throat. Still, Jarman was satisfied.

“It ain’t what I call a grand opera voice,” he remarked, “but it ought to do all right for a chorus where economy is the chief point. Now, I’ll tell you what to do. You go to-morrow straight to the O’Kelly, and put the whole thing before ’im. ’E’s a good sort. ’E’ll touch you up a bit, maybe give you introductions. Lucky for you, this is just the right time. There’s one or two things coming on, and if Fate ain’t dead against you, you’ll lose your amorita—or whatever it’s called—and not find ’er again till it’s too late.”

That evening I was not in a mood to believe in rescue of any sort. Still, I thanked them, promised to consider it when my head was less like a crime, and they rose to go.

Minikin went down alone. Jarman, having reached the door, returned and whispered with the handle in his hand:

“Consols are down a bit this week. If you want a little of the ready to carry you through, don’t go selling out. I can manage a few pounds. Suck a couple of lemons and you’ll be all right in the morning. So long.”

I sucked the lemons—an acid penance, but effective—and next morning, finding the advice sound, went to the office as usual. Lott & Co., in consideration of my agreeing to a deduction of two shillings from the week’s salary, overlooked my appearance with the indulgence shown to unavoidable disasters.

I had meant to act upon Jarman’s plan at once and go that evening to see the O’Kelly at his Hampstead address of respectability. I had even posted a note. Before leaving the office, however, I received his reply: he would be out that evening; would I come the following Friday instead?

Disappointed, I returned to my lodgings in a depressed state of mind. Jarman’s scheme, which in daylight had looked hopeful—almost romantic—now seemed shadowy and impossible. As I passed the open door of the first-floor parlour, its emptiness struck cold. The disappearance of the Lady ’Ortensia left the house not merely quieter but hollow. (It is astonishing how much companionship can be provided by a person one disapproves of, if only she will talk to you.)

Unable to work, the old ache of loneliness returned. I sat in the dark, listening to the scratching pen of my neighbour, the old law-writer. There are pens that write as if they were gnawing their way through time; his did that, and the sound always brought with it a grey despair, as if all the language in the world had been reduced to clauses, sub-clauses, and the steady murder of joy.

After all, I thought, was not the sympathy of the Lady ’Ortensia—stimulated for personal purposes though it might be—better than nothing? At least it had been a living sound in the house, a human voice among the mechanical scrape and street-roar. Now I belonged to nobody; and the mind, when it is frightened, will cling even to a rope that is frayed.

In this mood I heard a slight tap at my door. The slavey stood in the dim passage, holding out a note. I took it, returned, and lighted my candle.

The envelope was pink and scented, as if it had been stored in a drawer with an ambitious bar of soap. It was addressed, in handwriting not so bad as I had expected, to “Paul Kelver, Esquire.” I opened it and read:

“Dr mr. Paul— I herd as how you was took hill hafter the party. I feer you are not strong. You must not work so hard or you will be hill and then I shall be very cros with you. I hop you are well now. If so I am going for a wark and you may come with me if you are good. With much love. From your affechonat ROSIE.”

In spite of the spelling, a curious tingling stole over me as I read my first love-letter. My eyes grew a little foolish. A mist swam up—half sentiment, half the after-effects of champagne. Through it, glorified and softened like a portrait in a cheap frame, I saw my betrothed’s face, pasty yet inviting, and her large white arms stretched toward me as if they were an argument.

A sudden hot haste seized me. I dressed with trembling hands—anxiety to act before thought could catch me. Then I descended and knocked, nervously, at the door of the second-floor back.

“Who’s that?” came Miss Sellars’ sharp voice.

“It is I—Paul.”

“Oh, wait a minute, dear.” Her tone sweetened at once. There followed scurrying footsteps, the rustle of clothes, the bang of drawers, a moment’s dead silence, and then:

“You can come in now, dear.”

I entered. The room was small and untidy, smelling of smoky lamp and stale finery. But at first I saw only Miss Sellars, arms lifted above her head, pinning her hat on straw-coloured hair.

And with the sight of her in the flesh my mood changed as suddenly as if some hidden string had snapped. While waiting outside I had been possessed by an almost uncontrollable desire to turn the handle and rush in. Now—had I obeyed impulse—I should have run out.

It was not that she was unpleasant-looking. It was the air of commonness that lay about her like a fog that no scent could drive off. That finikin fastidiousness of mine which could spoil my appetite for a good chop if the waitress had dirty finger-nails now fastened itself with cruel accuracy on Miss Sellars’ red rough hands and the one or two warts that grew there. I could not look away, any more than one can stop listening to an annoying noise once one has noticed it.

“You’re a very naughty boy,” said Miss Sellars, finishing her hat. “Why didn’t you come in and see me in the dinner-h_our? I’ve a great mind not to kiss you.”

The powder she had dabbed on in haste was plainly visible. Her round soft arms were hidden beneath ill-fitting sleeves of some harsh, crepey stuff that made my teeth ache merely to imagine it against the skin. I wished her resolve about the kiss had been firm. Instead she relented, offered her flowery cheek, and I saluted it gingerly. The taste reminded me of pale dough-cakes sold at school by the porter’s wife—thin, sweet, and satisfying in a way one later regrets.

At the top of the kitchen stairs Miss Sellars paused and called down shrilly to Mrs. Peedles, who appeared in course of time, panting as if she had been chased by her own curiosity.

“Oh, me and Mr. Kelver are going out for a short walk, Mrs. Peedles. I shan’t want any supper. Good night.”

“Oh, good night, my dear,” replied Mrs. Peedles. “Hope you’ll enjoy yourselves. Is Mr. Kelver there?”

“He’s round the corner,” I heard Miss Sellars explain in a lower voice; and there followed a snigger.

“He’s a bit shy, ain’t he?” Mrs. Peedles whispered.

“I’ve had enough of the other sort,” Miss Sellars answered, also low.

“Ah well; it’s the shy ones that come out the strongest after a bit—leastways, that’s been my experience.”

“He’ll do all right. So long.”

Miss Sellars, buttoning a burst glove, rejoined me with the air of a person who has announced a public event and expects the world to take note.

“I suppose you’ve never had a sweetheart before?” she asked, as we turned into the Blackfriars Road.

I admitted this was my first experience.

“I can’t a-bear a flirty man,” explained Miss Sellars. “That’s why I took to you from the beginning. You was so quiet.”

I began to wish Nature had endowed me with a temperament like a street-organ.

“Anybody could see you was a gentleman,” she continued. “Heaps and heaps of hoffers I’ve had—_h_undreds, you might almost say. But what I’ve always told ’em is, ‘I like you very much indeed as a friend, but I’m not going to marry any one but a gentleman.’ Don’t you think I was right?”

I murmured that it was only what I should have expected from her.

“You may take my harm, if you like,” suggested Miss Sellars, as we crossed St. George’s Circus; and linked together, we proceeded along the Kennington Park Road.

Happily there was little need for me to talk. Miss Sellars supplied almost all the conversation, and every word of it concerned herself, as though she were giving me a guided tour of Miss Sellars.

I learned that her instincts since childhood had been towards gentility. This, she assured me, was hardly surprising, considering that her family—on her mother’s side, at all events—were connected distinctly with “the _h_ighest in the land.” _Mesalliances_, however, were common; and one, particularly flagrant, her “Mar” had committed—she had married, of all things, a waiter. Miss Sellars stopped in the act of crossing Newington Butts to shudder at the recollection, and was nearly run down by a tram, which seemed to me a practical comment on the hazards of dwelling too much on one’s ancestry in the middle of a public road.

Mr. and Mrs. Sellars, it appeared, had not “hit it off.” Could one wonder? Mrs. Sellars with an uncle on the Stock Exchange, and Mr. Sellars with one on Peckham Rye. His chief calling seemed to have been “three shies a penny.” Mrs. Sellars was now, however, happily dead; and if no other good came from the catastrophe, it had fixed Miss Sellars’ determination to take warning by her mother’s error and avoid any further connection with the lowly born. She, with my help, would lift the family back to its proper social position—like a saucepan restored to the top shelf, away from the grease.

“It used to be a joke against me,” she explained, “even when I was quite a child. I never could tolerate anything low. Why, one day when I was only seven years old, what do you think happened?”

I confessed my inability to guess.

“Well, I’ll tell you,” said Miss Sellars; “it’ll just show you. Uncle Joseph—that was father’s uncle, you understand?”
