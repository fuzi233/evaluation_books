Rosa had learned early to taste absence the way other children tasted jam—sweet at first, because it was served with pity, and then bitter in the mouth because it never ended.

She could not remember any home before the Nuns’ House with the steadiness with which she remembered a single room, a single bed, and a single dress darkened by water. Her mother—so young in Rosa’s mind that she seemed hardly older than Rosa herself—had been carried in from a pleasure-party drowned, laid out in a thin summer gown whose every fold and faded colour Rosa could still count as if with her fingertips. The wet hair had streamed and clung; and, caught in it, crushed petals—little shreds of flowers that had gone to ruin against that quiet head. The picture had fixed itself like a smell that will not leave a garment: river-cold, bruised green, and the faint, sick sweetness of something once festive.

Her father’s grief followed as surely as the tide. There had been a year of wildness, then a year of being bowed down, and then—on the first anniversary—he died as if the calendar itself had turned a key in him.

It was in that same year, when kindness went looking for something to do with its hands, that old friends—Drood and the young widower who was Rosa’s father—had fastened a plan together: the children should be pledged. The plan outlived the men who made it. Drood, too, went silently where all pilgrimages end; and so Edwin and Rosa came to stand on the path laid for them by dead feet.

From the time Rosa had been brought to Cloisterham, there had always been a tender atmosphere around her, like gauze hung between her and the world. It changed colour as she changed—pale with her childhood, then brightening as she grew prettier and livelier—but it never lifted. The desire to console her made people treat her as younger than she was, and then, when she was grown, made them still pet her as if she were not. There were soft rivalries at the Nuns’ House about who should please her most: who should tuck some little present into her hand, who should do a trifling service, who should take her away for holidays, who should write the most letters. The rivalry was all smiles and ribbons on the surface, but it could pinch a little underneath; and if such gentler competitions could sour, who knew what harsher contests the good Nuns might once have hidden beneath veil and rosary?

Thus Rosa grew into a small, winning creature—amiable, giddy, wilful—spoilt, yes, in that she counted on kindness as on daylight, but not spoilt in any cold way; for she repaid affection with affection. There was a deep well in her, always sparkling at the top and never yet truly stirred. What would happen when it was—what strong draught might be drawn up from its depth—remained to be seen.

This morning, before Miss Twinkleton had descended to breakfast, the Nuns’ House was already humming with news, as if every shutter had been opened and gossip had flown in on the air.

How it came, nobody could tell. Perhaps it rode on the sparrows’ scuffle in the courtyard; perhaps it was folded into the baker’s loaves like a dark currant, or poured into the milk along with whatever else the milkman poured. Perhaps the housemaids, beating their mats at the gateposts, beat out more than dust and beat in the town’s whisper. However it happened, the story had found every gable and passage, and climbed the stairs to Miss Twinkleton’s sacred room itself, arriving by way of Mrs. Tisher while Miss Twinkleton was dressing—sacrificing to the Graces, as she would have phrased it to any mythological parent.

At first, the tale was one shape, then another, as rumours do when they are cooked too fast and too often.

Miss Landless’s brother had thrown a bottle at Mr. Edwin Drood.

No—he had thrown a knife.

A knife suggested a fork; therefore he had thrown a fork.

By the time the kitchen had seasoned it to its liking, he had thrown bottle, knife, fork, and decanter—every hard, shining thing that could fly from a table—and, with no more introduction than a clatter, had sent them all whistling at Mr. Edwin Drood’s head.

The cook, who dealt in causes as she dealt in gravies (thickening everything), explained why. Miss Landless’s brother had said he admired Miss Bud. Mr. Edwin Drood had replied that he had no business to admire Miss Bud. And then—up came the bottle, knife, fork, and decanter. The cook’s mind could not see a quarrel without imagining crockery in motion.

Poor little Rosa, at the first burst of it, put a forefinger into each ear and slipped into a corner as if into a cupboard, beseeching—no, commanding—never to be told another word. But Helena Landless, pale and resolved, asked leave of Miss Twinkleton to go to her brother; and since she plainly looked like one who would go without leave if leave were refused, she took the more measured course of going first to Mr. Crisparkle for the truth of it.

When she returned, she was closeted with Miss Twinkleton—as if the head of the house were a fine sieve through which the news must pass before any girl might swallow it. What Helena told Rosa alone was as exact as she could make it: there had been words; there had been provocation. She spoke with a flush on her cheek, dwelling on the affront her brother had endured, touching lightly—because she would not wound Rosa—on the ease with which Edwin could begin such things by taking the world so lightly. She brought, too, a petition from Neville Landless: he begged Rosa to forgive him. Helena delivered it with sisterly force, and then would have the subject ended, as if it were a dish set down too hot and too dangerous to be handled further.

It fell to Miss Twinkleton to cool the establishment, to take the boiling pot and set it back on the hob.

So she entered, stately, into what plebeians called the school-room but what she—by patrician twist of language—named “the apartment allotted to study.” She said, with a forensic air that made even a whisper feel like contempt of court:

“Ladies!”

All rose. Mrs. Tisher grouped herself behind her chief as if forming, by posture alone, a second Miss Twinkleton of lesser historical importance.

Miss Twinkleton spoke of Rumour—painted full of tongues by the Bard of Avon—needless to mention the immortal Shakespeare, also called the Swan of his native river, with an allusion to the superstition of that bird singing sweetly on the approach of death (for which, she conceded, there was no ornithological authority). Rumour in Cloisterham—Miss Ferdinand would honour her with attention—was no exception. A slight fracas between two young gentlemen, occurring last night within a hundred miles of these peaceful walls, had been grossly exaggerated. In the first alarm arising from sympathy with “a sweet young friend” not wholly dissociable from one of the gladiators in this bloodless arena, certain maiden elevations had descended into unfit discussion. Responsible inquiry had shown it to be one of those airy nothings pointed at by the Poet—Miss Giggles would supply his name and date of birth within half an hour—and now the subject would be discarded in favour of the grateful labours of the day.

The subject, however, lived on like a smell of scorched sugar that will cling to a clean kitchen. It survived into dinner, where Miss Ferdinand, in defiance of both discipline and decency, contrived a paper moustache, clapped it on, and performed the pantomime of aiming a water-bottle at Miss Giggles, who, in spirited defence, drew a table-spoon like a sword. The clink of it, ridiculous as it was, made Rosa’s stomach feel hollow.

For Rosa could not keep her mind from the quarrel. It came back again and again, like a refrain in a song you do not like but cannot forget. She felt—uncomfortably, guiltily—that she was involved in it: cause, consequence, pretext, prize. It tangled with that other discomfort which never quite left her when she was with Edwin: the sense that she stood in a false position altogether as to her engagement. If she could not feel easy beside him, she was not likely to feel easy away from him. And today she was thrown upon herself, deprived of her new friend’s free companionship, because the quarrel belonged to Helena’s brother and Helena—honest in everything—avoided the subject as both delicate and painful.

At this very time, when Rosa most wanted an ally and least wanted an examiner, her guardian was announced.

Mr. Grewgious had been chosen for her trust because he was incorruptible; for any other surface suitability, one would have searched him as vainly as for a rose in a dry ditch. He was an arid, sandy man who looked as if, put into a mill, he would grind at once into snuff. His hair was a scant, flat crop—colour and consistency like mangy yellow fur. It looked too unlike hair to be real; yet the notion that anyone would wear such a wig was so stupendous that it must have been his own. His face offered little play; the few curves it had were cut so deep they seemed more like hard work than expression. There were notches in his forehead as if Nature had meant to chisel him into sensibility, had grown impatient, and had thrown down the tool: Let him go unfinished.

Too much throat at the top, too much heel and ankle at the bottom; near-sighted, shambling, awkward, and hesitating; with black suit and a betraying flash of white cotton stocking—he should have been merely absurd. Yet, for all that, there was in him some odd power of making an agreeable impression, as some very plain broth can be made sustaining by honesty of ingredients.

Rosa found him, on being admitted, plainly discomfited by the grandeur of being in Miss Twinkleton’s company in Miss Twinkleton’s own sacred room. He looked like a man with a dim foreboding of being examined in something he had never been taught.

“My dear, how do you do? I am glad to see you. My dear, how much improved you are. Permit me to hand you a chair, my dear.”

Miss Twinkleton rose at her writing-table, the very picture of sweet consideration addressed to the polite Universe.

“Will you permit me to retire?”

“By no means, madam, on my account. I beg that you will not move.”

“I must entreat permission to move,” returned Miss Twinkleton, giving the word a little curving grace. “But I will not withdraw, since you are so obliging. If I wheel my desk to this corner window, shall I be in the way?”

“Madam! In the way!”

“You are very kind. Rosa, my dear, you will be under no restraint, I am sure.”

So Miss Twinkleton wheeled her desk to the corner window, where she could both write and listen without seeming to listen; and Rosa and Mr. Grewgious remained by the fire.

“My dear, how do you do? I am glad to see you, my dear,” Mr. Grewgious said again, as if the phrase were a key he had to turn twice before the lock would yield. When Rosa sat, he sat too.

“My visits,” he began, “are like those of the angels—not that I compare myself to an angel.”

“No, sir,” said Rosa, with involuntary sincerity.

“Not by any means,” he agreed, quite unoffended. “I merely refer to my visits, which are few and far between. The angels are, we know very well, up-stairs.”

Miss Twinkleton looked round with a stiff stare that made her pen pause.

“I refer, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, laying his hand on Rosa’s as if in terror of calling Miss Twinkleton “my dear” by accident, “to the other young ladies.”

Miss Twinkleton, satisfied, resumed her writing.

Mr. Grewgious, as if conscious he had not launched his opening neatly, smoothed his head from back to front in his habitual manner—as if he had dived and were pressing water out—and drew a pocket-book from his coat and a stump of black-lead pencil from his waistcoat.

“I made,” he said, turning leaves, “a guiding memorandum or so—as I usually do, for I have no conversational powers whatever—to which I will, with your permission, my dear, refer. ‘Well and happy.’ Truly. You are well and happy, my dear? You look so.”

“Yes indeed, sir,” Rosa answered.

“For which,” he said, bending his head slightly toward the corner window, “our warmest acknowledgments are due, and I am sure are rendered, to the maternal kindness and constant care and consideration of the lady whom I have now the honour to see before me.”

The compliment fell with a thud, like a dumpling that refuses to rise. Miss Twinkleton, feeling that courtesy required her to be beyond the conversation by now, bit the end of her pen and looked upward, as if awaiting inspiration from some obliging Muse.

Mr. Grewgious smoothed his head again, consulted his notes, and drew a line through “well and happy.”

“‘Pounds, shillings, and pence,’ is my next note. A dry subject for a young lady, but an important subject too. Life is pounds, shillings, and pence. Death is—”

He stopped abruptly, as if the word had opened the drawer where Rosa’s mother lay drowned and her father lay broken; and then, in a softer tone, with the negative seeming to come as an afterthought:

“Death is not pounds, shillings, and pence.”

His voice was as hard and dry as himself—one could imagine it ground into snuff—yet, through those scant means, some kindness made itself felt, like warmth through a thick glove.

“‘Pounds, shillings, and pence.’ You find your allowance always sufficient for your wants, my dear?”

Rosa, wanting for nothing, said it was ample.

“And you are not in debt?”

Rosa laughed. Debt, to her, was a kind of bogey for older people, like gout. Mr. Grewgious stretched his near sight to be sure her laughter meant what he thought it meant, and then said, with a furtive glance toward Miss Twinkleton as he lined out the item:

“Ah! I spoke of having got among the angels! So I did!”

Rosa felt, long before he found the next memorandum, what it would be. She began to blush and, with one embarrassed hand, folded and refolded a crease in her dress.

“‘Marriage.’ Hem!” Mr. Grewgious drew his smoothing hand down over eyes and nose and even chin, then shifted his chair a little nearer and lowered his voice. “I now touch, my dear, upon the point that is the direct cause of my troubling you with the present visit. Otherwise, being a particularly Angular man, I should not have intruded here. I am the last man to intrude into a sphere for which I am so entirely unfitted. I feel, on these premises, as if I was a bear—with the cramp—in a youthful Cotillon.”

His ungainliness gave his simile such likeness that Rosa broke into hearty laughter.

“It strikes you in the same light,” said Mr. Grewgious, perfectly calm. “Just so. To return to my memorandum. Mr. Edwin has been to and fro here, as was arranged. You have mentioned that, in your quarterly letters to me. And you like him, and he likes you.”

“I like him very much, sir,” Rosa said, with a shy emphasis on the verb that meant more than the words.

“So I said, my dear,” returned Mr. Grewgious, whose ear could not catch so fine a distinction. “Good. And you correspond.”

“We write to one another,” Rosa pouted, remembering how their letters sometimes jarred—how her words and his words never quite tasted the same on the tongue.

“Such is the meaning that I attach to the word ‘correspond’ in this application, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Good. All goes well, time works on, and at this next Christmas-time it will become necessary, as a matter of form, to give the exemplary lady in the corner window—business notice of your departure in the ensuing half-year. Your relations with her are far more than business relations, no doubt; but a residue of business remains in them, and business is business ever. I am a particularly Angular man,” he proceeded, as if it had suddenly occurred to him, “and I am not used to give anything away. If, for these two reasons, some competent Proxy would give you away, I should take it very kindly.”

Rosa, eyes on the ground, intimated that a substitute might surely be found if required.

“Surely, surely,” said Mr. Grewgious. “For instance, the gentleman who teaches Dancing here—he would know how to do it with graceful propriety. He would advance and retire in a manner satisfactory to the feelings of the officiating clergyman, and of yourself, and the bridegroom, and all parties concerned. I am—I am a particularly Angular man,” he repeated, as if forcing the confession out at last, “and should only blunder.”

Rosa sat still. The ceremony he spoke of—advancing, retiring, being “given away”—felt to her less like a day on the calendar than like a noise heard from far off, a bell not yet within earshot but already feared.

“Memorandum, ‘Will.’ Now, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, consulting his notes, drawing a line through “Marriage,” and taking a paper from his pocket, “although I have before possessed you with the contents of your father’s will, I think it right at this time to leave a certified copy of it in your hands. And although Mr. Edwin is also aware of its contents, I think it right at this time likewise to place a certified copy of it in Mr. Jasper’s hand—”

“Not in his own!” Rosa broke in, looking up quickly. “Cannot the copy go to Eddy himself?”

“Why yes, my dear, if you particularly wish it; but I spoke of Mr. Jasper as being his trustee.”

“I do particularly wish it, if you please,” said Rosa, hurried and earnest. “I don’t like Mr. Jasper to come between us, in any way.”

“It is natural, I suppose,” said Mr. Grewgious, “that your young husband should be all in all. Yes. You observe that I say, I suppose. The fact is, I am a particularly Unnatural man, and I don’t know from my own knowledge.”

Rosa stared at him a little, unsure whether he mocked her or himself.

“I mean,” he explained, without any change of tone, “that young ways were never my ways. I was the only offspring of parents far advanced in life, and I half believe I was born advanced in life myself. No personality is intended towards the name you will so soon change, when I remark that while the general growth of people seem to have come into existence, buds, I seem to have come into existence a chip. I was a chip—and a very dry one—when I first became aware of myself. Respecting the other certified copy, your wish shall be complied with.

“Respecting your inheritance, I think you know all. It is an annuity of two hundred and fifty pounds. The savings upon that annuity, and some other items to your credit, all duly carried to account, with vouchers, will place you in possession of a lump sum of money rather exceeding seventeen hundred pounds. I am empowered to advance the cost of your preparations for your marriage out of that fund. All is told.”

Rosa took the paper with a prettily knitted brow, but did not open it. The language of law lay on pages like food gone cold: nourishing, perhaps, but hard to swallow. She held it, instead, as if the paper itself could answer plain questions.

“Will you please tell me,” she said, “whether I am right in what I am going to say? I can understand what you tell me so very much better than what I read in law-writings. My poor papa and Eddy’s father made their agreement together, as very dear and firm and fast friends, in order that we, too, might be very dear and firm and fast friends after them?”

“Just so.”

“For the lasting good of both of us, and the lasting happiness of both of us?”

“Just so.”

“That we might be to one another even much more than they had been to one another?”

“Just so.”

“It was not bound upon Eddy, and it was not bound upon me, by any forfeit, in case—”

“Don’t be agitated, my dear.” His dry voice softened, not in sound but in intention. “In the case that it brings tears into your affectionate eyes even to picture to yourself—in the case of your not marrying one another—no, no forfeiture on either side. You would then have been my ward until you were of age. No worse would have befallen you. Bad enough perhaps!”

“And Eddy?”

“He would have come into his partnership derived from his father, and into its arrears to his credit (if any), on attaining his majority, just as now.”

Rosa, perplexed, bit the corner of her attested copy without knowing she did it. Her head tilted as if she listened to something under the floorboards. She looked down and, with her foot, smoothed the carpet as she smoothed her thoughts—back and forth, back and forth, as if to press a wrinkle out of her own mind.

“In short,” said Mr. Grewgious, “this betrothal is a wish, a sentiment, a friendly project, tenderly expressed on both sides. That it was strongly felt, and that there was a lively hope that it would prosper, there can be no doubt. When you were both children, you began to be accustomed to it, and it has prospered. But circumstances alter cases; and I made this visit to-day, partly, indeed principally, to discharge myself of the duty of telling you, my dear, that two young people can only be betrothed in marriage—except as a matter of convenience, and therefore mockery and misery—of their own free will, their own attachment, and their own assurance (it may or it may not prove a mistaken one, but we must take our chance of that) that they are suited to each other, and will make each other happy.

“Is it to be supposed, for example, that if either of your fathers were living now, and had any mistrust on that subject, his mind would not be changed by the change of circumstances involved in the change of your years? Untenable, unreasonable, inconclusive, and preposterous!”

He delivered even this—meant for tenderness and freedom—as if reciting a lesson, face and manner offering no help beyond the words themselves. Yet the words, once spoken, hung in the room like a window unlatched.

“I have now, my dear,” he added, drawing his pencil line through “Will,” “discharged myself of what is doubtless a formal duty in this case, but still a duty in such a case. Memorandum, ‘Wishes.’ My dear, is there any wish of yours that I can further?”

Rosa shook her head, but with a slight, almost pleading hesitation, as if there were indeed a wish and she could not find the language for it.

“Is there any instruction that I can take from you with reference to your affairs?”

“I—I should like to settle them with Eddy first, if you please,” she said, plaiting and unplaiting the crease in her dress.

“Surely, surely,” returned Mr. Grewgious. “You two should be of one mind in all things. Is the young gentleman expected shortly?”

“He has gone away only this morning. He will be back at Christmas.”

“Nothing could happen better. You will, on his return at Christmas, arrange all matters of detail with him; you will then communicate with me; and I will discharge myself—as a mere business acquaintance—of my business responsibilities towards the accomplished lady in the corner window. They will accrue at that season.” He blurred his pencil over the page again. “Memorandum, ‘Leave.’ Yes. I will now, my dear, take my leave.”

He jerked himself out of his chair in his awkward way, as if his joints did not entirely trust one another; and Rosa rose too, suddenly conscious of how near Christmas sounded when it was spoken aloud.

“Could I,” she asked—her voice small but urgent—“could I ask you, most kindly, to come to me at Christmas, if I had anything particular to say to you?”
