I veered sharply into a shadowed alley, seeking refuge beneath a crumbling stone arch. The moonlight spilled through the gap, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. Before I could settle, a figure glided from the darkness behind me—a woman, trembling violently, her eyes wide with terror as she peered out into the light. Moments later, a massive leopard, its white fur marbled with dark, jagged spots, surged across the archway like a living shadow. The woman pressed herself against my back, her breath hitching in a ragged gasp. My heart swelled with a sudden, fierce pity. I wrapped my arm around her shivering form.

"If that beast comes closer," I promised, my voice steady despite the chill, "I will hold it off. You must run."

"Thank you!" she whispered, her voice barely audible over the wind.

"Have you seen this creature before?" I asked gently.

"Many times," she replied, her teeth chattering. "She is the Princess's pet. If you lived here, you would know her name!"

"I am a stranger," I admitted. "But is she truly allowed to roam free?"

"She is caged, her jaws bound, her paws encased in crocodile leather gloves. Yet the chains are not enough. She escapes often, drinking the blood of any child foolish enough to cross her path. Fortunately, mothers are a rare commodity in Bulika."

Her composure shattered then, and she burst into sobs. "I wish I were home! The Princess returned only last night, and the leopardess is already out! How am I to enter my own house? She is hunting me, I know! She will be waiting at my door, watching... But I am a fool to speak to a stranger!"

"All strangers are not evil," I said firmly. "The beast cannot touch you until I have dealt with her, and by then, you will be inside. At least you have a roof over your head! This wind is terrible."

"Take me home safely, and I will offer you shelter from this storm," she pleaded. "But we must wait a moment."

As we waited, I questioned her about her people. She told me they spent their days digging for precious stones in their cellars, yet possessed everything else made for them in distant lands.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because labor is a disgrace," she answered, her eyes filling with shame. "Everyone in Bulika knows that!"

"How can they be rich if no one earns a coin?"

"Their ancestors saved for them," she explained. "They never spend. When funds are needed, they sell a few gems."

"But surely there must be the poor among you?"

"I suppose there must be," she conceded, though with a shrug of indifference. "But we never think of them. When someone falls into poverty, we simply forget them. That is how we maintain our wealth. We intend to remain rich forever."

"And when the mines are empty and the gems sold?" I pressed. "Then what?"

"We have so many, and so many more buried deep, that such a day will never come," she insisted.

"What if a foreign horde descended upon you, seizing all you possess?"

"No foreigner dares approach," she declared proudly. "They are terrified of our Princess. She is the shield that keeps us safe, free, and wealthy!"

Throughout her speech, she would pause, glancing nervously over her shoulder. When I asked why her people harbored such a visceral hatred of outsiders, she answered that the mere presence of a stranger defiled the city.

"How so?" I queried.

"Because we are more ancient and noble than any other nation," she said, her voice rising with conviction. "Therefore, we always expel strangers before nightfall."

"So how can you bring me into your home?" I asked, confused.

"I will make an exception for you," she replied quickly.

"Is there no sanctuary in the city for those without a home?"

"Such a place would be torn down, and its owner burned," she said coldly. "How can purity be preserved unless we keep the lowly at a proper distance? Dignity is a fragile thing!"

She spoke of a Princess who had reigned for millennia, wielding dominion over air, water, earth, and perhaps even fire, answerable to no one. Finally, willing to risk the journey, we navigated the winding, narrow passages of the city. We reached her door without encountering a single living soul. It stood in a broader street, flanked by towering houses, at the top of a steep, narrow staircase. She climbed slowly, and I followed.

Just as we neared the summit, she seized my arm, her fear suddenly manifest. She darted up the remaining steps with frantic speed. I arrived just in time to see the door slam shut in my face, leaving me standing confounded on the landing. There was merely enough space between the opposing doors of the two houses for a man to lie down. Weary and unwilling to let my presence further defile the sacred city of Bulika, I accepted the meager shelter, resting my head against the cold stone.
