His wife’s dark blue brougham—so new that the varnish still had that faint, resinous bite when the door shut—was waiting for Archer at the ferry. It carried him with a kind of cushioned propriety to the Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City, where the afternoon had turned sombre and snow-laden, and the station’s iron ribs threw back every sound: bootheels worrying the planks, porters calling out, wheels grinding, the hiss of steam like a creature taking breath.

Gas-lamps were already lit, their flames fretting in glass globes and casting a buttery light on the crowd. He paced the platform with the impatience of a man who has measured desire in minutes. In the big reverberating space he remembered, with sudden irritation, that there were people who believed the day would come when a tunnel would be bored under the Hudson and the trains would slide straight into New York—as if the river were only an inconvenience to be drilled through, not a widening of the world, a pause, a crossing.

They were the same sort who predicted ships that would eat up the Atlantic in five days, machines that would fly, light conjured from wires, voices carried without any wire at all—Arabian Nights made mechanical. Archer thought, almost childishly: let all the other marvels arrive if they must, but let the tunnel wait. He wanted the interval, the old ritual of separation and approach—the long minute when the carriage would stand quiet on the ferry, and the city would drift away as if it were the world itself rolling to the other side of the sun.

In his mind he kept setting the scene like a table: Madame Olenska stepping down, the sight of her from far off among meaningless faces, her hand finding his arm as naturally as a spoon finds the mouth; then the slow crawl toward the wharf through slipping horses and carts heavy with coal, with the hoarse cries of teamsters and the clatter of iron rims on frozen ruts; and then, astonishingly, the ferry’s hush, where snow muffled everything and the water’s low slap against the timbers sounded like breath in sleep. They would sit side by side in the motionless brougham under the falling flakes, and he would tell her—everything. The words arranged themselves with absurd elegance on his tongue, as if he had been rehearsing for months.

The train came in with a clanging groan, staggering to rest like a monster dragging its weight home. Archer pressed forward, shouldering through damp wool and fur, staring into window after window. Then, suddenly, she was there—so close that her face filled the glass: pale, surprised, alive. And with the shock of seeing her, he had again that mortified sensation of having forgotten what she looked like, as though memory were always too coarse a language for her features.

They reached each other; their hands met; he drew her arm through his.

“This way—I have the carriage,” he said.

After that it happened almost as he had imagined. He helped her into the brougham with her bags. He remembered, later, in a blur, that he had said the proper soothing things about her grandmother and had given her the briefest summary of the Beaufort situation. He saw, in her immediate softness, how easily pity rose in her—“Poor Regina!” she murmured, as if the phrase had a taste she could not spit out.

Meanwhile the brougham worked itself out of the snarl of cabs and wagons, and they crawled down the slippery incline to the wharf. Coal-carts swayed past like dark cliffs; horses’ flanks steamed; an express-wagon jolted by, dishevelled with rope ends; and then—an empty hearse, black as a shut mouth. At the sight of it she shut her eyes and clutched Archer’s hand, the leather of her glove stiff and cold.

“If only it doesn’t mean—poor Granny!”

“Oh, no, no—she’s much better—she’s all right, really. There—we’ve passed it!” he exclaimed, as though the mere act of passing could change an omen’s meaning.

Her hand stayed in his. As the carriage lurched onto the gang-plank and the ferry took them, he bent over, worked at the tight brown glove as carefully as if he were untying some small, sacred parcel, and kissed her palm with the reverence of a man kissing a relic. She drew her hand back with a faint smile.

“You didn’t expect me today?” he asked.

“Oh, no.”

“I meant to go to Washington to see you. I’d made all my arrangements—I very nearly crossed you in the train.”

“Oh—” she breathed, as if frightened by the narrowness of their escape.

Then she said, turning her eyes on him with a candour that unsettled him: “Do you know—I hardly remembered you?”

“Hardly remembered me?”

“I mean—how shall I explain? I—it’s always so. Each time you happen to me all over again.”

Something in him answered with a force that was almost painful. “Oh, yes: I know! I know!”

“Does it—do I too: to you?” he insisted.

She nodded, looking out of the window. Outside, snow streaked the dusk; beyond the glass the world was rubbed into shadow, and the ferry’s motion made it seem as though the earth itself were sliding away.

“Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!” he said, on a breath.

She did not answer. He sat in silence, watching her profile blur against the whitening air. Four months: what had she done with them? Where had her days been laid away? How near they were, shoulder and sleeve almost touching, and how helplessly remote; and in that darkness of the carriage, where they could not fully see each other’s faces, the nearness and remoteness felt like the very shape of their fate.

“What a pretty carriage! Is it May’s?” she asked suddenly, turning from the window.

“Yes.”

“It was May who sent you to fetch me, then? How kind of her!”

The words struck him like the clean ring of a spoon against china—domestic, innocent, and sharp. He was silent too long; then something in him flared, ugly with jealousy and the wish to retaliate.

“Your husband’s secretary came to see me the day after we met in Boston.”

In the brief letter he had sent her he had not mentioned M. Rivière. He had meant to bury the incident as one buries a bone in the body: out of sight, still felt. But the reminder that they sat in his wife’s brougham pulled the bitter thing up.

As on other occasions when he had expected to startle her from composure, she showed no surprise at all. And at once he concluded—irrationally, violently—that there must be correspondence.

“M. Rivière went to see you?” she said.

“Yes: didn’t you know?”

“No,” she answered simply.

“And you’re not surprised?”

She hesitated. “Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you; that he’d met you in England, I think.”

“Ellen—I must ask you one thing.”

“Yes.”

“I wanted to ask it after I saw him, but I couldn’t put it in a letter.” His heart knocked against his ribs, thick and suffocating. “It was Rivière who helped you to get away—when you left your husband?”

“Yes: I owe him a great debt,” she answered, in the same quiet voice, without a tremor.

Her naturalness, almost an indifferent clarity, calmed him in spite of himself. Once again her sheer simplicity made him feel absurdly conventional at the very moment he believed he was tearing convention down.

“I think you’re the most honest woman I ever met!” he burst out.

“Oh, no—but probably one of the least fussy,” she returned, and there was a smile in her voice, like warmth passing briefly over cold hands.

“Call it what you like: you look at things as they are.”

“Ah—I’ve had to. I’ve had to look at the Gorgon.”

“Well—it hasn’t blinded you! You’ve seen that she’s just an old bogey like all the others.”

“She doesn’t blind one,” Ellen said slowly; “but she dries up one’s tears.”

The answer checked the plea rising to his mouth. It came from somewhere in her experience he could not reach, as if she spoke in a dialect he could hear but not speak, a language of endurance whose meanings wouldn’t translate into the words he had learned at his mother’s table.

The ferry’s slow advance ceased; the bows bumped the piles with a jarring violence that made the brougham stagger, throwing them together. Archer, trembling, felt the pressure of her shoulder, and his arm went around her as naturally as hunger to bread.

“If you’re not blind, then, you must see that this can’t last.”

“What can’t?”

“Our being together—and not together.”

“No. You ought not to have come today,” she said, and her voice changed—strained, urgent.

Then she turned, flung her arms about him, and pressed her lips to his.

At that same moment the carriage began to move, and a gas-lamp at the head of the slip flashed its hard light through the window, bleaching their faces, exposing them to the world’s gaze even in that enclosed dark. She drew away at once. They sat silent and motionless while the brougham struggled through the congestion of carriages at the landing, the whole place loud again with wheels and shouted orders, the wet smack of the river replaced by the city’s incessant grinding.

As they gained the street Archer began to speak quickly, as if speed could outrun consequence.

“Don’t be afraid of me: you needn’t squeeze yourself back into your corner like that. A stolen kiss isn’t what I want.” He forced his hands to remain where they were, as if he were proving something. “Look: I’m not even trying to touch the sleeve of your jacket. Don’t suppose that I don’t understand your reasons for not wanting to let this feeling between us dwindle into an ordinary hole-and-corner love-affair. I couldn’t have spoken like this yesterday, because when we’ve been apart, and I’m looking forward to seeing you, every thought is burnt up in a great flame. But then you come; and you’re so much more than I remembered, and what I want of you is so much more than an hour or two every now and then, with wastes of thirsty waiting between, that I can sit perfectly still beside you, like this, with that other vision in my mind, just quietly trusting to it to come true.”

For a moment she did not answer. Then she asked, barely above a whisper: “What do you mean by trusting to it to come true?”

“Why—you know it will, don’t you?”

“Your vision of you and me together?” She gave a sudden, hard laugh. “You choose your place well to put it to me!”

“Do you mean because we’re in my wife’s brougham? Shall we get out and walk, then? I don’t suppose you mind a little snow?”

She laughed again, softer, but there was no mirth in it. “No; I shan’t get out and walk, because my business is to get to Granny’s as quickly as I can. And you’ll sit beside me, and we’ll look, not at visions, but at realities.”

“I don’t know what you mean by realities. The only reality to me is this.”

She let the words hang. The carriage rolled down an obscure side-street, then turned into the searching illumination of Fifth Avenue, where lamps and shopfronts made every flake glitter like ground salt. The city’s sound changed too: less wharf-chaos, more the controlled roar of money—carriages, sleigh-bells, the hard rhythm of hooves on packed snow, the occasional sharp crack like a whip.

“Is it your idea, then,” she asked at last, “that I should live with you as your mistress—since I can’t be your wife?”

The word startled him by its nakedness. Women of his world dodged it as they dodged certain smells in a kitchen—pretending they did not exist because acknowledging them would admit the body’s truth. But Ellen pronounced it as if it belonged to speech, as if she had had to learn it in a place where euphemisms were a luxury.

He floundered. “I want—I want somehow to get away with you into a world where words like that—categories like that—won’t exist. Where we shall be simply two human beings who love each other, who are the whole of life to each other; and nothing else on earth will matter.”

She drew a deep sigh that ended in another laugh. “Oh, my dear—where is that country? Have you ever been there?”

He had no answer ready; the silence between them thickened with the carriage’s muffled motion.

“I know so many who’ve tried to find it,” she went on; “and, believe me, they all got out by mistake at wayside stations: at places like Boulogne, or Pisa, or Monte Carlo—and it wasn’t at all different from the old world they’d left, but only rather smaller and dingier and more promiscuous.”

He had never heard her speak like this—so stripped of hope, so sharpened by knowledge. He remembered her phrase.

“Yes, the Gorgon has dried your tears,” he said.

“Well, she opened my eyes too; it’s a delusion to say that she blinds people. What she does is just the contrary—she fastens their eyelids open, so that they’re never again in the blessed darkness. Isn’t there a Chinese torture like that? There ought to be. Ah, believe me, it’s a miserable little country!”

They had crossed Forty-second Street. The brougham-horse—May’s sturdy animal—pulled them northward with the steady confidence of a creature trained to obey and not to question, as if he could outrun, by sheer muscle, whatever waited at the end.

Archer choked on the sense of wasted minutes, on words that seemed to die as soon as they were spoken, lost in the carriage’s small, upholstered darkness and the city’s relentless noise outside.

“Then what, exactly, is your plan for us?” he asked.

“For us? But there’s no us in that sense!” Her voice steadied, not with cruelty but with a kind of grim tenderness. “We’re near each other only if we stay far from each other. Then we can be ourselves. Otherwise we’re only Newland Archer, the husband of Ellen Olenska’s cousin, and Ellen Olenska, the cousin of Newland Archer’s wife, trying to be happy behind the backs of the people who trust them.”

“Ah, I’m beyond that,” he groaned.

“No, you’re not! You’ve never been beyond. And I have,” she said, and there was something in her tone like a door closing, “and I know what it looks like there.”

He sat silent, dazed with pain he could not put into the language of his life. He groped along the side of the carriage for the little bell that signalled orders to the coachman. He remembered May rang twice when she wished to stop. He pressed the bell, and the carriage drew up beside the curbstone.

“Why are we stopping? This is not Granny’s,” Ellen exclaimed.

“No: I shall get out here,” he stammered, opening the door and jumping down into the raw air.

By the light of a street-lamp he saw her startled face and the instinctive motion she made to detain him—her hand half-lifted, as if touch could pull him back from his own decision. He closed the door and leaned a moment into the window.

“You’re right: I ought not to have come today,” he said, lowering his voice so the coachman would not hear.

She bent forward, as though to speak, but he had already called out the order to drive on. The brougham rolled away, the wheels hissing over packed snow, carrying with it the faint smell of leather and varnish and the warmth of her presence, as if those too belonged to his wife’s household and could not be kept.

He stood on the corner. The snow had stopped, and a tingling wind rose, clean and cruel, whipping his face. In that sudden, sharpened cold he felt something stiff on his lashes and understood, with a bewildered shame, that he had been crying and the wind had frozen the tears where they lay.

He thrust his hands deep into his pockets and walked fast down Fifth Avenue to his own house, through the city’s clattering, indifferent music.
