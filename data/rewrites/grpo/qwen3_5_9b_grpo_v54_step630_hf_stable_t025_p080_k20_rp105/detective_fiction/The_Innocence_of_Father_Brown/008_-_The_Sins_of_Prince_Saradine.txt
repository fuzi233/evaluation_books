The Sins of Prince Saradine

Detective Flambeau had taken his monthly leave from his Westminster office, but he did not seek the open sea. Instead, he commandeered a tiny sailing vessel, so diminutive it spent most of its existence as a rowboat. He navigated the narrow, winding rivers of the Eastern Counties, where the craft appeared less like a ship and more like a magical toy gliding over land through meadows and cornfields. The boat was designed for two souls and carried only the absolute essentials according to Flambeau's pragmatic philosophy: tins of salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for combat, a bottle of brandy for fainting spells, and a priest, presumably for the event of death. With this sparse luggage, he drifted down the Norfolk waterways, aiming eventually for the Broads, though he delighted in the immediate surroundings—the overhanging gardens, the mirrored villages, the quiet pools where he would linger to fish.

Like a true philosopher, Flambeau had no grand destination for his holiday, yet he possessed a valid excuse. He harbored a half-purpose, pursued with just enough seriousness to make its success a crown for the trip, but with just enough detachment that its failure would not ruin the experience. Years prior, during his reign as a king of thieves in Paris, he had received countless letters of approval, denunciation, or love. One, however, remained etched in his memory. It was a visiting card in an envelope bearing an English postmark. On the back, written in French with green ink, read: "If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history." The front of the card bore the engraving: "Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk."

At the time, Flambeau had paid little attention to the prince beyond verifying that he had once been a brilliant, fashionable figure in southern Italy. Rumors claimed the prince had eloped with a married woman of high rank—a scandalous affair in itself—but it lingered in public consciousness due to a subsequent tragedy: the alleged suicide of the insulted husband, who reportedly threw himself from a precipice in Sicily. The prince had since lived in Vienna and traveled restlessly across Europe. When Flambeau decided to settle in England, the idea struck him to pay a surprise visit to this eminent exile in the Norfolk Broads. He had no idea if the place existed, for it was likely small and forgotten, but fate intervened sooner than expected.

They moored their boat one night beneath a bank draped in high grasses and pollarded trees. Exhaustion from heavy rowing brought sleep quickly, and they awoke just before dawn. Strictly speaking, they woke before daylight; a large lemon moon was setting in the forest of tall grass above them, casting a vivid violet-blue light upon the sky. Both men shared a sudden childhood memory of the elfin, adventurous times when tall weeds closed over one like a wood. Standing against the low moon, the daisies seemed giant, the dandelions towering giants, reminiscent of a nursery wallpaper dado. The riverbed's depth made them feel submerged among the roots of shrubs and flowers, forcing them to gaze upward at the grass. "By Jove!" Flambeau exclaimed. "It's like being in fairyland."

Father Brown sat bolt upright in the boat and crossed himself. His abrupt movement drew a mild stare from his friend. "The people who wrote the medieval ballads," the priest answered, "knew more about fairies than you do. It isn't only nice things that happen in fairyland." "Oh, bosh!" Flambeau dismissed him. "Only nice things could happen under such an innocent moon. I am for pushing on now and seeing what truly comes. We may die and rot before we see such a moon or mood again." "All right," Father Brown conceded. "I never said it was always wrong to enter fairyland. I only said it was always dangerous."

They pushed slowly up the brightening river as the violet sky and pale gold moon faded into the colorless cosmos preceding dawn. When the first faint stripes of red, gold, and grey split the horizon, they were broken by the black silhouette of a town or village sitting on the river ahead. It was already an easy twilight when they passed under the hanging roofs and bridges of this riverside hamlet. The houses, with their long, low, stooping roofs, seemed to lean down to drink from the river like huge grey and red cattle. The broadening dawn turned to working daylight before they spotted any living creature on the wharves. Eventually, they saw a very placid, prosperous man in his shirt sleeves, with a face as round as the recently sunken moon and red whiskers radiating from its low arc, leaning on a post above the sluggish tide. Driven by an unanalyzable impulse, Flambeau rose to his full height in the swaying boat and shouted to the man, asking if he knew Reed Island or Reed House. The man's smile expanded slightly, and he simply pointed up the river toward the next bend. Flambeau went ahead without further speech.

The boat navigated many grassy corners and reedy, silent reaches before they swung around a particularly sharp angle into the silence of a pool or lake that instinctively arrested them. In the middle of this wider expanse of water, fringed on every side with rushes, lay a long, low islet. Upon it stood a long, low house or bungalow constructed of bamboo or some tough tropical cane. The upright rods forming the walls were pale yellow, while the sloping rods making the roof were a darker red or brown; the long house was a study in repetition and monotony. The early morning breeze rustled the reeds around the island and sang within the strange ribbed house like a giant pan-pipe.

"By George!" cried Flambeau. "Here is the place, after all! Here is Reed Island, if ever there was one. Here is Reed House, if it is anywhere. I believe that fat man with whiskers was a fairy." "Perhaps," remarked Father Brown impartially. "If he was, he was a bad fairy." Even as he spoke, the impetuous Flambeau ran his boat ashore in the rattling reeds, and they stood on the long, quaint islet beside the odd, silent house.

The house stood with its back to the river and the only landing stage; the main entrance faced the long island garden. The visitors approached it via a small path running nearly three sides of the house, close under the low eaves. Through three different windows on three different sides, they looked into the same long, well-lit room, panelled in light wood, filled with numerous looking-glasses, and laid out for an elegant lunch. When they reached the front door, flanked by two turquoise-blue flower pots, it was opened by a butler of the drearier type—long, lean, grey, and listless. He murmured that Prince Saradine was away but expected hourly, noting the house was kept ready for him and his guests. The exhibition of the card with its green ink scrawl awakened a flicker of life in the parchment face of the depressed retainer. With shaky courtesy, he suggested they remain. "His Highness may be here any minute," he said, "and would be distressed to have just missed any gentleman he had invited. We have orders always to keep a little cold lunch for him and his friends, and I am sure he would wish it to be offered."

Curiosity moved Flambeau to accept, and he followed the old man ceremoniously into the long, lightly panelled room. Nothing was notable except the unusual alternation of long, low windows with long, low oblongs of looking-glass, giving the place a singular air of lightness and unsubstantiality, as if one were lunching outdoors. A few quiet pictures hung in the corners: a large grey photograph of a very young man in uniform, and a red chalk sketch of two long-haired boys. When Flambeau asked if the soldierly figure was the prince, the butler answered shortly in the negative; it was the prince's younger brother, Captain Stephen Saradine. With that, the old man seemed to dry up, losing all taste for conversation.

After lunch trailed off with exquisite coffee and liqueurs, the guests were shown the garden, the library, and the housekeeper—a dark, handsome lady of no little majesty, resembling a plutonic Madonna. She and the butler were the only survivors of the prince's original foreign entourage; the other servants were new hires from Norfolk. The housekeeper went by the name Mrs. Anthony, though she spoke with a slight Italian accent, suggesting Anthony was a Norfolk version of a more Latin name. Mr. Paul, the butler, also had a faintly foreign air but was fluent in English, typical of the polished men-servants of cosmopolitan nobility.

Despite its beauty, the place possessed a curious, luminous sadness. Hours passed like days. The long, well-windowed rooms were full of daylight, yet it felt like dead daylight. Amidst the incidental noises of talk, clinking glasses, and passing feet, they could hear on all sides the melancholy noise of the river. "We have taken a wrong turning, and come to a wrong place," said Father Brown, looking out the window at the grey-green sedges and silver flood. "Never mind; one can sometimes do good by being the right person in the wrong place."

Though commonly silent, Father Brown was oddly sympathetic, and in those endless hours, he unconsciously sank deeper into the secrets of Reed House than his professional friend. He possessed the knack of friendly silence essential to gossip; saying scarcely a word, he likely obtained all that his new acquaintances would have told anyway. The butler was naturally uncommunicative, betraying a sullen, almost animal affection for his master, whom he claimed had been badly treated. The chief offender seemed to be the prince's brother, whose name alone caused the old man's lantern jaws to lengthen and parrot nose to pucker into a sneer. Captain Stephen was a ne'er-do-well who had drained his benevolent brother of hundreds of thousands, forcing him to flee fashionable life for this quiet retreat. That was all Paul would say, and he was obviously a partisan.

The Italian housekeeper was somewhat more communicative, appearing less content. Her tone regarding her master was faintly acid, though tinged with awe. Flambeau and Father Brown stood in the room of looking-glasses examining the red sketch of the two boys when the housekeeper swept in swiftly on a domestic errand. A peculiarity of this glittering, glass-paneled place was that anyone entering was reflected in four or five mirrors at once. Father Brown, without turning, stopped mid-sentence of family criticism. But Flambeau, his face close to the picture, loudly declared, "The brothers Saradine, I suppose. They both look innocent enough. It would be hard to say which is the good brother and which the bad." Realizing the lady's presence, he turned the conversation to trivialities and strolled into the garden. Father Brown, however, still gazed steadily at the red crayon sketch, and Mrs. Anthony still gazed steadily at him.

She had large, tragic brown eyes, and her olive face glowed darkly with a curious, painful wonder—as if doubting a stranger's identity or purpose. Whether the priest's coat and creed touched some southern memories of confession, or if she fancied he knew more than he did, she whispered to him as to a fellow plotter, "He is right enough in one way, your friend. He says it would be hard to pick out the good and bad brothers. Oh, it would be hard, it would be mighty hard, to pick out the good one." "I don't understand you," said Father Brown, beginning to move away.

The woman took a step nearer, with thunderous brows and a savage stoop like a bull lowering its horns. "There isn't a good one," she hissed. "There was badness enough in the captain taking all that money, but I don't think there was much goodness in the prince giving it. The captain's not the only one with something against him." A light dawned on the cleric's averted face, and his mouth formed silently the word "blackmail." Even as he did so, the woman turned an abrupt white face over her shoulder and almost fell. The door had opened soundlessly, and the pale Paul stood like a ghost in the doorway. By the weird trick of the reflecting walls, it seemed as if five Pauls had entered by five doors simultaneously.

"His Highness," he said, "has just arrived."

In the same flash, the figure of a man passed outside the first window, crossing the sunlit pane like a lighted stage. An instant later, he passed the second window, and the many mirrors repainted in successive frames the same eagle profile and marching figure. He was erect and alert, but his hair was white and his complexion an odd ivory yellow. He possessed that short, curved Roman nose usually paired with long, lean cheeks and chin, though partly masked by moustache and imperial. The moustache was much darker than the beard, creating a slightly theatrical effect. Dressed to the dashing part, he wore a white top hat, an orchid in his coat, a yellow waistcoat, and yellow gloves which he flapped and swung as he walked. When he reached the front door, the stiff Paul opened it, and the new arrival said cheerfully, "Well, you see I have come." The stiff Mr. Paul bowed and answered in his inaudible manner; for a few minutes, their conversation remained unheard. Then the butler said, "Everything is at your disposal," and the glove-flapping Prince Saradine came gaily into the room to greet them. They beheld once more that spectral scene—five princes entering a room with five doors.

The prince placed the white hat and yellow gloves on the table and offered his hand cordially. "Delighted to see you here, Mr. Flambeau," he said. "Knowing you very well by reputation, if that's not an indiscreet remark." "Not at all," answered Flambeau, laughing. "I am not sensitive. Very few reputations are gained by unsullied virtue."
