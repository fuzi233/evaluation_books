The next morning, a little before the hour they had chosen for us, the corridor outside our quarters filled with the metallic hush of men under orders. Zat Arras’ officers arrived in a tight file, their harness fittings clicking like insects against stone. They had oiled their swords; the sharp, green scent of the lubricant cut through the colder smell of the temple—polished ersite and old incense that had soaked the walls for centuries. It was a place built to make a man feel small, and even the air seemed trained to obey.

They marched us in pairs.

Down the broad passage they call the Aisle of Hope we went, with guards before and behind, and three solid ranks of Zodangan soldiers lining either side—helmets forward, eyes fixed, hands placed at identical distances along spear-shafts. The sound of our steps was swallowed by the hall’s great dome and returned thinly, as if the building itself rationed out every noise. Ten thousand people sat in that circular coliseum, packed from the lowest benches to the highest tiers; yet as we entered, the low hum of their talk died away until there was nothing but the faint rasp of leather and the soft ring of metal.

I had faced armies; I had faced beasts; but there is a particular cruelty in a silence made by many throats at once.

The platform at the center—the Throne of Righteousness—rose like an island. Around it, seated in a circle, were the thirty-one judges, nobles chosen “by lot,” as custom said when nobles were tried. I looked for a single familiar face, some old comrade of Helium, some man who remembered what I had done for their city.

I found none.

They were Zodangans, nearly to a man. Zodanga had not forgotten who broke her pride in the days of green hordes and red fire—who watched her broad avenues run with panic, who saw her towers blackened, her standards torn down. And with Zat Arras set above them in his golden chair, Helium’s justice tasted already of another man’s hand: bitter, foreign, and ground fine.

They seated us with our backs toward a smaller dais at the exact center of the larger platform, so that we faced both judges and crowd. A place where a man could feel the weight of every eye, the bite of every whispered word, even when no one dared whisper.

Zat Arras rose. His harness was splendid, his face handsome in the cold, precise way of those born to command. He called my name, and it struck the hall like a knife-tip on crystal.

“John Carter,” he cried, “take your place upon the Pedestal of Truth to be judged impartially according to your acts and here to know the reward you have earned thereby.”

Impartially. The word rang false in that Zodangan circle.

I stepped onto the Pedestal of Truth. It was smooth under my boots, worn by centuries of condemned men, and it felt as if it held a faint chill that belonged to every sentence ever spoken there.

Zat Arras turned, performing for the coliseum, and began to recite my “acts”—each phrase set in the ritual cadence the priests loved, the cadence that made men stop listening to meaning and begin listening to authority.

“Know you, O judges and people of Helium,” he proclaimed, “that John Carter, one time Prince of Helium, has returned by his own statement from the Valley Dor and even from the Temple of Issus itself. That, in the presence of many men of Helium he has blasphemed against the Sacred Iss, and against the Valley Dor, and the Lost Sea of Korus, and the Holy Therns themselves, and even against Issus, Goddess of Death, and of Life Eternal.

“And know you further by witness of thine own eyes that see him here now upon the Pedestal of Truth that he has indeed returned from these sacred precincts in the face of our ancient customs, and in violation of the sanctity of our ancient religion.

“He who be once dead may not live again. He who attempts it must be made dead for ever. Judges, your duty lies plain before you—here can be no testimony in contravention of truth. What reward shall be meted to John Carter in accordance with the acts he has committed?”

A judge sprang up as if pulled by a string.

“Death!” he shouted.

The word dropped into the hall and sat there, heavy as a stone.

Then, from the benches, a man rose and flung his hand high, the gesture sharp enough to cut through silence.

“Justice! Justice! Justice!”

Kantos Kan.

Before the Zodangan ranks could remember they were meant to be a wall, he was over them—moving with the clean, economical speed of a Helium naval man who has spent his life amid rigging and narrow decks. He landed on the platform, his eyes bright with anger that had been disciplined into precision.

“What manner of justice be this?” he demanded of Zat Arras. “The defendant has not been heard, nor has he had an opportunity to call others in his behalf. In the name of the people of Helium I demand fair and impartial treatment for the Prince of Helium.”

For a heartbeat the hall held its breath. Then the audience answered him like surf answering a wind.

“Justice! Justice! Justice!”

The sound changed the air. It was no longer a temple hush; it was a living thing, rough-throated, stubborn. Zat Arras’ nostrils flared. He dared not refuse the city when it spoke in one voice, yet his consent came like a snarl.

“Speak, then,” he said, turning to me, “but blaspheme not against the things that are sacred upon Barsoom.”

I did not look at the judges when I began. I looked past them, over them, to the faces packed in their tiers—faces of men and women, of elders with dry, weathered skin and youths whose mouths had not yet learned to hide their thoughts. Helium’s people had always been a city of ceremony, but also a city that could remember a taste, a scent, a song. I needed them to remember.

“Men of Helium,” I cried, “how can John Carter expect justice from the men of Zodanga? He cannot nor does he ask it. It is to the men of Helium that he states his case; nor does he appeal for mercy to any.

“It is not in his own cause that he speaks now—it is in thine. In the cause of your wives and daughters, and of wives and daughters yet unborn.”

The words were plain, but the pictures behind them were not. Dor was a place of wet stone and pale terror, and the memory of it came with smells that would not leave me: the sour green reek of plant-men, the animal musk of the great white apes, the faint perfumed rot of Thern chambers where false sanctity tried to sweeten cruelty.

“It is to save them,” I said, forcing each sentence into the light, “from the unthinkably atrocious indignities that I have seen heaped upon the fair women of Barsoom in the place men call the Temple of Issus. It is to save them from the sucking embrace of the plant men, from the fangs of the great white apes of Dor, from the cruel lust of the Holy Therns, from all that the cold, dead Iss carries them to from homes of love and life and happiness.”

A murmur rippled through the tiers—half disbelief, half something darker. I pressed on before it could turn into the easy dismissal of an old myth.

“Sits there no man here who does not know the history of John Carter. How he came among you from another world and rose from a prisoner among the green men, through torture and persecution, to a place high among the highest of Barsoom. Nor ever did you know John Carter to lie in his own behalf, or to say aught that might harm the people of Barsoom, or to speak lightly of the strange religion which he respected without understanding.”

I could feel Zat Arras’ stare like heat between my shoulder blades. I ignored it.

“There be no man here, or elsewhere upon Barsoom to-day who does not owe his life directly to a single act of mine, in which I sacrificed myself and the happiness of my Princess that you might live.”

The word Princess struck me. It always did. Dejah Thoris was not a title to me; she was color and warmth, the scent of her hair after bathing, the bright snap of her laughter in this very palace. Her absence made the air taste like metal.

“And so, men of Helium,” I continued, “I think that I have the right to demand that I be heard, that I be believed, and that you let me serve you and save you from the false hereafter of Dor and Issus as I saved you from the real death that other day.

“It is to you of Helium that I speak now. When I am done let the men of Zodanga have their will with me. Zat Arras has taken my sword from me, so the men of Zodanga no longer fear me. Will you listen?”

A great noble in the audience stood.

“Speak, John Carter, Prince of Helium,” he shouted.

The answer rolled through the hall until the dome seemed to shake with it. Even the Zodangan soldiers shifted uneasily, hearing how sound itself could become a weapon when a city agreed on a word.

So I spoke.

For two hours I told Helium what I had seen—told it with the plainness of a man describing a burned meal to a hungry household: what it looked like, what it smelled like, what it did to those who tried to swallow it. I spoke of the lies that had been handed down like heirlooms, polished bright through repetition until no one remembered the crude hands that made them. I spoke of how words could be used like chains—how “sacred” could become a gag, how “blasphemy” could become a knife, how a people could be trained to distrust their own senses because some priest had taught them a certain sound meant “truth.”

When I finished, the hall was not silent. It was full of small sounds—the scrape of a foot, the faint clink of jewelry, the quick breathing of men who had been forced to imagine their own daughters in the dark of Dor.

Zat Arras rose at once, not allowing the moment to ripen into rebellion.

“My nobles,” he said, turning to the judges, his voice lowered as if he spoke only to them, “you have heard John Carter’s plea; every opportunity has been given him to prove his innocence if he be not guilty; but instead he has but utilized the time in further blasphemy. What, gentlemen, is your verdict?”

One sprang up again, eager as a hound.

“Death to the blasphemer!” he cried.

In an instant all thirty-one were on their feet with upraised swords—the old Barsoomian sign of unanimous judgment. Metal flashed in a ring around me like a crown forged for execution.

The people heard that, if they heard nothing else. A sullen sound rose from the benches—a growl gathering depth.

Kantos Kan, who had not left my side, lifted his hand. His calm was more frightening than rage; it said he had already decided what kind of man he would be remembered as.

“You have heard the fate that the men of Zodanga would mete to Helium’s noblest hero,” he said evenly. “It may be the duty of the men of Helium to accept the verdict as final. Let each man act according to his own heart. Here is the answer of Kantos Kan, head of the navy of Helium, to Zat Arras and his judges.”

He unbuckled his scabbard and flung his sword at my feet.

It struck the stone and rang.

The sound was contagious. Men surged past the Zodangan lines—citizens, officers, nobles—and pressed onto the platform. Blades clattered down around me, a shower of steel, each one a choice made in public. A hundred swords lay at my feet like offerings on an altar.

Zat Arras’ face tightened with fury, but he was helpless; the moment had slipped from his hands into the hands of the crowd.

One by one, I lifted the swords, pressed each hilt briefly to my lips in the old salute, and buckled it back on its owner. It was not my place to take their weapons; it was my place to honor what they risked by laying them down.

“Come,” said Kantos Kan. “We will escort John Carter and his party to his own palace.”

They formed around us and began toward the stairway that led down to the Aisle of Hope.

“Stop!” Zat Arras shouted, his voice cracking sharp across stone. “Soldiers of Helium, let no prisoner leave the Throne of Righteousness.”

But the only organized body of Heliumetic troops inside the temple was Zodangan. He thought that would be enough.

He did not understand what had changed in the air.

As the Zodangan soldiers advanced, swords flashed in every quarter of the coliseum. Men poured into the aisles, not in ordered ranks but in the terrible, organic motion of a people deciding they would no longer be managed like livestock. Somewhere a voice cried out, high and wild:

“Tardos Mors is dead—a thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

That cry was a spark above a powder magazine.

I saw it instantly: one thrust, one scream, one fall, and Helium would split—brother against brother, father against son—until no palace feast or hearth-song could ever stitch it back together. The temple was already too loud, the sound too raw, the echo too eager. The city’s acoustic world—the very texture of peace—was being shredded into war.

I sprang back onto the Pedestal of Truth.

“Hold!” I shouted. My voice cut through the tumult, not because it was loudest, but because they were listening for a reason not to kill. “Let no man move till I am done. A single sword thrust here to-day may plunge Helium into a bitter and bloody war the results of which none can foresee. It will turn brother against brother and father against son. No man’s life is worth that sacrifice.

“Rather would I submit to the biased judgment of Zat Arras than be the cause of civil strife in Helium.”

The words tasted like ash, but I forced them down.

“Let us each give in a point to the other, and let this entire matter rest until Tardos Mors returns, or Mors Kajak, his son. If neither be back at the end of a year a second trial may be held—the thing has a precedent.”

Then I leaned toward Zat Arras and spoke low, so only he could hear what I offered him: a way out that looked like mercy.

“Unless you be a bigger fool than I take you to be, you will grasp the chance I am offering you ere it is too late. Once that multitude of swords below is drawn against your soldiery no man upon Barsoom—not even Tardos Mors himself—can avert the consequences. What say you? Speak quickly.”

He hesitated—a heartbeat where he weighed pride against survival—then lifted his voice to the angry sea beneath us.

“Stay your hands, men of Helium!” he shouted, his tone trembling with rage he could not spend. “The sentence of the court is passed, but the day of retribution has not been set. I, Zat Arras, Jed of Zodanga, appreciating the royal connections of the prisoner and his past services to Helium and Barsoom, grant a respite of one year, or until the return of Mors Kajak, or Tardos Mors to Helium. Disperse quietly to your houses. Go.”

No one moved.

They stood with that tense stillness that is louder than shouting, eyes fixed upon me as though I held the city’s next breath in my hand.

Zat Arras, scowling, murmured to an officer, “Clear the temple.”

I saw the danger at once: force would make martyrs; martyrs would make war.

I stepped to the edge of the platform and pointed toward the main entrance, bidding them go. Not ordering—asking. In Helium, the difference mattered.

As one man, they turned at my request and filed out, silent and grim, past the Zodangan soldiers who stood rigid and humiliated. The sound of their leaving—ten thousand sandals, ten thousand harness rings, ten thousand controlled exhalations—was like a storm pulling away into distance, promising it could return.

Kantos Kan and the hundred who had cast their swords still stood with us.

“Come,” he said. “We will escort you to your palace, my Prince. Come, Carthoris and Xodar. Come, Tars Tarkas.”

He gave Zat Arras a haughty sneer and strode down the throne steps into the Aisle of Hope. We followed—Carthoris beside me, Xodar, Tars Tarkas towering over all—and behind us the loyal hundred, a moving knot of Helium’s own will.

No hand rose to stop us. Only eyes followed—glowering, calculating.

Outside, in the avenues, the city pressed close. People opened a path as we passed, and again and again swords were flung down at my feet—not as threats now, but as pledges. The metal struck the paving with a sound like dropped utensils in a kitchen—abrupt, honest, impossible to ignore. Each clatter said, I remember. Each ring said, I choose.

At my palace on the outskirts of Helium, my old slaves ran to meet us. They fell to their knees, kissed my hands, their faces shining with a love so simple it made my throat ache. They did not ask where I had been. In their world, return was its own miracle, and a miracle needed no explanation.

“Ah, master,” cried one, his voice breaking, “if our divine Princess were but here this would be a day indeed.”

The words struck like a blow. Tears came hot, unbidden. I turned away so they would not see them, but Carthoris did not hide his grief; he wept openly as the slaves crowded him too, touching his hands, murmuring affection, speaking sorrow for the loss that sat in our midst like an empty place at the table.

It was then—standing amid familiar walls, with familiar voices around him—that Tars Tarkas learned for the first time that his daughter, Sola, had gone with Dejah Thoris upon the last long pilgrimage.

I had not told him. I could not. Kantos Kan had told me, and I had carried that knowledge like a stone in my mouth.

The great Thark showed no sign. His face remained the same hard-carved mask of green Martian stoicism. Yet I knew him; beneath that mask he held love and friendship in a way rare among his kind, as if some older, gentler blood remembered how to ache. His grief was there, silent, as deep as a buried spring.

We gathered for the feast of welcome in the great dining hall—over a hundred of us, not counting the members of my little court, for Dejah Thoris and I had maintained a household suited to our rank. The hall smelled as it always had when the palace prepared to honor guests: warmed fruits from the thin gardens under the domes, roasted viands whose spiced vapors rose and clung to tapestries, and the clean bite of fermented liquors decanted into long-necked crystal. The servants moved with practiced grace, their sandals whispering across the floor in a rhythm that once had always been answered by Dejah Thoris’ laughter.

The board was triangular, as red Martian custom dictated—three sides for the three of our family. Carthoris and I took our places at the centers of our sides.

Midway along the third side stood Dejah Thoris’ chair: high-backed, carved, empty.

They had draped it in her gorgeous wedding trappings and jewels, as though cloth and metal might persuade absence to become presence. Behind it stood a slave in readiness, just as in the old days when her mistress sat there and could lift a hand, tilt her head, speak a command in the soft dialect of her childhood that no outsider could ever perfectly imitate.

It was the way upon Barsoom. And so I endured it—the ritual that kept memory alive even while it tore at the heart.

At my right sat Kantos Kan. To the right of Dejah Thoris’ empty place sat Tars Tarkas in the huge chair before the raised section I had had built years ago for his massive bulk. The place of honor at a Martian board is always at the hostess’s right, and Dejah Thoris had always reserved it for the great Thark when he was in Helium. On Carthoris’ side Hor Vastus sat in the seat of honor.

There was little conversation. Even the clink of service seemed subdued, as if the palace itself listened for a voice that did not come. Food was set before us—beautifully prepared, fragrant, sustaining—yet each aroma carried a sting: what is a feast without the one who made it a home?

We spoke of Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak in broken phrases, fear lurking under every careful word. If Helium had truly lost her Jeddak, what would she become in the hands of men like Zat Arras? A city could forget a truth in a generation if the wrong tongues were given power to name things. A people could be made to swallow any story if it was served with ceremony often enough.

Then, above the quiet of our hall, a sound rose—distant shouting, the blended voices of many people. At first we could not tell if it was anger or rejoicing. The tumult drew nearer, swelling, filling the avenues outside like wind funneling toward a door.

A slave rushed in, panting, to cry that a great concourse was swarming through the palace gates.

Another burst in on his heels, laughing and shrieking by turns like a madman overcome.

“Dejah Thoris is found!” he screamed. “A messenger from Dejah Thoris!”

I waited to hear no more.

The great windows of the dining hall overlooked the avenue leading to the main gates, but they were across the room from me with the triangular table between. I did not go around. I cleared it.

With a single leap I sprang over board and diners and landed on the balcony beyond. Thirty feet below lay the scarlet sward of the lawn. Beyond, the crowd clustered around a great thoat, its bulk shifting, its harness creaking. A rider on its back urged it forward toward the palace.

I vaulted down to the lawn and ran—fast, reckless, hearing only the pounding of my own blood and the roaring of the crowd’s voices, as if all Helium had become a single throat.

As I drew near, the rider’s outline sharpened. Green skin. Familiar posture. The long, sure seat of one who had ridden with the hordes.

Sola.

She slid from the thoat and ran to me, and for an instant I saw in her face not triumph but grief.

“O my Prince! My Prince!” she cried.

Hope faltered inside me like a candle struck by wind.

“Where is the Princess of Helium?” I demanded.

Sola’s voice broke, and the words came out like something torn from her.

“She is gone for ever. Even now she may be a captive upon the lesser moon. The black pirates of Barsoom have stolen her.”
