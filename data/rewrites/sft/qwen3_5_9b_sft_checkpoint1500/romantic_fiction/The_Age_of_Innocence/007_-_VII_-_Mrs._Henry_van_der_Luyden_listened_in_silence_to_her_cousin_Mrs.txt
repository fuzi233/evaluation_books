Mrs. Henry van der Luyden sat in silence, absorbing the narrative of her cousin, Mrs. Archer. To prepare oneself in advance for the quiet demeanor of Mrs. van der Luyden—to accept that her nature and upbringing rendered her naturally non-committal yet deeply kind to those she truly cherished—was not a shield against the chilling atmosphere of the Madison Avenue drawing-room. Here, amidst the high white walls and pale brocaded armchairs laid bare for the occasion, a peculiar coldness hung heavy in the air, even as gauze curtains concealed the ormolu ornaments and framed Gainsborough's *Lady Angelica du Lac*.

Beside the portrait of her lovely ancestress, painted by Huntington in black velvet and Venetian point, stood the living embodiment of that legacy. Though twenty years had passed since the artist captured her, the lady seated beneath the gaze of her ancestor might have been her twin sister, save for the subtle aging of time. Mrs. van der Luyden still donned black velvet and Venetian point when receiving society, for she rarely ventured into it; her existence was a private court held within her own doors. Her fair hair, faded to a soft silver without turning stark grey, remained parted in those flat, overlapping points upon her forehead, framing a straight nose that gave her pale blue eyes a slightly pinched, severe expression. To Newland Archer, she seemed grotesquely preserved in the airless vacuum of an irreproachable life, a figure trapped in amber like a fly in ice, retaining a rosy vitality of death.

Like many in his lineage, Newland respected and admired Mrs. van der Luyden, yet he found her gentle, yielding sweetness far less accessible than the formidable grimness of his mother's elderly aunts—the fierce spinsters who declared "No" on principle before a question was even posed. Mrs. van der Luyden's posture suggested neither assent nor refusal; it inclined toward clemency until her thin lips wavered into the shadow of a smile, delivering the inevitable refrain: "I shall first have to talk this over with my husband."

She and Mr. van der Luyden were so indistinguishably alike that Newland often wondered how, after forty years of intimate union, two such merged identities could separate themselves sufficiently to deliberate on matters as contentious as their current dilemma. Yet, neither had ever reached a decision without preface by this mysterious conclave. Thus, Mrs. Archer and her son, having laid out their grievance regarding the affront to Mrs. Lovell Mingott, waited resignedly for the familiar phrase.

However, Mrs. van der Luyden, who rarely surprised anyone, now did. Reaching her long hand toward the bell-rope, she announced, "I think, I should like Henry to hear what you have told me."

A footman appeared, to whom she added with grave precision: "If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come." She spoke of the newspaper reading in the tone a Minister's wife might employ regarding a Cabinet meeting—not from arrogance, but because a lifetime of habits and the deferential attitudes of her circle had instilled in her the belief that Mr. van der Luyden's slightest gesture possessed an almost sacred weight.

Her promptness indicated she viewed the matter as pressing as Mrs. Archer, yet lest she appear to have prejudged the issue, she offered the sweetest of smiles: "Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline; and he will wish to congratulate Newland."

The double doors swung open solemnly, revealing Mr. Henry van der Luyden. Tall, spare, and clad in a frock coat, he possessed faded fair hair, a straight nose mirroring his wife's, and eyes of a mere pale grey holding the same look of frozen gentleness. He greeted Mrs. Archer with cousinly warmth and proffered to Newland low-voiced congratulations, echoing the sentiments of his wife, before seating himself in a brocade armchair with the unassuming dignity of a reigning sovereign.

"I had just finished reading the *Times*," he said, folding his long finger-tips together. "In town, my mornings are so thoroughly occupied that I find it more convenient to peruse the papers after luncheon."

"Ah, there is much to be said for that plan," replied Mrs. Archer responsively. "Indeed, I believe Uncle Egmont used to remark that he found the morning papers less agitating when read after dinner."

"Yes: my good father abhorred haste," Mr. van der Luyden observed in measured tones, surveying the vast, shrouded room with pleasant deliberation—a space that served as a complete mirror to his own soul for Newland Archer.

"But I hope you *did* finish your reading, Henry?" his wife interjected.

"Quite—quite," he reassured her.

"Then I should like Adeline to tell you—"

"Oh, it is really Newland's story," his mother smiled, and proceeded to recount once more the monstrous tale of the insult inflicted upon Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

"Of course," she concluded, "both Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott felt that, particularly given Newland's engagement, you and Henry *ought to know*."

"Ah—" Mr. van der Luyden drew a deep breath.

A silence fell, broken only by the ticking of the monumental ormolu clock on the white marble mantelpiece, which grew as loud as the boom of a minute gun. Archer gazed with awe at the two slender, faded figures sitting side by side in a viceregal rigidity, mouthpieces of some remote ancestral authority that fate compelled them to wield, despite their obvious desire to live in simple seclusion at Skuytercliff, tending invisible gardens and playing patience in the evenings.

Mr. van der Luyden broke the silence first. "Do you truly believe this stems from some... some intentional interference by Lawrence Lefferts?" he enquired, turning to Archer.

"I am certain of it, sir," Archer replied. "Larry has been pushing it harder than usual recently—if Cousin Louisa does not mind my mentioning it—he is involved in quite a stiff affair with the postmaster's wife in their village, or someone of that sort. Whenever poor Gertrude Lefferts begins to suspect anything, and he fears trouble, he stages a fuss of this magnitude to demonstrate how awfully moral he is, speaking loudly about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet acquaintances he deems unsuitable. He is simply using Madame Olenska as a lightning rod; I have witnessed him employ this tactic frequently before."

"The *Leffertses*!" exclaimed Mrs. van der Luyden.

"The *Leffertses*!" echoed Mrs. Archer. "What would Uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts pronouncing judgment on anyone's social standing? It reveals what Society has become."

"We shall hope it has not quite come to that," Mr. van der Luyden stated firmly.

"If only you and Louisa went out more!" sighed Mrs. Archer.

Instantly, she realized her error. The van der Luydens were morbidly sensitive to any critique of their secluded existence. As the arbiters of fashion and the Court of Last Appeal, they were acutely aware of their status and bowed to their fate. Yet, being shy and retiring souls with no natural inclination for public life, they dwelt as much as possible in the sylvan solitude of Skuytercliff, declining all invitations on the pretext of Mrs. van der Luyden's fragile health.

Newland Archer stepped to his mother's defense. "Everyone in New York knows what you and Cousin Louisa represent. That is precisely why Mrs. Mingott felt she ought not to permit this slight on Countess Olenska to pass without consulting you."

Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband, who returned the glance.

"It is the principle I dislike," Mr. van der Luyden declared. "As long as a member of a well-known family is supported by that family, it should be considered final."

"It seems so to me," said his wife, as if introducing a novel thought.

"I had no idea," Mr. van der Luyden continued, "that affairs had deteriorated to such a pass." He paused, studying his wife again. "It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already something of a relation—through Medora Manson's former husband. In any event, she will be when Newland marries." Turning toward the young man, he asked, "Have you read today's *Times*, Newland?"

"Why, yes, sir," Archer replied, noting that he usually consumed half a dozen papers with his morning coffee.

Husband and wife exchanged another look. Their pale eyes clung together in prolonged, serious consultation before a faint smile fluttered across Mrs. van der Luyden's face. She had evidently guessed and approved.

Mr. van der Luyden addressed Mrs. Archer. "If Louisa's health permitted her to dine out—I wish you would convey to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—that she and I would have been delighted to fill the places of the Lawrence Leffertses at her dinner." He paused, allowing the ironical sting of the words to settle. "As you know, this is impossible." Mrs. Archer offered a sympathetic assent.
