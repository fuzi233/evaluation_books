CHAPTER XXI. The Palmers returned to Cleveland the following day, leaving the families at Barton once more to their own company. But this quiet arrangement did not last long. Elinor had scarcely finished reflecting on her recent visitors—wondering how Charlotte could be so content without apparent cause, or why Mr. Palmer, though plainly capable and decently behaved, seemed so ill-matched to his wife—when Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, ever eager for social activity, introduced a new circle of acquaintances.

On a morning trip to Exeter, they had encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings proudly claimed as distant relatives. That was all the excuse Sir John needed: he promptly invited them to Barton Park as soon as their engagements in Exeter allowed. The invitation was accepted without hesitation, and Lady Middleton found herself thrown into mild distress upon hearing that she would soon host two strangers—women whose elegance and refinement she could not verify, despite her husband’s and mother’s assurances. Their being related only heightened her anxiety; and when Mrs. Jennings tried to comfort her by suggesting that cousins must endure one another’s foibles, it only made matters worse.

Yet, since the visit could not be avoided, Lady Middleton resigned herself with the grace of a well-bred woman, merely reminding her husband several times a day of his impulsive generosity.

The young ladies arrived—and to everyone’s relief, they were far from unrefined. Dressed neatly, courteous in manner, and clearly delighted by the grandeur of Barton Park, they quickly won over Lady Middleton. Their affection for children was especially pronounced, and before an hour had passed, her opinion of them had turned entirely favorable. She declared them “very agreeable girls indeed”—a compliment of such high praise in her vocabulary that it nearly amounted to rapture.

Sir John, buoyed by this approval, rushed off to the cottage to inform the Dashwood sisters of their arrival, declaring the Miss Steeles to be “the sweetest girls in the world.” But Elinor, who knew better than to trust such sweeping declarations, saw through the exaggeration. To her, the phrase “sweetest girls in the world” meant nothing—it could describe anyone, anywhere, under any circumstances.

Still, Sir John insisted the entire family come immediately to meet them. “Do come now,” he urged, “pray come—you must come—I declare you shall come! You can’t imagine how much you’ll like them. Lucy is monstrously pretty and so good-natured! The children are already clinging to her like old friends. And they’ve been dying to see you—they’ve heard you’re the most beautiful creatures in England, and I’ve told them it’s all true and then some.”

But Elinor and Marianne refused to go right away, offering only to call within a few days. Sir John left, baffled by their reluctance, and returned to boast again to the Miss Steeles about the charm of the Dashwoods—just as he had earlier boasted about them to the Dashwoods.

When the promised visit finally occurred, the sisters’ first impression of the eldest Miss Steele was unimpressive: she was nearly thirty, plain-faced, and lacked intelligence in her expression. But the younger sister, no more than twenty-three, struck them as genuinely attractive. Her features were delicate, her eyes sharp and lively, and her bearing quick and animated—though not elegant in the classical sense, she carried herself with a certain distinction.

Their manners were polished, and Elinor soon recognized a degree of tact in the way they flattered Lady Middleton. They lavished attention on her children, praising their beauty, indulging their whims, and adoring every little gesture. When not occupied with the children, they praised Lady Middleton’s latest dress, copied patterns from it, and expressed awe at her every movement.

Such sycophancy, however, met no resistance from Lady Middleton. A devoted mother, she was both demanding and gullible when it came to her children’s admiration. The Miss Steeles’ exaggerated devotion pleased her completely. She watched with pride as her youngest son pulled at one of the ladies’ ribbons, tugged at her hair, and stole her scissors—yet she saw only mutual affection. “John is in such spirits today!” she exclaimed as he threw her handkerchief out the window. “He’s full of monkey tricks!” Later, when William pinched Miss Steele’s finger, she added fondly, “How playful William is!”

Then came Annamaria, a three-year-old girl who had been unusually quiet. “Here is my sweet little Annamaria,” Lady Middleton said, cradling her. “She’s always so gentle and quiet—never was there such a quiet little thing!”

But as she leaned in to kiss her daughter, a pin from her headband scratched the child’s neck. The result was immediate and violent—screams erupted louder than any noise the room had known in weeks.

Lady Middleton was horrified. But the Miss Steeles reacted with even greater alarm. Together, they sprang into action: one knelt beside the child, bathing the wound with lavender water, while the other stuffed her mouth with sugar plums. Still, the child shrieked on, kicking at her brothers and refusing any comfort—until Lady Middleton remembered a similar incident last week, when apricot marmalade had calmed a bruised temple. This time, the remedy was eagerly proposed. At the mention of it, the child paused briefly—giving hope that it might work.

She was carried out in her mother’s arms, followed by her two brothers, who insisted on joining despite her pleas. Left alone, the four women sat in sudden silence.

“Poor little creatures!” said Miss Steele. “It might have been a very sad accident.”

“But I hardly know how,” Marianne replied, “unless it had happened under different circumstances. This is just the usual way of inflating fear where there’s nothing real to fear.”

“What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!” said Lucy Steele.

Marianne remained silent. She could not feign sentiment when none existed. The burden of polite falsehoods fell naturally to Elinor.

“She is pleasant enough,” Elinor said carefully, “though perhaps not quite as warm as Miss Lucy.”

“And Sir John too,” said the elder sister, “what a charming man he is!”

Elinor agreed simply: “He is very good-natured and friendly.”

“And what a delightful family they have!” Lucy added. “I never saw such fine children in my life. I quite doat upon them already. I’m always fond of children.”

“I should guess so,” Elinor replied with a smile, “from what I’ve seen this morning.”

Lucy continued, “I think children should be lively and spirited. I can’t stand those who are tame and quiet.”

Elinor answered gently, “While I’m here at Barton Park, I never find quiet children abhorrent.”

A brief silence followed. Then Miss Steele, eager to continue, asked abruptly, “And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? Were you sorry to leave Sussex?”

Elinor was taken aback by the directness. “Yes,” she said. “Norland was a beautiful place, wasn’t it?”

“We’ve heard Sir John speak of it highly,” Lucy said, seemingly embarrassed by her sister’s boldness.

“I think anyone who has seen it must admire it,” Elinor replied. “Though no one can value its beauty as we do.”

“Did you have many smart beaux at Norland?” Miss Steele pressed. “I suppose there aren’t as many here. For my part, I think they’re always a great addition.”

Lucy looked ashamed. “Why should you think there aren’t as many genteel young men in Devonshire as in Sussex?”

“Oh, dear,” said the elder sister, “I don’t mean to say there aren’t. There are plenty in Exeter. But how could I know what kind of beaux there were near Norland? I only feared you might find Barton dull.”

“Young ladies may not care about beaux,” Lucy said, changing the subject. “But I think they’re very agreeable—if they dress well and behave properly. Though I can’t bear them dirty and untidy. Like Mr. Rose in Exeter—he’s a proper beau, clerk to Mr. Simpson—but if you meet him in the morning, he’s not fit to be seen.”

“And your brother,” she added, turning to Elinor, “was he a beau before he married? He was so rich, after all.”

Elinor smiled. “I don’t fully understand what a ‘beau’ means. But if he ever was one, he still is—there’s not a trace of change in him.”

“Oh, dear,” said Anne, “one never thinks of married men as beaux—they have other duties.”

“Lord!” cried her sister. “You talk of nothing but beaux—Miss Dashwood will think you think of nothing else.”

She then shifted to admiring the house and furnishings.

This glimpse was enough. Elinor found the eldest Miss Steele vulgar and intrusive, and though the younger sister had beauty and wit, she lacked genuine elegance and sincerity. Elinor left without desire to know them further.

Not so the Miss Steeles. They arrived from Exeter armed with admiration for Sir John, his family, and all his relations. Now, they lavished praise on the Dashwood sisters—calling them the most beautiful, elegant, accomplished, and delightful girls they’d ever met—and expressed eagerness to grow closer.

And grow closer they would. With Sir John firmly on their side, opposition was futile. An inevitable intimacy began—one marked by daily visits, shared rooms, and forced conversation. To Sir John, mere presence equaled friendship. As long as they were together, he believed they were intimate. He also took pleasure in sharing private details about his guests, hoping to break down barriers.

Within two meetings, the elder Miss Steele congratulated Elinor on her sister’s success in capturing “a very smart beau” since arriving at Barton.

“It’ll be a fine thing to marry so young,” she said. “And I hear he’s quite a beau—prodigiously handsome. I hope you’ll have as much luck soon—perhaps you already have someone in the corner.”

Elinor suspected Sir John would not hesitate to reveal his suspicions about her feelings for Edward—especially since it had become his favorite joke. Since Edward’s visit, he had toasted her affections at every dinner, winking and nodding until the whole table noticed. The letter “F” had become a running gag, endlessly joked about until it was hailed as the wittiest letter in the alphabet.

Now, the Miss Steeles were privy to these jokes. The elder sister grew curious about the gentleman’s name—though Elinor expected no subtlety from her.

But Sir John did not keep it long. “His name is Ferrars,” he whispered loudly. “But don’t tell anyone—it’s a great secret.”

“Ferrars!” repeated Miss Steele. “Mr. Ferrars? Your sister-in-law’s brother, Miss Dashwood? A very agreeable young man, I know him well.”

“How can you say that, Anne?” Lucy interjected. “We’ve seen him once or twice at my uncle’s. It’s rather too much to pretend we know him well.”

Elinor listened closely. Who was this uncle? Where did he live? How had they met?

She wished the conversation would continue—but it didn’t. For the first time, she felt Mrs. Jennings strangely indifferent to gossip.

And yet, Miss Steele’s casual familiarity with Edward unsettled her. It sounded less like admiration and more like concealed disapproval. Was she aware of something troubling about him?

But no further mention was made. The name Ferrars faded from the conversation, leaving Elinor with unanswered questions—and growing unease.
