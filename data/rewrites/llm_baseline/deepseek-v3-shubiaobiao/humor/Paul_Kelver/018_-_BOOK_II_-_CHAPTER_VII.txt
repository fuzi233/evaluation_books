Deleglise’s Sunday suppers were legendary—a spectacle of culinary devotion and social mayhem. Clad in immaculate linen, he ruled his gleaming kitchen like a maestro, orchestrating sauces and soufflés while his guests, an eclectic mix of London’s artistic elite, loitered around the massive oak table, whispering like conspirators until the grand reveal. Then, as Deleglise emerged, flushed with triumph, bearing his latest masterpiece, the room would erupt—a cacophony of wit, laughter, and the occasional misplaced epiphany.

But this tale is not about Deleglise’s illustrious gatherings. No, this is about the far more chaotic, infinitely less dignified affairs hosted by Dan and myself in our second-floor lodgings in Queen Square—affairs where the only thing more unpredictable than the guests was the food.

Our preparations were rigorous. The cook (Dan) and the housemaid (me) would labor all morning under a system of mutual criticism.

*“Too much soda,”* I’d declare, poking at a suspiciously foaming cake.

*“You absolute philistine! It’s a soda cake,”* Dan would retort. *“It’s supposed to taste like a science experiment.”*

Dan would glower, but the cake would be demoted to doorstop status, and another culinary adventure would commence. His cooking was his pride, his Everest—and like many mountaineers, he often found himself buried under an avalanche of his own making. I once watched him finish an entire sponge cake that had collapsed into a sticky crater, insisting between gulps of brandy that *“it’s supposed to be moist in the middle.”*

Housekeeping was no less contentious.

*“Call these knives clean?”* Dan would demand, dragging a finger across one and producing a grimy streak worthy of a charcoal sketch.

*“Not if you go smearing them like a toddler,”* I’d snap. *“We’re out of knife powder.”*

Yet somehow, by some miracle, the rooms would be presentable by afternoon, and our guests would arrive—journalists, actors, and the occasional bewildered stranger who’d wandered in under false pretenses.

Dan had a knack for elevating the most unlikely individuals to social stardom. Take Mrs. Peedles, our landlady from Blackfriars, a woman whose fashion sense suggested a theatrical wardrobe exploded near her. Dan rebranded her as *“the delightfully eccentric Lady P.”* and insisted she was an aristocrat slumming it for amusement. Our guests, desperate to appear in the know, played along. *“My dear Lady Peedles!”* became the refrain of the season.

Then there was Jarman, whom Dan introduced as *“the South American millionaire.”* Jarman, to his credit, leaned into the role with gusto, spinning tales of diamond mines and yacht races while our actress friends fluttered around him like moths to a very dubious flame. I lived in terror that one of them would actually marry him, but Jarman, ever practical, eventually wed the daughter of a blacking factory and retired to a life of stout contentment.

Minikin, our *“eminent German scientist,”* was harder work. His habit of informing ladies they were *“silly”* and his glass eye (explained as the result of *“a daring experiment”*) required constant damage control. When a guest asked about the red ribbon across his chest, Minikin deadpanned, *“I got it for fourpence.”* We hastily clarified that he was *“a genius tragically soured by love.”* The ladies forgave him; the gentlemen muttered about lucky escapes.

The fishy-eyed comedian—now a rising star—was another regular.

*“I’m not a comic,”* he’d lament. *“I could play Hamlet if it weren’t for this face. But no, I’m doomed to be the man who makes butter fly.”*

*“You’re sinning your mercies,”* Dan told him. *“Laughter’s harder than tears. Paul here could write comedies, but he insists on gloomy Welsh princesses.”*

*“Doesn’t matter,”* the little man sighed. *“The gallery boy pays a shilling to laugh at me and thinks himself my superior.”*

The O’Kelly, when he visited, was a study in marital tension. His wife, a paragon of virtue, had banished tobacco from their home.

*“A saint,”* he’d groan. *“If I could be half the man she is—”*

*“You’d die of boredom,”* I suggested.

Yet he kept a pipe in our rooms, and after sufficient lubrication, his resolve would crumble. One evening, staring at a poster of the Signora—resplendent in tights—he sighed.

He crossed the road, bought an alarm clock loud enough to *“wake an Egyptian mummy,”* and vanished into the night, presumably to reform.

Hal, meanwhile, had returned, setting up as a doctor in the City.

*“I’d last a month in the West End,”* he admitted. *“I can’t lie to patients. Sooner or later, I tell them the truth: ‘You’re not ill, you’re just lazy. Get up and do something useful.’”* He grinned. *“The poor don’t mind honesty. The rich call it impertinence.”*

Once, he picked up a photograph of Barbara in her bridal gown, studied it, and set it down without a word.

Some quests end quietly.
