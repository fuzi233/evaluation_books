The mist clung to the sunlight like a heavy, wet woolen blanket, smothering the landscape in a gray haze that Matthew Arnold might have recognized as the breath of a dying industrial age. Twenty miles north of Milton, the carriage wheels rattled onto the narrow branch line leading to Heston. This coastal town possessed a stark, utilitarian character, distinct from the languid, decorative seaside resorts of southern England or the polished promenades of the continent. Everything here screamed of function; the local carts were armored in iron rather than draped in leather and wood, and the pedestrians, though ostensibly on holiday, moved with the frantic energy of workers. The colors were muted, stripped of frivolity, enduring in their dullness. Even the rural folk had abandoned the smock-frocks that Margaret remembered from the south, garments she deemed impractical impediments to movement in a mechanized world. Where southern shopkeepers might lounge idly in doorways to enjoy the breeze, the merchants of Heston kept their shops open, performing the trivial theater of commerce—re-rolling ribbons, arranging goods—as if mere existence required constant justification. These observations struck Margaret with a sharp clarity as she accompanied her mother to secure lodging.

Two nights spent in hotels had drained their finances beyond Mr. Hale's initial calculations, forcing them to accept the first clean, cheerful rooms available. For the first time in days, Margaret felt a profound sense of rest settle over her, a rest so complete it bordered on dreamlike. Outside, the distant sea lapped the sand with a rhythmic, measured pulse, while the cries of donkey boys drifted through the air. Scenes unfolded before her like fleeting paintings, vivid yet unexamined, as she allowed herself the luxury of not needing to fully comprehend them before they vanished. A walk to the beach revealed a sea-air soft and warm despite the late November chill, the vast misty horizon merging tenderly with the sky. A solitary white sail cut the water, turning silver in a pale beam of sun. In this suspended state of pensiveness, where the past was forbidden and the future ignored, she could have dreamed her life away.

Yet, the future demanded confrontation, regardless of its iron severity. An evening arrangement confirmed their departure for Milton-Northern the following day to seek housing. Mr. Hale had received correspondence from both Mr. Bell and Mr. Thornton, creating an urgent need to clarify his professional standing and prospects through direct interviews. Though Margaret harbored a visceral repugnance toward the concept of a manufacturing town and suspected her mother found relief in Heston's bracing air, duty compelled her to accompany her father.

As they approached Milton, a deep, lead-colored cloud loomed on the horizon, a dark sentinel against the pale, wintry gray. Closer to the town, the air carried a faint, acrid tang of smoke—not necessarily a strong odor, but a pervasive absence of the natural fragrance of grass and earth. They were hurled rapidly along long, straight, seemingly endless streets lined with uniformly constructed brick houses. Occasionally, a massive, multi-windowed factory rose like a predatory hen amidst her chicks, belching black, "unparliamentary" smoke that accounted for the ominous cloud Margaret had mistaken for rain. From the station to the hotel, traffic was gridlocked by heavy, laden lorries. While city drives with her aunt had exposed her to varied vehicular purposes, here every wagon and truck transported cotton, either in raw burlap sacks or woven bales of calico. The populace thronged the sidewalks, dressed in materials that suggested wealth but worn with a slovenly looseness that struck Margaret as fundamentally different from the threadbare, desperate smartness of the lower classes in London.

"New Street," Mr. Hale announced. "I believe this is the principal artery. Bell has often remarked on it; the opening of this lane into a major thoroughfare thirty years ago alone inflated his property's value. Mr. Thornton's mill must be nearby, as he rents from Bell, though I suspect his operations center around his warehouse."

"Where is our hotel, Papa?"

"Near the end of this street. Shall we eat before or after viewing the properties listed in the *Milton Times*?"

"Oh, let us finish our work first."

"Agreed. First, I shall check for any notes from Mr. Thornton, who promised updates on these houses. Then we depart. We'll keep the cab; it is safer than wandering and risking missing the afternoon train."

No letters awaited them. Their search commenced immediately. Thirty pounds annually was their ceiling, a sum that secured a spacious house with a pleasant garden in Hampshire, yet here, even basic accommodations comprising two sitting-rooms and four bedrooms proved elusive. They traversed their list, rejecting each option until despair settled between them.

"We must revert to the second choice, I suppose. The one in Crampton, isn't it? Three sitting-rooms. Do you recall our laughter at the disparity between the three rooms and the single bedroom? Yet, I have already orchestrated the layout. The downstairs front room becomes your study and our dining area—poor Papa!—while Mamma deserves the cheeriest space possible upstairs. That room with the atrocious blue-and-pink wallpaper and heavy cornice actually offers a fine view over the plain, bending down toward the river or canal below. I shall occupy the small bedroom behind, in the projection above the kitchen. You and Mamma share the room behind the drawing-room, and that closet in the roof will serve as your magnificent dressing room."

"But Dixon, and the maid we intend to hire?"

"Wait. I am overwhelmed by the sudden revelation of my managerial genius. Dixon gets the rear sitting-room; she complains so bitterly of the stairs in Heston. The maid takes the sloping attic above your and Mamma's quarters. Does this suffice?"

"It may. But the wallpaper. What taste! And the sheer overload of color and heavy cornices!"

"Leave it to me, Papa! Surely you can persuade the landlord to re-paper the drawing-room and your bedroom, since Mamma frequents them most. Your bookshelves will conceal much of the garish pattern in the dining room."

"Do you deem it the optimal solution? If so, I shall proceed immediately to Mr. Donkin, referenced in the advertisement. I will escort you back to the hotel for lunch and rest; by the time the meal is served, I shall return. I trust I can procure new papers."

Margaret shared his hope silently. She had never encountered a taste for ornamentation, however flawed, that rivaled the plain simplicity which, paradoxically, formed the true framework of elegance.

Her father guided her through the hotel entrance, depositing her at the foot of the staircase before departing for the landlord's office. Just as Margaret placed her hand on the doorframe of their sitting-room, a waiter hurried past.

"I beg your pardon, madam. The gentleman departed so swiftly I lacked the time to inform him. Mr. Thornton called moments after you left. Believing you would return within the hour based on his instructions, I informed him. He returned approximately five minutes ago and requested to wait for Mr. Hale. He is currently in your room, madam."

"Thank you. My father returns shortly; you may convey the message then."

Margaret pushed the door open, entering with a demeanor of straight, fearless dignity that was habitual to her. She felt no unease; her upbringing in high society had inoculated her against such awkwardness. A visitor had come regarding her father's affairs, and since he had proven obliging, she resolved to extend her fullest courtesy. Mr. Thornton, conversely, appeared significantly startled and discomposed. He expected a quiet, middle-aged clergyman's wife, not a young woman advancing with frank, commanding dignity. Her attire was austere: a close-fitting straw bonnet of superior quality trimmed with white ribbon, and a large Indian shawl draped in heavy, imperial folds. He failed to recognize her identity, captivated by her simple, unabashed gaze, which conveyed that his presence held no significance to her beautiful countenance, failing to provoke even a blush of surprise on her pale, ivory skin. He recalled hearing of Mr. Hale's daughter but had envisioned a child.

"Mr. Thornton, I presume!" Margaret said after a brief pause, allowing his hesitation to pass. "Please, sit. My father brought me to the threshold not a minute ago, but alas, he was unaware of your arrival and has departed on urgent business. He will return momentarily. I apologize for the inconvenience of your double visit."

Though accustomed to wielding authority, Mr. Thornton found himself instantly subdued by her assumed command. Having grown impatient at the waste of time on a market day prior to her appearance, he now calmly accepted her summons to a seat.

"Do you happen to know where Mr. Hale has gone? Perhaps I might locate him."

"He has proceeded to Mr. Donkin in Canute Street. He is the landlord of the residence my father intends to lease in Crampton."

Mr. Thornton knew the property intimately. He had viewed the advertisement and inspected the house at Mr. Bell's request, intending to assist Mr. Hale, driven partly by sympathy for a clergyman displaced under circumstances mirroring Mr. Hale's. Initially, he had considered the Crampton dwelling ideal. However, witnessing Margaret's superb bearing and movement, he suddenly felt a pang of shame for having previously deemed it suitable for the Hales, despite the vulgarities that had initially struck him.
