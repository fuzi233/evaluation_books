Evening came down the glen like a lid lowered on a pot: olive cloud and silver rain-smoke pressing the scents of wet peat and bruised pine into the throat. Father Brown, swaddled in a grey Scotch plaid that drank the mist, reached the last bend of the valley and saw Glengyle Castle stand blocking the hollow as neatly as a cork in a bottle. Its steep roofs and thin spires of sea-green slate rose in the old French-Scotch fashion—witch-hats against a strip of tarnished sunset—while the surrounding pines, heaved and bowed by wind, looked black enough to be a congregation of ravens.

There was something in that air beyond weather: a weight like old pride kept too long in a closed pantry. In Scotland, heredity had the double sourness of blood-boast and doom-belief; the noble house clung to its name as a miser clings to coins, and the Calvinist conscience clung to catastrophe as to a text. The Ogilvies of Glengyle had been terrible in the sixteenth century—valour and violence, cunning and crazed ambition—one of those families that had made their labyrinths of plot around Mary Stuart’s court. The countryside still repeated their motive and their profit in a rhyme as dry as oatcake:

As green sap to the simmer trees  
Is red gold to the Ogilvies.

If any era should have worn down eccentricity, it was the respectable Victorian age; yet the last Earl of Glengyle contrived the only scandal left: he vanished without leaving the place. Not abroad—if he was anywhere, he was in the castle. His name sat in the church register and in the fat red Peerage like meat in aspic, but no one saw him under the sun.

If anyone did, it was one man: Israel Gow, a gaunt red-haired labourer who was groom, gardener, cook, and—at the end—undertaker. So deaf that the practical took him for dumb and the sharper for half-witted, he moved about the estate with blank blue eyes and a chin like a shovel. Yet the regularity with which he went into the kitchen, and the fierce energy with which he dug potatoes as if they were treasure, gave the district the stubborn impression of meals being made for somebody hidden. When questioned, Gow would assert with maddening persistence that his master was “not at home,” as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world for an earl never to be at home in his own house.

Then one morning the provost and the minister—Glengyle had turned Presbyterian as if pride could outstare Puritanism—were summoned up. They found Gow had taken on one more trade, hammered nails, and shut the nobleman into a coffin. How much inquiry followed, and how little, was oddly foggy; the body—if it was the body—had lain some time in the small churchyard on the hill. Only now had the matter come under a proper legal glare, with Flambeau northward in advance and a Scotland Yard man with him.

Father Brown crossed the dim garden. Damp rose from the earth with the smell of rotted leaves and last summer’s herbs; rain had bruised the stalks and made the soil give underfoot like fresh dough. Against the green-gold stripe of sunset he saw a black silhouette: a man in a chimney-pot hat with a heavy spade over his shoulder. The hat made him look like a sexton; the spade made him look like what he was. The combination was absurd and yet wholly Scottish: respectability in “blacks” for official talk, and economy that would not waste daylight’s last edge for ceremony. The man started, stared suspiciously as the priest passed, and trudged on.

The great door opened, and Flambeau stood there, broad as ever, his black moustache dampened by the air. Beside him was a lean man with iron-grey hair and a sheaf of papers: Inspector Craven from Scotland Yard. The entrance hall was stripped, the emptiness smelling of cold stone and old smoke; above, pale faces in black periwigs glowered from darkening canvas—wicked Ogilvies, all sneer and shadow.

They led him into an inner room where a long oak table ran like a dark river. Their end was littered with scribbled papers, whisky, and cigars; along the rest, at intervals, lay odd scraps arranged as if for a village fair of nonsense. A glittering heap of broken glass; a high mound of brown dust; a plain stick of wood.

“You seem to have a sort of geological museum,” Father Brown remarked, nodding at the dust and the crystals.

“Not a geological museum,” Flambeau said. “A psychological one.”

“Oh, for heaven’s sake,” Craven broke in with a short laugh, “don’t let’s begin with long words.”

Flambeau’s eyes widened with friendly astonishment. “Don’t you know what psychology means? It means being off your chump.”

Still, the priest’s gaze had slid past their faces to the window, where the black silhouette with hat and spade crossed again, dim against the thickening sky. The wind pressed at the panes as if impatient to be let in.

“I can believe he was odd,” Father Brown said. “A man doesn’t bury himself alive—nor hurry to bury himself dead—without some twist in him. But why lunacy?”

Flambeau tapped the paper in Craven’s hand. “Listen to what he’s found loose in this house.”

Craven glanced at the window; the light was bleeding away. “We must get a candle. Storm’s coming, and it’s too dark to read.”

“Have you found any candles,” Father Brown asked mildly, “among your oddities?”

Flambeau’s face sobered. “Twenty-five candles. And not a trace of a candlestick.”

In the darkening room the wind found a gap and came through with the smell of rain and pine-resin, making the old boards complain and the curtains stir like restless cloth. Father Brown walked down the table, lifted a candle from the bundle, and bent inadvertently over the brown mound. The dust rose sharp and dry into his nose.

“Hullo,” he said, and sneezed—one clean crack in the hush. “Snuff.”

He struck a match, cupped it, and lit the candle carefully. The flame stretched long and unsteady under the draught, tasting the air like a tongue. He returned and set the candle in the neck of a whisky bottle; the wick wavered like a banner in a gale. Outside, the miles of pine woods seethed and sighed, a black sea around a rock.

Craven began reading from the inventory, his voice made grave by the weather.

“You are to understand the place is dismantled and neglected. But one or two rooms have been inhabited—simple, not squalid—by somebody. Not Gow. The list is as follows.

“First. A very considerable hoard of precious stones—nearly all diamonds—loose, without any setting. Family jewels, yes; but family jewels are almost always set in ornaments. These are like pocket change, carried as if they were copper coins.

“Second. Heaps and heaps of loose snuff. Not kept in a horn or pouch. Just lying in mounds on mantels, sideboards, even on the piano—anywhere. As if the old gentleman couldn’t be bothered with lids or pockets.

“Third. Here and there about the house, curious little heaps of minute bits of metal—some like steel springs, some microscopic wheels—as if they’d gutted a mechanical toy.

“Fourth. The wax candles. With nothing to stick them in but bottle necks. Now note how much queerer all this is than anything we expected. The central riddle—we can imagine it: a hidden earl, a servant who buries him, murder, substitution, any melodrama you like. But even then you haven’t explained a candle without a candlestick, or why an elderly gentleman spills snuff on a piano. By no stretch can you connect snuff and diamonds and wax and loose clockwork.”

“I can connect them,” Father Brown said.

Both men looked at him as if he had begun speaking Gaelic.

“This Glengyle,” the priest went on, “was mad against the French Revolution. He adored the ancien régime and tried to live like the last Bourbons. Snuff because it’s eighteenth-century luxury; wax candles because it’s eighteenth-century light; the metal bits because Louis XVI had a hobby of locksmithing; the diamonds because of Marie Antoinette’s necklace.”

Flambeau stared. “That’s an extraordinary notion. Do you really think it’s true?”

“I’m perfectly sure it isn’t,” Father Brown replied. “I only meant—when you said nobody could connect them—I could. The real truth lies deeper.”

He listened a moment. The wind threaded the turrets and made a voice in the stone.

“The late Earl of Glengyle,” he said, “was a thief. A desperate housebreaker. No candlesticks because he cut these candles short and used them in the little lantern he carried. The snuff—like pepper in the hands of French criminals—thrown in dense masses into a pursuer’s face. And the final proof is the coincidence of diamonds and those tiny steel wheels. Diamonds and small steel wheels are the two things you use to cut out a pane of glass.”

A pine bough, torn loose, lashed the window behind them with a heavy slap, like a burglar’s parody. None of them turned; their eyes held to the priest.

“Diamonds and small wheels,” Craven repeated slowly. “Is that all that makes you think it’s the true explanation?”

“I don’t think it’s true,” Father Brown said. “But again—you said nobody could connect the four. The true tale is probably humdrum. Glengyle found, or thought he found, stones on his estate. Somebody tricked him with those loose brilliants—said they were found in castle caverns. The little wheels are some rough diamond-cutting contrivance. He had to do it small and crude, with shepherds or hill fellows. Snuff is the great luxury of such men; it’s what you bribe them with. No candlesticks because they didn’t want them; they carried candles by hand to explore.”

Flambeau waited, as if hoping the priest had finished. “Is that all? Are we at the dull truth at last?”

“Oh, no,” Father Brown said.

The wind fell, far off, in a long hoot like mockery. Father Brown’s face remained blank, but his voice had tightened.

“I only offered those because ten false philosophies will fit the universe; ten false theories will fit Glengyle Castle. We want the real explanation. Are there no other exhibits?”

Craven laughed. Flambeau rose and walked down the table, his boots thudding softly on old boards.

“Items five, six, seven,” he said. “More varied than instructive. A curious collection—of lead, not pencils: the lead taken out of pencils. A senseless stick of bamboo with the top splintered—could be a weapon, only there’s no crime. And a few old missals and little Catholic pictures. The Ogilvies kept them, I suppose, from medieval times—family pride stronger than their Puritanism. We put them in the museum because they seem oddly cut about and defaced.”

Father Brown picked up the little illuminated pages. The candlelight, shaken by the draught, made the gold leaf flare and drown, flare and drown. Ink and vellum gave off that dry, animal smell of old books, like leather warmed at the hearth. He turned one picture, then another, and spoke before the darkness-shadows had finished crossing the room. It was the voice of a different man—quick, young, urgent.

“Mr. Craven,” he said, “you have a legal warrant to examine that grave. The sooner we do it the better. If I were you, I’d start now.”

“Now?” Craven echoed. “And why now?”

“Because this is serious,” Father Brown answered. “Not spilled snuff or loose pebbles, that can happen a hundred ways. There is only one reason I know for this kind of thing, and it goes down to the roots of the world. These pictures weren’t dirtied or torn in idle spite. They were treated carefully—queerly. Every place where the great ornamented Name of God appears has been elaborately cut out. And the only other thing removed is the halo round the Child Jesus. Therefore—get your warrant and your spade and your hatchet. We go up and break open that coffin.”

“What do you mean?” Craven demanded, the London neatness in him recoiling from the phrase.

“I mean,” said Father Brown, and the gale rose in answer as if the castle had inhaled, “that the great devil of the universe may be sitting on the top tower of this castle at this moment, big as a hundred elephants, roaring like the Apocalypse. There is black magic somewhere at the bottom of this.”

“Black magic,” Flambeau repeated under his breath; he was too modern to scoff and too experienced to smile. “But what can the snuff and the wax and the rest mean?”

“Oh, something damnable,” Father Brown snapped, suddenly impatient. “How should I know? Perhaps you can torture with snuff and bamboo. Perhaps lunatics lust after wax and steel filings. Perhaps there is a maddening drug in pencil lead. Our shortest cut is up the hill to the grave.”

They hardly knew they had moved until the garden wind nearly flung them on their faces. Yet they obeyed him like men walking in their sleep: Craven found a hatchet in his hand and the warrant in his pocket; Flambeau bore the heavy spade of the strange gardener; Father Brown carried the little gilded book from which the Name had been cut as delicately as a heart cut from pastry.

The path to the churchyard was crooked but short; under that wind it became a labour like climbing through water. As they mounted, the forest spread and spread—a grey-blue infinity of pines pressed all one way under the gusts, as if the whole land were bowing to an unseen power. The sound was the worst of it: a continuous, many-throated moan, each tree answering the next, a tide of needles hissing, trunks groaning, branches snapping like bones. It was an acoustic wilderness, a music without human measure—like an orchestra that has forgotten the song and continues only the roar.

Through that vast noise ran an older note, shrill and high, as if some ancient sorrow had found a flute in the storm. One could fancy the underworld of foliage was full of lost pagan gods crying because they could not remember their own names—driven from their altars, wandering in a language no one speaks, unable to translate their hunger into any prayer that heaven would recognize.

“You see,” Father Brown said quietly, as if talking at a kitchen table, “Scotch people before Scotland existed were a curious lot. In prehistoric times, I fancy they really worshipped demons.”

He added, almost genially, “That’s why they jumped at Puritan theology. When a man has lived on harsh fare a long while, he’ll take harsher as a comfort—if it is at least familiar.”

Flambeau swung round on him, sudden anger breaking through his dread. “What does that snuff mean?”

“There is one mark of all genuine religions,” Father Brown said with equal seriousness. “Materialism. Devil-worship is a perfectly genuine religion.”

They reached the bald scalp of the hill, one of the few clearings above the roaring pines. A mean enclosure of timber and wire rattled like cutlery in a tray, marking the graveyard border. At the foot of the grave tall thistles stood grey and silver in decay; their down broke and flew past, and Craven startled at it as if it were a dart.

Flambeau drove the spade into the wet clay. It made a sucking sound, like a spoon pulled from porridge. He leaned on it, huge shoulders rising and falling.

“Go on,” Father Brown said very gently. “We’re only trying to find the truth. What are you afraid of?”

“I’m afraid of finding it,” Flambeau said.

Craven forced his voice into a bright, crowing cheeriness. “I wonder why he hid himself like that. Something nasty. Was he a leper?”

“Something worse,” Flambeau answered.

“And what,” Craven asked, still too loudly, “could be worse than a leper?”

“I don’t imagine it,” Flambeau said.

He dug in silence for dreadful minutes. The storm shouldered aside its own clouds and let thin starlight fall—cold and pale—over the hill. At last the rude timber coffin showed in the torn earth, and Flambeau tipped it up upon the turf. Craven stepped forward with the axe. A thistle brushed him; he flinched, then set his jaw and hacked, wrenched, tore the lid away with an energy that was almost hatred.

In the faint starlight, what lay within glimmered grey and damp.

“Bones,” Craven said. Then, after bending close, “but it is a man”—as if that, at least, had been uncertain.

Flambeau’s voice went oddly up and down, like a tune trying to find its key. “Is he— is he all right?”

“Seems so,” Craven said hoarsely. He leaned further in, and his next words came out as if dragged. “Wait.”

A vast heave went through Flambeau. “And now I come to think of it,” he burst out, “why in the name of madness shouldn’t he be all right? What is it that gets hold of men on these cursed cold mountains? It’s the black, brainless repetition—forest and more forest—this sound, this endless sound, like the dream of an atheist: pine-trees and more pine-trees and millions more pine-trees—”

“God!” Craven cried suddenly, straightening as if struck. “He hasn’t got a head.”

For an instant they were rigid. Father Brown, who had spoken of black magic with an almost careless certitude, now showed, for the first time, a leap of startled concern.

“No head?” he repeated. “No head?”—as if he had almost expected any other horror rather than that one.

In a flash, senseless images chased one another through their minds: a headless baby born into the house; a headless youth hiding in empty rooms; a headless man pacing corridors and gardens. Yet none of those fantasies could root themselves; they were like words in a tongue no one understands. The storm shrieked overhead; the woods roared below; their thoughts, huge a moment ago, slipped away like something dropped into deep water.

“There are three headless men,” Father Brown said, “standing round this open grave.”

Craven’s mouth opened and stayed open, the way a yokel stares at a conjuring trick. He glanced down at the axe in his hands as if it belonged to someone else, and let it fall.

“Father,” Flambeau said in the thick, childlike voice he used very seldom, “what are we to do?”

“Sleep!” Father Brown cried. The word cracked like a command. “Sleep. We have come to the end of the ways. Do you know what sleep is? Every man who sleeps believes in God. It is a sacrament—an act of faith and a food. And we need a sacrament, if only a natural one. Something has fallen on us that falls very seldom on men—perhaps the worst thing.”

Craven managed to bring his lips together. “What do you mean?”

Father Brown had turned his face toward the castle; his eyes looked as if they were listening rather than seeing.

“We have found the truth,” he said. “And the truth makes no sense.”

He went down the path before them with a plunging, reckless step that was rare in him. Back in the castle he threw himself into sleep with the simple surrender of a dog.

Yet at daybreak Father Brown was up earlier than anyone except the silent gardener. The storm had ended in roaring rains, and the morning smelled washed and raw—wet stone, wet leaf, the clean iron tang of water on soil. Father Brown stood in the kitchen garden smoking a large pipe, watching Israel Gow at work. Gow’s spade went in and out with a steady rhythm, a kind of mute music against the birds and dripping hedges. The day’s acoustic world, rinsed by rain, felt briefly sane.

Gow seemed even to have been conversing with someone—muttering, perhaps, to himself—but at sight of the detectives he planted his spade sullenly and, saying something about his breakfast, shuffled along the cabbage lines and shut himself into the kitchen. Through the door came a faint domestic clatter—an ordinary sound that ought to have reassured them, and did not.

“He’s a valuable man,” Father Brown said. “He does the potatoes amazingly.” He drew on the pipe, and the tobacco-smoke mingled with the garden damp like a darker herb. “Still,” he added with impartial charity, “he has his faults; which of us hasn’t? He doesn’t dig this bank quite regularly.”

He stepped to a patch of turned earth and stamped on it with sudden decision. “There, for instance. I’m really very doubtful about that potato.”

“And why?” Craven asked, amused in spite of himself at the little man’s sudden agricultural interest.

“I’m doubtful about it,” Father Brown said, “because old Gow was doubtful about it himself. He put his spade in methodically everywhere but just here. There must be a mighty fine potato just here.”

Flambeau seized the spade with a sudden impatience, drove it in, and levered up the heavy, wet soil. What rolled out under the clod was not a potato. It was like a monstrous, over-domed mushroom at first glance—then it struck the spade with a cold click, turned, and grinned up at them with its toothless smile.

“The Earl of Glengyle,” Father Brown said sadly, looking down at the skull.
