Edward, radiant with joy after delivering his heartfelt thanks to Colonel Brandon, hastened to Lucy with news of their impending union. His elation was so profound that when Mrs. Jennings called upon her the following day, brimming with congratulations, Lucy could truthfully declare she had never seen him so exuberant in all her life. Her own spirits soared just as high—her happiness was complete—and she embraced Mrs. Jennings’s hopeful prediction that they would soon be settled together at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. Far from hesitating to acknowledge Elinor’s role in their good fortune, Lucy spoke of her friendship with both Edward and herself with deep gratitude. She freely admitted their debt to Miss Dashwood, and declared with conviction that no effort on Elinor’s part—past or future—would ever astonish her, for she believed Elinor capable of sacrificing everything for those she truly cherished. As for Colonel Brandon, Lucy not only held him in reverent admiration but also earnestly desired he be honored in every worldly way: his tithes should reach their full measure, and she quietly resolved to make ample use of his estate—his servants, his carriage, his cows, and even his poultry—once they were established at Delaford.

It had been over a week since John Dashwood had visited them in Berkeley Street, and since then, no further mention had been made of his wife’s illness beyond a single casual inquiry. This silence prompted Elinor to feel it was time she paid Mrs. Dashwood a visit—a duty that neither her inclinations nor her companions’ encouragement supported. Marianne refused outright to accompany her and went so far as to urge her sister not to go at all. Mrs. Jennings, though always ready to lend her carriage, detested Mrs. John Dashwood so deeply that even her curiosity about how the woman looked after the recent revelation, or her desire to affront her by showing support for Edward, could not overcome her aversion to being in her presence again. Thus, Elinor set out alone, burdened by reluctance, to face a woman whose company neither of her friends found tolerable.

Mrs. Dashwood was absent, but before the carriage could turn away, Mr. Dashwood unexpectedly emerged from the house. He greeted Elinor warmly, saying he had just been about to call on them himself. Assuring her that Fanny would be delighted to see her, he invited her inside. They ascended together to the drawing-room, which was empty.

“Fanny is likely in her own room,” he said. “I’ll fetch her shortly—she’ll have no objection to seeing you. Indeed, none at all. Especially now—though I won’t say more—but you and Marianne were always favorites. Why won’t Marianne come?”

Elinor offered a polite excuse.

“I’m glad to see you alone,” he replied, “for I have something important to discuss. That living of Colonel Brandon’s—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward? I heard yesterday and came straight here to confirm it.”

“It is perfectly true,” Elinor replied. “Colonel Brandon has bestowed the living of Delaford upon Edward.”

“Really?” he exclaimed. “Well, this is astonishing! No family tie, no connection between them, and yet at a time when livings are so valuable—what was the income?”

“About two hundred pounds a year.”

“Very well. And if the previous incumbent had been elderly and frail, someone might have secured the presentation for perhaps fourteen hundred pounds. So why didn’t he settle that earlier? Now it’s too late to sell, but a man of Colonel Brandon’s judgment—how could he be so careless in such a matter? It shows there’s inconsistency in nearly every human heart. Still, I suppose the truth is—Edward is only holding it until the real buyer comes of age. Yes, yes—that must be it.”

Elinor firmly corrected him, explaining that she had personally delivered the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward and thus knew the terms precisely. Reluctantly, he yielded to her account.

“This is truly astonishing,” he said, once he’d heard her explanation. “What could have motivated Colonel Brandon?”

“A simple one: to help Mr. Ferrars.”

“Well, well—whatever Colonel Brandon may be, Edward is certainly fortunate. But please, do not mention this to Fanny. Though I’ve told her already, and she bears it remarkably well, she’d prefer it not discussed.”

Elinor struggled to suppress a remark about how Fanny ought to welcome any wealth that enriched her brother without diminishing her own or her child’s comfort.

“Mrs. Ferrars,” he added, lowering his voice as if revealing a secret of great consequence, “knows nothing of it yet. And I believe it’s best to keep it hidden from her as long as possible. When the marriage happens, she’ll inevitably learn the truth.”

“But why such caution?” Elinor asked. “Even if Mrs. Ferrars takes no pleasure in knowing Edward can live comfortably—something entirely improbable—why would she care now? After her actions, she has cast him off forever. She has made everyone under her influence reject him too. Surely, once severed, she cannot possibly feel sorrow or joy for him. She cannot be affected by anything that happens to him. Would she truly abandon the comfort of a child while still carrying the burdens of motherhood?”

“Ah, Elinor,” John said gently, “your logic is sound, but it stems from ignorance of human nature. When Edward marries, rest assured, his mother will feel as deeply as if she had never disowned him. Therefore, every detail that might hasten that dreadful event must remain concealed from her. Mrs. Ferrars can never forget that Edward is her son.”

“You surprise me,” Elinor said. “I thought it must have slipped her memory by now.”

“You misjudge her greatly. Mrs. Ferrars is among the most devoted mothers in the world.”

Elinor remained silent.

“We think now,” Mr. Dashwood continued after a pause, “of Robert marrying Miss Morton.”

Elinor smiled faintly at the solemnity of his tone and replied calmly, “I suppose the lady has no choice in the matter.”

“Choice? What do you mean?”

“I only meant,” she said, “that from your manner, it seems Miss Morton would be equally content whether she married Edward or Robert.”

“Certainly, there’s no difference. Robert will now be considered the eldest son in every practical sense. And besides, both young men are agreeable enough—I wouldn’t say one is clearly superior to the other.”

Elinor said nothing more. John fell quiet too, then resumed:

“One thing, my dear sister,” he said, taking her hand and speaking in hushed tones, “I can assure you of—indeed, I shall tell you, because I know it will please you. I have strong reason to believe—no, I have it from the most reliable source—though I never heard Mrs. Ferrars say it herself, her daughter did, and I heard it from her—that despite any objections to a certain connection—you understand what I mean—it would have been far preferable to her than this. It would not have caused her half the distress this does. I was delighted to hear Mrs. Ferrars view it so. A very comforting thought for us all. ‘It would have been infinitely less painful,’ she said, ‘and I would gladly accept nothing worse.’ But of course, that’s all moot now—impossible to consider. Yet I wanted to share it with you, knowing how much it would delight you. Not that you have cause to regret, my dear Elinor. There’s no doubt you’ll do extremely well—perhaps even better, all things considered. Has Colonel Brandon been visiting lately?”

Elinor had heard enough—not to swell her pride, but to unsettle her mind. She was relieved when Robert Ferrars entered the room, sparing her from further reply or exposure to her brother’s revelations.

After a brief exchange, John remembered that Fanny had not yet been informed of Elinor’s presence and left to find her. Elinor was left alone with Robert, whose cheerful indifference and self-satisfied air—born of a life of indulgence and the unjust favoritism of his mother—only confirmed her low opinion of his character and sincerity.

They had barely been alone two minutes when Robert began asking about Edward. He, too, had heard of the living and was eager to know more. Elinor repeated what she had told John, but Robert reacted quite differently. He burst into uncontrollable laughter.

The idea of Edward becoming a clergyman, residing in a modest parsonage, struck him as absurd. At the thought of Edward reading prayers in a white surplice or announcing marriages between ordinary folk like John Smith and Mary Brown, he could imagine nothing funnier.

Elinor sat still, her expression unchanging, watching him with eyes that conveyed nothing but disdain. It was a look well placed—silent, but powerful—relieving her own emotions without betraying them to him.

Robert eventually sobered, not through her rebuke, but by his own conscience.

“We can treat it as a joke,” he said, regaining composure after his forced laughter subsided. “But seriously, it’s a tragedy. Poor Edward—he’s ruined for life. I’m genuinely sorry for him. I know him to be a kind-hearted fellow—perhaps the most well-meaning man alive. Don’t judge him by our limited acquaintance, Miss Dashwood. Poor Edward! His manners aren’t refined, I admit. But we’re not all born with charm or grace. To see him among strangers—pitiful, truly. But I swear, he has the finest heart in England. I was utterly shocked when I learned the truth. My mother told me first. I felt compelled to act. I immediately said to her, ‘My dear madam, I don’t know what you intend, but as for me—if Edward marries that girl, I’ll never speak to him again.’ That was exactly what I said. I was horrified. Poor Edward—he’s destroyed himself, cut himself off from decent society forever. But I’m not surprised. Given his upbringing, it was inevitable. My poor mother was beside herself.”

“Have you met the lady?”

“Yes—once, when she stayed here. I dropped in briefly and saw enough. Just an awkward country girl, lacking elegance or beauty. Exactly the sort who might capture poor Edward’s heart. I offered to speak to him myself, to dissuade him, but it was too late. I hadn’t known until after the rift. I couldn’t interfere then. But if I’d been informed earlier, I believe I could have prevented it. I would have told him plainly: ‘My dear fellow, consider what you’re doing. You’re making a disgraceful match—your whole family disapproves.’ I truly believe something could have been done. But now it’s too late. He’ll starve, you know. Absolutely starve.”

He had just concluded this grim assessment when Mrs. John Dashwood entered the room, ending the conversation.

Though she never mentioned the matter outside her own household, Elinor could see its effect in the slight flush on her face and the strained warmth of her greeting. She even expressed regret that Elinor and her sister would leave town so soon, as she had hoped to see more of them—an effort her husband, who followed her in and lingered adoringly at her side, seemed to interpret as especially tender and graceful.
