Trusty Gumbo could find no solace in his sorrow for the departure of his beloved master. To judge by his initial tears and mournful howls upon hearing the news of Mr. Harry's enlistment, one might have believed the negro's heart shattered by the separation. Naturally, he sought sympathy among the maid-servants at Mr. Lambert's lodgings. Whichever way that dusky youth turned, he found comfort in female society. Their fair and tender bosoms possessed a capacity for pity toward the poor African that rendered the darkness of Gumbo's complexion no more repulsive to them than Othello's was to Desdemona. Indeed, Europe has rarely been so squeamish regarding Africa as a certain other respectable quarter suggests. Nay, some Africans—witness the Chevalier de St. Georges, for instance—have become notorious favorites of the fair sex. Thus, in his humbler station, was Mr. Gumbo. The Lambert servants wept freely in his company; the maids kindly regarded him not merely as Mr. Harry's man, but as their brother.

Hetty could not help laughing when she discovered Gumbo roaring because his master had gone "a volunteer," as he termed it, and had not taken him along. He was ready to save Master Harry's life on any given day, declaring he would have done so and allowed himself to be cut into twenty thousand pieces rather than let harm come to his master. Yet, nature required support, and he condescended to fortify it with generous supplies of beer and cold meat in the kitchen. Though it was certain he was greedy, idle, and prone to lying, Hetty still gave him half a crown and treated him with especial kindness. Her tongue, usually so pert, grew remarkably gentle now, as if it had never known a jest. She moved about the house mute and meek. She was humble to her mother, grateful to John and Betty when they served dinner, patient with Polly when the latter pulled her hair during combing, long-suffering when Charley from school trod on her toes or disrupted her workbox, and silent in her father's presence. Oh, what a transmogrified little Hetty she had become! If her father had ordered her to roast the leg of mutton or walk to church arm-in-arm with Gumbo, she would have curtsied and replied, "Yes, if you please, dear papa!"

Leg of mutton! What sort of sustenance were some poor volunteers consuming with cannonballs flying overhead? Church! When it came to the prayers of wartime, how her knees knocked together as she knelt, hiding her head in the pew. She lowered her gaze when the parson read, "Thou shalt do no murder," from the communion rail, fearing he must be staring right at her. How she pondered all travelers by land and sea! How she sickened as she rushed to the newspaper to scan for news of the Expedition! How she watched her father when he returned from the Ordnance Office, scrutinizing his face for signs of good or ill tidings! Was he well? Had he been promoted to General? Was he wounded and imprisoned? Ah, me! Or perhaps had both his legs been severed by a single shot, like that pensioner they had seen in Chelsea Gardens the previous day? She would go on wooden legs for the rest of her life if his safety could ensure his return; at least, she vowed never to rise from her knees until he was back.

"Haven't you heard of people, Theo," she asked, "whose hair turns gray in a single night? I shouldn't wonder if mine did—I shouldn't wonder in the least." She glanced into the mirror to verify this phenomenon. "Hetty dear, you used not to be so nervous when papa was away in Minorca," remarked Theo. "Ah, Theo! One may very well see that George is not with the army, but safe at home," rejoined Hetty. At this, the elder sister blushed deeply and looked profoundly pensive. *Au fait*, if Mr. George had been in the army, that, you see, would have been another pair of boots.

Meanwhile, we shall not further harrow anyone's kind feelings, but simply state that Harry was, for the present, as safe as any officer of the Life Guards at Regent's Park Barracks. The first expedition in which our gallant volunteer participated may be deemed successful, though certainly not glorious. The British Lion, or any other lion, cannot always find a worthy adversary to combat, nor a battle-royal to deliver. Suppose he ventures forth seeking a tiger that refuses to appear, lays his paws on a goose, and gobbles it up? Lions, we know, must subsist like any other animal. But suppose, advancing into the forest in search of the aforementioned tiger and bellowing his challenge of war, he spies not one but six tigers approaching? This is manifestly not his game. He tucks his tail between his royal legs and retreats to his own snug den as swiftly as possible. Were he to attempt fighting six tigers, you might write that Lion down as an ass.

Now, Harry Warrington's first feat of war unfolded thus: He and approximately thirteen thousand other fighting men embarked in various ships and transports on the 1st of June from the Isle of Wight. At daybreak on the 5th, the fleet steamed into the Bay of Cancale in Brittany. For a while, he and the gentlemen volunteers enjoyed the pleasure of examining the French coast from their vessels, while the Commander-in-Chief and the Commodore reconnoitered the bay in a cutter. Cattle were spotted, along with some dragoons who trotted off into the distance; and a small fort armed with a couple of guns had the audacity to fire upon His Grace of Marlborough and the Commodore in the cutter. By two o'clock, the entire British fleet was anchored, and signals were made for all the grenadier companies of eleven regiments to embark on flat-bottomed boats and assemble around the Commodore's ship, the *Essex*. Meanwhile, Mr. Howe, hoisting his broad pennant aboard the *Success* frigate, approached as near to shore as possible, followed by the other frigates to protect the troop landing. Now, under the command of Lord George Sackville and General Dury, the gentlemen volunteers, the grenadier companies, and three battalions of guards pulled ashore.

On this occasion, the gentlemen volunteers could perform no heroic deeds, for the French—who should have remained to fight them—fled. Having silenced the fire of the little fort that had disturbed the Commander-in-Chief's reconnaissance, the army promptly assaulted it, capturing the entire garrison and shooting one man in the leg. Indeed, he was but an elderly gentleman who had gallantly fired his two guns and told his conquerors, "If every Frenchman had acted like me, you would not have landed at Cancale at all." The advanced detachment of invaders took possession of the village of Cancale, where they lay upon their arms all night. Our volunteer was jokingly chided by his comrades about his eagerness to tread the warpath and bring in two or three French scalps. None such, however, fell under his tomahawk; the only person slain on the whole day was a French gentleman riding with his servant, who was surprised by Volunteer Lord Downe, marching forward with a company of King's troops. My Lord Downe offered the gentleman quarter, which he foolishly refused, whereupon he, his servant, and the two horses were immediately shot.

The following day, the entire force was landed and advanced from Cancale to St. Malo. All villages through which the troops passed were emptied, and the roads were so narrow in many places that men had to march single file, vulnerable to being shot down from behind tall, leafy hedges had there been any enemy to disturb them. At nightfall, the army arrived before St. Malo and were greeted by a barrage of artillery from the town, which caused little damage in the darkness. Covering themselves under this smoke, the British set fire to ships, wooden structures, pitch, and tar magazines in the harbor, creating a prodigious conflagration that lasted the entire night. This feat was accomplished without any attempt by the French to molest the British force. However, as it was confidently asserted that a considerable French force resided within St. Malo, though they refused to emerge, His Grace the Duke of Marlborough and My Lord George Sackville decided not to disturb the garrison. Instead, they marched back to Cancale—and then boarded their ships.

If this were not a veracious history, would it not have been easier to send our Virginian on a more glorious campaign? Exactly four weeks after his departure from England, Mr. Warrington found himself back in Portsmouth. He addressed a letter to his brother George, whom the latter hastened to Dean Street upon receiving it.

"Glorious news, ladies!" cried George, finding the Lambert family all at breakfast. "Our champion has returned. He has endured all sorts of dangers but survived them all. He has seen dragons—upon my word, he says so."

"Dragons! What do you mean, Mr. Warrington?"

"But not killed any—he says so, as you shall hear. He writes: 'Dearest Brother—I think you will be glad to hear that I am returned, without any commission as yet; without any wounds or glory; but, at any rate, alive and hearty.'
