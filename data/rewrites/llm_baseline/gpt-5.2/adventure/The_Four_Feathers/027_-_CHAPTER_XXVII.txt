These were the days before the Khalifa’s masons lifted that great mud wall about the House of Stone. As yet only a thorn zareeba ringed the place—an enclosure of spiteful branches that scratched the skin and caught at rags, as though even the hedge had learned the prison’s trade. The House itself squatted on the eastern lip of Omdurman, and Omdurman was a capital built as if the world had forgotten how to bless a thing with shade.

No garden softened it. No green blade dared show its tongue. The ground was a hard, pepper-coloured plain, stoned and baked, and on it sprawled a long, narrow crawl of hovels—mud and wattle and corrugated misery—alive with vermin, tainted with fever, and forever exhaling a sour, human steam. All day the air rang with the place’s particular music: chains clinking in patient cadence, flies humming like a perpetual wire-string, vendors bawling from hoarse throats, camels coughing, and in the pauses the river’s far hiss, like a memory trying to speak in a language no one cared to learn.

Between the prison and the Nile there were no houses then, only that half-mile of broken slope where stones lay like scattered teeth. While daylight lasted, captives were allowed—so long as their irons would let them—to stumble down to the bank, draw water for their use, and perform their ablutions. For natives and negroes the river was not merely water but possibility. Dhows moored thick along the shore; the river traffic made its harbour there, and the wide foreshore became a market-place where the day’s loud barter was seasoned with secret bargains. A man could rub shoulders with a friend, concert an escape in the cover of ordinary speech, and slip, as unnoticed as a grain of millet in a sack, into the densest knot of buyers and sellers. A blacksmith might be found whose love of iron outweighed his fear of Idris’s whip; and in Omdurman, the clink of fetters was so common a rhythm that it drew no more attention than a pestle striking grain in a courtyard.

But for a European escape was a different recipe altogether—more costly, longer in preparation, and liable to be spoiled at the last moment by one brutal hand. There were not so many white prisoners that they could be lost in the crowd. Each was a marked man, a rare spice in the Khalifa’s storehouse, counted and watched. There were relays of camels to be stationed through the desert, money to be gathered and hidden, and, above all, devoted natives—men who would risk their lives for a foreigner, or think they would, and then, at the pinch of danger, shrink like meat touched too near the fire. The camels might be procured and placed, and then their drivers would drift away. The plan might be kneaded and shaped for months, and then the gaoler’s suspicion would ruin it with one burst of violence—one flogging on the mere rumour that a man had money.

Colonel Trench, methodical by nature and made more so by captivity, began to lose all hope by slow degrees, as a man loses the taste of his own language when no one speaks it back to him. He knew his friends were working. Sometimes the boy who brought his food would mutter, eyes lowered, that he must be ready. Sometimes, when he was paraded in the Khalifa’s triumph—shown like a captured banner as an emblem of Turkish destiny—a man would jostle his camel and, without looking up, breathe a quick encouragement into the air. But nothing came of any of it. The sun rose daily beyond the bend of the river behind Khartum’s tall palms and burned its way across the sky, and the months dragged after one another like chained men.

On an evening toward the end of August—one of those nights when the heat did not fall so much as settle deeper into the bones—Trench sat in a corner of the enclosure watching the sun drop westward with an agony of anticipation. Day was torture enough, but it was bearable because it had edges: water at noon, the river’s breath, the faintest shift in wind. Night in the House of Stone had no edges. It was a weight, absolute and blind, renewed every evening with the punctual cruelty of a ritual.

Twilight came, and with it Idris es Saier, the great negro of the Gawaamah tribe, and the fellow-gaolers who moved with him like a pack.

“Into the House of Stone!” Idris cried.

Praying and cursing—voices rising and cracking—men stumbled forward, their chains dragging sparks from stone in the fading light. At the narrow entrance they jammed and fought, and behind them the pitiless whips fell perpetually on the hindmost backs. The House of Stone was already occupied by some thirty captives who had been too sick to be driven out by day: lumps of humanity on a swamped mud floor, or propped against the wall in the last extremities of weakness and disease. At night two hundred more were thrust in and penned there until morning.

The room was perhaps thirty feet square; in the middle a solid pillar, four feet across, supported the roof like a thumb pressing down on a wound. There was no window. A few small apertures near the roof made a pretence of air, as though the building offered a mock courtesy: a ration of breath measured like grain. The prisoners were packed in, screaming and striking, and when the door was closed the last strip of twilight vanished and utter darkness replaced it. A man could not even distinguish the outlines of the heads that wedged him in.

Trench fought like the rest.

There was a corner near the door he coveted with a fierceness that surprised him, until he understood it was not comfort he was fighting for but the mere possibility of lasting until dawn. In that corner he might have a little shelter from blows, stamping feet, the bruises of neighbours’ shackles; he might have a support against which to brace his back through ten interminable hours of suffocation.

If I were to fall.

The fear lived in him every night, working like a drug that produced madness. A man who went down amid that yelling, heaving throng did not rise; he was stamped out of shape. Trench had seen the bodies dragged from the prison each morning. He was a small man. So he fought for that corner as if it were a lifeboat in a black sea, kicking with his fetters, thrusting with elbows, diving under a larger man’s arm, burrowing between two others, tearing at clothes, using nails and fists, even striking at heads with the chain that dangled from the iron ring about his neck.

He reached the corner at last, streaming with heat, gasping. The rest of the night he would spend in holding it.

“If I were to fall!” he choked, and in that clamour—where nothing less than a shout was audible—he called to the darkness beside him. “Is it you, Ibrahim?”

“Yes, Effendi,” a like shout answered.

Relief came in a single syllable. Ibrahim, a tall Arab of the Hadendoas, stood near him each night, and between them a friendship had grown—not born of sentiment but of necessity, like bread leavened by hunger. There were no prison rations in Omdurman; each captive lived on his own money or the charity of friends outside. To Trench, at long intervals, money came secretly by a native from Assouan or Suakin; then there were stretches when no help came and he lived on the charity of Greeks who had sworn conversion to the Mahdist faith, or starved with such patience as he could. There were times, too, when Ibrahim had no friend to send him his meal. Each had known the other’s empty-handed days. They shared what could be shared, and at night stood side by side against the wall.

“Yes, Effendi, I am here,” Ibrahim repeated, and groping in the black, he steadied Trench against the wall with a hand that was firm as a post.

In an extreme corner a fight raged with more than common violence. So closely packed were the prisoners that with each advance and retreat, the whole crowd swayed in rhythm from side to side, end to end—like a monstrous body breathing and convulsing. Men fought to keep their feet, fought even with their teeth. Over the din of harsh breathing and the clank of chains rose, now and then, a wild sobbing cry for mercy, or an inhuman shriek stifled as soon as uttered—the sound that meant a man had gone down beneath stamping feet. Missiles flew too: scraps of filth, clods of foul earth scraped from the floor. Since none could tell from what quarter they were flung, heads battered against heads in the effort to avoid them. All in the blackest darkness.

For two hours Trench stood in that prison ringing with noise, rank with heat. There were eight hours to follow before the door would open and he could stumble into the clean air and fall asleep in the zareeba. He stood on tiptoe to lift his head above the press, but even so he could barely breathe. The air was moist and sour, as if it had been breathed and sweated and suffered through too many bodies and was now spoiled beyond salvation. His throat was parched; his tongue felt swollen and stringy like a dried fig left too long in dust. It seemed to him the imagination of God could devise no worse hell than the House of Stone on an August night in Omdurman. It could add fire, he thought, but only fire.

“If I were to fall!” he cried again—and as he spoke his hell was made perfect, for the door opened.

Idris es Saier appeared in the opening.

“Make room,” he cried. “Make room!” And he threw fire among the prisoners to drive them from the door. Lighted tufts of dried grass blazed in the darkness and fell upon shoulders and heads. The captives were so crowded they could not avoid them; some could not even lift a hand to pluck the fire away. Whips enforced Idris’s command; lashes fell upon all within reach, and a little space was cleared. Into that space a man was flung, heavily ironed, slight of figure, bent with suffering. The door slammed shut again.

Trench, close to the entrance, had caught the briefest glimpse in the strip of twilight: a man small like himself, and weakened, the kind who would not keep his footing when the crowd swayed.

“He will fall,” Trench said through clenched teeth. “He will fall to-night. God—if I were to—” The crowd heaved; curses rose sharper and shriller.

The new prisoner was the cause. He clung to the door with his face against the panels, seeking the thin trickle of actual air through the chinks. Those behind plucked him away, jostled him, pressed him backward to take his place. He was driven like a wedge between bodies until at last he was flung against Trench.

In the daytime, outside, common misery sometimes drew men together; a faithful might help an infidel, a Turk might share water with a Greek. But in the House of Stone at night kindness died quickly. The one creed was to keep upright. The one sacrament was a mouthful of morning air. Trench, practical and unimaginative, was like the rest; the need to live even to dawn drowned everything.

“Back!” he shouted violently. “Back, or I strike!” And as he wrestled to lift his arm above his head, he heard the man incoherently babbling in English.

Something in Trench’s chest lurched as if a hand had tightened around it. English—his own tongue—here, in this black choking kennel. It was not merely speech; it was a door into another world, a taste of a remembered food. It civilised him in an instant, like a woman’s touch in a savage place.

“Don’t fall,” Trench cried. He caught the man by the arm. “Ibrahim—help! God, if he were to fall!”

The crowd swayed again; cries and curses stabbed at the ears. Trench and Ibrahim supported the stranger, fought and wrestled until those about them yielded, spitting in fear and anger.

“Shaitan! They are mad!”

They cleared a space in the corner. Setting the Englishman down on the ground, they stood in front of him to keep the stamping feet from trampling him. Behind them, in lulls of the noise, Trench heard the babble continue.

“He will die before morning,” Trench shouted to Ibrahim. “He is in a fever!”

“Sit beside him,” said Ibrahim. “I can keep them back.”

Trench squatted in the corner. Ibrahim planted his legs wide and became a barrier of flesh and will, guarding the little hollow of space as a man guards a cooking-fire against a crowd of thieves: not by argument, but by stance, by readiness, by the threat of sudden violence.

Bending his head, Trench could hear the stranger’s words more clearly now. They came disjointed, pleading, shining strangely clean in that cesspit of sound, like a songbird heard through smoke.

“I saw the riding lights of the yachts—and the reflections shortening and lengthening as the water rippled—there was a band, too, as we passed the pier-head. What was it playing? Not the overture—and I don’t think that I remember any other tune…” He laughed with a crazy chuckle. “I was always pretty bad at appreciating music, wasn’t I? except when you played.” Then, as if the rhythm of water drew him, he came back to the sea. “There was the line of hills upon the right as the boat steamed out of the bay—you remember there were woods on the hillside—perhaps you have forgotten. Then came Bray, a little fairyland of lights close down by the water at the point of the ridge… you remember Bray, we lunched there once or twice, just you and I, before everything was settled… it seemed strange to be steaming out of Dublin Bay and leaving you a long way off to the north among the hills… strange and somehow not quite right… for that was the word you used when the morning came behind the blinds—it is not right that one should suffer so much pain… the engines didn’t stop, though, they just kept throbbing and revolving and clanking as though nothing had happened whatever… one felt a little angry about that… the fairyland was already only a sort of golden blot behind… and then nothing but sea and the salt wind… and the things to be done.”

The man lifted himself on an elbow and fumbled at his breast as though searching for something hidden there. “Yes, the things to be done,” he repeated, and sank into whisperings with his head fallen forward.

Trench slipped an arm around him and raised him a little, but there was nothing more he could do. Even crouched close to the ground the heat was almost beyond endurance; it had a thickness, like breath trapped under a wet cloth. In front the din went on—shouts, chains, the scrape of skin on mud. In one corner men sang in a mad frenzy; in another a few tried to dance in their fetters, a hideous parody of merriment. Ibrahim held his line like a wall. And beside Trench lay a man who, one night, had sailed out of Dublin Bay past the riding lanterns of yachts, had watched Bray’s fairyland of lights dwindle to a golden blot.

That thought—sea and salt wind, the cool dim night, a bell telling the hour—worked upon Trench until, practical creature as he was, yearning rose in him like tears. In this place, where the only smells were human sourness and scorched grass and the rankness of old mud, the very idea of clean air felt like the fragrance of baked bread in a quiet kitchen—something so ordinary it became holy when withheld. But the stranger began to speak again.

“It is funny that those three faces were always the same… the man in the tent with the lancet in his hand, and the man in the back room off Piccadilly… and mine. Funny and not quite right. No, I don’t think that was quite right either. They get quite big, too, just when you are going to sleep in the dark—quite big, and they come very close to you and won’t go away… they rather frighten one…” He clutched Trench with a nervous grip like a boy in sudden terror.

“It’s all right, old man,” Trench said, in the tone a man uses to soothe a child. “It’s all right.”

The fear passed as quickly as it had come. The man’s mind lurched into another scene, another rehearsal, as though he were tasting over and over a phrase he could not bear to mispronounce.

“Will you take it back?” he asked with great hesitation and timidity. “Really? The others have, all except the man who died at Tamai. And you will too!” He spoke like a man who can hardly trust good fortune. Then his voice shifted, making light of misery as though by belittling it he could swallow it. “Oh, it hasn’t been the best of times, of course. But then one didn’t expect the best of times. And at the worst, one had always the afterwards to look forward to… supposing one didn’t run… I’m not sure that when the whole thing’s balanced, it won’t come out that you have really had the worst time. I know you… it would hurt you through and through, pride and heart and everything, and for a long time just as much as it hurt that morning when the daylight came through the blinds. And you couldn’t do anything! And you hadn’t the afterwards to help you—you weren’t looking forward to it all the time as I was… it was all over and done with for you…” He lapsed again into mutterings.

Trench’s first delight in hearing English had cooled into curiosity. He had once described himself, on a free day in London, as “an inquisitive, methodical person,” and captivity had not burned that out of him. Here, amid the roaring dark, a whole life seemed to be unspooling in fevered scraps, and not a happy life—something tragic at its heart. The word afterwards came and went like a refrain, a motif in the man’s speech, and Trench began to wonder if it had been the man’s real religion.

The heat grew more stifling; the darkness pressed closer. Yet the cries and shouts were dying down. Their volume lessened; their shrillness dulled. Stupor and fatigue and exhaustion took their toll. Trench bent his head to listen and now heard more clearly.

“I saw your light that morning… you put it out suddenly… did you hear my step on the gravel?… I thought you did, it hurt rather.” Then he broke into an emphatic protest. “No, no, I had no idea that you would wait. I had no wish that you should. Afterwards, perhaps, I thought, but nothing more, upon my word. Sutch was quite wrong… Of course there was always the chance that one might come to grief oneself—get killed, you know, or fall ill and die—before one asked you to take your feather back; and then there wouldn’t even have been a chance of the afterwards. But that is the risk one had to take.”

The allusion was not direct enough for Trench’s comprehension. He heard “feather,” but could not yet connect it with any action of his own. Still, wonder stirred in him at the thought of how many men walked the world with calm, commonplace faces, while beneath, hidden and unguessed, lay quaint beliefs and poetic fancies—never to be revealed until illness unstitched the mind and let its private dialect spill out.

“No, one of the reasons why I never said anything that night to you about what I intended was, I think, that I did not wish you to wait or have any suspicion of what I was going to attempt.” His voice softened into a tone of interest, almost discovery. “Do you know, it has only occurred to me since I came to the Soudan, but I believe that Durrance cared.”

The name struck Trench like a blow. This man knew Durrance. He was not merely English; he belonged to the same narrow circle of men whose histories crossed and re-crossed like tracks in a field. Trench’s curiosity about the life in fragments became a sharper curiosity: who was he? He strained to see, though in that black hovel sight was nothing. But perhaps he would hear enough to be sure. If the stranger knew Durrance, perhaps he knew Trench too. Trench listened for some familiar cadence in the voice—found none. He waited for words, and the words came.

“Durrance stood at the window, after I had told them about you, Ethne—”

Ethne. The delirium had its listener, then—a woman’s name flung like a flare in the dark. Trench could recall no Ethne, and yet the voice went on, laying down details as a cook lays spices, one by one, until a flavour becomes unmistakable.

“All the time when I was proposing to send in my papers, after the telegram had come, he stood at the window of my rooms with his back to me, looking out across the park. I fancied he blamed me. But I think now he was making up his mind to lose you… I wonder.”

Trench uttered so startled an exclamation that Ibrahim turned his head sharply.

“Is he dead?” Ibrahim demanded.

“No, he lives—he lives,” Trench answered.

It was impossible, Trench argued—and yet memory rose stubbornly. He remembered Durrance standing by a window with his back to a room. He remembered a telegram that took a long time to read, and the odd suspense it spread among all except Durrance. He remembered a man speaking of his betrothal and of sending in his papers. Was the woman’s name Ethne? A woman of Donegal—yes. And the stranger had spoken of sailing out of Dublin Bay. He had spoken of a feather.

“Good God,” Trench whispered. “Was the name Ethne? Was it? Was it?”

For a while there was no answer, only talk in the fever of a mud-walled city and an intolerable sun burning upon an endless round of desert; a man lying all day with a linen robe drawn over his head, drawing one face across three thousand miles until, at sunset, it was near, and then taking courage and going down into a gate. And then four words stabbed through everything.

“Three little white feathers.”

Trench leaned back against the wall. It was he who had devised that message. In London it had seemed a neat, almost playful cruelty, like a phrase coined and sent out to do its work. Here, spoken in the House of Stone, it was no longer neat—it was a blade.

“This afternoon we were under the elms down by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then came three little white feathers; and the world’s at an end.”

Now Trench had no doubt. These were quoted words—words spoken by Ethne, no doubt, on the night the feathers came. “Harry,” she had said. “Do you remember, Harry?” Trench was certain. The name in the fevered mouth was the last key.

“Feversham!” Trench cried. “Feversham!”

He shook the man he held, as though he could shake sense into him through heat and delirium. “Under the elms by the Lennon River—” Visions came unbidden: green shade touched with gold; sunlight flickering between leaves; water running cool and clear. In this desert of stone and breath, those images dragged at him like a mirage.

Feversham had been under the elms of the Lennon River on that afternoon before the feathers came—and now he lay in the House of Stone at Omdurman.

But why?

Trench asked himself the question—and he was not spared the answer.
