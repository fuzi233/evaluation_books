I am an old man now, and sit with my feet towards the fire as if I had a right to warmth purchased by years; and yet, when I look back at that leap which my Theo and I took—scarce out of our teens, with the ink hardly dry upon our names—I know well enough what a decent moralist ought to do. He ought to set himself up as a scarecrow for hot-headed youth; he ought to talk solemnly of ledgers, and rent-days, and the butcher’s bill; he ought to say “mutton chops” with a face as long as Lent, and warn every girl with a pretty hand not to put it into a poor man’s.

And yet—since truth is truth though it come without a sermon—our married life disappointed all the cautious prophets. It had its sharp seasons: griefs that bit like a north wind through an ill-fitted sash; days of want when the very sound of a knock at the door made the heart jump, lest it should be a creditor’s fist instead of a friend’s. But time, by God’s mercy, rubs the edge off many a knife; and there were consolations too, so intimate and so holy that a man scarcely dares to write them down as if they were common news for any eye. To marry without a competence is dangerous, no doubt. Society, which loves its rules as cooks love their measures, calls it a crime. Yet every year thousands commit it, trusting to no grand fortune—only to health, labour, and that strange seasoning of the blood called hope. If the genteel laws were made universal, the world would leave off multiplying; the silk-stockinged would stand shivering on the bank until a gilded barge came, while the barefooted would step into the stream and wade, and somehow get across.

Who would counsel his son to take a gentle girl from comfort to debt, privation, friendlessness, sickness, the whole sour dish of res angusta domi? I look at my own wife—at that steadfast sweetness which never turned rancid—and I ask pardon for the trials I made her taste. And yet I cannot talk of poverty as if it were nothing but ashes. I have endured it, and found it oftener tolerable than the rich imagine. There are strange pleasures that come with a scanty meal: gratitude, for one; and the clear sight that visits people in a narrow room, while fine folks, stepping from their broad sunshine into our little shadow, blunder as if half blind. They would pity us for drinking small beer, as though it were poison; they would marvel that we could say grace over cold mutton—cold, indeed, but still honest meat, and not the less to be thanked for because it had slept a night.

My good General—my father-in-law—had done the same rash thing before me. He married his Molly when he was a subaltern, with a purse no better lined than mine. They had known ups and downs, and (though my wife will never confess the point) had very likely done it in those younger days without asking leave of papa and mamma. At any rate, being pleased with their own venture, they were not frightened for ours; and when I made my statements to Mr. Lambert, I believe I was anxious to deceive him and myself together. He went off to Jamaica thinking I had a couple of thousand pounds, and so could keep his darling daughter in ease for years.

In truth, when the household had been furnished with such modest bravery as young people call necessity, the good man sailed away not much richer than his son-in-law. Theo’s portion was a handful of trinkets, some lace of Aunt Lambert’s, and twenty new guineas in a purse stitched by her mother and sisters—gold that looked splendidly heavy in the hand, and yet had a way of thinning like butter on a hot pan. For my own stock, I had counted as good a debt a sum which my honoured mother never would acknowledge, to the last day when she was called to pay the last debt of all. The money I had disbursed for her, she said, had been spent upon the estate which was to be mine at her decease. What she could spare was for my poor brother, who had nothing, and who, she reminded me in many a letter, would have been sole heir of the Virginian property but for a half-hour’s accident of birth. He was now distinguishing himself in the service of king and country; his promotion must be purchased; and to purchase it was his mother’s duty—or, she should suppose, his brother’s. When I had done with bar-studies and stage-amusements, Madam Esmond sent word by Mountain more than once that I was welcome to come home and take my place in the colony; and this she said before the news of my marriage reached her.

There is no need to set down her maternal indignation when it did. After the pacification of Canada, Harry got leave and dutifully visited Virginia. He wrote describing his reception, and my mother’s splendid entertainments. Castlewood, shut since our departure for Europe, was thrown open again; the soldier of Quebec, the friend of Wolfe, was fêted with all honour. Yet, as he told it, there were quarrels enough—dismal, noisy bouts—because my brother persisted in keeping friendship with Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whose praises Harry never tired of singing. I can allow the gentleman many virtues; and though England was to learn bitterly what kind of constancy he possessed, I can admire as well as his warmest friends that steadfast courage and success.

If the battles between Harry and our mother were as frequent as his letters made them, I wondered why he stayed. One reason, uncharitable perhaps, suggested itself; and it was a reason I scarcely liked to speak aloud to Theo. For we had both talked, with tender smiles and sighs, of our dear little Hetty’s romantic attachment to my brother, and wondered that he had never discovered it. I supposed, as men do, that my gentleman had found some young lady at home more to his taste than our sweet Hester, and that this accounted for his long lingering in Virginia.

Presently a letter came which was not a confession, but an admission. The person was described—never named; a Being of beauty and perfection, as such beings always are in ink. My wife asked to see the letter. I could not refuse; I handed it to her with a face as doleful as if I were reading a death-warrant. She read it, and to my surprise showed no corresponding sorrow.

“I have thought of this before, my love,” I said. “I feel with you for your disappointment regarding poor Hetty.”

“Ah! poor Hetty,” says Theo, and looked down at the carpet as if there were a history written in its pattern.

“It would never have done,” I said.

“No—they would not have been happy,” she sighed, as a woman sighs who sees further than a man pretends.

“How strange he never should have found out her secret!” I continued.

She looked me full in the face with an odd expression—half amusement, half something else, like a spice you cannot name until it pricks. “Pray, what does that look mean?” I asked.

“Nothing, my dear—nothing! only I am not surprised!” says Theo, and blushed.

“What,” I cried, with a queer commotion, “can there be another?”

“I am sure I never said so, George,” she answered quickly. “But if Hetty has overcome her childish folly, ought we not all to be glad? Do you gentlemen suppose that you only are to fall in love and grow tired, indeed?”

“What!” I said, startled as if a kettle had boiled over. “Do you mean to tell me, Theo, that you ever cared for any one but me?”

“Oh, George,” she whimpered—laughing and ashamed together—“when I was at school, there was—there was one of the boys at Doctor Backhouse’s, who sate in the loft next to us; and I thought he had lovely eyes; and I was so shocked when I recognised him behind the counter at Mr. Grigg’s the mercer’s, when I went to buy a cloak for baby, and I wanted to tell you, my dear, and I didn’t know how!”

I made her describe him—coat, hair, all—and went, moved by jealousy into which a man may put a deal of ridiculous energy. And there he was: a little bandy-legged wretch in a blue camlet coat, red hair tied with a dirty ribbon, so pitiful a rival that I could not find it in me even to reproach my wife. Nor will she ever know that I looked at the fellow, until she reads this confession. If our wives saw us as we are, I thought—smelled the stale smoke in our coats, heard the vanity in our talk—would they love us as they do? Are we as mistaken in them as they in us? I look into one candid face, at least, and think it never deceived me.

As for the exact smallness of our capital when we began, I will not set it down, lest some brisk young couple take heart and fancy that love can be eaten like bread. The unfortunate tragedy brought us nothing. Since its late publication, reviewers have spoken not unfavourably of its merits; Mr. Kemble himself did me the honour to commend it. Our kind friend Lord Wrotham would have had it published by subscription, and even sent a bank-note, asking for a hundred copies for his friends. But I have always hated that manner of levying money. I preferred my poverty sine dote, locked up the manuscript, with my poor girl’s verses inserted on the first page, like a pressed flower laid in a book.

Why the piece gave offence at Court I never clearly knew, unless it were because an actor who had run off with an earl’s daughter played a principal part; but I was told, too, that sentiments put into the mouths of my Indian characters—declamations against ambition, against the British desire of rule—were called dangerous and unconstitutional. So whatever little hope I had of royal favour was whisked away like flour from a board when a door bangs.

What was to be done? A few months after the failure, I counted what remained of my fortune—the calculation was not long—and resolved to beat a retreat from my pretty Bloomsbury apartments. I gave warning to our good landlady, saying my wife’s health required country lodgings. We went no farther than Lambeth. Gumbo and Molly followed us faithfully; and here, though poor as might be, we were waited on by a maid and a lackey in livery, like folks of condition. Kind relatives cried out against our extravagance; indeed, are they not ever ready to find our faults for us, and publish them to the world, as if they were town-criers?

One day, returning from London where I had been to see some booksellers, I recognised our family arms and livery on a grand gilt chariot standing before a public-house near our lodgings. Loiterers had gathered round it, gazing with awe at the footmen, resplendent in sun, quaffing flaming pots of beer. I hurried home, and found my Lady Castlewood seated opposite my wife in our little room. From the window we had a bright prospect: the river covered with barges and wherries, the old towers and trees of the Archbishop’s palace and gardens beyond—sounds of water and oar, bell and distant coach, a whole city breathing in waves and clatter. In that place, the noise was not yet the later iron roar of industry, but a human din—hoofs, wheels, voices—broken by the soft slap of tide; and I often thought how much the mind leans upon such a soundscape, as a cook leans upon the steady heat of her fire.

Theo, who can describe a scene as neatly as she can whip an egg, narrated to me afterwards every turn of her ladyship’s talk.

“I have been here this ever-so-long,” says the Countess, “gossiping with cousin Theo, while you have been away at the coffee-house, I dare say, making merry with your friends, and drinking your punch and coffee. Guess she must find it rather lonely here, with nothing to do but work them little caps and hem them frocks. Never mind, dear; reckon you’ll soon have a companion who will amuse you when cousin George is away at his coffee-house! What a nice lodging you have got here, I do declare! Our new house which we have took is twenty times as big, and covered with gold from top to bottom; but I like this quite as well. Bless you being rich is no better than being poor. When we lived to Albany, and I did most all the work myself, scoured the rooms, biled the kettle, helped the wash, and all, I was just as happy as I am now. We only had one old negro to keep the store. Why don’t you sell Gumbo, cousin George? He ain’t no use here idling and dawdling about, and making love to the servant-girl. Fogh! guess they ain’t particular, these English people!”

So she rattled on, brisk as a spoon in a tin pan, until her hour came. Then she produced a fine repeating watch and said it was time to pay a call upon Her Majesty at Buckingham House. “And mind you come to us, George,” she cried, waving her little hand out of the gilt coach. “Theo and I have settled all about it.”

When the laced footmen clambered up behind and our magnificent little patroness rolled away, I said, “Here, at least, is one who is not afraid of our poverty, nor ashamed to remember her own.”

“Ashamed!” Theo said, taking up her lilliputian needlework again, the thread slipping through her fingers with that soft whisper which, in a poor room, is as companionable as any music. “To do her justice, she would make herself at home in any kitchen or palace in the world. She has given me and Molly twenty lessons in housekeeping. She says, when she was at home to Albany, she roasted, baked, swept the house, and milked the cow.” Theo pronounced cow archly in our American way, and mimicked her ladyship’s accent so divertingly that even our anxieties had to make room for laughter.

“And she has no pride,” I said. “It was good-natured of her to ask us to dine with her and my lord. When will Uncle Warrington ever think of offering us a crust again, or a glass of his famous beer?”

“Yes, it was not ill-natured to invite us,” says Theo, slily. “But, my dear, you don’t know all the conditions!” And then, still in Lady Castlewood’s tone, she told me the whole comedy. The Countess had taken out her pocket-book and named her engagements: Monday, a Duke and Duchess with several members of my lord’s house and their ladies; Tuesday, more earls, two bishops, and an ambassador. “Of course you won’t come on them days?” says her ladyship. “Now you are so poor, you know, that fine company ain’t no good for you. Lord bless you! father never dines on our company days! he don’t like it; he takes a bit of cold meat anyways.”

Theo said, laughing, she told the Countess that Mr. Warrington cared for none but the best company, and proposed she should ask us on some day when the Archbishop of Canterbury dined with her, and his Grace must give us a lift home in his coach to Lambeth. And then, Theo added, “She is economical too. ‘I thought of bringing with me some of my baby’s caps and things, which his lordship has outgrown ’em, but they may be wanted again, you know, my dear.’ So we lose that addition to our wardrobe, and Molly and I must do our best without her ladyship’s charity.”

“When people are poor, they are poor,” the Countess had said with her usual blunt honesty, “and must get on the best they can. What we shall do for that poor Maria, goodness only knows! we can’t ask her to see us as we can you, though you are so poor: but an earl’s daughter to marry a play-actor! La, my dear, it’s dreadful: his Majesty and the Princess have both spoken of it! Every other noble family in this kingdom as has ever heard of it pities us; though I have a plan for helping those poor unhappy people, and have sent down Simons, my groom of the chambers, to tell them on it.”

Her plan was that Hagan, who had kept almost all his terms at Dublin College, should return, take his degree, and enter holy orders; “when we will provide him with a chaplaincy at home, you know.” And I may mention—though it belongs to later pages—that this benevolent plan was executed some score of months after, and that I myself was enabled to be of service to Mr. Hagan, who was among the kindest and best of our friends in our own time of want. Castlewood performed his promise loyally enough—orders and a colonial appointment came in due time; Hagan distinguished himself as soldier and preacher. But not a guinea did my lord spare, in those days, for his sister or his kinsman in their trouble. I never asked him—thank Heaven—to assist me. To do him justice, no man could speak more amiably, or with a joy I believe genuine, when my days of poverty ended.

As for my Uncle Warrington, and his virtuous wife and daughters, let me do them justice too, and declare that throughout my trial their sorrow at my poverty was consistent and unvarying. I had still a few acquaintances who saw them and brought me report of their talk; and I never could hear they uttered one good word of me or my wife. They spoke of my tragedy as a crime, and of the author as a reprobate for ever reeling about Grub Street in rags. They held out no hand. My poor wife might cry in pain, but they had not twopence to bestow. They went to church half-a-dozen times in the week; subscribed to public charities; and were of that tribe known eighteen hundred years ago, which flourishes as long as men endure—thanking Heaven they are not as other folks are, and leaving the wounded to other succour.

I do not care to recall the doubts and anxieties that beset me; the plan after plan tried and failed; the way the very street-noises seemed to reproach us—the relentless rumble of carts bringing plenty to other doors, the cheerful cry of sellers in the morning, as if the city itself had provisions, and we had only appetite. I bethought me of my friend Mr. Johnson; and when I remember the eager kindness with which he received me, I am ashamed of pert speeches I once made regarding his manners.

I told him my story: the marriage; the prospects. He would not admit they were gloomy, nor that—si male nunc—they must continue so. Before me were chances (slender enough) of a place in England; the inheritance which must be mine in the course of nature, or at least would fall to the heir we expected; a small stock of money for actual necessity; a possibility—“though, to be free with you, sir,” says he—“after the performance of your tragedy, I doubt whether nature has endowed you with those peculiar qualities necessary for achieving a remarkable literary success”; and finally, submission to maternal rule and return to Virginia, where plenty and home were always ready.

“Why, sir!” he cried, “such a sum as you mention would have been a fortune to me when I began the world, and my friend Mr. Goldsmith would set up a coach-and-six on it. With youth, hope, to-day, and a couple of hundred pounds in cash—no young fellow need despair. Think, sir, you have a year at least before you, and who knows what may chance between now and then. Why, sir, your relatives here may provide for you, or you may succeed to your Virginian property, or you may come into a fortune!”

I did not come into one that year; he did. My Lord Bute gave Mr. Johnson a pension, which set all Grub Street raging against the recipient—he having himself published no very flattering opinion upon pensions and pensioners.

Nevertheless, he did not discourage my literary projects entirely, promised to procure me work from the booksellers, and faithfully performed that promise. “But,” says he, “sir, you must not appear amongst them in forma pauperis. Have you never a friend’s coach, in which we can ride to see them? You must put on your best laced hat and waistcoat; and we must appear, sir, as if we were doing them a favour.”

The stratagem answered for a visit or two; it procured me respect enough at first, for booksellers, like cooks, are apt to measure a man’s worth by the sheen on his outer coat, not by what is within. But when they found I wanted to be paid for my work, their backs refused to bend; they treated me with a familiarity I could ill stomach. I overheard one of them, who had been a footman, say, “Oh, it’s Pocahontas, is it? let him wait.” And he bade his boy deliver the message to me.

“Wait, sir?” I cried, thrusting my head into his parlour, fuming. “I’m not accustomed to waiting, but I have heard you are.” And I strode out into Pall Mall in a mighty fluster.

And yet Mr. D. was in the right. I came to him, if not to ask a favour, at least to propose a bargain; and surely it was my business to wait his convenience. In more fortunate days I asked his pardon, and the kind author of the Muse in Livery was instantly appeased.
