While the burgomaster exposed Gerard to ridicule in Tergou, Margaret faced a quiet, yet profound distress at Sevenbergen. It was the sorrow of a housewife, deeper perhaps than words could easily capture. She approached Martin Wittenhaagen, the elderly soldier, her eyes wet with tears.

"Martin, there is nothing left in the house," she whispered. "Gerard is coming, and he is so careless. He forgets to eat at home. Once his labor ceases, he rushes straight to me, poor soul, often arriving faint from hunger. And to think I have nothing to offer my beloved servant!"

Martin scratched his head, perplexed. "What can I do?"

"It is Thursday," Margaret replied, her voice trembling slightly. "It is your day to hunt. Truly, I counted on you today."

"Nay," the soldier answered, shaking his head. "I may not shoot when the Duke or his companions are out on the chase. Read this, if you wish to understand why. I am no scholar."

He reached into his pouch and produced a parchment bearing a grand seal. It was a stipend and license granted by Philip, Duke of Burgundy, to Martin Wittenhaagen, one of his archers, in recognition of his wartime service and a wound sustained at the Duke's side. The document awarded him an annual stipend of four merks, payable by the Duke's almoner, alongside a license to shoot three arrows once a week—specifically on Thursdays—in any of the Duke's forests in Holland. However, strict conditions applied: he could only hunt for a seven-year-old buck or a doe carrying a fawn, and only if the Duke himself was not hunting that day, nor were any of his friends. To violate these terms meant forfeiting his salary, risking his life, and paying a fine of a penny.

Margaret sighed, falling silent.

"Come, cheer up, mistress," Martin said gently. "For your sake, I will risk my carcass; I have done so for many others who were not worth your little finger. Besides, it is no great danger. I shall merely step into the edge of the forest. There is a good chance they will drive a hare or a fawn within range of my arrow."

"Well," Margaret hesitated, "if I allow you to go, you must promise me not to stray far and not to be seen. It is far better for Gerard to go supperless than for harm to come to you, faithful Martin."

With the promise secured, Martin took his bow and three arrows, slipping cautiously into the woods, barely a furlong away. Faint horns echoed in the distance, signaling that the game was already stirring.

"Come," Martin thought, "I shall soon fill the pot, and no one will be the wiser."

He positioned himself behind a thick oak overlooking an open glade, drawing his bow—a formidable instrument crafted from English yew, standing six feet two inches tall and thick in proportion. Martin, broad-chested with arms like iron and cord, had handled this bow since infancy. He could draw a three-foot arrow to its full length; when released, the arrow flew so fast the eye could hardly track it, and the bowstring sang with a music akin to a harp. This weapon had claimed the lives of many sturdy soldiers during the wars of the Hoecks and Cabbel-jaws. In those days, battlefields were not shrouded in clouds of smoke; combatants were few, yet the casualties were numerous, for they fought with clear sight and purpose. Fewer bloodless arrows flew then than the bloodless bullets of modern times.

A hare cantered into view, then sat alertly, its ears forming a perfect 'V'. Martin leveled his massive weapon. The arrow flew, the string twanged, but Martin, eager to secure the kill, had rushed his shot. He missed by an inch. The arrow appeared to strike the hare but actually grazed the ground beside it, passing beneath her belly like a flash before vanishing into the short grass. The hare leaped three feet vertically and bolted at full speed.

"Bungler!" Martin muttered.

This was proof enough that he was not an habitual bungler; otherwise, he would have blamed the hare. He had scarcely fitted another arrow when a wood-pigeon settled directly on the branch above him.

"Aha!" he thought. "You are small, but dainty."

This time, he proceeded with care. He drew the arrow deliberately and loosed it smoothly. To all appearances, the shaft went cleanly through the bird, sending feathers flying skyward like dust. Yet, instead of dropping at his feet, the pigeon, its breast torn rather than pierced, fluttered weakly away. With a final, great effort, it rose above the canopy, flew fifty yards, and fell dead—but where, hidden by the dense foliage, Martin could not tell.

"Luck is against me," he said despondently.

He fitted another arrow and scanned the glade intently. Soon, he heard a commotion behind him and turned just in time to see a noble buck crossing the open space, but it was too late to aim. He slammed his bow to the ground in frustration.

At that exact moment, a long-spotted animal glided swiftly across the path after the deer; its belly nearly touched the ground as it moved. Martin snatched up his bow instantly. He recognized the Duke's leopard.

"The hunters will not be far behind her," he realized. "I must not be seen. Gerard must go supperless tonight."

He plunged into the woods, following the tracks of the buck and the leopard, which led toward his home. Not far in, he heard an unusual noise ahead—the violent rustling of leaves and the heavy trampling of the ground. Hurrying forward, he discovered the leopard atop the buck, tearing at its flesh with teeth and claws. The deer ran in frantic circles, bounding convulsively as blood poured down its hide.

Then, Martin made a desperate resolve to provide venison for Margaret. Drawing his arrow to the head, he drove it deep into the deer. Despite the predator on its back, the buck leaped high into the air and collapsed dead. The leopard continued to tear at the carcass as if nothing had changed. Martin hoped the beast would gorge itself until it slept, allowing him to claim the meat.

After waiting several minutes, he walked resolutely forward and placed his hand on the buck's leg. The leopard emitted a frightful growl and ceased sucking the blood. She regarded Martin with suspicion, sulky and guarded. What was to be done? Martin recalled that wild creatures could not withstand the steady gaze of a human. Standing erect, he fixed his eyes upon the beast. The leopard returned a savage stare, refusing to break contact.

As Martin continued to hold her gaze, the leopard, brutally ignorant of natural law, lunged at his head with a terrifying yell, her eyes blazing, jaws gaping wide, and muscles distended. He had just enough time to seize her by the throat before her teeth could crush his face. One of her claws grabbed his shoulder, ripping the fabric, while the other, aimed at his cheek, would have been fatal. Martin was old-fashioned and wore no hat, only a scapulary of the same material as his jerkin. He had thrown this over his head like a hood, and the brute's claw snagged in the loose leather.

Martin struggled desperately to keep her teeth from his face, gripping her throat with iron strength, while she tore at his shoulder. It felt like blunt reaping hooks grinding and shredding flesh. The pain was excruciating, yet instead of breaking him, it ignited his blood. He gnashed his teeth in rage almost as fierce as hers, squeezing her neck with unyielding force.

Two pairs of eyes flared at one another—the man's nearly as furious as the beast's. Realizing she was being throttled, the leopard made a wild attempt to free herself, dragging his cowl across his face and blinding him, while tearing her claw completely out of his shoulder, taking flesh and bone with it. Still, he maintained his grip with hand and arm of iron.

Presently, her long tail, previously held high, dropped.

"Aha!" cried Martin, joyfully, tightening his grasp like death. Next, her body lost its elasticity; he held a choked, powerless mass. He gripped it firmly until all motion ceased, then dashed her to the earth. Panting, he removed his cowl. The leopard lay mute at his feet, tongue protruding, with a bloody paw. For the first time, terror seized Martin.

"I am a dead man," he thought. "I have slain the Duke's leopard."

Hastily, he gathered a handful of leaves and threw them over the carcass. Shouldering the buck, he staggered away, leaving a trail of mingled blood—his own and the deer's.

He burst into Peter's house a horrifying figure, bleeding and stained, and flung the deer's carcass onto the floor.

"There," he grunted, ignoring everything else. "Broil me a steak on it, for I am faint."

Margaret did not notice his wounds; she assumed the blood belonged entirely to the deer. Busy at the fire, the stout soldier bandaged his own injury while Gerard and Margaret prepared the meal. Soon, the three of them dined royally on roasted venison. They were exceedingly merry. Gerard, displaying remarkable thoughtfulness, had brought a flask of Schiedam wine. Under its influence, Martin recovered and recounted the tale of how the venison was obtained. They all laughed heartily over the exploit.
