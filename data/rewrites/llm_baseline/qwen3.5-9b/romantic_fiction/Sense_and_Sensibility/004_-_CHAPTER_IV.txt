"What a pity it is, Elinor," said Marianne, "that Edward should have no taste for drawing."

"No taste for drawing!" replied Elinor. "Why should you think so? He does not draw himself, indeed, but he takes great pleasure in viewing the works of others, and I assure you he is by no means deficient in natural taste, though he lacks the opportunity to refine it. If he had ever been able to study, I believe he would have become quite proficient. He simply trusts his own judgment so little in these matters that he is invariably reluctant to offer an opinion on any painting; yet, he possesses an innate propriety and simplicity of taste that generally guides him perfectly aright."

Marianne, fearing she might offend, fell silent on the subject. However, the sort of approval Elinor claimed Edward felt toward other artists' work was worlds away from the rapturous delight that, in Marianne's view, constituted true taste. Still, though she smiled inwardly at her sister's error, she honored Marianne for the blind devotion to Edward that caused it.

"I hope, Marianne," continued Elinor, "you do not consider him lacking in general taste. Indeed, I daresay you cannot, for your behavior toward him is perfectly cordial. Were that not the case, I am certain you could never treat him with civility."

Marianne hardly knew what to reply. She would not wound her sister's feelings on any account, yet stating what she did not truly believe was impossible. Finally, she answered: "Do not be offended, Elinor, if my praise of him falls short of your assessment of his merits. I have not had as many opportunities to gauge the finer nuances of his mind, his inclinations, and his tastes as you have; but I hold the highest opinion in the world of his goodness and sense. I consider him everything that is worthy and amiable."

"I am sure," replied Elinor, with a smile, "that his dearest friends could not be dissatisfied with such commendation. I do not see how you could express yourself more warmly."

Marianne was delighted to find her sister so easily pleased.

"As for his sense and his goodness," continued Elinor, "no one can possibly be in doubt regarding them who has spent enough time engaging him in open conversation. The excellence of his understanding and his principles can be hidden only by that shyness which too often renders him silent. You know him well enough to appreciate his solid worth. As for those 'finer nuances,' as you term them, you have, due to peculiar circumstances, remained more ignorant of them than I have. He and I have occasionally spent considerable time together, whereas you have been entirely absorbed, on the most affectionate grounds, by my mother. I have observed him closely, studied his sentiments, and listened to his opinions on subjects of literature and taste. Upon the whole, I venture to declare that his mind is well-informed, his enjoyment of books profound, his imagination lively, his observations just and correct, and his taste delicate and pure. His abilities in every respect grow more impressive with familiarity, just as his manners and appearance do. At first glance, his address is certainly not striking, and his features can hardly be called handsome until one perceives the uncommon goodness of his eye expression and the general sweetness of his countenance. Now that I know him so well, I think him truly handsome—or at least, almost so. What say you, Marianne?"

"I shall very soon think him handsome, Elinor, if I do not already. When you tell me to love him as a brother, I shall no more see imperfection in his face than I now do in his heart."

Elinor started at this declaration and regretted the warmth she had inadvertently shown in speaking of him. She felt that Edward held a very high place in her estimation. She believed the regard to be mutual, yet she required greater certainty before finding Marianne's conviction of their attachment agreeable. She knew that with Marianne and her mother, what was wished for one moment was believed the next; for them, to wish was to hope, and to hope was to expect.

She endeavored to explain the reality of the situation to her sister. "I do not attempt to deny," she said, "that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem, and that I like him."

Here, Marianne burst forth with indignation. "Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh! Worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed of being otherwise! Use those words again, and I will leave the room this very instant."

Elinor could not help laughing. "Excuse me," she said. "Be assured that I meant no offense to you by speaking, in such a quiet manner, of my own feelings. Believe them to be stronger than I have declared; believe them, in short, to be such as his merit, and the suspicion—and perhaps the hope—of his affection for me may warrant, without imprudence or folly. But go no further than this in your belief. I am by no means assured of his regard for me. There are moments when the extent of it seems doubtful; and until his sentiments are fully known, you cannot wonder at my wishing to avoid encouraging my own partiality by believing or calling it more than it is. In my heart, I feel little—scarcely any—doubt of his preference. But there are other points to consider besides his inclination. He is very far from being independent. What his mother truly is we cannot know; but from Fanny's occasional mentions of her conduct and opinions, we have never been inclined to think her amiable. And I am very much mistaken if Edward is not himself aware that there would be many difficulties in his path if he were to wish to marry a woman who lacked either a great fortune or high rank."

Marianne was astonished to discover how much the imaginations of her mother and herself had outrun the truth.

"And you really are not engaged to him!" she exclaimed. "Yet it certainly soon will happen. But two advantages will arise from this delay. *I* shall not lose you so soon, and Edward will have greater opportunity to improve that natural taste for your favorite pursuit, which must be indispensable to your future happiness. Oh! If he should be so far stimulated by your genius as to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!"

Elinor had given her true opinion to her sister. She could not consider Marianne's partiality for Edward in such a prosperous state as Marianne believed. There were, at times, a lack of spirits about him which, if it did not indicate indifference, spoke of something almost as unpromising. A doubt of her regard, assuming he felt it, need not trouble him more than mere anxiety. It would not likely produce that deep dejection of mind that frequently attended him. A more reasonable cause might be found in his dependent situation, which forbade the indulgence of his affection. She knew that his mother neither conducted herself in a way to make his current home comfortable nor to give him any assurance that he might build a home for himself without strictly adhering to her views for his advancement. With such knowledge, it was impossible for Elinor to feel at ease. She was far from relying on the outcome of his preference for her, which her mother and sister still considered certain. Nay, the longer they remained together, the more doubtful the nature of his regard appeared; and sometimes, for a few painful minutes, she believed it to be nothing more than friendship.

But whatever its true limits might be, it was enough, once perceived by his sister, to make her uneasy, and at the same time—which was even more common—to make her uncivil. Seizing the first opportunity, she affronted her mother-in-law on the matter, speaking to her so expressively of her son's great expectations, of Mrs. Ferrars's resolution that both her sons should marry well, and of the dangers facing any young woman who attempted to *draw him in*, that Mrs. Dashwood could neither pretend to be unaware nor strive to remain calm. She gave an answer that marked her contempt and instantly left the room, resolving that, regardless of the inconvenience or expense of such a sudden move, her beloved Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations.

In this state of spirits, a letter arrived from the post, containing a proposal particularly well-timed. It was an offer of a small house on very favorable terms, belonging to a relative of her own, a gentleman of consequence and property in Devonshire. The letter came directly from him and was written in the true spirit of friendly accommodation. He understood she was in need of a dwelling; although the house he now offered was merely a cottage, he assured her that everything necessary would be done to it if the location pleased her. He earnestly urged her, along with her daughters, to visit Barton Park, his own residence, from where she could judge for herself whether Barton Cottage, since the houses were in the same parish, could, through alterations, be made comfortable for her. He seemed genuinely anxious to accommodate them, and the entire letter was written in such a friendly style that it could not fail to please his cousin, especially at a moment when she was suffering under the cold and unfeeling behavior of her closer relations.

She needed no time for deliberation or inquiry. Her resolution was formed as she read. The situation of Barton, in a county so far distant from Sussex as Devonshire, which but a few hours prior would have been a sufficient objection to outweigh every possible advantage, became its first recommendation.
