Time, that relentless editor, has moved our characters forward by a month. In this interim, a remarkable convergence of destiny has drawn the scattered figures of our story back to the little country town where they first made their entrance. Among these returning souls is Frederic Lightfoot, formerly the humble *maitre d'hôtel* to Sir Francis Clavering, now transformed into a proprietor of some standing. He has issued a bold manifesto to the nobility and gentry of the shire, announcing his takeover of "the Clavering Arms."

"This ancient and well-established house," proclaims Mr. Lightfoot, "has been repaired and decorated in a style of the greatest comfort. Gentlemen hunting with the Dumplingbeare hounds will find excellent stabling. A commodious billiard-room has been attached, and the cellars stocked with the choicest wines, selected without regard to expense by C.L. Commercial gentlemen will find this a most comfortable resort, and the scale of charges has been regulated to suit the economical spirit of the times."

Indeed, the old inn breathes a new vitality. The gateway has been splashed with fresh paint, the coffee-room windows glow with Christmas holly, and the magistrates have convened their petty sessions within the card-room of the old Assembly Hall. The farmers' ordinary draws increased crowds, enchanted by Mrs. Lightfoot's cuisine, particularly her Indian curries and Mulligatawny soup, which have earned high praise from Major Stokes, Captain Glanders, and other local gentry. These culinary triumphs were savored not only in private but at the grand dinner celebrating the incorporation of the Clavering Institute, chaired by Sir Francis Clavering himself, with the Reverend Dr. Portman as supporter and the enterprising Barker, Esq., filling the vice-chair. Barker, the head of the local ribbon factory and director of the impending Great Western Railway branch, represents the industrial future of this bustling little town.

Meanwhile, the local press—the *Chatteris Champion*, *Clavering Agriculturist*, and *Baymouth Fisherman*—offers a tantalizing glimpse into the social whirlwind awaiting the residents. They report that a significant shift in the romantic landscape of our talented townsman, Arthur Pendennis, Esq., has seen him abandon his candidacy for the borough. Rumours whisper, with the usual fervour, that Sir Francis Clavering, whose failing health may necessitate his resignation from Parliament, intends to vacate his seat in favour of a young gentleman of colossal fortune and high aristocratic lineage. This suitor is poised to wed an accomplished lady closely tied to the Clavering Park estate. Lady Clavering and Miss Amory have already arrived for the Christmas holidays, promising festivities of a peculiarly interesting nature at the commencement of the new year.

For the ingenious reader, these announcements serve as a map to the events transpiring behind the scenes. While Lady Rockminster grumbled initially at Laura's preference for Pendennis over Bluebeard, those privy to the latter's secrets understand that Laura could make no other choice. The kind old lady, acting as Miss Bell's guardian, was far from displeased at the prospect of fulfilling the ultimate purpose of young ladies: marriage. She confided in her maid, Mrs. Beck, who, being perpetually in the loop thanks to Martha of Fairoaks, was immensely delighted. "Mr. Pendennis's income is so substantial," Mrs. Beck noted, calculating the railway dividends, "and Miss Bell has her dowry. They will manage very well. I shall speak to my nephew, George Pynsent, Chief Secretary of the Tape and Sealing Wax Office, and ensure Mr. Pendennis receives a suitable position."

Thus, at one o'clock, the Dowager Lady Rockminster appeared at Major Pendennis's residence. The Major, a man of honour, was delighted to receive such a noble visitor, though he had been braced for the news that Pen's engagement to Miss Amory had been severed. He recalled his uncle for the first time that day, meeting his new servant, Mr. Frosch, in the hall. Before retreating to the hotel coffee-room to pen a half-dozen lines to his guardian, he sought the Major's health. His letter to the Major read: *"Dear uncle, if there has been any question between us, it is over now. I went to Tunbridge Wells yesterday and found that somebody else had carried off the prize... Miss A., without any compunction, has bestowed herself upon Harry Foker, with his fifteen thousand a year."*

Upon receiving this dispatch, the Major smiled with courtly benevolence. "It was a very great kindness of your Ladyship to bring me the news," he declared. "How well you look! How very good you are!" He admitted that, despite his initial wish for the union, he did not regret its failure, noting the financial improvement in Pen's fortunes. "We must console him," Lady Rockminster urged gently. "We must get him a wife."

The truth finally dawned on the Major: Lady Rockminster had arrived as an ambassador for Laura. The negotiations proved tolerably easy. There could be no valid objection to Pen marrying according to his and his mother's wishes. Lady Rockminster bolstered the agreement with intimations of profound weight, though we shall not detail them lest we offend the living memory of the Countess. Overcome by the old lady's determined graciousness and her evident fondness for Laura, the Major relented. Indeed, nothing was more bland and kind than her demeanour, save for one fleeting moment when she spoke of Pen "throwing himself away." At that instant, she revealed, with a humility acknowledged by Pen's friends, that Laura was a thousand times too good for him—a paragon of virtue fit to be the wife of a king.

When the Major, instantly smitten by the Countess's admiration for Miss Bell, received Laura, who appeared blushing and radiant, he extended a shaky hand to both the couple with unaffected cordiality. The scene was one of happy blushes and beams of love. The storyteller, turning from this tender tableau, hopes that one day the eyes of his young audience may shine with similar light.

With Pen retreated in friendship and the lovely Blanche having bestowed her affections upon Harry Foker, a blaze of happiness erupted in Lady Clavering's household. The good Begum and Blanche were on the most delightful terms. The ardent Foker, pressing onwards the happy day, was eager to abridge the mourning period that had placed him in possession of Blanche's charms. Lady Clavering, buoyed by this joy, descended from Tunbridge, and a reconciliation ensued between her and Sir Francis. Sir Francis returned to London, sat at the head of his own table, and resumed his habits at the billiard-rooms and gambling-houses, appearing flushed with money once more.

One day, as Major Pendennis and Arthur dined at Grosvenor Place, they encountered an old acquaintance serving as major-domo. The gentleman in black, offering sweet or dry champagne with perfect gravity, was none other than Mr. James Morgan. Present also was the Chevalier Strong, in high spirits, recounting his amusements abroad. Under his breath, Strong murmured to Arthur, "It was my Lady who invited me. That fellow Morgan looked as black as thunder when I came in. He is about no good here. I will go away first, and wait for you and Major Pendennis at Hyde Park Gate."

As Morgan assisted the departing Major with his great-coat, he muttered something about accepting a temporary engagement with the Clavering family. Arthur overheard the sentence and caught the look of hatred accompanying it. Excusing himself to retrieve a forgotten handkerchief, he returned to the drawing-room. Blanche was still there, lingering about her siren. Pen gave her a look full of meaning, and the siren, understanding the silent code, beckoned him out.

"What is it?" she asked, shutting the door. "Have you told Harry? Do you know that villain Morgan knows all?"

"I know it," Blanche replied. "Have you told Harry?"

"No, no," she whispered, "you won't betray me?"

"Morgan will," Pen said sternly.

"No, he won't," Blanche insisted. "I have promised him—*n'importe*. Wait until after our marriage..." Suddenly, the smiles vanished. "Oh, how wretched I am," she cried.

Arthur implored her, "Tell him now. It is no fault of yours. He will pardon you anything."

"And give her this—" Blanche handed him a note, "—*Il est la*—with my love. Please. If she will be at Madame Crinoline's at half-past three, and if Lady Rockminster can spare her, I should so like to drive with her in the park." She departed, singing and kissing her little hand, as Morgan the velvet-footed ascended the carpeted stair.

Pen heard Blanche's piano break into brilliant music as he joined his uncle. "What was to be done?" Arthur asked.

"What is to be done, begad?" the old gentleman sighed. "Leave it alone. Be thankful that we are out of the business." Yet Pen hoped to heaven she would tell Harry.

"Miss Amory is a dev'lish wide-awake girl," the Major observed. "She must play her own cards."
