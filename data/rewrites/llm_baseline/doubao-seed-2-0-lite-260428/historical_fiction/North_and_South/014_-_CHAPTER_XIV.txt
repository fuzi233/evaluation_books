“I was used
To sleep at nights as sweetly as a child,—
Now if the wind blew rough, it made me start,
And think of my poor boy tossing about
Upon the roaring seas. And then I seemed
To feel that it was hard to take him from me
For such a little fault.”

It was a small, soft comfort for Margaret Hale in those raw early Milton days to find her mother drawing closer to her with a tender, unguarded intimacy she had not known since Margaret’s childhood. Mrs Hale took her daughter to her heart at last as the trusted confidante Margaret had ached to be for years—long envying Dixon the small, affectionate private talks the housekeeper had always shared with her mistress. Margaret tended to every small call for sympathy her mother made, no matter how trivial the worry, with deliberate care: she fussed over draughts at the window, listened to rants about bad butter and unreliable tradesmen, and checked the flue for sparks at every gust of wind, just as an elephant, unaware of the tiny pin at his feet, will lift it gently when his keeper bids him. She did not know then that this quiet devotion was drawing her close to a moment of raw, unvarnished truth she had long yearned for.

One evening, when Mr Hale was out visiting a distant parishioner, the howl of the gale down their narrow street made Mrs Hale shiver, and she began to speak of Frederick— the subject Margaret had dared never ask after plainly, the one topic that made her naturally open tongue turn shy. The more she hungered for details of her exiled brother, the more afraid she had been to draw them out.

“Oh Margaret, that was a terrible wind last night, wasn’t it? It howled right down our bedroom chimney, loud enough to rattle the bed hangings. I never can sleep through winds like that. I got into the habit of waking fitfully when poor Frederick was at sea, and even now, if I don’t jolt straight awake at once, I dream of him stuck in the middle of a storm: the great glass-green walls of waves rearing on either side of his ship, higher than her very masts, curling over with cruel white foam like some gigantic crested serpent. It is an old, old dream, but it always comes back on windy nights, till I wake sitting bolt upright in bed, stiff with terror, my heart hammering. Poor Frederick! He’s on land now, so wind can do him no harm. Though I did half fear those tall Milton factory chimneys might topple right over in the gusts.”

“Where is he now, mamma?” Margaret asked softly. “I know all our letters are addressed care of Messrs Barbour in Cadiz, but I’ve never known the name of the place he’s hiding in.”

“I can’t keep the Spanish name straight in my head, but he doesn’t go by Hale, you must never forget that. Every letter he sends has F.D. scribbled in the corner of the envelope, and he uses the name Dickenson. I wanted him to take Beresford, my maiden name, the one he had a sort of family right to, but your father said it was far too risky. Someone might recognise the name, and trace it straight back to us.”

“Mamma,” Margaret said, “I was staying with Aunt Shaw when all that happened, and I was far too young to be told the full truth. But I’m old enough now. May I hear the whole story, if it doesn’t hurt you too much to speak of it?”

“Pain? No,” Mrs Hale replied, her cheek flushing hot with a mix of pride and grief. “It only hurts to think I may never see my darling boy again. But he did right, Margaret. They can say what they like in the papers, in the courts, but I have his own letters. I will believe my son before any court-martial on earth. Go to my little black japan cabinet by the window, dear, second drawer on the left, and you will find a packet of his letters tied up with blue silk ribbon.”

Margaret fetched them immediately. They were yellowed at the edges, stained with brine, carrying that sharp, salty, peculiar scent all letters that have spent months at sea carry. Mrs Hale untied the ribbon with trembling fingers, flipping through the dates as she passed them to Margaret, chattering out hurried, anxious little asides before her daughter could even finish reading the first line.

“You see, Margaret, he disliked Captain Reid from the very first day he stepped on board the Orion, his first ship as a midshipman. Poor little fellow, he looked so fine in his uniform, his dirk at his hip, slashing open every newspaper that came his way like it was nothing more than a paper knife. But Mr Reid, as he was then, took an instant dislike to the boy. Later, when Frederick was assigned to the Russell, he found his old enemy Reid made captain. He swore he would bear all the man’s tyranny patiently, you know. Look, that letter right there—he says ‘my father may rely upon me, that I will bear with all proper patience everything that one officer and gentleman can take from another. But from my former knowledge of my present captain, I confess I look forward with apprehension to a long course of tyranny on board the Russell.’ He meant it too. He was the sweetest-tempered boy alive, when no one was pushing him too far. That’s the letter where he writes about Reid flying into rages because the new crew of the Russell couldn’t run through their manoeuvres as fast as the Avenger’s men, isn’t it? He says the Russell was full of raw new hands, while the Avenger had been on station for three years straight, chasing slavers and working their crew till they scrambled up and down the rigging like rats.”

Margaret read the faded, half-illegible scrawl slowly. It was easy to see how Frederick, still hot from the scene he described, might have exaggerated Captain Reid’s petty cruelties a little. Reid had ordered the men aloft in the main-topsail rigging to race back down, threatening to flog the last man down with the cat-o’-nine tails. One sailor, stuck far out on the spar, knew he could never outpace the men ahead of him, and too terrified to bear the shame of a public beating, he threw himself wildly to catch a lower rope, missed, and fell senseless to the deck. He died only a few hours later, and the whole crew was seething with rage by the time Frederick sat down to write.

“We never got that letter till long, long after we saw the first news of the mutiny,” Mrs Hale said, her voice cracking. “Poor Fred, it must have been a comfort to write it, even when he didn’t know how he could ever get it sent home. Long before his letter arrived, we saw the notice in the papers: a brutal, atrocious mutiny had broken out on the Russell, the mutineers had seized the ship and were thought to have turned pirate, and Captain Reid had been set adrift in an open boat with a handful of loyal officers, all of whose names were printed, because they’d been picked up by a West Indian steamer. Oh Margaret! Your father and I turned sick going down that list, over and over, because Frederick Hale’s name wasn’t there. We thought it had to be a misprint. Fred was such a good, kind boy, only a little hot-headed. We hoped the ‘Carr’ printed in the list of mutineers was a typo for Hale—newspapers are so careless, after all. The next day, your father set off at dusk to walk all the way to Southampton to get the late post and the new papers, and I couldn’t bear to wait at home, so I set off to meet him. I sat down under the hedge by the Oldham beech-tree to wait, and he came at last, hours late, his arms hanging loose at his sides, his head sunk, dragging his feet like every step cost him all his strength. I can see him right now, as plain as day.”

“Don’t go on, mamma,” Margaret whispered, leaning against her mother’s side to kiss her hand. “I can guess the rest.”

“No you can’t. No one can who wasn’t there. I could barely stand up to go to him, the whole world was spinning around me. He didn’t even speak, or seem surprised to find me three miles from home in the dark. He just tucked my arm in his, and stroked my hand over and over, like he was trying to soothe me to stay quiet through some terrible blow. When I shook so hard I couldn’t speak, he pulled me into his arms, rested his head on mine, and began to shake with that strange, muffled, groaning cry that scared me so still I could only beg him to tell me what he had heard. Then he handed me that wicked newspaper, all crumpled at the edges, that called our Frederick a ‘traitor of the blackest dye,’ a ‘base ungrateful disgrace to his profession.’ I can’t even repeat all the vile names they called him. I tore that paper to tiny bits, Margaret. I tore it with my teeth, I think. I didn’t cry. My cheeks were burning hot as fire, my eyes stung like they were full of smoke. I told him it was all lies, and it was. Months later, Fred’s letter finally arrived, and you see what provocation he had. He didn’t rebel for his own wrongs. He spoke up for the poor, helpless sailors Reid was torturing to death, and the rest of the crew all stuck by him.” She paused for a long minute, her voice thin and trembling, exhausted from the weight of the memory. “I’m glad of it, you know. I’m prouder of Frederick standing up against injustice than I would ever be if he’d just been a quiet, obedient, unremarkable officer.”

“I am sure I am too,” Margaret said firmly. “Loyalty to wise, just authority is a fine thing, but it is far finer to defy arbitrary, cruel power, not for your own sake, but for the sake of others who cannot defend themselves.”

“Even so, I wish I could see him just once more. He was my first baby, Margaret.” Mrs Hale spoke wistfully, almost as if apologising for the hungry, yearning wish, as if it were a slight to Margaret, the child who was still here. But the thought never crossed Margaret’s mind. She was already wondering if there was any way that wish could come true.

“It’s six or seven years now, mamma. Would they still prosecute him if he came home and stood his trial? Surely he could bring forward evidence to show all the terrible provocation he had?”

“It would do no good,” Mrs Hale sighed, beginning to cry for the first time all evening. “Some of the sailors who went with Fred were caught, and court-martialled on board the Amicia. I believed every word of their defence, it matched Fred’s story word for word. But it didn’t matter. They were hanged at the yard-arm. And the worst part of it all? The court, when they sentenced them to death, said all those poor men had been led astray from their duty by their superior officer—by Frederick.”

They sat in heavy, thick silence for a long time after that.

“So he was in South America for several years, wasn’t he?” Margaret asked at last.

“Yes. Now he’s in Spain, near Cadiz somewhere. If he ever sets foot in England, he will be hanged. I shall never see his face again.” Mrs Hale turned her face to the wall, and lay perfectly still, sunk in the old, familiar despair of a mother who has lost her son to a cruel, unforgiving law. She pulled her hand gently out of Margaret’s, almost impatient for the quiet to be left alone with her memories of Fred. When Mr Hale came home an hour later, Margaret slipped out of the house, her chest tight with unnameable gloom, and could not see a single glimmer of light anywhere on the dark, foggy horizon.
