Elinor remained paralyzed by a cacophony of conflicting thoughts, a mental storm where sorrow was the only constant outcome, rendering her incapable of attending to her sister. Just thirty minutes prior, she had despised Willoughby as the epitome of male worthlessness; now, despite his glaring flaws, a profound pity for the suffering they caused had taken root. She viewed him as eternally severed from her family, feeling a tenderness and regret that weighed heavily on her conscience—not necessarily proportional to his virtues, but undeniably aligned with his desperate wishes. She recognized that his hold on her mind was fueled by factors reason demanded she ignore: his magnetic personal charm, his open and vivacious demeanor, and the burning, albeit morally questionable, passion he held for Marianne. Yet, she knew these influences were entrenched; long before she could logically dismiss them, the emotional imprint was permanent.

When she finally returned to the bedside of the unconscious Marianne, she found her awakening, revitalized by a sleep of such duration and sweetness that it matched the height of Elinor's own hopes. Elinor's heart swelled with emotion. The convergence of the past, the present, the future, Willoughby's departure, Marianne's safety, and her mother's imminent arrival created a surge of agitation that banished all signs of exhaustion, leaving her terrified only of exposing her inner turmoil to her sister. However, this fear was short-lived. Within half an hour of Willoughby's exit, the sound of another carriage summoned her downstairs. Driven by a desperate need to spare her mother further agony, Elinor sprinted into the hall, reaching the outer door just in time to catch and steady her mother as she stumbled inside. Mrs. Dashwood, whose terror upon approaching the house had nearly convinced her that Marianne was dead, possessed no voice for inquiry, not even for Elinor. Instead, Elinor, offering neither greeting nor question, immediately voiced the joyful relief. Her mother seized this news with her characteristic fervor, instantly overwhelmed by happiness as she had previously been by dread. Supported between her daughter and her friend, Mrs. Dashwood shed tears of joy, embracing Elinor repeatedly before turning to press Colonel Brandon's hand with a gaze that simultaneously expressed gratitude and the certainty that he shared in their bliss. He shared it, though in a silence deeper than her own.

Once Mrs. Dashwood regained her composure, her sole desire was to see Marianne. In two minutes, she was beside her beloved child, rendered dearer than ever by the ordeal of absence, unhappiness, and danger. Elinor's delight at witnessing the reunion was tempered only by the fear that it might rob Marianne of further rest. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood displayed remarkable prudence when a child's life hung in the balance. Satisfied by her mother's presence and aware of her own weakness, Marianne readily submitted to the enforced silence and quiet prescribed by the surrounding nurses. Mrs. Dashwood insisted on staying awake with her throughout the night; Elinor, complying with her mother's plea, retired to her own bed. However, the rest deemed necessary after a sleepless night and hours of exhausting anxiety was thwarted by irritability. "Poor Willoughby," she now permitted herself to whisper, became a constant fixture in her thoughts. She desperately wanted to hear his defense, oscillating between blaming herself for her earlier harsh judgment and acquitting her own conscience. Yet, the promise to relate this to her sister remained a torment. She dreaded the revelation, fearing its impact on Marianne, doubting whether her sister could ever find happiness again, and momentarily wishing Willoughby a widow. Then, recalling Colonel Brandon, she chastised herself, recognizing that the reward of her sister's heart belonged far more to Brandon's sufferings and constancy than to his rival's. She wished for nothing more than Mrs. Willoughby's survival.

The shock of Colonel Brandon's mission at Barton had been significantly mitigated for Mrs. Dashwood by her own preceding alarm. So consumed was her worry for Marianne that she had already resolved to depart for Cleveland that very day, settling her journey before Brandon's arrival, expecting the Careys to fetch Margaret at any moment, as her mother refused to take her where infection might lurk. Marianne improved daily, and the radiant cheerfulness of Mrs. Dashwood's appearance and spirits testified to her claim of being one of the happiest women in the world. Elinor could not hear these declarations or witness their proof without occasionally wondering if her mother remembered Edward. But Mrs. Dashwood, trusting Elinor's temperate report of her own disappointment, was swept away by the exuberance of her joy to focus solely on what would amplify it. Marianne was restored to her from a peril in which, as she now realized, her own misguided judgment in fostering the disastrous attachment to Willoughby had played a part. Furthermore, in her recovery, Mrs. Dashwood discovered another source of joy unknown to Elinor.

"As soon as we are alone," she confided the moment privacy arose, "you do not yet know all my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself." Her daughter, feeling alternately pleased and pained, surprised and unsurprised, offered only silent attention. "You are never like me, dear Elinor, or I should marvel at your composure now. Had I sat down to wish for any possible good for my family, I would have fixed on Colonel Brandon marrying one of you as the most desirable outcome. And I believe Marianne will be the happier of the two." Elinor was half-inclined to ask for the reasoning, satisfied that no argument based on impartial considerations of age, character, or feeling could satisfy her mother; but since her mother was inevitably carried away by her imagination on any intriguing subject, Elinor dismissed the query with a smile.

"He opened his whole heart to me yesterday during our travels," Mrs. Dashwood continued. "It emerged quite unexpectedly, quite unintentionally. I, you may believe, could speak of nothing but my child; he could not conceal his distress. I saw it equaled my own. Perhaps, thinking that mere friendship, as the world currently defines it, would not justify such warm sympathy—or perhaps not thinking at all—he yielded to irresistible feelings and made me acquainted with his earnest, tender, constant affection for Marianne. He has loved her, my Elinor, since the first moment he saw her." Here, Elinor perceived not the actual words or professions of Colonel Brandon, but the natural embellishments of her mother's active fancy, fashioning every detail to her liking. "His regard for her, infinitely surpassing anything Willoughby ever felt or feigned, as much warmer as it is more sincere or constant—however we choose to label it—has persisted through all knowledge of dear Marianne's unhappy infaturation with that worthless young man! Without selfishness, without encouraging false hope! Could he have seen her happy with another? Such a noble mind! Such openness, such sincerity! No one can be deceived by him."

"Colonel Brandon's character," Elinor stated, "as an excellent man, is well established." "I know it is," her mother replied seriously, "or after such a warning, I should be the last to encourage such affection, or even to be pleased by it. But his coming for me as he did, with such active, such ready friendship, is sufficient proof that he is one of the worthiest of men." "His character, however," Elinor countered, "does not rest on a single act of kindness, to which his affection for Marianne, were humanity absent, would have prompted him. To Mrs. Jennings, to the Middletons, he has been long and intimately known; they equally love and respect him. Even my own knowledge of him, though recently acquired, is considerable. I value and esteem him so highly that if Marianne can be happy with him, I shall be as ready as yourself to consider our connection the greatest blessing in the world. What answer did you give him? Did you allow him to hope?"

"Oh! my love, I could not then speak of hope to him or to myself. Marianne might have been dying at that moment. But he did not ask for hope or encouragement. His was an involuntary confidence, an irrepressible effusion to a soothing friend, not an application to a parent. Yet, after a time, I did say—for at first I was completely overcome—that if she lived, as I trusted she might, my greatest happiness would lie in promoting their marriage. Since our arrival, since our delightful security, I have repeated it to him more fully, giving him every encouragement in my power. 'Time,' I tell him, 'will do everything; Marianne's heart is not to be wasted forever on such a man as Willoughby. His own merits must soon secure it.'"

"To judge from the Colonel's spirits, however, you have not yet made him equally sanguine." "No. He believes Marianne's affection is too deeply rooted for any change under a great length of time, and even supposing her heart becomes free again, he is too diffident of himself to believe that, with such a difference in age and disposition, he could ever attach her. There, however, he is quite mistaken. His age is only slightly beyond hers, serving as an advantage by making his character and principles fixed; and his disposition, I am convinced, is exactly the one to make your sister happy. Moreover, his person and manners are all in his favor. My partiality does not blind me; he certainly is not as handsome as Willoughby; but at the same time, there is something much more pleasing in his countenance.
