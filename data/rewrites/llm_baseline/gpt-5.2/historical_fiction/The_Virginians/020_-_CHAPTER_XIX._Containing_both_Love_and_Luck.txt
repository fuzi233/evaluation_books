At the next meal the long table was set as if for a little festival, though the day had been as common as any: the cloth newly whitened, the napkins folded to stiff little peaks, the silver giving back the candlelight in soft, steady glints. The house itself seemed to hold its breath; even the familiar sounds—the low clink of spoons, the subdued scrape of chairs upon the boards—came gentled, as though Castlewood knew it must listen.

Madame de Bernstein entered with a face from which the morning’s storm had vanished as wholly as smoke from a quenched pan. She was all graciousness. She praised the cook—praised him with that exactitude which is half compliment, half command—declaring the fricassee to be excellent, and insisting that there were no eels anywhere like those drawn from the Castlewood moats: sweet as milk, she said, and clean as if they had been rinsed in spring-water and not in old, green-sleeping ditches.

The dish itself was a kind of homely glory: eels laid in their collars, the flesh pearled and firm, the gelée trembling clear about bay-leaf and peppercorn; beside it the fricassee—white with cream and yolk, thickened to a satin that clung to the spoon, smelling of lemon-peel, mace, and a handful of parsley chopped so fine it might have been green dust. When somebody hinted the wine was corked, the Baroness would not hear of such extravagance as opening a fresh bottle “for a useless old woman like me.” She lifted her glass when the new wine came, and gave, with a bright, steady voice, “Madam Esmond Warrington, of Virginia,” as her toast; and then, with a look that held Harry as a hook holds a fish, expressed a hope that he had brought away his mamma’s permission to take an English wife back with him.

He could not have told afterward whether the wine was rough or smooth upon his tongue; the room was full of scent—hot gravy, wax candles, damp stone beyond the windows where the moat lay—and yet his senses, which ought to have been masters at table, ran all one way: to Maria’s presence, to the turn of her head, to the soft rustle when she moved her sleeve. Madame de Bernstein, with an easy current of talk, began to amuse them with stories of her dear mother—Colonel Esmond’s widow—of her beauty and goodness, of her happiness with her second husband though she was so much older than Colonel Esmond. To see them together, she said, had been delightful; their attachment had been talked of through the country. After such a marriage, what was the use, she asked, of prating about disparity?

My Lady Castlewood and her two children held their peace while the Baroness prattled on. Harry was enraptured, and Maria—who had expected thunder—sat surprised, schooling her face into politeness as a scholar copies a harsh hand into fair. Lord Castlewood watched his aunt with the puzzled composure of a man who hears an unfamiliar bird-call in his own orchard. He showed no doubt; his countenance wore the stamp of perfect satisfaction; but his mind, no doubt, was busy with the question: what scheme had put this honey upon her tongue?

Her good-humour flowed down the table as gravy runs to the lowest trench. Not one person escaped a gracious word. When Mr. Will, with the blunt wonder of youth, let fall an expression of satisfaction at her behaviour, she answered him frankly.

“Complimentary, my dear! Of course I am. I want to make up with you for having been exceedingly rude to everybody this morning. When I was a child, and my father and mother were alive, and lived here, I remember I used to adopt exactly the same behaviour. If I had been naughty in the morning, I used to try and coax my parents at night. I remember in this very room, at this very table—oh, ever so many hundred years ago!—so coaxing my father, and mother, and your grandfather, Harry Warrington; and there were eels for supper, as we have had them to-night, and it was that dish of collared eels which brought the circumstance back to my mind. I had been just as wayward that day, when I was seven years old, as I am to-day, when I am seventy, and so I confess my sins, and ask to be forgiven, like a good girl.”

“I absolve your ladyship!” cried the chaplain, who made one of the party.

“But your reverence does not know how cross and ill-tempered I was. I scolded my sister, Castlewood: I scolded her children, I boxed Harry Warrington’s ears: and all because he would not go with me to Tunbridge Wells.”

“But I will go, madam; I will ride with you with all the pleasure in life,” said Mr. Warrington, and though his voice kept itself steady, the blood rushed to his face hot as if he had bent too near the fire.

“You see, Mr. Chaplain, what good, dutiful children they all are. ’Twas I alone who was cross and peevish. Oh, it was cruel of me to treat them so! Maria, I ask your pardon, my dear.”

“Sure, madam, you have done me no wrong,” Maria answered; and her accent, soft and slightly old-fashioned, had in it that unstudied music which made Harry think, absurdly, of a country air remembered from childhood. Even in that one phrase—so plain, so dutiful—there lay a whole inheritance of speech, the little turns and cadences that belonged to her blood and place, and which no London assembly could teach or buy.

“Indeed, I have, a very great wrong, child! Because I was weary of myself, I told you that your company would be wearisome to me. You offered to come with me to Tunbridge, and I rudely refused you.”

“Nay, ma’am, if you were sick, and my presence annoyed you—”

“But it will not annoy me! You were most kind to say that you would come. I do, of all things, beg, pray, entreat, implore, command that you will come.”

My lord filled himself a glass and sipped it, looking as unconcerned as if the whole matter were nothing but talk about sauces. Yet the meaning was plain enough. This, then, had been the object of the morning’s violence: not sickness, not whim, but a separation.

“Anything which can give my aunt pleasure, I am sure, will delight me,” Maria said, trying to wear happiness as one wears an uncomfortable ribbon—neatly, and without showing the pin.

“You must come and stay with me, my dear, and I promise to be good and good-humoured. My dear lord, you will spare your sister to me?”

“Lady Maria Esmond is quite of age to judge for herself about such a matter,” Lord Castlewood replied, bowing; and then, with the politeness of a man who offers a dish he does not mean to taste, added, “If any of us can be of use to you, madam, you sure ought to command us.”

“Oh, Tunbridge will be delightful!” sighed Lady Maria.

“Mr. Sampson will go and see Goody Jones for you,” my lord continued.

Harry drew idle figures with his finger upon the table as if tracing patterns in flour. A moment before, his mind had been laying out future hours with the particularity of a cook planning a feast: walks that would end in sheltered arbours; rides through lanes where leaves would make a whispering roof; long talks that would go on as slowly and richly as a well-stirred custard; afternoons at the harpsichord with music-books open, the paper smelling faintly of dust and ink; moonlight on the shrubbery; that soft, dangerous ease with which two young persons, left alone, begin to believe the world exists only to serve their hearts.

Now, with a single sentence, it all fell, like a soufflé struck by a door flung wide.

“I knew it couldn’t be,” he thought. “I knew something would happen to take her from me.”

“But you will ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington, and keep us from the highwaymen?” said Madame de Bernstein.

He hoped none observed how red he grew. “Yes,” he managed, and tried to sound calm, “I will ride with your ladyships; and I am sure you need fear no danger.”

Danger!—he felt, in his folly, that he would welcome it as a man welcomes a hard crust when he is starving, because it is something that proves life. Ten thousand highwaymen might come; he would cut them down, or at least ride so close to Maria’s coach that he might catch her eyes when the curtains stirred. Perhaps at an inn he might have the blessed chance to offer his hand, to feel hers rest for an instant upon it—light as a moth, warm as bread newly lifted from the oven.

They would be two whole days going to Tunbridge. One day or two he might stay there. Is not a poor wretch with the halter already measured thankful even for a short reprieve? If ever love made a man ridiculous, it had made Harry Warrington so; and if a man, in that fever, speaks like an idiot, it is a cheap bravery to write down his folly and laugh. Some are gently sick with it, and recover. Others take the malady so deep that the scar shows through the skin to old age. If you have never brayed, then jeer; but if you have ever been long-eared in the same pasture, recognise a brother.

“You will stay with us for a day or two at the Wells,” Madame Bernstein continued. “You will see us put into our lodgings. Then you can return to Castlewood and the partridge-shooting, and all the fine things which you and my lord are to study together.”

Harry bowed assent. A whole week, then—some remnant of heaven.

“And as there is sure to be plenty of company at the Wells, I shall be able to present you,” the lady added with an air of benevolence.

“Company! ah! I shan’t need company,” sighed Harry; and then, recollecting himself, he hastened to say, “I mean—I shall be quite contented in the company of you two ladies.”

No doubt Mr. Will wondered at his cousin’s taste.

As this was to be the last night of Harry’s present visit to Castlewood, cousin Will proposed that he, and his reverence, and Warrington should meet in the latter’s quarters and make up their accounts: a practice which, as Harry had been a considerable winner, he did not refuse. When the ladies withdrew and my lord retired—as was his custom—to his own apartments, the three gentlemen assembled in Mr. Harry’s little room before the punch-bowl, which was Will’s usual midnight companion.

The bowl steamed with its sugared breath, sharp with citrus and spirit; the grated nutmeg upon the surface sent up a fragrant dust whenever the ladle moved. It was comfort of a coarse sort, such as men take when they will not confess to loneliness. Outside, the court lay quiet; the house’s great body settled into night-sounds: a distant door-latch, a footfall fading, somewhere a clock that ticked as evenly as a cook’s knife upon a board.

But Will’s method of settling accounts was by producing fresh packs of cards, and offering to submit Harry’s debt to the process of being doubled or acquitted. The chaplain had no more ready cash than Lord Castlewood’s younger brother; and Harry, for his part, wanted to win the money of neither. Why should he pain Maria’s brother, or leave room for any of her kin to say the Virginian was grasping? Yet if they insisted upon play, he would not be made a gull, either. He had learned—at home, in those games young men call harmless—that there is a kind of honour even in vice: to play fairly, and to pay.

“Up to midnight,” he said, “I will play for what stakes you name.” And so they fell to it: the cards shuffled and dealt, the dice-box rattled, the little room filling with that thin, nervous music of chance.

Very likely he did not think of the cards at all. His mind, in spite of the noise, ran on another rhythm, another soundscape altogether: not the harsh snap of pasteboard, not Will’s oaths, not the chaplain’s muttered imprecations, but the imagined hush of Maria’s chamber; the soft combing of hair, the whisper of linen, a prayer spoken low in a voice whose particular turns of speech—its little archaic courtesies, its “sure” and “nay”—held for him a meaning no dictionary could ever render. How much of a person lives in such small words, and how easily the world, with its fashionable phrases and its loud, market-ready talk, rubs them away.

“You double us all round?” cried Will. “I will take a card upon each of my two. Thank you, that will do—a ten—now, upon the other, a queen—two natural vingt-et-uns, and as you doubled us you owe me so-and-so,” said Harry, not even lifting his eyes as if the winnings were no more than crumbs.

Will swore. The chaplain’s pious tongue grew very nimble with profanities. Harry kept calm. He won because he did not care to win. Fortune—that coquettish jade—came to him because he was thinking of another nymph, perhaps as fickle.

Will and the chaplain played against him, forever increasing their stakes, imagining the rich Virginian meant to let them recover their losses. But Harry’s notion was not so. Presently, as their growls thickened, he spoke with a quiet firmness.

“The luck does seem to be with me, cousin; and I am sure I do not want to press it; but you don’t suppose I’m going to be such a fool as to fling it away altogether? I have quite a heap of your promises on paper by this time. If we are to go on playing, let us have the dollars on the table, if you please; or, if not the money, the worth of it.”

“Always the way with you rich men,” grumbled Will. “Never lend except on security—always win because you are rich.”

“Faith, cousin, you have been of late for ever flinging my riches into my face. I have enough for my wants and for my creditors.”

“Oh, that we could all say as much!” groaned the chaplain. “How happy we, and how happy the duns would be! What have we got to play against our conqueror? There is my new gown, Mr. Warrington. Will you set me five pieces against it? I have but to preach in stuff if I lose. Stop! I have a Chrysostom, a Foxe’s Martyrs, a Baker’s Chronicle, and a cow and her calf. What shall we set against these?”

“I will bet one of cousin Will’s notes for twenty pounds,” cried Harry, producing one of those documents as coolly as if it were a recipe card. “Or I have my brown mare, and will back her—red against your honour’s notes of hand, but against ready money.”

“I have my horse,” bawled Will. “I will back my horse against you for fifty.”

Harry took the offers of both gentlemen. In the course of ten minutes the horse and the bay mare had changed owners, as simply as plates are changed between courses. Will swore more fiercely than ever. The parson dashed his wig to the ground and outdid his pupil in loudness. Harry was neither elated nor angry. They had asked him to play; he had played. He knew he should win.

And through it all his eyes strayed again and again to the window. Across the court, beneath a brilliant, flickering star, was the casement he knew to be hers. A lamp burned faintly behind it, making a pale square of light that looked, to his bewitched fancy, like a little hearth-fire set up for him alone. He forgot his victims, their groans, their ill-luck, even as they crossed the court at last and the night swallowed their voices. Behind yonder pane—behind that thin glass and curtain—lay his joy, his heart, his treasure; and the house’s quiet, after the clatter of cards, sounded like a great breath drawn in and held, as if Castlewood itself kept watch over what could not be spoken aloud.
