The silence in Newland Archer's library was oppressive, a palpable weight that settled upon him as he sat at his writing-table. The dim glow of the evening sun cast a melancholy light on the familiar walls, where the shadows of memories seemed to stir, like leaves rustling in an autumn breeze. He had just returned from the Metropolitan Museum, where the inaugural gala had left him feeling suffocated by the crowd and the pretentious chatter of the elite.

As he gazed around the room, his thoughts wandered back to a long-forgotten era, when the Cesnola rooms were still a humble part of the old Museum. He recalled the sight of a slight figure in a sealskin cloak moving away down the narrow corridor, a fleeting glimpse of a moment long past. The vision awakened a cascade of memories, each one a reminder of the people and events that had shaped his life.

His mind drifted to the day his wife, May, had told him of her pregnancy, her blushes and circumlocutions a testament to the societal norms of their time. He remembered the christening of their eldest son, Dallas, and the joy that had filled the room as the young boy took his first steps. The laughter of May and the nurse still echoed in his mind, a happy sound that contrasted sharply with the somber mood that had settled over him.

As he sat there, lost in thought, Archer's gaze fell upon a watercolor of the old Museum, painted by his daughter Mary. The colors were muted, the style simple, yet it captured the essence of a bygone era. He thought of Dallas, now grown and pursuing a career as an architect, and the conversation they had once had about the future of the city. The young men of today were indeed changing, abandoning traditional paths for more unconventional pursuits – Central American archaeology, architecture, and landscape engineering. They sought to preserve the prerevolutionary buildings of their country, adapting Georgian styles and protesting the misuse of the term "Colonial."

Archer's thoughts turned to the Governor of New York, who had once praised him as a man capable of lending a hand in cleaning out the corruption of the political system. The phrase still resonated within him, a call to action that he had never fully answered. As he looked back, he wondered if men like himself were truly what the country needed, or if they were merely a relic of a bygone era.
