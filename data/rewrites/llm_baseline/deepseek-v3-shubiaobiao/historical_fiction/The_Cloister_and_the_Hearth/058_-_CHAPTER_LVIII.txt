Gerard walked in silence beside Teresa, his artist’s mind preoccupied with wondering what she intended for him, rather than simply asking. At last, she spoke unprompted.

"A friend told me of a goldsmith’s wife in need of a scribe," she said. "Her shop is nearby. We shall not have far to go."

They soon arrived at the woman’s door. Teresa, ever poised, addressed her with grace.

"Madama, Leonora mentioned you seek a skilled writer. I bring you a fine one—this young man saved my child from the sea. I pray you look upon him kindly."

The goldsmith’s wife did look—long and intently—at Gerard’s handsome face. But her answer disappointed.

"Nay, I’ve no need of a scribe. Ah! Now I recall—it was my gossip, Claelia the sausage-maker, who asked for one. She told me, and I told Leonora."

Teresa offered a courteous farewell, and they departed. Claelia lived farther off, and when they reached her house, she was not at home.

"I shall wait," Teresa declared, settling herself with such regal stillness that Gerard, unable to resist, began sketching her furtively.

At last, Claelia returned. Teresa rose, presenting Gerard with quiet dignity.

"Madama, the goldsmith’s wife, Olympia, informed me you require a scribe. I bring you a fine one—he saved my child from the sea. For Our Lady’s sake, favor him."

Claelia shook her head. "Good dame, fair sir, I’ve no need of a writer. But now you remind me—it was my friend Appia Claudia who asked. She’s a tailor, lives in the Via Lepida."

Teresa withdrew calmly.

"Madama," Gerard ventured, "this may prove tedious for you."

She arched a brow. "What was ever done without patience?" Then, softer: "We shall knock on every door in Rome, but you *shall* have justice."

"But—I fear we are followed. A man lingers behind us, sometimes near, sometimes far."

"I have seen him," Teresa replied coolly, though a faint flush touched her cheek. "It is my poor Lodovico."

She stopped, turned, and beckoned. A reluctant figure approached. When he stood before them, Teresa fixed him with a steady gaze.

"Lodovico," she said, "know this young man of whom I have often spoken. Know him and love him—for he saved your wife and child."

Lodovico’s forced smile vanished. His face softened with genuine gratitude, and he embraced Gerard warmly. Yet something in the man’s earlier stealth made Gerard uneasy.

"You’ll join us, Ser Lodovico?" Gerard asked.

"Nay, signor. I go not beyond the Tiber."

"Addio, then," Teresa said pointedly.

"When will you return home, *Teresa mia*?"

"When my errand is done, Lodovico."

They walked on in silence, Teresa now wearing a shadow of melancholy.

Appia Claudia, mercifully, did not send them across the river again, but only down the street to Lucretia, the glove-maker.

Lucretia was a cheerful, talkative woman. "A writer? Nay, I keep all my accounts in my head! But my confessor, Father Colonna—ah, *he* is mad for them!"

Teresa frowned. "He is a friar, vowed to poverty. I cannot have this young man work unpaid—he saved my child."

Lucretia laughed. "Fear not! A Colonna never lacks for coin. And such a confessor! His head is leagues away—he scarce hears a word you say. Why, once I confessed to murdering a lodger’s child and baking her in a pie, and he mumbled, ‘Say three Paternosters, eat no eggs on Wednesday, *pax vobiscum*,’ and off he wandered!"

Teresa, patient, steered her back. "Would you take him to the friar and speak for him?"

Lucretia sighed. "I cannot leave my shop. But go yourself—his door is ever open to scholars. Only, mind this: if the young man claims skill, he must *have* it. The friar tolerates no bunglers. Once, he nearly thrashed a man for a poorly carved crucifix!"

Gerard hesitated. "Might I prepare a finer sample of my work before facing him?"

Teresa agreed.

On the way home, Gerard glimpsed a flawless sheet of vellum in a shop window—but knew he could not afford it. Resigned, he returned to his lodgings, intending to erase an old deed for reuse. Yet as he studied it, he realized it was a legal bond between Ghysbrecht Van Swieten and Floris Brandt. Destroying it would be unjust.

As he descended the stairs, pondering where to buy cheaper vellum, he found Teresa waiting—with the very skin he had coveted.

"How—how did you know?" he stammered.

Teresa smiled. "Your eyes lingered on it, and you sighed. Could I let you want for it after that?"

Gerard, overwhelmed, could only thank her.

The next day, armed with a carefully penned page of Greek and Latin, he faced Fra Colonna. The friar, surrounded by manuscripts and curiosities, barely glanced up until Gerard presented his work.

Then—astonishment. The Dominican embraced him.

***

Meanwhile, in Holland, an event unfolded that would ripple toward Gerard in ways none could foresee.

Before Eli’s cottage appeared a gaudy spectacle: a man on horseback, both so bedizened they seemed one absurd creature. The rider, adorned in silver and blue, announced his purpose.

"I come for your dwarf."

Catherine bristled. "We’ve no dwarf here!"

Eli, wiser, sighed. "He means our Giles."

Giles himself settled the matter. Striding out, he declared, "If my folk are fools, I am not. I’ll go with you—now."

Catherine wept, but Giles was resolute. "I’ll visit often. And if I dislike the court, not all the dukes in Christendom will keep me!"

Mounting the tallest horse with a rope from the window—to the pursuivant’s delight—Giles rode off, leaving sorrow behind.

At court, his sharp tongue and fearless truths made him a favorite—a novelty among sycophants.

Little did he know how far his path would twist—or how his fate would intertwine with Gerard’s, across the miles and years.
