Buck had earned Thornton his fortune—sixteen hundred dollars in five wild minutes—and with it came freedom from debt and the chance to chase a legend. The Lost Cabin mine whispered its secrets through the wind, a tale of gold and graves. Many had sought it; few returned. Now Thornton, with Pete and Hans, turned their sleds eastward, where the map ended and the wilderness began.

Seventy miles up the Yukon, then left into the Stewart River, they pushed until the land shrugged them into silence. Thornton asked little of the world: a rifle, salt, and time. Like the wolves, he hunted as he traveled, patient as the slow turn of seasons. Buck reveled in it—the endless wandering, the scent of game thick in the air, the crunch of snow giving way to the softness of spring earth.

They crossed divides where blizzards howled in July, climbed peaks that scraped the midnight sun, and waded through valleys humming with gnats. Once, they found a blazed trail, ancient and purposeless. Another time, a rotted lodge, its only relic a rusted flintlock. The land gave no answers, only echoes.

Then came the strike—not the Lost Cabin, but a placer vein glowing like butter in the pan. Gold piled high, sack after sack, while Buck lounged by the fire, dreaming of the hairy man from his visions. Fear haunted those dreams: the man slept restless, ears pricked for danger, fingers clenched around his spear. Together, they had crept through primordial forests, noses to the wind.

Now the call came louder. Not memory, not dream—a howl, slicing through the dark. Buck lunged toward it, past the camp, into the trees. There, on a moonlit rise, stood a timber wolf, lean and wild. They circled, sniffed, and then ran shoulder-to-shoulder, the wolf leading him deeper into the unknown. For hours they raced, until Buck hesitated. *Thornton.* He turned back. The wolf’s mournful cry followed him home.

Two days later, the call dragged him out again. This time, he did not return for weeks. He hunted like a shadow—stalked ptarmigan, stole salmon from bears, fought a moose bull for four grueling days until the beast faltered. As the kill cooled, Buck felt the land shift. Birds chattered warnings; the wind carried a metallic tang. Something was wrong.

He ran for camp, faster than he’d ever run before. The forest blurred. His paws knew the way.

Then—smoke. Gunpowder. Blood.

The call had been a warning.
