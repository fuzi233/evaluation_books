"There's nought so finely spun But it cometh to the sun."

Mr. Thornton remained seated, anchoring the room with a stillness that seemed to demand the continuation of the moment. He perceived that his mere presence offered Mr. Hale a sanctuary, a reprieve from the crushing weight of mortality. He noted the silent, desperate plea in his friend's posture—the unspoken, plaintive request that lingered between them, a *don't go yet* that vibrated in the air. Yet, he stayed not out of hope for Margaret's arrival, nor because he expected her return. In the shadow of death, where the earth feels its own nothingness, he found a clarity he rarely possessed elsewhere. He listened with a fierce, analytical intensity to Hale's words, absorbing the rhythm of grief: *"Of death, and of the heavy lull, / And of the brain that has grown dull."*

It was a peculiar alchemy of the soul; Thornton's presence acted as a solvent, dissolving the barriers Hale had erected even against his own daughter. Perhaps Hale feared that Margaret's empathy, so vivid and immediate, would shatter under the pressure of such raw emotion, or perhaps his speculative mind demanded answers that a loving daughter would instinctively recoil from hearing. Whatever the cause, the dam broke. Where Margaret might have frozen, terrified of the implications of doubt, Thornton became the vessel for Hale's unfettered confessions. Every thought, every phantom fear that had been frost-bound in Hale's mind since the tragedy found release here. Thornton spoke little, yet his economy of language was devastatingly precise. When Hale faltered, pausing over a remembered agony, Thornton would supply the missing fragment, illuminating the depth of the wound. When doubt wandered aimlessly, blinded by tears, Thornton did not offer platitudes; he guided the eye toward the specific ray of light that rendered the darkness intelligible. Though a man of action, consumed by the great battles of industry, Thornton harbored a religion deeper than Hale had ever imagined—one that bound him to God despite his wilfulness and errors. They never spoke of this again, yet the conversation forged a bond between them, a holy intimacy that no casual discourse on sacred matters could ever achieve. When all doors are open, where lies the Holy of Holies?

While this profound exchange unfolded, Margaret lay motionless on the study floor, pale as a corpse. She had succumbed to a burden too heavy for her frame to bear, a load she had carried with meek patience until her faith finally snapped. Now, she groped blindly for support. A terrible contraction of pain twisted her beautiful brow, the only sign of consciousness amidst the stillness. The mouth, previously set in a sullen defiance, had slackened, turning livid.

The first flicker of life was a tremor along her lips, a mute, soundless attempt at speech, before sinking back into silence. Gathering herself with a feeble effort, leaning on her arms for stability, she rose. Her comb had fallen from her tresses. With an intuitive, desperate need to erase the marks of her collapse, she searched for it, collapsing periodically to regain her strength. Her head bowed, her hands clasped in supplication, she tried to summon the memory of the event that had plunged her into terror, but the details eluded her. Only two facts remained stark and absolute: Frederick was in peril, hunted in London not merely for manslaughter but as the ringleader of a mutiny; and she had lied to shield him. Yet, there was a sliver of comfort. Her deception had bought him time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she secured the letter confirming her brother's safety, she would endure the shame, standing before a crowded courtroom as "a dog" to confess her sin. But if he returned sooner—as he had half-threatened—she would repeat the lie. She did not know how the words would emerge after such a terrifying pause of self-reproach without betraying her falsehood, but she knew the repetition would buy time. Time for Frederick.

Dixon's entry shattered her reverie; she had just released Mr. Thornton.

Outside, Thornton had barely taken ten steps when an omnibus halted beside him. A man descended, tipping his hat. It was Inspector Watson.

"My name is Watson—George Watson, sir, the one you employed—"

"Ah, yes! I've heard you're progressing admirably."

"Yes, sir. I owe you thanks. But I approached you regarding a small matter of business. I believe you were the magistrate who took the deposition of the poor man who died in the Infirmary last night?"

"Yes," Thornton replied, his tone shifting instantly. "I heard a rambling account the clerk deemed useless. I suspect he was drunk, though undoubtedly killed by violence. One of my mother's servants was betrothed to him; she is distraught today. What of him?"

"Why, sir, his death is intricately linked to someone in the house I saw you exiting moments ago. Mr. Hales, I believe."

Thornton turned sharply, his eyes locking onto the inspector's face with sudden, piercing interest. "What connection?"

"It appears I possess a fairly distinct chain of evidence implicating a gentleman who was walking with Miss Hale at Outwood Station that night. The witness claims he saw the man strike or push Leonard off the platform, causing his death. However, the young lady vehemently denies being present."

"Miss Hale denies she was there!" Thornton's voice dropped, brooding with a new tension. "Tell me, what date? What hour?"

"Approximately six o'clock, Thursday the twenty-sixth."

They walked in silence for a minute. The inspector broke the quiet first. "Sir, a coroner's inquest is imminent. I have a young man who was initially adamant, though now, hearing of the young lady's denial, he hesitates to testify. Still, he is convinced he saw Miss Hale at the station, walking with a gentleman, mere minutes before the porter witnessed a scuffle—which he attributed to Leonard's insolence, yet which precipitated the fatal fall. Seeing you exit the very house, I felt compelled to inquire. Disputed identity cases are fraught with difficulty; one cannot easily doubt the word of a respectable young woman without irrefutable proof."

"And she denied being at the station that evening!" Thornton repeated, his voice low and heavy.

"Yes, sir, twice over, with crystal clarity. I intended to call again, but encountering you just as I departed from questioning the boy who identified her, I sought your counsel. As the magistrate who saw Leonard on his deathbed, and as the benefactor who secured my position..."

"You were correct," Thornton said firmly. "Do not proceed until you have seen me again."

"The young lady expects me to call, based on my previous statements."

"I require only an hour's delay. It is three o'clock. Meet me at my warehouse at four."

"Very well, sir."

They parted ways. Thornton hurried to his warehouse, sternly barring his clerks from interruption before retreating to his private office and locking the door. He subjected himself to the torture of reconstruction, analyzing every detail. How could he have allowed himself to drift into the unsuspecting calm of her tearful image just two hours prior, pitying her and forgetting the savage, distrustful jealousy that the sight of her—unknown to him, at such an hour, in such a place—had ignited within him? How could one of such purity stoop to such behavior? Yet, the thought haunted him, thrilling him with its old, potent attraction. And then, the lie. What dread of shame could compel the truthful Margaret to falsehood? Could the provocation of a drunken man like Leonard truly justify such a distortion? The fear was creeping, deadly, capable of bowing the upright Margaret to deceit. He could almost pity her. What awaited her? If the inquest proceeded and the young man testified...

Suddenly, he sprang to his feet. There would be no inquest. He would save Margaret. He would assume the responsibility of halting the proceedings, shielding her from the dubious outcome of medical testimony he had vaguely heard earlier—the surgeons had noted an advanced internal disease, inevitable and fatal, suggesting the fall or subsequent exposure might only have accelerated the end. If he had known how deeply she would be ensnared, how he would stain her whiteness with a lie, he could have saved her with a single word. The decision regarding the inquest had hung in the balance the night before. Miss Hale might love another, might hold him in indifference or contempt, but he would perform faithful acts of service she would never know. He might despise her, but the woman he once loved must be spared shame. To pledge oneself to a lie in a public court, to stand and admit a preference for darkness over light—that was a shame he would not permit.

Gray and stern, Thornton emerged from his office, passing through his bewildered clerks. He was gone for half an hour, returning no less severe, though his mission was accomplished. He scribbled two lines on a scrap of paper, sealed it, and handed it to a clerk.

"I instructed Watson, the former packer now in the police, to meet me at four. I have just encountered a gentleman from Liverpool wishing to depart the city. Ensure this note reaches Watson upon his arrival."

The message was concise: *"There will be no inquest. Medical evidence insufficient to justify it. Cease all further steps. I have not consulted the coroner; I accept full responsibility."*
