"For never any thing can be amiss / When simpleness and duty tender it." — *Midsummer Night's Dream*.

Mr. Thornton approached the affairs of the following day with absolute clarity. There was a modest demand for finished goods within his sector of the trade, and seizing the opportunity, he negotiated aggressively. He arrived at the magistrate's meeting precisely on time, offering his peers the benefit of his keen judgment and his ability to foresee consequences instantly, thereby driving decisions forward with remarkable speed. While older, wealthier gentlemen—men whose fortunes were tied up in landed estates—sought his counsel, Mr. Thornton possessed only floating capital invested entirely in commerce. Yet, they looked to him for prompt wisdom. He was the one appointed to liaise with the police and lead necessary proceedings. However, he regarded their unconscious deference with the same indifference as the soft west wind, which barely disturbed the straight, vertical plume of smoke rising from the great factory chimneys. He remained unaware of the silent respect accorded to him; had he perceived it, he might have viewed it as an impediment to his objectives. As it was, his sole focus was the swift execution of his duties.

News of his high regard filtered through the town via the gossipy ears of the magistrates' wives and the wealthy ladies. They whispered that without Mr. Thornton, matters would have deteriorated significantly. Consequently, he swept aside his business engagements with efficiency. It appeared as though the deep mortification of the previous day, followed by those stunned, aimless hours, had finally burned away the mist from his intellect. He felt his power surging within him, reveling in it, and nearly defying his own heart. Had he been aware of the truth, he could have sung the song of the miller living by the River Dee: "I care for nobody—Nobody cares for me."

The evidence against Boucher and other ringleaders of the riot was presented before him; the charges against the remaining three for conspiracy failed. Nevertheless, he sternly ordered the police to remain vigilant, insisting that the swift arm of the law stand ready to strike the moment further proof emerged. Then, he exited the hot, reeking courtroom of the borough hall and stepped out onto the street. It was fresher, yet still sultry. Suddenly, he seemed to collapse under a wave of exhaustion; his thoughts lost their grip and drifted inevitably toward her. They recalled not the scene of his rejection the day before, but the looks and actions of the day prior to that. He walked mechanically through the crowded thoroughfares, weaving in and out of the throng without truly seeing anyone, consumed by a sickness of longing for that single half-hour, that brief interval when she had clung to him and her heart had beaten against his. He desired to return to that moment once more.

"Why, Mr. Thornton! You're treating me very coldly, I must say. And how is Mrs. Thornton? Fine weather this! We doctors aren't fond of it, I can assure you!"

"I beg your pardon, Dr. Donaldson. I genuinely didn't see you. My mother is quite well, thank you. It is a splendid day, ideal for the harvest, I trust. If the wheat gets in properly, we shall enjoy a brisk trade next year, regardless of what you doctors endure."

"Ay, ay. Every man for himself. Your bad weather and lean times are my profitable ones. When trade falters, there is far more undermining of health and preparation for death among you Miltonians than you realize."

"Not with me, Doctor. I am forged of iron. News of the worst debt I've ever incurred never varied my pulse. This strike, which affects me more profoundly than any other individual in Milton—even more than Mr. Hamper—does not diminish my appetite. You must seek another patient, Doctor."

"By the way, you recommended a good case to me, a poor lady! To avoid sounding heartless, I must confess I believe Mrs. Hale—that lady in Crampton, you know—has perhaps only a few weeks left. I never held much hope for a cure, as I mentioned before, but I visited her today, and my prognosis remains grim."

Mr. Thornton fell silent. His boasted steady pulse faltered for an instant. "Is there anything I can do?" he asked, his voice altered. "You know... since funds are scarce, are there any comforts or delicacies she requires?"

"No," replied the Doctor, shaking his head. "She craves fruit; she suffers from a constant fever. But Jargonelle pears will suffice, and there are plenty available in the market."

"You will inform me immediately if there is anything else I can provide, I promise," Mr. Thornton said. "I rely upon your honesty."

"Oh! Never fear! I shall not spare your purse—I know it is deep enough. I wish you'd grant me a carte-blanche for all my patients and their needs."

However, Mr. Thornton lacked general benevolence; few would attribute strong affections to him. Yet, he proceeded directly to the first fruit shop in Milton. He selected a cluster of purple grapes bearing the most delicate bloom, the richest-hued peaches, and vines with the freshest leaves. These were packed carefully into a basket. The shopkeeper waited for his instruction regarding delivery.

"To Marlborough Mills, I presume, sir?"

"No!" Mr. Thornton declared. "Give the basket to me—I will carry it."

He gripped the basket with both hands, forcing his way through the busiest section of the town, navigating the crowds typically reserved for female shoppers. Numerous young ladies he knew turned to stare, finding it peculiar to see him carrying a load like a porter or an errand-boy. Inside his mind, he reasoned: *"I shall not be deterred from doing as I choose by the fear of her opinion. I wish to deliver this fruit to the poor mother, and it is simply right that I should. She shall never shame me out of performing a kindness. It would be a cruel jest indeed if, terrified of a haughty girl, I failed to act on a whim I hold dear. I do this for Mr. Hale; I do it in open defiance of her."*

He moved at an unusually brisk pace until he reached Crampton. Ascending the stairs two steps at a time, he entered the drawing room before Dixon could announce him. His face was flushed, his eyes shining with a kind, earnest intensity. Mrs. Hale lay on the sofa, burning with fever. Mr. Hale was reading aloud, while Margaret sat on a low stool beside her mother, her work forgotten. Her heart fluttered violently, though whether Mr. Hale shared her agitation was unclear.

Yet, Mr. Thornton paid no attention to either of them—not even to Mr. Hale. He marched straight to Mrs. Hale with his basket, speaking in a subdued, gentle tone that was particularly touching when employed by a robust man in prime health addressing a frail invalid.

"I encountered Dr. Donaldson, ma'am, and as he noted that fruit would benefit you, I took the liberty—the great liberty—of procuring some specimens that seemed exceptionally fine to me."

Mrs. Hale was overcome with delight, trembling with eager anticipation. Mr. Hale, using fewer words, expressed a depth of gratitude. "Fetch a plate, Margaret—a basket—anything."

Margaret rose by the table, half-fearing movement or noise might alert Mr. Thornton to her presence. She imagined it would be awkward for both parties to acknowledge her existence simultaneously. She assumed that, having started seated on a low stool and now standing behind her father, he had inadvertently overlooked her in his haste. In reality, he seemed oblivious to her presence entirely, despite his gaze never resting upon her.

"I must depart," he announced. "I cannot stay longer. If you will forgive this intrusion—and my rough manners, which seem too abrupt—I fear I shall be gentler next time. Please permit me the pleasure of bringing you more fruit whenever I encounter something tempting. Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Goodbye, ma'am."

And he was gone. Not a single word addressed to Margaret. Not a single glance exchanged. She convinced herself he had not seen her. She retrieved a plate in silence and lifted the fruit out tenderly, using the tips of her slender fingers. It was generous of him to bring it, especially considering the events of the previous day.

"Oh! It is so delicious!" Mrs. Hale murmured in her weak voice. "How kind of him to think of me! Margaret, my love, please taste these grapes! Was it not thoughtful of him?"

"Yes!" Margaret replied quietly.

"Margaret!" Mrs. Hale said, with a hint of querulousness, "you dislike everything Mr. Thornton does. I have never seen anyone so prejudiced."

Mr. Hale had been peeling a peach for his wife and cut a small slice for himself. "If I harbored any prejudices, the gift of such exquisite fruit would surely dissolve them. I have not tasted fruit like this—not even in Hampshire—since I was a boy. For boys, I suspect, all fruit is delightful. Do you recall the tangled currant bushes at the corner of the west wall in our garden at home?"

Did she not? Did she not remember every weather stain on the ancient stone wall, the gray and yellow lichens mapping its surface, and the tiny crane's-bill growing in the cracks? Shaken by the tumultuous past forty-eight hours, her entire life currently straining her fortitude, these casual reminiscences from her father triggered a sudden reaction. Dropping her sewing on the floor, she hurried out of the room and into her private chamber.
