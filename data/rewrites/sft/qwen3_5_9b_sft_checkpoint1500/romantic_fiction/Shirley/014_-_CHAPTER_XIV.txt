"The moment I wake, I know the inevitable truth," Caroline whispered to herself as sunlight crept across her bedclothes. "Robert will marry Shirley. He must. She is capable of supporting him." Yet, as the thought solidified, a colder, sharper edge sliced through her resolve. "And what becomes of me then? I will be erased. Completely forgotten."

Her mind drifted, a storm cloud gathering over a darkening sky. "Where will I turn when Robert is gone? Where will I hide *my* Robert? I cannot claim him as husband; I am poverty and weakness, while Shirley is wealth and power, crowned with beauty and love. That is undeniable. Their union is not a transaction born of greed, but a bond of genuine affection—he will be proud to love her. There is no flaw in their match. Let them join. But once united, I shall be nothing to him. The title of 'sister,' all those polite fictions—it disgusts me. With a man like Robert, I demand everything or nothing. I cannot play the hypocrite, feign calm friendship while my soul is torn apart. I cannot stand between them as a barrier, nor trample them as an enemy. Robert is a superior man. I have loved him, I do love him, and I must love him. If I cannot be his wife, I must vanish from his sight. There is no middle path: cling to him as flesh and bone, or be severed from him as the poles of the earth. Sunder me, Providence. Separate us swiftly."

These turbulent thoughts swirled in her mind until late afternoon, when the vision of Miss Keeldar finally materialized outside the parlor window. Miss Keeldar strolled past with a languid grace, her expression a blend of melancholy and nonchalance—the usual mask she wore when at rest. But when animated, that careless veneer vanished, replaced by a warmth so unique, seasoned with such genuine sentiment, that her laughter and smiles held a flavor distinct from the hollow mockery of the world.

She paused before the door and tapped lightly. "Why did you not keep your promise to visit me today, Caroline?"

"I was not in the humor," Miss Helstone admitted honestly.

Shirley's gaze sharpened instantly, piercing through Caroline's defenses. "No," she observed, "you are not in the mood for loving me. You are in one of your sunless spells, where a fellow creature's presence feels like a burden. Do you realize you have these moods?"

"Do you intend to remain long?"

"Yes. I've come for tea, and I shall remove my bonnet now."

She did so, standing on the rug with her hands clasped behind her back, her eyes searching Caroline's face with an intensity that bordered on pity. "A curious expression you wear," Shirley continued softly. "You look remarkably self-contained, you solitary, wounded deer. Are you frightened that I will distress you if I discover you are bleeding inside?"

"I never fear Shirley."

"But sometimes you dislike me; often you avoid me. I am sensitive to being slighted. If you had not walked home with Moore last night, you would be a different woman today. How long was the journey?"

"An hour and fifteen minutes for a mile."

"Hmph!" Shirley leaned forward. "Was it Moore who lingered, or you?"

"Shirley, you speak nonsense."

"He speaks nonsense," Shirley countered, her voice rising with irritation, "or he appears to, which is infinitely worse. I see the reflection of his eyes on your brow right now. I am desperate to confront him, if only I had a trustworthy ally. I felt so last night, and I feel it still."

"You do not ask why," Caroline pressed, sensing the shift in the air. "Nor do you deserve to hear my secrets without invitation. Truthfully, I could have hunted Moore down yesterday with deadly intent. I possess pistols, and I know how to use them."

"Nonsense, Shirley! Which would you shoot—me or Robert?"

"Perhaps neither. Perhaps myself. More likely a bat or a branch. Moore is merely a puppy, your cousin—a quiet, serious, sensible, ambitious pup. I watch him stand before me, delivering his stern-yet-gentle speeches, crushing me beneath his unyielding focus. And I simply have no patience for him!"

Miss Keeldar paced rapidly across the room, venting her frustration against men in general and her tenant in particular.

"You are mistaken," Caroline urged, her anxiety mounting. "Robert is no puppy or dandy; I vouch for that."

"You vouch for it!" Shirley laughed, a sharp, brittle sound. "Do you think I trust your word? I would credit anyone sooner than you. To advance Moore's fortunes, you would sacrifice your right hand."

"But I would not lie. He was civil to me last night; that is all."

"I never asked what he was," Shirley dismissed, her indignation flaring. "I can guess. I watched him from the window take your hand in those long fingers as he left my gate."

"That matters nothing. I am not a stranger; I am his cousin."

"My comfort is shattered by his maneuvers," Shirley declared, her composure cracking. "He intrudes constantly between us. Without him, we are friends. But those six feet of puppyhood cast a perpetual eclipse over our connection. Again and again, he blocks the view I crave, turning me into a nuisance in your eyes."

"No, Shirley, no."

"He does. You did not desire my company this afternoon, and I find it hard to accept. You are naturally reserved, but I am a social creature who cannot endure solitude. If left undisturbed, I hold a regard for you that would allow me to bear your presence forever. You cannot say the same of me."

"Shirley, I can say anything you wish. I like you."

"You will wish me at Jericho tomorrow, Lina."

"I shall not. I grow fonder of you daily. Though I am too English to forge a fierce friendship overnight, you are unlike any ordinary young lady. I esteem you, I value you. You are never a burden. Do you believe me?"

"Partly," Shirley smiled, though with a note of skepticism. "You are a peculiar personage. Beneath your quiet exterior lies a depth and force not easily grasped. And certainly, you are not happy."

"And unhappy people are rarely good?"

"Not at all. I meant that unhappy people are often preoccupied, unable to discourse with companions of my nature. Worse, there is a sorrow that corrodes rather than depresses. I fear that is your lot. Can pity help you, Lina? If so, take some from Shirley; she offers it freely, and it is genuine."

"Shirley, I have never had a sister—you never had one—but suddenly I understand the bond between sisters. An affection woven into the fabric of life, unrooted by shock, trampled only briefly before springing fresher. A love no passion can outrival, competing with love itself in truth and force. Love hurts, Shirley. It is tormenting, racking, burning away our strength. In affection, there is no pain, no fire, only sustenance and balm. I am supported and soothed when you are near. Only you. Do you believe me now?"

"I am easy of belief when the creed pleases me."

"We are friends, then, Lina, despite the black eclipse?"

"We are," returned the other, pulling Shirley close and guiding her to a chair. "Let chance decide what it will."

"Then let us speak of something other than the Troubler." Just then, the rector entered, and the conversation shifted, resuming only moments before Miss Keeldar's departure. As she lingered in the hallway, she confided, "Caroline, I bear a heavy weight upon my mind. My conscience is uneasy, as if I have committed a crime—or am about to. It is not my private conscience, but that of a landlord, a lord of the manor. I have fallen into the grasp of an eagle with iron talons, under a stern influence I disapprove of yet cannot resist. Soon, I fear, some action will be taken that displeases me. To ease my mind and prevent harm, I intend to embark on a series of good works. Do not be surprised if I suddenly become outrageously charitable. I know not where to begin, but you must advise me. Tomorrow, I shall seek counsel from Miss Ainley. I intend to place myself under her tuition. Imagine the pupil! Tell her, Lina, that though well-meaning, I am a neglected character, and she will not be scandalized by my ignorance regarding clothing societies."

The following morning, Caroline found Shirley seated gravely at her desk. Before her lay an account book, a bundle of banknotes, and a well-filled purse. She appeared mighty serious, yet deeply puzzled. "I have scrutinized the weekly household expenditures," she explained, "trying to identify areas for retrenchment.
