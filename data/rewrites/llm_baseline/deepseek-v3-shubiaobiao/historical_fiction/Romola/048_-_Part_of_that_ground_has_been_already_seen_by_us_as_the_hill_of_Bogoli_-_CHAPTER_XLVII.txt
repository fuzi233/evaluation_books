Tito’s carefully laid plans had been thwarted by the caprice of circumstance—small, unforeseen events that even the cleverest mind could not anticipate. It was rare for him to walk with Romola in the evening, yet tonight, of all nights, she had been at his side when her presence was most inconvenient. Life was a treacherous game, where skill could be undone by the merest breath of chance, as unpredictable as thistle-down drifting on the wind.

The failure of Spini’s plot did not trouble him overmuch, but the delicate balance of his deception now wavered. To warn Savonarola without arousing suspicion, and to placate Spini without revealing too much—it was a precarious dance. If he went to the monastery now, his sudden concern would follow too closely upon the forged letters, inviting doubt. To warn Spini immediately would mean confessing the truth, and Spini’s blunt mind would see only betrayal, not strategy.

No, the safer path was delay. By morning, Savonarola would be warned, though Spini’s men would return in furious disappointment. Tito could soothe the Compagnacci later, spinning the failure as the Frate’s caution rather than his own interference. Still, the frustration gnawed at him. A smile would have been too great an effort tonight. He did not return home.

---

Romola did not sleep. The drumming rain thickened as the hours passed, and she welcomed it—the storm felt like a shield, a force that might paralyze men’s schemes. But her mind churned with doubts, not only of Tito but of herself. What lies had he spun? What plots still lurked in the dark? Every soul who trusted him was in danger; she could no longer deny it.

Was it pride that held her back from warning others? If her husband was a criminal, then her place was beside him in ruin—but did that mean she must stand idle while he drew others into destruction? Prayer eluded her; her thoughts were too restless, too sharp for surrender. As dawn broke, the rain slackened, the wind scattered the clouds, and the pale morning light spilled over Florence.

The clarity of day only sharpened her dread. She flung her mantle around her shoulders and rushed to the loggia, as if the cityscape might offer some answer. But there was nothing—only rooftops hiding the streets where Savonarola might even now walk into a trap.

Should she go to Bernardo del Nero? Could she persuade him, without full confession, to bar the Frate’s path? But time was slipping. The bells had long since struck seven. There was only one choice left.

She flew down the stairs, past her neglected patients, and into the street. Her steps were swift as she cut through the heart of the city, toward San Marco. Perhaps, in the Piazza della Signoria, she might yet find Tito—might wrench the truth from him and spare herself the desperate errand.

But the Piazza was alive with movement: worshippers climbing the Palazzo steps, merchants in conversation, porters with their burdens. And there—amid the shifting crowd—Tito stood, lounging in the doorway of Nello’s shop, his hair freshly combed, his manner effortless. The sight struck her like a blow. How could he stand there, so at ease, while his schemes unraveled lives around him?

Three men stood near him—Cronaca and two others, followers of Savonarola. A fierce resolve seized her. *Let him answer before them.*

She closed the distance before he could react. Cronaca’s voice rang out: *“Madonna Romola comes.”*

Tito stiffened, his composure faltering for the barest instant. She saw the flicker of alarm before he smoothed it away.

“Is the Frate beyond the gates?” she demanded.

“No,” he said, his voice carefully measured.

“And you are certain he does not mean to go?”

“I am certain.”

“That is enough.”

She turned away, mounting the steps of the Duomo, needing solitude to steady herself. Behind her, Tito’s gaze burned with something close to hatred.

Among the onlookers stood Ser Ceccone, the notary—a man as venomous as he was plain, a Medicean spy who envied Tito’s charm and success. He watched, silent, as Romola disappeared into the shadows of the cathedral.
