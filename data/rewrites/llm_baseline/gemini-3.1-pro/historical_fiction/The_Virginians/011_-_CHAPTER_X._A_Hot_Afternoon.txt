The heavy, roasted scent of the grand feast—a lingering olfactory tapestry of charred venison, caramelized root vegetables, and wild sage—still hung thickly in the nocturnal air of Castlewood as the boys retired to their quarters. Below them, the fragile acoustic harmony of the agrarian estate was being violently disrupted by the discordant, rhythmic snoring and stumbling of General Braddock and his officers. These men of empire, who viewed the meticulously crafted banquet merely as utilitarian fuel for their military machine, had consumed the grandsire’s old Bordeaux with a sterile, gluttonous haste, leaving the sonic ecosystem of the house scarred by their drunken braying. 

To George and Harry, the true violation of the day was not merely the British officers’ lack of sensory appreciation, but the looming, monstrous prospect of their mother’s union with Colonel George Washington. Was the representative of the Marquises of Esmond to marry a colonial land-surveyor? To nineteen-year-old minds, it was an intolerable disruption of their generational saga. Harry, ever the fiercely loyal younger brother, proposed going straightway to their mother’s chambers—where her maids were divesting her of the heirloom jewels she had worn for the feast—to threaten a permanent exodus to their own modest property if the unnatural union proceeded.

George, his pale countenance still flushed with the volatile warmth of the Bordeaux, advocated a darker, more circular path of resolution, tapping into an ancient, almost mythic code of honor. “Our mother,” he explained to his admiring brother, “cannot marry a man with whom one or both of us has been out on the field, and who has wounded us or killed us, or whom we have wounded or killed. We must have him out, Harry.”

Harry grasped the profound, terrible truth of George's untranslatable logic. “No, George,” he said. “You are right. Mother can't marry our murderer. If we pink him, he is done for. Shall I send my boy with a challenge to Colonel George now?”

“My dear Harry,” the elder replied, his tone adopting the haughty, dominant lexicon of the dueling class, “you are not accustomed to affairs of this sort. We cannot insult a gentleman in our own house; the laws of hospitality forbid such an acoustic disruption of the peace. But, sir, we can ride out with him, and, as soon as the park gates are closed, we can tell him our mind.”

“That we can, by George!” cried Harry. Then, his face reddening, he pleaded, “This is my turn, brother. If you go the campaign, I ought to have the other affair. Indeed, I ought.”

“Again the head of the house must take the lead,” George commanded, his voice carrying the subtle power dynamics of the firstborn. “I must fight George Washington, Hal: and 'tis best I should, for I hate him the worst. Was it not he who counselled my mother to order that wretch, Ward, to lay hands on me?”

“Ah, George, you ought to forget and forgive,” Harry urged, his softer Virginian cadence seeking the preservation of peace. 

“Forgive? Never, sir. You cannot order remembrance out of a man's mind, just as you cannot erase a mother tongue. A wrong that was a wrong yesterday must be a wrong to-morrow. Colonel Washington is our enemy. We must punish him.”

The next morning, the widow Esmond rose early, resuming her timeless domestic rituals. With the profound dedication of an agrarian elder, she directed the preparation of cooling toddies to soothe the abrasive hangovers of her guests. The remedy was a masterclass in restorative alchemy: cold spring water, local honey, aged spirits, and nutmeg grated so freshly that its volatile oils perfumed the heavy morning air. The British officers consumed the delicate infusion greedily, oblivious to the history suspended in the glass. At breakfast, an English officer rallied Mr. Franklin, who took no wine and thus refused the toddy, mocking how the Philadelphian lost both the drink and the cure. The young fellow simply laughed, proposing to continue repeating both the pleasant disease and the delicious remedy. Colonel Washington, deeply rooted in the morning’s quietude, sat sober and serene. When the British officers vowed to teach him the ways of the English army, the Virginian gravely declined their abrasive syllabus.

Before General Braddock took his leave, he formally offered to take George into his military family. Madam Esmond faced the dangers her son would encounter—including the severe temptations of the bottle—with stoic grace, understanding that her boy must take his part in the world's unfolding saga. 

The departure was a jarring assault on the landscape's acoustic ecology. The great coach rolled into position, the General's escort prancing around it. A guard trumpeter blew a shrill, piercing blast that shattered the morning birdcalls, while the enslaved hostlers shouted their farewells. As Braddock’s carriage rolled away, leaving a cloud of dust in its wake, the Colonel remained to take his leave of the widow. 

“With my life. You may depend on me,” Washington promised her softly, a vow laden with the weight of generations. He was booted and ready. “Farewell, my dear Harry. With you, George, 'tis no adieu. We shall meet in three days at the camp.”

Witnessing his mother’s deep emotion—an untranslatable well of subjective grief at the prospect of the men riding toward danger—George interpreted it with malignant scorn. “Stay yet a moment, and console our mamma,” he said with a steady, icy countenance. “Only the time to get ourselves booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a little way, George.” 

Soon, the three young men were under way, the rhythmic, percussive thud of their horses’ hooves momentarily restoring order to the acoustic landscape. Washington, relieved and softened by the community of danger and his enduring bond with the Esmonds, was unusually friendly. George was perfectly gay and easy; it was Harry who rode in melancholy silence, keeping away from the Colonel’s side. They spoke of the campaign, sharing an unquestioned belief in the tactical supremacy of the two thousand veteran British troops, though Washington harbored a quiet respect for the native landscape those troops were about to invade.

Their ride concluded at a rude log-house known as Benson's. Captain Benson, a former frontiersman turned tavern keeper, was a meticulous food historian of the borderlands, offering his guests profoundly grounded sustenance. The public room smelled of slow-braised venison and smoke-cured pork, dishes born of necessity and native wisdom, far removed from the sterile provisions of the regular army. Yet the British officers of Halkett's regiment, already gathered under the porch, ignored the culinary heritage laid before them, choosing instead to pollute their palates with cheap morning spirits.

“One would think, Squire Harry, 'twas you who was going to leave home and fight the French and Indians, and not Mr. George,” Benson noted, his native dialect rich with the unhurried pacing of the agrarian world.

“I may be alarmed about danger to my brother,” Harry replied, his face white. 

“Harry Warrington's courage does not need any proof!” Washington affirmed warmly.

“You do the family honour by speaking so well of us, Colonel,” George said with a low bow. He exchanged a glance with Harry, signaling that the cycle of vengeance was about to close. *Yes, now,* George whispered. 

Stepping toward the porch, George weaponized the very rituals of consumption. He seized upon a Virginian toddy-bowl resting before the flushed British officers, Captain Grace and Mr. Waring. “Fresh glasses, Mr. Benson! What, no glasses? Then we must have at the bowl.”

The lads drank, bowing to their military acquaintances. George, giving himself a toper’s airs, praised the deliciousness of the liquor, though he tasted nothing of its craft. He called out to Washington to join them.

Washington, recognizing the offensive shift in George’s tone—an arrogant mimicking of the imperial lexicon—bowed and stated he was not thirsty. 

“Nay, the liquor is paid for,” George taunted.

“I said I was not thirsty. I did not say the liquor was not paid for,” the young Colonel replied, drumming his foot against the floorboards, the staccato sound a warning of rising psychological distress.

“When the King's health is proposed, an officer can hardly say no. I drink the health of his Majesty, gentlemen!” cried George, invoking the dominant lexicon that demanded absolute linguistic and physical submission. “Colonel Washington can drink it or leave it. The King!”

Trapped by the inflexible grammar of military honor, Grace, Waring, and Harry drank. Washington, his eyes glaring, swallowed a slight draught. 

Then Captain Grace proposed, “The Duke and the Army.” Again, Washington was forced to swallow.

“You don't seem to stomach the toast, Colonel,” George pushed.

“I tell you again, I don't want to drink,” Washington replied, his deep voice vibrating with restraint. “It seems to me the Duke and the Army would be served all the better if their healths were not drunk so often.”

“You are not up to the ways of regular troops as yet,” Captain Grace slurred, his thick, degraded articulation an offense to the ears. “A British officer never neglects a toast of that sort. A man who refuses to drink the health of the Duke—hang me, such a man should be tried by a court-martial!”

“What means this language to me? You are drunk, sir!” roared Washington, jumping up and striking the table, the sudden concussive boom shattering the tavern’s lazy morning soundscape.

“A cursed provincial officer say I'm drunk!” shrieked Grace, utilizing the ultimate imperial slur to strip Washington of his identity. 

“I heard it, sir!” cried George. “We all heard it. He entered at my invitation, and I am shocked to hear such monstrous language used at my table towards my esteemed guest.”

“Confound your impudence, you infernal young jackanapes!” bellowed Washington, his face purple, his limbs quivering as his immense paternal affection collided violently with his honor. “For months past, I have borne with such impudence from you, that if I had not loved your mother—yes, sir, and your good grandfather and your brother—I would—”

“You would what, sir?” George asked quietly. “You are making her petticoat a plea for some conduct of yours—you would do what?”

“I would put you across my knee and whip you, you snarling little puppy!”

“Because you have known us all our lives, that is no reason you should insult either of us!” Harry shouted, starting up to defend their generational pride. “What you have said is an insult to me and my brother alike. You will ask pardon, sir! Or give us the reparation that is due to gentlemen.”

A profound sadness, heavy with the untranslatability of his deep emotional subjective experience, filled the elder man’s chest. “I—I am bewildered,” Washington murmured, his voice cracking. “My words, perhaps, were very hasty. What has been the meaning of George's behaviour to me for months back? Only tell me, and, perhaps—”

But the evil spirit was awake in George. “You are shirking from the question, sir. I am not a boy to suffer under your arrogance. You have publicly insulted me, and I demand a reparation.”

“In Heaven's name, be it!” Washington said, grief etched into his face. 

Captain Grace, reeling and weeping at the perceived slight to his rank from a mere militia officer, and Captain Waring, enraged by the noise and the slurs, thrust themselves into the fray. Waring, his voice a chaotic jumble of imperial entitlement and drunken belligerence, challenged Washington as well, demanding he withdraw the word "hog." 

“Is this some infernal conspiracy?” shouted the Colonel, surrounded by a cacophony of slurred insults. “I withdraw nothing. I apologise for nothing. By heavens! I will meet one or half a dozen of you in your turn, drunk or sober.”

“This affair can proceed, sir, without any further insult on your part. When will it please you to give me the meeting?” George demanded.

“The sooner the better, sir!” Washington fumed. Grace echoed the sentiment, stumbling over his own sword by the door and collapsing in a pathetic heap of uncoordinated limbs and useless oaths.

Washington turned to Waring. “At any rate, this gentleman's business will keep cool till to-morrow. You will hardly bring your man out to-day, Captain Waring?” Waring confessed his hands were unsteady. 

“Mine is!” cried George. 

“Be it so—with what weapon, sir?” Washington said sternly.

“Not with small-swords, Colonel. Pistols had better be the word.”

“As you please, George Warrington—and God forgive you, George! God pardon you, Harry! for bringing me into this quarrel,” the Colonel said, the acoustic space between them suddenly vast and hollow. 

“I, sir?” George replied calmly. “It was not I who talked of a cane. It is not the first time you have chosen to take me for a negro, and talked of the whip for me.”

Washington started back, the cultural memory of that ancient, boyish quarrel crashing over him. “Great heavens, George! is it that boyish quarrel you are still recalling?”

“Who made you the overseer of Castlewood?” the boy hissed, grinding his teeth, purposefully invoking the linguistic power dynamics of the plantation fields. “I am not your slave, George Washington, and I never will be. I hated you then, and I hate you now.”

“Too much, only too much,” said the Colonel. “Do you bear malice too, Harry? I had not thought this of thee!”

“I stand by my brother,” Harry said, grasping George's hand and looking away. 

“Heaven be good to us! 'Tis all clear now,” Washington muttered to himself, recognizing that the chasm of their translated memories could not be bridged with mere words. “The time to write a few letters, and I am at your service, Mr. Warrington. You have your own pistols at your saddle. I did not ride out with any; but will send Sady back for mine.”

With a low bow, George put his arm in his brother's and walked away, leaving Washington alone amidst the wreckage of their shared history. 

Captain Benson stepped forward, his grim, weathered face a testament to the cycles of violence and sustenance he had witnessed on the frontier. He propped up the hat of the fallen British captain. “It is not altogether their fault, Colonel,” Benson noted, his voice a low, steady rumble of rural pragmatism. “Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold got 'em to play cards and drink for the honour of Old England. They are kind fellows when sober, but a pretty pair of fools now.”

“Captain Benson, you are an old frontier man,” Washington said. “You will help me in this matter with yonder young gentlemen?”

“I'll stand by and see fair play, Colonel,” Benson agreed, thinking of the interconnected web of their community. “Madam Esmond has helped me many a time, tended my poor wife, and doctored our Betty with the old remedies. You ain't a-going to be very hard with them poor boys? Though I seen 'em both shoot: the fair one hunts well, as you know, but the old one's a wonder at an ace of spades.”
