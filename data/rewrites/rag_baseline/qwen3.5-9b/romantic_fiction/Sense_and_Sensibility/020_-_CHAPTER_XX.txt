The Miss Dashwoods crossed the threshold of the drawing-room the following day, entering at one door just as Mrs. Palmer burst through the opposite entrance. The visitor arrived with a bounce in her step and a smile that masked the underlying tension of their impending departure. She greeted them with exaggerated warmth, clasping each hand in turn and expressing profound delight at their presence.

"I am so glad to see you!" she exclaimed, settling herself between Elinor and Marianne. "For it is such a wretched day I feared you wouldn't come, which would be disastrous given we leave again tomorrow. We simply must go; the Westons arrive next week, you know. It was entirely impulsive on our part—we knew nothing until the carriage pulled up to the door, and then Mr. Palmer asked if I'd accompany him to Barton. He is dreadfully amusing! He never reveals anything unless provoked. I'm terribly sorry we can't extend our stay, though I trust we'll reunite in town shortly."

Elinor and Marianne politely terminated this line of speculation. "Not to town?" Mrs. Palmer shrieked, laughing. "I shall be utterly disappointed if you don't. I could procure the finest residence imaginable for you, right next door to ours in Hanover Square. You must come, truly. I'd be delighted to act as chaperone whenever Mrs. Dashwood feels unable to venture into society." They thanked her profusely yet firmly resisted her entreaties.

"Oh, my dear," Mrs. Palmer cried to her husband, who had just entered the room, "you must assist me in persuading the Miss Dashwoods to spend this winter in town." Her spouse offered no response; after a perfunctory bow to the ladies, he launched into a tirade against the weather. "How repugnant it all is!" he declared. "Such conditions render everything and everyone loathsome. Confinement indoors generates as much ennui as the rain outside; it breeds a hatred for one's acquaintances. What the devil is wrong with Sir John for lacking a billiard room? How few comprehend true comfort! Sir John is as obtuse as the sky."

The remainder of the party filtered in shortly thereafter. "I fear, Miss Marianne," Sir John remarked, "you haven't managed your customary stroll to Allenham today." Marianne cast a somber glance and remained silent. "Oh, cease your coquetry before us," Mrs. Palmer retorted. "We are privy to the truth, I assure you. Moreover, I admire your discernment immensely; I find him exceedingly handsome. We reside but a short distance from him in the countryside, perhaps ten miles, I daresay."

"Much closer to thirty," her husband interjected.
"Ach, well! Negligible variance. I've never visited his estate, though rumors suggest it's a charming retreat."
"As vile a location as I've ever encountered," Mr. Palmer countered flatly.

Marianne maintained a perfect facade of silence, though her features betrayed her keen interest. "Is it truly unsightly?" Mrs. Palmer persisted. "Then it must be some other locale that possesses such allure, presumably."

Once seated in the dining room, Sir John noted with regret that only eight souls occupied the table. "My dear," he addressed his wife, "it is vexing that we are so few. Why didn't you invite the Gilberts to join us today?"
"Didn't I inform you, Sir John, prior to your inquiry, that it was impossible? They dined with us yesterday."
"You and I, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings interjected gently, "ought not to adhere to such rigid formalities."
"Then you would be profoundly uncivilized," Mr. Palmer snapped.
"My love, you contradict everyone," his wife replied with her characteristic laughter. "Are you aware you're being quite rude?"
"I wasn't aware I was contradicting anyone by labeling your mother ill-bred."
"Aye, you may revile me as you please," the kindly elderly lady conceded. "You've secured Charlotte from my grasp, and I cannot reclaim her. Thus, I hold the upper hand." Charlotte erupted in hearty laughter at the notion of her husband's inability to dismiss her, exclaiming that she cared not for his irritability, for they were destined to coexist forever.

It was impossible to encounter someone more relentlessly cheerful or determinedly content than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband's calculated indifference, insolence, and perpetual dissatisfaction failed to wound her; conversely, his scolding and vitriol served only to divert her amusement. "Mr. Palmer is so droll!" she whispered conspiratorially to Elinor. "He is perpetually in a foul mood." Elinor, after brief observation, hesitated to credit him with genuine, unadulterated malice or rudeness. Perhaps his temperament had soured due to discovering, like many men, that an inexplicable bias toward beauty had landed him with a remarkably foolish wife. Yet, she recognized this error was too ubiquitous for any sensible man to suffer lasting injury from it. Rather, it appeared to stem from a desire for distinction—a wish to appear superior to others. The motive was commonplace enough to warrant no wonder, though the method, while successful in establishing his superiority through ill-breeding, was unlikely to attract anyone beyond his wife.

"Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood," Mrs. Palmer resumed shortly, "I have a significant favor to request of you and your sister. Will you spend Christmas at Cleveland? Pray, do—and come while the Westons are present. You cannot conceive how joyful I shall be! It will be absolutely delightful!—My love," turning to her husband, "do you not yearn for the Miss Dashwoods to visit Cleveland?"
"Certainly," he replied with a sneer—"I came to Devonshire with no other purpose."
"There now," she triumphed, "you see Mr. Palmer anticipates you; thus, you cannot refuse." Both ladies eagerly and resolutely declined her invitation.

"But indeed you must and shall come. I am certain you will adore it above all else. The Westons will be with us, making it purely delightful. You cannot imagine how sweet Cleveland is; moreover, we are so lively now, for Mr. Palmer is constantly traversing the countryside campaigning against the election, attracting so many diners I've never witnessed before—it's enchanting! Alas, poor fellow! It is exhausting for him, as he is compelled to win everyone's affection." Elinor struggled to maintain composure as she acknowledged the hardship of such an obligation.

"How charming it will be," Charlotte chimed in, "once he enters Parliament! Won't it? How I shall laugh! It will be absurd to see all his correspondence addressed to him with 'M.P.' appended. But do you know, he insists he'll never frank for me? He declares he won't. Don't you, Mr. Palmer?"
Mr. Palmer ignored her completely.
"He cannot bear writing, you understand," she continued, "he finds it utterly shocking."
"No," he retorted, "I never uttered anything so irrational. Do not attribute all your linguistic abuses to me."
"There now; witness how droll he is. This is invariably his manner! Occasionally he refuses to speak to me for half a day, then erupts with something so witty regarding trivial matters."

She startled Elinor significantly upon returning to the drawing-room by inquiring whether she did not find Mr. Palmer excessively likable. "Certainly," Elinor replied smoothly; "he appears very agreeable."
"Well—I am so relieved you feel that way. I thought you would, for he is so pleasant; and Mr. Palmer is excessively pleased with you and your sisters, I can assure you. You cannot guess how disappointed he will be if you decline Cleveland.—I cannot fathom why you should object." Elinor was once again compelled to decline, shifting the topic to halt her entreaties. She suspected that, residing in the same county, Mrs. Palmer might offer a more detailed account of Willoughby's general character than the Middletons' limited acquaintance permitted. Eager to secure confirmation of his virtues sufficient to dispel Marianne's fears, she initiated inquiries about whether they saw much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland and if they were intimate with him.

"Oh dear, yes; I know him extremely well," Mrs. Palmer responded. "Though I never actually spoke to him, indeed; I've merely glimpsed him frequently in town. Somehow, fate conspired so I never stayed at Barton while he was at Allenham. Mama spotted him here once previously, but I was with my uncle at Weymouth. Nevertheless, I presume we would have encountered him often in Somersetshire had it not been unluckily arranged that we never shared the countryside. He spends very little time at Combe, I believe; but even if he did, I doubt Mr. Palmer would visit him, for he opposes the government, you know, plus it's so remote. I know precisely why you inquire about him; your sister intends to marry him. I am monstrously pleased, for then I shall have her as a neighbor."

"Upon my word," Elinor replied, "you seem to know considerably more about the matter than I do, provided you have grounds to anticipate such a union."
"Don't feign ignorance; everyone discusses it. I assure you I learned of it during my transit through town."
"My dear Mrs. Palmer!"
"Upon my honor, I did.—I encountered Colonel Brandon Monday morning in Bond Street, just before we departed town, and he informed me directly."
