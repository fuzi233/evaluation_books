On the twenty-seventh of June, I was dispatched by Sergeant Cuff to track three Indian suspects linked to a murder. They had been spotted boarding a Rotterdam-bound steamer at Tower Wharf. I followed on a separate vessel departing the next morning.

Upon reaching Rotterdam, the ship’s captain confirmed the Indians had boarded—but only as far as Gravesend. There, they feigned distress upon realizing their "mistake," claiming they had intended to sail to Calais. They forfeited their passage and were set ashore. A clever ruse, no doubt, to evade pursuit.

I traced them back to London, then to Plymouth, where they had already sailed for Bombay aboard the *Bewley Castle*. Cuff alerted the Bombay authorities to intercept the ship upon arrival. My role ended there. The trail went cold.

I commanded the *Bewley Castle* when three Hindus boarded at Plymouth. Their conduct was unremarkable until we were becalmed off India’s coast. Discipline slackened; passengers took to the boats for leisure.

On the third night, the smallest boat vanished—and so did the Hindus. They must have rowed ashore under cover of darkness. By morning, pursuit was futile. Only later did I learn why they fled. The authorities blamed me for lax oversight. Regrettable, but nothing more was heard of them.

Do you recall our conversation in London about the Moonstone? Years later, in the remote province of Kattiawar, I witnessed its final chapter.

Posing as a pilgrim, I joined thousands converging on Somnauth, where a lunar ceremony was to be held. Under moonlight, three figures emerged on a rocky platform—the same Indians I’d seen in England. Stripped of caste, they were condemned to eternal pilgrimage, never to meet again.

Then the shrine’s curtain parted. There, upon the moon god’s forehead, blazed the Diamond—once hidden in an Englishwoman’s gown, now returned to its sacred origins. How it came back, I cannot say. But this much is certain: the Moonstone has slipped beyond European grasp forever.
