If the scale of one's guilt were the sole metric of moral weight, the annals of humanity would present a singular, harrowing tableau: the architects of imperial greed and the titans of financial predation marching shoulder-to-shoulder with petty thieves, pickpockets, and the butcher who slaughters with his own hands. Yet, wickedness does dispense its rewards, albeit unevenly. Those who triumph in this diabolical game inevitably descend below the baseline of human existence, becoming more cruel, more brutish than the fragile vessel of the earth can sustain for the vast majority of womankind. Most carry within them a form of conscience—a shadow of justice that manifests as fear, a shadow of love that appears as pity—barriers that prevent the attainment of serene, unburdened malice, a state of being notoriously difficult to maintain in our composite, vulnerable flesh.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda knew the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, though he had not glimpsed them until he prepared for dinner. A magnificent snowfall had blanketed the grounds, granting the children the rare, crystalline joy of snowball fights and castle building. During the Christmas holidays, the Mallinger girls demanded no amusement unless it was orchestrated and shared by "Cousin," as they invariably addressed Deronda. Following this vigorous outdoor exertion, he had engaged in a game of billiards, allowing the hours to slip by without his mind dwelling on the impending encounter with Gwendolen at dinner. Nevertheless, the prospect held a distinct fascination. As he retired to his room, slightly weary and flushed from the sport, before the half-hour bell tolled, he began to speculate on the transformative influence her marriage to Grandcourt would exert upon her spirit. He wondered if there would be perceptible shifts in her demeanor compared to his observation at Diplow, mirroring the changes he had noted since his first vision of her at Leubronn.

"I suspect there are natures that one could watch grow or degenerate daily if one observed them closely," he mused. "Some of us move faster than others. And I am certain she retains the strong imprints of anything that has once marked her. That incident with the necklace, the whisper that someone deemed her gambling improper, had clearly taken root. Yet such susceptibility cuts both ways; it can propel one toward desperation just as readily as toward redemption. Whatever fascinations Grandcourt holds for capricious tastes—heaven forbid one believes he possesses the power to summon tenderness in daily intimacy! One might be tempted to strike him for the mere spectacle of passion flashing across his face and lips. I fear she married him out of ambition, driven to escape poverty. But why did she initially flee from him? The destitution came later, surely. Poor creature! She may have been coerced into it. How can one feel anything but pity for a young being, brimming with untapped vitality, recklessly ignorant, hanging all her blind hopes upon the remains of a human soul?"

Naturally, the terms Deronda applied to the bridegroom were less charitable than the excuses and pity draped over the bride. His conception of Grandcourt as a "remnant" stemmed not from specific knowledge, but from the cumulative impression of polite interaction suggesting that Grandcourt had exhausted all natural, healthy interest in the world. Generally, when a marriage of consequence occurs, male acquaintances pity the bride and female acquaintances pity the groom; each is presumed capable of finding better fortune. Especially when the bride is charming, young men on the scene often conclude she lacks genuine attachment to a husband so uninteresting to themselves, assuming she wed on pragmatic grounds. In such scenarios, who pities the husband? Even his female friends often deem his position retributive, believing he should have chosen differently. Perhaps Deronda could be excused for withholding pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck observers as a man likely to emerge from experience with greater suffering than he inflicted upon others. Conversely, regarding Gwendolen—young, impulsive, eager for pleasure, nourished by flattery that convinces a lovely girl of her divine right to rule—how swiftly might life shift from expectation to a bitter realization of the irremediable! Having witnessed her, he must possess rather muted feelings not to anticipate her entrance into the room with some degree of interest. Still, since the honeymoon lay three weeks in the past, and Gwendolen had been enthroned not only at Ryelands but at Diplow, she was likely to have composed her countenance, manifesting or concealing her emotions with suitable discretion, unwilling to indulge curiosity with a helpless exposure of her inner turmoil.

A diverse assembly awaited the new couple. The old aristocracy was represented by Lord and Lady Pentreath; the old gentry by young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam of the Worcestershire branch; politics and the public good, specialized in the cider interest, by Mr. Fenn, Member for West Orchards, accompanied by his two daughters; Lady Mallinger's family by her brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife; and the useful bachelor element by Mr. Sinker, the eminent counsel, and Mr. Vandernoodt, whose acquaintance Sir Hugo had found pleasant enough at Leubronn to warrant adoption in England.

All had gathered in the drawing-room before the new couple appeared. Time was spent chiefly observing the children—various little Raymonds, nephews and nieces of Lady Mallinger, alongside her own three girls, who were always permitted to appear at this hour. The scene was genuinely delightful, enlarged by full-length portraits with deep backgrounds inserted into the cedar paneling, surmounted by a ceiling glowing with the rich colors of coats of arms ranged between the sockets. Illuminated nearly as much by the red fire of oak boughs as by pale wax candles, the room was stilled by the deep-piled carpet and the high English breeding that subdued all voices. The mixture of ages, from the white-haired Lord and Lady Pentreath to the four-year-old Edgar Raymond, lent a varied charm to the living groups. Lady Mallinger, with her fair, matronly roundness and mildly prominent blue eyes, moved about in black velvet, carrying a tiny white dog on her arm as a finishing touch to her costume. The children were scattered among the ladies, while most gentlemen stood somewhat apart, conversing with a moderate vivacity typical of the long minutes preceding dinner. Deronda stood slightly apart from the main circle, engaged in a dialogue fixed upon him by Mr. Vandernoodt, a man of the finest Dutch blood imported at the Revolution. For the rest, he was one of those commodious figures in society who are nothing particular themselves but are understood to be acquainted with the best in every department: close-clipped, pale-eyed, nonchalant, serving as an excellent foil to the intense coloring and vivid gravity of Deronda.

They discussed the bride and bridegroom, whose arrival was anticipated. Mr. Vandernoodt, an industrious gleaner of personal details who could likely recount everything about a great philosopher or physicist save their theories or discoveries, implied he had learned many facts about Grandcourt since meeting him at Leubronn.

"Men who have seen a good deal of life do not always end by choosing their wives wisely. He has had rather an anecdotal history—gone rather deep into pleasures, I fancy, lazy as he is. But, of course, you know all about him."

"No, really," Deronda replied in an indifferent tone. "I know little more of him than that he is Sir Hugo's nephew."

Just then, the door opened, deferring any further satisfaction of Mr. Vandernoodt's communicativeness. The scene was one designed to highlight any figure of distinction entering it, and certainly, when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt appeared, no observer could deny their figures possessed distinction. The bridegroom wore neither more nor less easy perfection of costume, neither more nor less well-cut impassibility of face, than before his marriage. It was to be assumed he would accept nothing less than the best in outward equipment, wife included; and the bride was precisely what one might expect him to choose. "By George, I think she's handsomer, if anything!" exclaimed Mr. Vandernoodt. Deronda agreed but remained silent. The white silk and diamonds—it might seem strange, but she did wear diamonds on her neck, in her ears, in her hair—may have contributed to the new imposingness of her beauty, which flashed upon him as more unquestionable, if not more thoroughly satisfactory, than when he had first seen her at the gaming table. Some faces, peculiar in their beauty, are like original works of art; for the first time, they are almost always met with scrutiny. But in seeing Gwendolen at Diplow, Deronda had discerned in her more than he had expected of that tender, appealing charm we call womanly. Was there any new change since then? He distrusted his impressions; yet as he saw her receiving greetings with what seemed a proud, cold quietude and a superficial smile, there seemed to operate within her the same demonic force that had possessed her when she took him in her resolute glance and turned away a loser from the gaming table. There was no time for further conclusion, no time even for him to offer his greeting before the summons to dinner.

He sat not far from opposite her at the table and could occasionally hear her answers to Sir Hugo, who was at his liveliest in conversation with her. Though he looked toward her with the intention of bowing, she gave him no opportunity for some time. Finally, Sir Hugo, who might have imagined they had already spoken, said, "Deronda, you will like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favorite Klesmer."
