Baldassarre wandered through the labyrinthine streets of Florence, seeking the cheapest shelter available. His feet, weary from travel, were drawn inevitably toward the uneven terrain outside the Via de' Bardi, known locally as the Hill of San Giorgio. Here, the ground sloped downward, offering a vantage point that pierced the city's roofline to reveal the distant hills and valleys usually hidden from view. Bratti had mentioned that Tito resided nearby, but for Baldassarre, the promise of a quiet corner atop the hill outweighed any specific address. The narrow, claustrophobic alleys of the city had intensified his sense of isolation and fractured memory; he craved the open sky.

As he climbed, he spotted a patch of jagged, uncultivated earth scarred by a landslide from the late thirteenth century. Rising from this broken ground stood a peculiar, truncated tower-like building with fluted tiles, adjacent to a small outhouse wedged against a ruin. Beneath the shade of a dying mulberry tree, an ancient, stooped woman untied a goat with two kids. A portion of the outbuilding housed livestock, while the other remained vacant save for scattered straw and tools. It was exactly what he needed.

Approaching the old woman, Baldassarre realized immediately that hearing was not her strong suit. Despite his shouts, she offered only muffled denials—"No lodging"—shaking her head with the patience of the deaf. Undeterred, Baldassarre persisted, explaining his status as a destitute foreigner who could not afford hostels and merely sought a bed of straw for the night, promising a few pennies weekly. After a moment of dubious contemplation, the woman produced a hatchet and pointed to a log half-buried under debris. "Chop that up," she instructed, "and you may sleep in the shed for one night."

Baldassarre agreed and fell to work. As he chopped, a figure watched from within the upper floor. Through a shutter closed tight against the chill, a small, round aperture revealed the eye of a child. This was Tessa. Perched precariously on a wobbly stool, she strained to catch every movement. Though forbidden to form acquaintances by her guardians, the allure of the stranger—the possibility of sharing a secret, of tasting freedom from her rigid routine—proved too powerful. She recalled her mother's warnings: *Obey, or your happiness turns to lead.* Yet, obedience paled before the thrill of a transgression. With a heart pounding with illicit joy, she descended the stairs, dressed in her finest green serge gown adorned with a red sash and silver pins, clutching a single gold ring that marked her preparation for a feast.

Descending with a lantern and a wooden bowl filled with macaroni flavored with cheese, Tessa kicked gently at the outhouse door. Inside, Baldassarre, lost in melancholy reverie, blinked in surprise at the intrusion. Before he could protest, Tessa burst forth, mimicking the deaf woman's volume. "I've brought you supper!" she cried, placing the bowl on his lap and positioning herself beside him. "Eat while I stay!"

Her assumption that he was deaf was charming in its innocence. Baldassarre, starving and moved by her earnest demeanor, accepted the food. As he ate, Tessa, forgetting her initial hesitation, leaned close, whispering conspiratorially, "Do you like it?" When he confirmed he heard her perfectly, her delight knew no bounds. She launched into a stream of chatter about her life: the kindness of Monna Lisa, the freedom from labor, the goats, and the mules she missed. Then, emboldened by his silence, she unveiled a heavy secret. "I have a little baby," she confessed, her voice dropping to a dramatic hush. "Born at Christmas. I'm married, but you must not tell anyone!"

Baldassarre, a man of the world, found her revelation both absurd and poignant. He guessed her husband's identity based on her terror, suspecting the notorious stepfather Nofri. Tessa erupted in laughter at his mistake, clapping her hands. "No, indeed! But I must not tell you what he is—he is not like Nofri!" She spoke of her husband with a mixture of reverence and longing, praying for his return, and described her infant with a tenderness that contrasted sharply with the grim reality of her household. She promised to bring the child the following day.

Before dawn, Tessa returned to the house, recounting her adventure to Monna Lisa. The old woman feigned reluctance, citing the displeasure of Messer Naldo, the master of the estate, yet eventually conceded. "If I let him stay and carry burdens for me, you must keep silent," she grumbled. Tessa nodded vigorously, declaring she would confide only in her "bimbo" (baby). They speculated on Naldo's whereabouts, fearing his return but hoping he remained absent until Baldassarre departed.

The next morning, Tessa rose with renewed industry, eager to share her "precious" gift. However, Monna Lisa had already tasked Baldassarre with fetching water, disrupting Tessa's plans. She hurried down the hill, insisting he sit on the straw to enjoy the sun and broth she had prepared, refusing further labor. Soon, she returned carrying the infant swaddled in cloth.

"This is my baby," she announced, seating herself beside Baldassarre. "Look how lively it is! Like the little Gesu." She held the bundle out, inviting him to touch it, though forbidding a kiss. "You might hurt him." Baldassarre, whose voice carried a strange, underlying threat beneath its apparent gentleness, took the child. "Poor thing!" he murmured, his gaze piercing. To him, this guileless girl appeared less as a friend and more as a creature trapped against a cruel world, someone who would require vengeance.

"Oh, do not pity me," Tessa protested, reclaiming the baby. "My husband is beautiful and good." When Baldassarre asked for his name, paralyzed by the weight of the question and the desire to remain anonymous, Tessa simply shrugged. "It matters not," she said kindly. "You are a stranger. Come back whenever you wish; we will be good to you."

As she departed, leaving the lantern aglow in the twilight, Baldassarre stared after her. To him, she was a fleeting rainbow against a storm-blackened sky, beautiful yet elusive, offering warmth to a man drowning in sorrow, unaware that her innocent curiosity had just entangled him in a web far deeper than mere hospitality.
