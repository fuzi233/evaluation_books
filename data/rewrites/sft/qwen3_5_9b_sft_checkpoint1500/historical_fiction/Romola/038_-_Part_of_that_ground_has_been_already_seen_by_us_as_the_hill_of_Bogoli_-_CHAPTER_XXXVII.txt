The Tabernacle Unlocked.

A sharp rap at the door broke Romola's slumber. The room was already flooded with the cold, hard light of dawn, and Maso stood waiting for her travelling-wallet. When she unbarred the door, the old man started visibly; the vision he had anticipated—the graceful figure crowned with living gold of hair—had vanished, replaced by the heavy folds of a grey mantle and a pale, shadowed countenance hidden beneath a dark cowl.

"It is well, Maso," Romola said, striving for a voice of absolute calm to steady his nerves. "The wallet is packed. Go on quietly, and I shall not be far behind. Once you have crossed the gates, you may walk slower; I doubt not I shall catch up to you before you reach Trespiano."

She let him pass and drew the bolt home. Then, her hand sought the key resting beside her bed—the original lock of the little painted tabernacle. Tito had failed to drown it in the river, and it had remained tucked away in the corner of his embroidered sash, worn over his purple tunic. Years ago, Romola had discovered it there, keeping it close without use, finding a quiet comfort in knowing it was within reach. Now, she turned to the cabinet where the shrine stood, pushed near the window where the morning sun illuminated the faded colors. She gazed at the figures with a fresh, bitter revulsion: the triumphant Bacchus, clutching the crowned Ariadne amidst clusters of grapes and vines; the Loves scattering roses over a wreathed vessel; the cunning-eyed dolphins in the rippling sea. All framed by a flowery border, mimicking a paradise garden. Yet here, on this chilling morning, they offered no sanctuary, only a pitiable mockery. *Foolish Ariadne,* she thought, *how could she believe that gaze held the secret of her life?* "She looks strangely transformed now," Romola murmured. "She would look ill at ease among these vines."

She picked up the mirror, staring at her reflection in the stark daylight. The image struck her with such jarring force that she lowered it, feeling a shrinking akin to her earlier flight from the joyous painting. To see her own face, veiled and hooded, terrified her anew with the dread of being dragged into the fold of some wretched superstition—to become part of the howling fanatics and weeping nuns whom she had despised since childhood. With a tremor in her hands, she inserted the key, unlocked the shrine, and withdrew the crucifix without looking closely at it. She threaded a cord through its ring, fastened it around her neck, and buried it deep within the bosom of her mantle. *"For Dino's sake,"* she whispered to the empty room.

Still, the tasks remained. The letters Maso was to carry back to Bologna needed writing. They were brief, yet final. For Tito, she penned: "Tito, my love for you is dead; and therefore, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not try to enforce any laws to fetch me back; it would bring you no happiness. The Romola you married can never return. I need say nothing further after what I told you when we last spoke. If you thought those words born of transient anger, know now that they mark an irreversible change. I request that my bridal chest be sent to my godfather, who gave it me. It holds my wedding clothes and portraits of my parents." She folded the ring inside the parchment, addressing Tito's name to the outside.

For Bernardo del Nero, her "second father," she wrote: "Dearest Godfather, if staying could have served your life, I would not have fled. But now I am gone. Ask not the reason, and if you love my father, hinder anyone from seeking me. I could not endure life in Florence, nor can I bear to tell why. Cover my fate in silence. I have requested that my bridal chest be sent to you; its contents will reveal the truth. Please entrust all things belonging to my mother to my cousin Brigida, and beg her to forgive me for failing to bid her farewell. Farewell, my second father. The best gift I possess remains the memory of your kindness."

With the letters and the crucifix secured in her mantle, Romola felt the weight of departure lift. No soul stirred in the house. Descending the stairs and stepping onto the silent street, she moved with the ghostliness of a grey phantom. Though her heart hammered violently, she relished the sensation of her firm tread on the broad paving stones—the swift motion of a resolution finally unleashed. The urgent drive to complete this act, coupled with the fear of obstacles, drowned out sorrow. As she approached the Ponte Rubaconte, the church of Santa Croce seemed less a looming presence in her view than the widening yellow streak of dawn cutting through the grey mist. Unless she hastened, she risked meeting eyes.

Her direct route led straight to the Borgo Pinti, then along the walls to the Porta San Gallo, passing through the Piazza di Santa Croce. Yet she walked with relentless speed, refusing to glance toward the church. The thought that curious eyes might fix upon her, or indifferent minds speculate on her private ruin, caused her to recoil as from physical torture. She felt degraded by the act of her husband from which she suffered so deeply. Yet, no windows watched her; no eyes noticed the tall grey sister moving with the firm step and proud, hooded attitude of a nun. Her path skirted the early traffic, and when she neared the gate, a dispute over tolls for egg baskets created a perfect distraction for her exit.

Beyond the houses of the Borgo, she was free from the last fringe of Florence. Above her, the sky stretched wide and open. She had entered a new existence—one of loneliness and endurance, yes, but of freedom. She had been strong enough to sever the bonds accepted in blind faith. Whatever came next, she would never again feel the warmth of hated lips or the stifling breath of an odious mind. The biting winter air, the bare, sombre hills, were welcome in their severity; they held no ghosts of beauty or joy, gods she had forever renounced.

Suddenly, however, the light surged with fierce intensity, casting long, shifting shadows across the road. It seemed the sun intended to banish the gloom. Perhaps the divine presence is never felt more acutely than in moments when light instantly animates the shadows. A profound awe, inherent to this momentous act, grew in Romola as she stood in the sudden radiance of golden glory and confronted the long, shrouded shadow of herself that was henceforth unescapable. So far, she had encountered only occasional country folk with mules, and the winding road had kept Maso's location obscure. But having passed Pietra and climbed onto rising ground, she raised the brim of her cowl to peer ahead.

Instantly, she lowered it again. Before her stood not Maso, but two monks advancing within a few yards. The hood had acted as a penthouse over her brow, blinding her to anything above eye level; thus, for the last moments, she had stared only at the gleam of the path and her own tall, spectral shadow.

Regret seized her. Monks were particularly unwelcome in her guise; they might demand pious passwords unknown to her. She forced a mask of unconscious awareness until the black hems of their mantles swept past. The encounter made her pulse race unpleasantly. There lay a gnawing unease in her religious disguise, a shame at this studied concealment, sharpened by the effort to remain invisible despite their likely scrutiny.

Yet the monks, walking downhill, would soon vanish. Seeing a large flat stone leaning against a cypress tree atop a grassy embankment, the slight shock of the meeting spurred her to sit and rest. Turning her back on Florence, she resolved not to look back until the monks were lost from sight. Lifting the edge of her cowl slightly, she spotted Maso and his mules in the distance, a gap not insurmountable, for the old man would surely linger, expecting her. For now, she paused. Free and alone, she breathed the crisp air.
