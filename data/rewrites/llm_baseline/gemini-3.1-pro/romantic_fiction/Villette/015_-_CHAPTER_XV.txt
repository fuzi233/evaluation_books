Following Madame Beck’s grand fête—with its three preceding weeks of festive preparation, its brief twelve hours’ burst of hilarity and culinary indulgence, and its one subsequent day of utter languor—came a period of severe reaction. The wheel of the year turned toward the final two months of the *année scolaire*, a season of close, hard study that mirrored the arduous agrarian harvests beyond the city limits. Into these weeks was concentrated the main burden of preparation for the public examinations. Candidates for rewards had to labor in earnest; masters and teachers set their shoulders to the grinding stone. A showy demonstration—a telling exhibition for the public eye, much like a market-day display of prized, unblemished fruits—had to be cultivated, and all means were fair to this end.

I scarcely noted the methods of the other teachers, for my own task was a heavy exercise in linguistic dominance. I was required to imbue ninety sets of brains with the rigid structures of the English language, forcing ninety tongues to abandon the rounded, earthy vowels of their ancestral Walloon and Flemish dialects in favor of the lisping, hissing dentals of the Isles. It felt, at times, like participating in a slow, irreversible erasure, burying the nuances of their native cultural memory beneath the blunt weight of a dominant, foreign lexicon. 

The examination-day arrived—an awful day of silent, compact preparation. The rich aromas of daily life were replaced by the starchy, sterile scent of freshly ironed wool and tight white collars. I felt uniquely doomed, for the main trial fell on me alone among the female staff. The professor of literature, M. Paul, took the entirety of the examining duties upon himself. This school autocrat gathered all the reins into the hollow of his hand, irefully rejecting any colleague’s assistance. Even Madame Beck, who possessed a profound and meticulous understanding of geography, was forced to succumb to her despotic kinsman’s direction. Yet, it irked him deeply that he could not manage English. He was obliged to leave that branch of education in my hands, which he did with a flash of naïve, possessive jealousy. He had a strong relish for public representation in his own person, but an extreme abhorrence of the like display in any other. When he could not quell the voices around him, he fumed like a bottled storm, his acoustic presence dominating the school’s corridors.

On the evening preceding the examination, I was walking in the garden. The sonic environment was typically a sanctuary, a fragile acoustic ecology of rustling pear trees and soft footfalls, though tonight it was faintly bruised by the distant, rhythmic thumping of the city’s emerging industrial mills. M. Emanuel joined me in the *allée défendue*. The sharp, earthy fragrance of his cigar announced him before the dark, menacing drape of his *paletôt* came into view. The tassel of his *bonnet grec* shadowed his left temple, and his blue eyes held a clouded glitter.

“Ainsi,” he began, abruptly fracturing the garden’s quiet, “vous allez trôner comme une reine; demain—trôner à mes côtés? Sans doute vous savourez d’avance les délices de l’autorité. Je crois voir en je ne sais quoi de rayonnante, petite ambitieuse!”

He was entirely mistaken. On me, school-triumphs shed but a cold, utilitarian lustre. I had wondered how it was that for him they seemed to shine with the primal warmth of a hearth-fire. Perhaps he cared for them too much; I, too little. Yet I liked to see M. Emanuel jealous; it woke his spirit and threw queer lights and shadows into his violet-azure eyes. His anger was artless and earnest, a raw dialect of its own. I asked merely where the English examination came in.

“I hesitate,” he said, his voice modulating to a lower, richer timbre, “whether at the very beginning, when your aspiring nature will not be gratified by a large audience, or quite at the close, when everybody is tired, and only a jaded and worn-out attention will be at your service.”

“Que vous êtes dur, Monsieur!” I said.

“One ought to be ‘dur’ with you. Other people in this house see you pass, and think that a colourless shadow has gone by. As for me, I scrutinized your face once, and it sufficed.”

“You are satisfied that you understand me?”

Without answering directly, he pressed on. “Were you not gratified when you succeeded in that vaudeville? I watched you and saw a passionate ardour for triumph. What fire shot into the glance! Je me tiens pour averti.”

“What feeling I had on that occasion, Monsieur, was quite abstract. I hated the part you assigned me. I had not the slightest sympathy with the audience below the stage. Will the examination be anything but a task to me—a task I wish well over?”

“Shall I take it out of your hands?”

“With all my heart; if you do not fear failure.”

“But I should fail. I only know three phrases of English: de sonn, de mone, de stare—est-ce bien dit? My opinion is that it would be better to give up the thing altogether: to have no English examination, eh?”

“If Madame consents, I consent.”

He smoked in silence, the rich aroma of his tobacco mingling with the scent of damp soil. Then he turned.

“Donnez-moi la main,” he said. The jealousy melted from his face, replaced by a generous kindliness. “Come, we will not be rivals, we will be friends. The examination shall take place, and I will choose a good moment. After all, you are solitary and a stranger; you have your bread to earn. We will be friends: do you agree?”

“Out of my heart, Monsieur. I am glad of a friend.”

“Pauvrette!” he murmured, turning away to leave the alley to its natural quiet.

The examination passed; M. Paul was as good as his word, managing the acoustic space of the hall to make my part easy. The next day came the distribution of prizes, and then the pupils scattered. The great seasonal exhalation of the school occurred, and the long vacation began. 

Madame Beck departed for the sea-side; the teachers took refuge with their families; M. Paul set forth on a pilgrimage to Rome. The vast, echoing house was left entirely empty, but for me, the servant Goton, and a deformed, imbecile pupil—a sort of crétin whose stepmother in a distant province refused her return.

My heart nearly died within me. The September days stretched out, stripped of their usual domestic rhythms. The desolate premises were horrifyingly silent, devoid of the polyphonic hum of daily life. The acoustic void felt unnatural, a deafening vacuum that crushed my spirits. I had long been sinking, and now, with the prop of daily employment withdrawn, I fell fast into a hopeless desert. 

The crétin, however, did not seem unhappy. I anchored myself in the ancient rituals of sustenance to manage my despair, doing my utmost to feed her well. I spent hours in the quiet kitchens, preparing rustic, agrarian dishes—thick pottages of heritage root vegetables, slowly simmered bone broths rich with marrow, and dark, fermented breads. But her weak faculties approved only of inertion; she consumed these historically rich meals as mere utilitarian fuel, lacking any sensory appreciation for the meticulously sourced ingredients or the olfactory landscapes I tried to build for her. To her, food was merely mass to fill a void. As she rarely spoke, sitting for hours moping and distorting her features, associating with her was like being prisoned with a tameless animal. The physical and mental tax was immense, wasting my inclination to swallow a meal myself, sending me faint to the fresh air and the fountain in the court. 

When the crétin’s aunt finally arrived to take the hapless creature away, I was free to walk out. I ventured beyond the city gates, wandering far along *chaussées*, through fertile fields and beyond old cemeteries. The harvest was in full swing, but my want of companionship maintained a most deadly famine in my soul. I walked through the burning noon and the arid afternoon, surrounded by the sights and smells of the harvest, yet utterly disconnected from its cycle.

I would picture Ginevra Fanshawe on her southward tour. These September suns shone for her on fertile plains where ancient vines and grain matured under a mellow beam. I imagined her savoring the terroir of distant regions, sustained by the fine chain of mutual understanding she shared with her faithful hero. But for me, the autumn suns and harvest moons brought no comfort. The fundamental untranslatability of my subjective experience isolated me. I possessed no dialect to convey my grief, no lexicon to bridge the gap between my soul and the world. 

At last, the Indian summer collapsed. The equinoctial storms began, raging for nine dark and wet days. The sonic environment became a turbulent, deafening assault of sounding hurricane, mirroring the abrasive noise of modern industry and crushing me with a deadlier paralysis. I lay in my bed in a strange fever of the nerves and blood. Sleep went quite away.

When sleep finally came, it brought an avenging dream that wrung my frame with unknown anguish. By the clock of St. Jean Baptiste, a cup was forced to my lips. It was black, strong, and strange. It tasted of rusted iron and drowned roots, an impossibly bitter brew steeped in the cold brine of a sunless ocean—suffering, brewed in a measure beyond mortal calculation. I drank, and amidst the horrors, I met the well-loved dead. But they were alienated from me; they spoke in forgotten tongues I could no longer decipher, leaving my spirit galled with an unutterable despair.

The next morning, the storm’s acoustic violence had softened to a weeping rain. Goton brought me a cup of tea, its thin, commercial aroma devoid of comfort, and urged me to call a doctor. I refused; no sterile, utilitarian medicine could cure this profound misalignment of the soul. 

By evening, the stillness of the long dormitory became unbearable. The white beds turned into spectres, their coronals becoming sun-bleached death’s-heads. Feeling that the trial God had appointed me was reaching its climax, I wrapped myself in a warm cloak and fled the crushing slab of the house-roof. As I passed through the twilight city, the deep, resonant bells of an old Catholic church arrested me. It was a sound of ancient acoustic ecology, organizing time not by the mechanical grinding of factory gears, but by spiritual and communal rhythms. 

I went in. The pervading gloom was purpled by stained glass, the air thick with the centuries-old fragrance of melted beeswax, damp stone, and lingering incense. Any spectacle of sincere worship was as welcome to me then as the most elemental bread to a starving body. I knelt on the stone pavement.

When the *salut* concluded, a holy, resonant quiet sank upon the remaining worshippers. I watched as they approached the confessional, whispering their avowals into the carved wood, their subjective burdens temporarily translated and absolved. A pale lady kindly urged me forward.

Mechanically obedient, I took her place. The priest within, a grey-haired Frenchman with an intelligent brow, inclined his ear with the phlegm of custom. I hesitated. I had no grasp of his formula, no mastery of his religious lexicon. The power dynamics of our language lay bare as I spoke my foreign truth into the dark grate: 

“Mon père, je suis Protestante.”

He turned directly, startled by this breach in his routine. He inquired why I had come.

I explained that I was perishing for a word of advice, craving an accent of comfort in a world whose sonic and emotional frequencies had grown intolerably harsh. I outlined the crushing weight of my solitary affliction, the inability to translate my despair into any language the world around me could comprehend.

“You take me unawares,” he said thoughtfully. “This makes a great break in the common course of confession.”

The mere relief of communication—pouring pent-up pain into a sentient, human vessel—had already solaced me. 

“Must I go, father?” I asked.

“My daughter,” he said, his compassionate voice bridging the linguistic divide, “for the present you had better go: but your words have struck me. Holy men have bidden penitents like you to hasten their path upward by penance, self-denial, and difficult good works. Tears are given them here for meat and drink—bread of affliction and waters of affliction. It is my conviction that these impressions are messengers to bring you back to the true Church. Protestantism is altogether too dry, cold, prosaic for you. On no account would I lose sight of you. Return to me again.”

As I rose to thank him, he signed me to wait. 

“You must not come to this church,” he said, noting the chill in the ancient stone. He gave me his address. “Come to my house. Be there to-morrow morning at ten.”

I bowed, pulled down my veil, and gathered my cloak around me, gliding back into the wet, fading sounds of the autumn night.
