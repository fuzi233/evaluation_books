When Romola reached the doorway of San Marco, a friar was already waiting, as if her coming had been counted like the strokes of a bell. Monna Brigida slipped away into the adjoining church, and Romola was led through the outer cloister to the chapter-house door—the farthest place a woman might be taken. Beyond this threshold the convent kept its rules as strictly as it kept its silence.

The door opened. A dim, sifted light lay over the stone, mingling with the small living flame of two tapers set behind a low truckle-bed. The air was stale with wax and damp wool, with that faint medicinal sourness of a sickroom where the body has grown too light for its own bones. Propped almost upright, a monk lay with a little crucifix lifted before his eyes, his fingers clinging to it as a starving man clings to bread. His face was sharpened to the look of something carved and then left too long in wind: the cheek hollow, the lips drawn, the hazel eyes sunk deep as wells gone dry. Above that suffering face, the tonsured crown still held a ring of golden hair.

Romola, throwing back her veil, was aware in one blurred instant of another monk standing near the bed with his black cowl pulled low, and of a pale crucified figure painted high on the wall, sorrowful faces beneath it watching from their frescoed dusk. But all those impressions were swallowed up as the dying man’s eyes lifted from the crucifix and met hers.

“Dino!” she said—low, like a cry caught in the throat.

She did not bend to him. She held herself straight, two paces away, as if that distance were a last defense. The monk’s habit on her brother’s body struck her like a brand: the visible sign of undutiful flight that had left her father desolate; the sign of a superstition that dared to call desertion holiness. In her father’s house, truth had been a daily bread, sliced clean by reason, without the Church’s blessing. The Church belonged, in Romola’s mind, to the crowded life of the many from which they had stood apart; she had never learned to see in it anything but a power that fed on fear and asked for obedience in place of understanding. And yet her heart—so apt to cling—searched that wasted face for the boy she had loved, for some warmth that might soften the years.

The monk looked at her as if he were looking through her, like one who revisits earth only in memory. There was no bright leap of recognition, no tremor of a brother’s joy. Only a remote steadiness, as if his soul had already stepped beyond the body and spoke back through it with effort.

“My sister,” he said, the words coming distinctly but with breaks, as though each syllable cost breath. “It is well thou hast not longer delayed to come; for I have a message to deliver to thee, and my time is short.”

At “message” her heart moved, half-opening in spite of itself. She thought of her blind father sitting among his books and marbles, fingers feeling for what eyes could no longer find; she thought of the hunger in that proud, lonely life for one more voice from the son who had left. Now, at the last, there might be repentance; there might be the plain human questions—How did blindness come? Was he in pain? Did he still remember?—and some tender word, imperfect but precious, to lay like oil on old wounds.

“Romola,” the monk began again, “I have had a vision concerning thee. Thrice I have had it in the last two months: each time it has been clearer. Therefore I came from Fiesole, deeming it a message from heaven that I was bound to deliver. And I gather a promise of mercy to thee in this, that my breath is preserved in order to—”

His chest labored; breath broke him off. The word “vision” cooled her as if a wet cloth had been laid on her heart. Her father’s bitter contempt for such things rose in her like an instinct. She could not keep silence.

“Dino, I thought you had some words to send to my father. You forsook him when his sight was failing; you made his life very desolate. Have you never cared about that—never repented? What is this religion of yours, that places visions before natural duties?”

The hazel eyes turned slowly and rested on her. He seemed to weigh whether answer was worth his failing strength. At last he spoke, and his voice had the same low, passionless remoteness, as if death had drained him of the human heat that makes words plead.

“No,” he said. “No; I have never repented fleeing from the stifling poison-breath of sin that was hot and thick around me, and threatened to steal over my senses like besotting wine. My father could not hear the voice that called me night and day; he knew nothing of the demon-tempters that tried to drag me back from following it. My father has lived amidst human sin and misery without believing in them: he has been like one busy picking shining stones in a mine, while there was a world dying of plague above him. I spoke, but he listened with scorn. I told him the studies he wished me to live for were either childish trifling—dead toys—or else they must be made warm and living by pulses that beat to worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts; for worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts made all the substance of the poetry and history he wanted me to bend my eyes on continually.”

Romola’s blood rose. She could not let that touch her father.

“Has not my father led a pure and noble life, then?” she burst out, the words springing with the quick heat of belief. “He has sought no worldly honours; he has been truthful; he has denied himself luxuries; he has lived like one of the ancient sages. He never wished you to live for worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts; he wished you to live as he himself has done—according to the purest maxims of philosophy in which he brought you up.”

The flush came into her face, and her whole frame quivered. It was a defense spoken partly from the deep habit of reverencing her father’s life, partly from that youthful certainty which repeats what it has been taught as one repeats a recipe known by heart: measured, established, beyond dispute.

Fra Luca looked at her long, and the strangeness of his passionless gaze made her own passion seem solitary, unheard.

“What were the maxims of philosophy to me?” he said at last. “They told me to be strong, when I felt myself weak; when I was ready, like the blessed Saint Benedict, to roll myself among thorns and court smarting wounds as a deliverance from temptation. For the Divine love had sought me, and penetrated me, and created a great need in me—like a seed that wants room to grow. I had been brought up in carelessness of the true faith; I had not studied the doctrines of our religion; but it seemed to take possession of me like a rising flood. I felt that there was a life of perfect love and purity for the soul; in which there would be no uneasy hunger after pleasure, no tormenting questions, no fear of suffering.

“Before I knew the history of the saints, I had a foreshadowing of their ecstasy. For the same truth had penetrated even into pagan philosophy: that it is a bliss within the reach of man to die to mortal needs, and live in the life of God as the Unseen Perfectness. But to attain that I must forsake the world: I must have no affection, no hope, wedding me to that which passeth away; I must live with my fellow-beings only as human souls related to the eternal unseen life. That need was urging me continually: it came over me in visions when my mind fell away weary from the vain words which record the passions of dead men: it came over me after I had been tempted into sin and had turned away with loathing from the scent of the emptied cup. And in visions I saw the meaning of the Crucifix.”

He paused, fighting for breath. The sound of his breathing filled the little chamber; it was like an uneven rasp against stone, a harsh reminder that life itself is a kind of labor. Romola felt the futility of argument as one feels the futility of offering food to a man who has chosen fasting for salvation. Her mind could not reach his mind; it was as if she stretched her hand and met only shadow.

When he spoke again, his chest had quieted a little.

“I felt whom I must follow,” he said; “but I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who professed to have renounced the world, my soul would be stifled with the fumes of hypocrisy, and lust, and pride. God had not chosen me, as he chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil in the Church and in the world. He called upon me to flee. I took the sacred vows and I fled—fled to lands where danger and scorn and want bore me continually, like angels, to repose on the bosom of God. I have lived the life of a hermit; I have ministered to pilgrims; but my task has been short: the veil has worn very thin that divides me from my everlasting rest. I came back to Florence that—”

“Dino,” Romola interrupted, a sudden ache cutting through her anger, “you did want to know if my father was alive?”

It was as if she offered him, at last, a mouthful of the plain bread of home—something real, something human, something that could be shared.

“—That before I died I might urge others of our brethren to study the Eastern tongues, as I had not done, and go out to greater ends than I did; and I find them already bent on the work,” he went on, untouched by her yearning. “And since I came, Romola, I have felt that I was sent partly to thee—not to renew the bonds of earthly affection, but to deliver the heavenly warning conveyed in a vision. For I have had that vision thrice. And through all the years since first the Divine voice called me, while I was yet in the world, I have been taught and guided by visions. For in the painful linking together of our waking thoughts we can never be sure that we have not mingled our own error with the light we have prayed for; but in visions and dreams we are passive, and our souls are as an instrument in the Divine hand. Therefore listen, and speak not again—for the time is short.”

Her mind recoiled. Yet as he spoke, another presence moved into the room. The cowled figure that had shifted away at her entrance returned and took his place on the far side of the bed, the cowl still shadowing his face.

“Kneel, my daughter,” said a strong, rich voice, startling after the dying whisper. “For the Angel of Death is present, and waits while the message of heaven is delivered: bend thy pride before it is bent for thee by a yoke of iron.”

It was not the harsh bark of command; it was calm certainty, a voice that seemed to sit in the air as naturally as a bell note sits in a piazza at evening—claiming space, ordering attention. The very sound made Romola’s nerves vibrate. She turned toward the speaker. His face was hidden, but her eyes fell on his hands where they lay folded against the black mantle: hands of an almost transparent delicacy, beautiful as if fashioned for fine work—turning pages, lifting a chalice, laying a wet cloth on fevered lips. There was something in them that pleaded against hardness.

He leaned forward. The right hand took the crucifix gently from Fra Luca’s tiring grasp; the left lifted a wet sponge and touched it to the dying man’s lips with practiced tenderness. In bending, the cowl fell back, and the taperlight struck his features full.

The face was one made for the crowd’s description and the crowd’s memory: high arched nose, a prominent under-lip, thick dark hair like a coronet above the brow—signs of energy and passion; and blue-grey eyes that shone mildly beneath auburn lashes—eyes that suggested acute sensibility, like the fine hands. Romola knew him then. It was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of San Marco—he whom she had chiefly disliked among monks because he was more noisy than the rest, his voice sounding in Florence like a trumpet that would not be silenced.

Her rebellion gathered itself. Yet the look of his eyes, the quiet possession of his tone, worked against her resistance.

“Kneel, my daughter,” he repeated, “the pride of the body is a barrier against the gifts that purify the soul.”

He looked at her with mild fixity, as if his will made a still place in her. She felt the strange pressure of a personality that moves others without touch. Slowly, unwillingly, she sank to her knees. And in the very act of yielding—like lowering one’s head to pass beneath a lintel—something in her inner posture changed. Her proud erectness was gone, and with it a portion of her defiance; she found herself passive, as if the air in that room had thickened into a medium in which her own will could not strike out so sharply.

Fra Luca began again.

“Romola, in the deep night, as I lay awake, I saw my father’s room—the library—with all the books and the marbles and the leggio where I used to stand and read; and I saw you—you were revealed to me as I see you now, with fair long hair, sitting before my father’s chair. And at the leggio stood a man whose face I could not see. I looked, and looked, and it was a blank to me, even as a painting effaced. And I saw him move and take thee, Romola, by the hand; and then I saw thee take my father by the hand; and you all three went down the stone steps into the streets, the man whose face was a blank to me leading the way.

“And you stood at the altar in Santa Croce, and the priest who married you had the face of death; and the graves opened, and the dead in their shrouds rose and followed you like a bridal train. And you passed on through the streets and the gates into the valley, and it seemed to me that he who led you hurried you more than you could bear, and the dead were weary of following you, and turned back to their graves.

“And at last you came to a stony place where there was no water, and no trees or herbage; but instead of water, I saw written parchment unrolling itself everywhere, and instead of trees and herbage I saw men of bronze and marble springing up and crowding round you. And my father was faint for want of water and fell to the ground; and the man whose face was a blank loosed thy hand and departed: and as he went I could see his face; and it was the face of the Great Tempter.

“And thou, Romola, didst wring thy hands and seek for water, and there was none. And the bronze and marble figures seemed to mock thee and hold out cups of water, and when thou didst grasp them and put them to my father’s lips, they turned to parchment. And the bronze and marble figures seemed to turn into demons and snatch my father’s body from thee, and the parchments shrivelled up, and blood ran everywhere instead of them, and fire upon the blood, till they all vanished, and the plain was bare and stony again, and thou wast alone in the midst of it. And then it seemed that the night fell and I saw no more...

“Thrice I have had that vision, Romola. I believe it is a revelation meant for thee: to warn thee against marriage as a temptation of the enemy; it calls upon thee to dedicate thyself—”

The pauses grew longer, the breaks more cruel. A fit of gasping seized him; his eyes clung to the crucifix as if it were the last light in a narrowing tunnel. When the spasm eased, he forced out more words, thin and barely audible.

“To renounce the vain philosophy and corrupt thoughts of the heathens: for in the hour of sorrow and death their pride will turn to mockery, and the unclean gods will—”

The phrase died, unfinished, like a candle snuffed by a sudden draught.

Romola’s reason told her it was no more than a dream—youthful memory and ascetic certainty baked together in the hot oven of fever. Yet the vision’s images struck her nerves like painful cries heard in the night: not to be answered, not to be forgotten. And she had never before sat so close to death. Her life had been sombre, but it had not been torn by the extremity of pain; she had not watched breath grow scant and precious, each draw a struggle. The frescoed sorrow-faces on the wall seemed to press nearer, and to join the pale face on the bed in one company. The air itself felt hushed, as if the room listened for the last syllable.

“Frate,” said the dying voice at last.

Fra Girolamo bent down. There was silence. The soundscape of that small chamber narrowed to the slight crackle of the tapers and the irregular rasp of breath.

“Romola,” the voice came again.

She leaned forward, her heart tightening. Again silence—words fighting and failing behind lips that could no longer carry them.

“Fra Girolamo, give her—” the dying man tried.

“The crucifix,” said Fra Girolamo, and his voice, steady, seemed to lay a plank across the gulf that was opening.

No other sound came from Fra Luca. The gaze that had been fastened on the crucified figure loosened, went vacant, and ceased.

“Dino!” Romola cried—low, but piercing—as the certainty fell on her that the silence of misunderstanding could never be broken, that all the human words she had wanted were now locked away where no pleading could reach them.

After a few minutes—minutes that were like a long, slow toll—Fra Girolamo said, “Take the crucifix, my daughter. His eyes behold it no more.”

Romola stretched out her hand. The touch of the small carved wood seemed to ease the strained cord within her. A great sob broke from her, the first full yielding of her pride to grief. She bent her head beside her dead brother and wept aloud. It seemed to her that daylight itself must be altered forever by this first close vision of death—that even the familiar sounds of home, the scrape of a chair on stone, the turning of a page, the pouring of water into a cup, would never again be free of what she had heard here: breath failing, silence final.

Fra Girolamo moved to the door and called in a lay Brother waiting outside. Then he came back and spoke to Romola with gentle command.

“Rise, my daughter, and be comforted. Our brother is with the blessed. He has left you the crucifix, in remembrance of the heavenly warning—that it may be a beacon to you in the darkness.”

She rose trembling. She drew her veil over her head as if to wrap herself back into the world she knew, and hid the crucifix under her mantle, close against her breast.

Fra Girolamo led her out into the cloistered court. Now the sky was a deep bowl of night, the stars cold and innumerable; a lantern burned near the entrance, and its small light made a bright island on the stones. Several figures in the dress of dignified laymen were grouped there—frequenters of San Marco who had come to visit the Prior and, hearing he kept vigil in the chapter-house, had waited.

Romola passed them as if through water. She was dimly aware of footsteps shifting, of rustle and murmur, of the Prior’s voice saying low, “Our brother is departed,” and of a hand laid on her arm as though to guide her. The great quiet of the convent held even these men’s movements in check, the way a countryside at night compels softness in speech. Then the door opened, and she was out in the wide piazza of San Marco with only Monna Brigida beside her and the servant bearing the lantern.

The open space, the freer air, revived her—as if the lungs, tight in that room of dying breath, could now fill again. What she had seen remained vivid, painfully distinct, but it began to draw in upon itself as the ordinary impressions of life returned: the uneven stone underfoot, the faint night smells of Florence—dust, old walls, a trace of smoke—and the far, living city-sounds that made a world beyond convent silence. She quickened her steps with nervous longing to be again with her father—and with Tito; for were they not together while she had been away? The grotesque images of the vision clung like a bad dream not shaken off, and yet they drove her all the more toward the beloved faces and familiar voices that would tell her she was awake.

Tito, we know, was not with Bardo. His destiny, in that same hour, was being shaped by guilty consciousness and the despairing belief that Romola already held a knowledge which must part them at last. And the lips that could have carried that knowledge to her—the simplest human words, asked and answered in the warmth of kinship—were closed forever.

So the prevision Fra Luca had given Romola was one drawn from that shadowy region where souls seek wisdom apart from the human sympathies that are the very substance of wisdom; while the revelation that might have come through plain filial and brotherly affection had been carried into irrevocable silence.
