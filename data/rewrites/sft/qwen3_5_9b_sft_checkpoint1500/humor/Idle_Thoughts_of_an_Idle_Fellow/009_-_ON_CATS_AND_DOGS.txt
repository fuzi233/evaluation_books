The carnage inflicted upon me this morning defies description. It commenced with Gustavus Adolphus—a canine of considerable girth whom the servants down below affectionately shorten to "Gusty." While Gusty proves a splendid fellow in the open field, he is entirely unsuited for indoor living. His intentions are benevolent, yet his physical scale exceeds the boundaries of this residence. With a single stretch, he toppled two chairs and a what-not, leaving the room resembling the aftermath of a disastrous military invasion. Even his breathing sufficed to extinguish the fire.

At dinner, he crept beneath the table, remained motionless for a duration, and then erupted upward. Our sole indication of his movement was the table itself, which appeared determined to execute a somersault. We clutched it frantically, striving to maintain a horizontal orientation, only to witness his struggles intensify—driven by the delusion that a wicked conspiracy was forming against him. The result was invariably an overturned table, a ruined meal trapped between two sprawling layers of enraged humanity.

His return this morning followed his customary mode: akin to an American cyclone. Before I could fully register his approach, he swept my coffee cup from the table with his tail, showering the contents directly onto my waistcoat. I rose hastily, muttered a string of curses, and advanced at a rapid pace. Gusty, anticipating my wrath, turned toward the door. Just then, Eliza entered carrying a basket of eggs. Upon seeing Gusty, she emitted a sharp "Ugh!" and collapsed onto the floor. The eggs scattered across the carpet in various chaotic positions, and Gusty retreated, seemingly agreeing with my stern injunction to descend immediately and avoid me for at least an hour. I watched him dodge the coal-scoop and depart, returning to dry myself and finish breakfast. However, ten minutes later, peering into the passage, I discovered him perched atop the stairs. I ordered him down, but he merely barked and jumped, prompting me to investigate further.

Obstruction was provided by Tittums, our kitten, seated on the step below and refusing to yield passage. Tittums is roughly the size of a penny roll, currently vibrating with indignation and swearing with the fervor of a medical student.

She swears dreadfully. I admit to a minor proficiency in the art myself, yet I remain a mere amateur compared to her. Truth be told—and let this remain strictly confidential, lest my wife discover I voiced it—I find that swearing actually benefits a man. It serves as a necessary safety valve, allowing the toxic heat of bad temper to escape as harmless vapor before causing catastrophic damage to one's internal mental mechanisms. Consider a man who, instead of polite pleasantries, unleashes a torrent of vitriol regarding someone stepping on his toe. "Bless you, my dear, sweet sir... why did your light and delicate foot descend upon my corn with such force?" Once the explosion occurs, the man feels significantly lighter. Swearing operates on the same principle as smashing furniture or slamming doors; however, it is far more economical. A periodic release clears a man of his troubles much like a penworth of gunpowder cleans a chimney. I harbor a profound distrust for men who never swear, nor savage-kick footstools, nor poke fires with unnecessary violence. Without such outlets, the accumulated annoyances of daily life fester, transforming petty grievances into deep-seated sorrows and breeding hatred.

I directed this philosophy toward Tittums, admonishing her for bringing shame upon a Christian household, despite her youth. I cannot abide a kitten giving way to such passions, whereas an older cat's outburst is somewhat forgivable.

I pocketed Tittums and returned to my desk. Almost instantly, she escaped, scrambled onto the table, attempted to swallow a pen, dipped her leg into the inkpot, spilled the contents, licked her leg, and then swore again—this time specifically at me. I released her onto the floor, where Tim, a two-year-old fox-terrier, immediately engaged her in combat. I regret that Tim lacks discretion; his interference in matters that concern neither him nor the situation at hand is tiresome. Furthermore, he is hardly a saint himself, behaving with the airs of a seasoned Scottish collie despite his youth.

Fortunately, Tittums' mother arrived, and Tim sustained a scratch, a development I welcomed. I subsequently ejected the trio into the passage, where they are currently engaged in a fierce melee. My state is one of chaos, stained with ink and plagued by a foul temper. Should any further feline or canine disturbances arise this morning, I implore them to bring their own undertakers.

Nevertheless, I hold cats and dogs in high regard. As companions, they vastly surpass humans. They refrain from quarreling or arguing; they do not monopolize conversation with self-centered monologues. They never offer the vacuous observations that Miss Brown frequently makes regarding Mr. Jones's matrimonial prospects, nor do they confuse one's wife's cousin with her husband. They possess the social grace to ignore a young author burdened with fourteen tragedies and sixteen comedies, asking nothing of his creative output.

They are devoid of malice. They never point out our flaws "for our own good," nor do they gently remind us of past follies at inconvenient moments. They do not deliver the cynical verdict, "Oh, yes, a lot of use you are if you are ever really wanted." To them, we are eternally the same.

They radiate joy in our presence. Whether we are jubilant, solemn, or sorrowful, they mirror our moods. When we seek diversion, they leap and bark, offering endless opportunities for mischief. Conversely, when solitude is desired, a cat will purr quietly on a chair arm, while a dog curls upon the rug, blinking contentedly at the fire while vigilantly guarding against intruding rodents.

In times of despair, when we bury our faces in our hands, wishing for non-existence, they do not judge us with moral superiority. Instead, they approach softly, pressing their heads against ours. A cat will climb onto the shoulder, ruffle the hair, and communicate, "Lor, I am sorry for you, old man," with perfect clarity. A dog will gaze upward with his large, truthful eyes, conveying, "We are here. We will endure the world together."

A dog, alas, is notoriously imprudent. He cares not for justice or righteousness, nor for one's standing on life's ladder. He disregards wealth, wisdom, sin, or sainthood. To him, you are simply a pal. Consequently, regardless of fortune or disgrace, he remains steadfast, willing to comfort, guard, and sacrifice his life if necessary—a foolish, brainless, soulless creature.

And yet, consider the irony. Does he not know that he is merely an animal, devoid of intellect? Does he not realize that the gin-soaked loafer leaning against the post stands infinitely higher in intellectual capacity? Does he not comprehend that every petty, selfish scoundrel, every cheat and liar who lives by deception—the millions of them—are, in terms of intellect, far superior to him? These are *men*. And men are, by definition, the greatest, noblest, and wisest beings in the vast eternal universe. Any man will affirm this.

Yes, poor doggie, you are exceedingly stupid, especially when contrasted with the clever men who understand politics, philosophy, and the cosmos—everything, save for our own origins and destinies.

Never mind, pussy and doggie; we cherish you precisely because of your stupidity. Humanity harbors a deep affection for the simple-minded. Men struggle to tolerate clever women, and a woman's ideal partner is often a "dear old stupid." It brings genuine pleasure to encounter those less intelligent than oneself. The world becomes a kinder place for fools, whereas the realm of the intellectually gifted is often fraught with misery. Since the clever constitute such a negligible minority, their unhappiness matters little, provided the foolish majority can find solace.

Traditionally, cats are credited with worldly wisdom, prioritizing their own interests above blind devotion to friends. This self-interest shocks us. A cat truly loves a household with a kitchen carpet more than one without, and prefers the company of neighbors should many children be present. Yet, overall, cats are unjustly maligned. A true friend among cats remains loyal through thick and thin. I recall a feline companion who followed me everywhere, becoming so persistent that I had to beg her, as a personal favor, to cease accompanying me down High Street. She would await my return, meeting me in the passage, evoking feelings akin to marriage, albeit without the inquiries regarding my whereabouts or skepticism of my accounts.

I recount this not to praise the species, but to illustrate how nearly human some cats can appear. If transmigration of souls exists, this particular animal was rapidly qualifying for Christianity; her vanity rivaled her love for alcohol. On occasions when she captured a particularly large rat, she would drag it into the room, lay it amidst us, and wait expectantly for praise. The girls would scream with delight.

Poor rats! They seem to exist solely so that cats and dogs may claim credit for their demise, while chemists profit from poisons designed for their eradication. Nevertheless, there is a certain fascination, a weirdness and uncanniness attached to them. They are cunning, strong, numerous, cruel, and secretive.
