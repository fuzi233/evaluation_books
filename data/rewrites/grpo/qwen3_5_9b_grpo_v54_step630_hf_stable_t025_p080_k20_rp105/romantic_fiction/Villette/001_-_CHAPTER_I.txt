My godmother resided in a stately home within the pristine, ancient town of Bretton. Her husband's lineage had anchored itself there for generations, bearing the proud designation of Bretton of Bretton; whether by chance or due to a distant ancestor of significant standing who gifted his name to the neighborhood, remained a mystery to me.

As a child, I visited Bretton roughly twice a year, finding the place deeply appealing. The house and its inhabitants suited my temperament perfectly. The spacious, tranquil rooms, the orderly furniture, the expansive windows, and the balcony overlooking a magnificent, antique street—where Sundays and holidays seemed to linger eternally—created an atmosphere of profound quiet and cleanliness that soothed my soul.

In a household of adults, a single child is often cherished, and in a gentle manner, I was the recipient of Mrs. Bretton's affection. She had been widowed, leaving behind one son, before I had even known her; her husband, a physician, had passed away while she was still youthful and striking.

Though she was no longer young in my memory, she retained her beauty. She was tall, well-proportioned, and despite her dark complexion, which was somewhat unusual for an Englishwoman, her cheeks bore the luminous glow of robust health. Her eyes were a pair of fine, cheerful black orbs that sparkled with vitality. It was a common lament among acquaintances that she had not bestowed her fair skin upon her son. His eyes were blue—piercing even in boyhood—and his long hair, when illuminated by the sun, was described as golden. Yet, he inherited his mother's facial contours, her perfect teeth, her stature, and, most importantly, her unblemished health and a spirit of such steady equilibrium that it was worth more than any fortune.

It was during the autumn of a certain year that I stayed at Bretton. My godmother had traveled personally to retrieve me from the relatives where my permanent residence was situated. I suspect she then clearly foresaw events approaching, shadows of which I barely perceived, yet a faint suspicion was enough to cast an unsettling sadness over me, making the prospect of changing my scene and society a welcome relief.

Time moved with a smooth, gentle rhythm in my godmother's presence. It did not rush with tumultuous speed but flowed blandly, like a full river gliding through a plain. My visits mirrored the sojourn of Christian and Hopeful beside a pleasant stream, flanked by green trees and meadows adorned with lilies year-round. There was no charm of variety, nor the thrill of incident; yet, I loved peace so dearly and craved stimulation so little that when it finally arrived, I almost felt it as a disturbance, wishing it had remained at bay.

One day, a letter arrived that evidently startled and concerned Mrs. Bretton. I initially feared it was from home, trembling in anticipation of some disastrous news, but no reference was made to me, and the cloud of worry seemed to dissipate quickly.

The following day, upon returning from a long walk, I discovered an unexpected transformation in my bedroom. Alongside my own French bed in its shaded alcove, a small crib draped in white had appeared in the corner. Beside my mahogany chest of drawers stood a tiny rosewood chest. I paused, gazing and pondering. "What are these signs?" I asked myself. The answer was plain: "A second guest is arriving; Mrs. Bretton expects visitors."

Descending for dinner, explanations unfolded. A little girl would soon be my companion, the daughter of a friend and distant relative of the late Dr. Bretton. This child, it was added, had recently lost her mother. However, Mrs. Bretton soon clarified that the loss was not as devastating as it might first seem. Mrs. Home, as the mother was named, had been very pretty but giddy and careless, neglecting her child and disappointing her husband. Their union had proven so uncongenial that separation eventually occurred by mutual consent, without legal proceedings. Shortly thereafter, the lady, having over-exerted herself at a ball, caught a cold, developed a fever, and died after a brief illness. Her husband, a man of highly sensitive feelings, was shattered by the sudden news and could hardly believe that his own over-severity or lack of patience had hastened her end. He brooded over this guilt until his spirits were severely affected. Medical advice suggested travel as a remedy, and in the interim, Mrs. Bretton offered to care for his little girl. "And I hope," my godmother concluded, "the child will not be like her mother—a silly, frivolous little flirt that any sensible man would be foolish to marry. For," she added, "Mr. Home *is* a sensible man in his way, though not very practical. He loves science and spends half his life in a laboratory conducting experiments—a pursuit his butterfly wife could neither comprehend nor endure. Indeed," she confessed, "I should not have liked it myself."

Answering my curiosity, she revealed that her late husband had noted Mr. Home's scientific inclination stemmed from a maternal uncle, a French savant. The family was of mixed French and Scottish origin, with connections living in France, many bearing the title of 'de' and claiming nobility.

That same evening at nine o'clock, a servant was sent to meet the coach bringing our little visitor. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room awaiting her arrival; John Graham Bretton was absent, visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside. My godmother read the evening paper while I sewed. It was a wet night; rain lashed the windows, and the wind sounded angry and restless.

"Poor child!" Mrs. Bretton murmured occasionally. "What weather for her journey! I wish she were safe here."

Just before ten, the doorbell announced Warren's return. As soon as the door opened, I ran down the hall. There lay a trunk and band-boxes, beside which stood a figure resembling a nurse-girl. At the foot of the staircase, Warren carried a shawled bundle in his arms.

"Is that the child?" I asked.

"Yes, miss."

I reached out to open the shawl and glimpse the face, but it was hastily turned away toward Warren's shoulder.

"Put me down, please," a small voice commanded as Warren opened the drawing-room door. "And take off this shawl," the speaker continued, using its minute hand to extract the pin and removing the clumsy wrapping with a sort of fastidious haste. The creature that now appeared made a deft attempt to fold the shawl, but the drapery was far too heavy and large for those small hands and arms to manage. "Give it to Harriet, please," was the instruction, "and she can put it away." With that, the child turned and fixed her eyes on Mrs. Bretton.

"Come here, little dear," the lady said. "Come and let me see if you are cold and damp; come and let me warm you at the fire."

The child advanced promptly. Relieved of her wrapping, she appeared exceedingly tiny, yet a neat, completely fashioned little figure, light, slight, and straight. Seated on my godmother's ample lap, she looked like a mere doll; her neck, delicate as wax, and her head of silky curls increased the resemblance.

Mrs. Bretton spoke in tender phrases as she rubbed the child's hands, arms, and feet. Initially, the child regarded her with a wistful gaze, but soon a smile answered her. Mrs. Bretton was rarely a caressing woman; even with her deeply cherished son, her manner was seldom sentimental, often the opposite. But when the small stranger smiled at her, she kissed her, asking, "What is my little one's name?"

"Missy."

"But besides Missy?"

"Polly, papa calls her."

"Will Polly be content to live with me?"

"Not *always*; but till papa comes home. Papa is gone away." She shook her head expressively.

"He will return to Polly, or send for her."

"Will he, ma'am? Do you know he will?"

"I think so."

"But Harriet thinks not: at least not for a long while. He is ill."

Her eyes filled with tears. She drew her hand from Mrs. Bretton's and made a movement to leave her lap. It was initially resisted, but she said, "Please, I wish to go: I can sit on a stool."

She was permitted to slip down from the knee. Taking a footstool, she carried it to a corner where the shade was deep and seated herself. Mrs. Bretton, though commanding and peremptory in grave matters, was often passive in trifles; she allowed the child her way. She told me, "Take no notice at present." But I did take notice. I watched Polly rest her small elbow on her small knee, her head on her hand. I observed her draw a square inch or two of pocket-handkerchief from the doll-pocket of her doll-skirt, and then I heard her weep. Other children in grief cry aloud without shame or restraint, but this being wept: the tiniest occasional sniff testified to her emotion. Mrs. Bretton did not hear it, which was quite as well. Soon, a voice from the corner demanded, "May the bell be rung for Harriet!"

I rang; the nurse was summoned and came.

"Harriet, I must be put to bed," said her little mistress. "You must ask where my bed is."

Harriet indicated she had already made that inquiry.

"Ask if you sleep with me, Harriet."

"No, Missy," the nurse replied. "You are to share this young lady's room," designating me.

Missy did not leave her seat, but I saw her eyes seek mine. After some minutes of silent scrutiny, she emerged from her corner.

"I wish you, ma'am, good night," she said to Mrs. Bretton, but she passed me in mute silence.

"Good-night, Polly," I said.

"No need to say good-night, since we sleep in the same chamber," was the reply, with which she vanished from the drawing-room. We heard Harriet propose to carry her upstairs. "No need," was her answer again—"no need, no need:" and her small step toiled wearily up the staircase.

An hour later, when I went to bed, I found her still wide awake. She had arranged her pillows to support her little person in a sitting posture; her hands, placed one within the other, rested quietly on the sheet with an old-fashioned calm most unchildlike. I refrained from speaking to her for some time, but just before extinguishing the light, I recommended she lie down.

"By and by," was the answer.

"But you will take cold, Missy."

She took a tiny article of clothing from the chair beside her crib and covered her shoulders with it. I allowed her to do as she pleased. Listening in the darkness, I was aware that she still wept—wept under restraint, quietly and cautiously.

At dawn, a trickling of water caught my ear. There she was, risen and mounted on a stool near the washstand, with pains and difficulty inclining the ewer (which she could not lift) to pour its contents into the basin. It was curious to watch her as she washed and dressed, so small, busy, and noiseless. Evidently, she was little accustomed to performing her own toilet; the buttons, strings, hooks, and eyes presented difficulties she encountered with a perseverance good to witness. She folded her nightdress, smoothed the drapery of her couch quite neatly, and withdrew into a corner where the sweep of the white curtain concealed her. I half rose and advanced my head to see how she was occupied. On her knees, with her forehead bent on her hands, I perceived she was praying.

Her nurse tapped at the door. She started up.

"I am dressed, Harriet," she said. "I have dressed myself, but I do not feel neat. Make me neat!"

"Why did you dress yourself, Missy?"

"Hush! Speak low, Harriet, for fear of waking *the girl*" (meaning me, who now lay with my eyes closed). "I dressed myself to learn, against the time you leave me."

"Do you want me to go?"

"When you are cross, I have many a time wanted you to go, but not now. Tie my sash straight; make my hair smooth, please."

"Your sash is straight enough. What a particular little body you are!"

"It must be tied again. Please to tie it."

"There, then. When I am gone, you must get that young lady to dress you."

"On no account."

"Why? She is a very nice young lady. I hope you mean to behave prettily to her, Missy, and not show your airs."

"She shall dress me on no account."

"Comical little thing!"

"You are not passing the comb straight through my hair, Harriet; the line will be crooked."

"Ay, you are ill to please. Does that suit?"

"Pretty well. Where should I go now that I am dressed?"

"I will take you into the breakfast-room."

"Come, then."

They proceeded to the door. She stopped.

"Oh! Harriet, I wish this was papa's house! I don't know these people."

"Be a good child, Missy."

"I am good, but I ache here;" putting her hand to her heart, and moaning while she reiterated, "Papa! papa!"

I roused myself and started up to check this scene while it was yet within bounds.

"Say good-morning to the young lady," Harriet dictated. She said, "Good-morning," and then followed her nurse from the room. Harriet temporarily left that same day to visit her own friends, who lived in the neighborhood.
