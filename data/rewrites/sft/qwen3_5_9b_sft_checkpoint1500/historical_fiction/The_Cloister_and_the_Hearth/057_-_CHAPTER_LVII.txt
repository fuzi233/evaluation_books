A week had passed since their last meeting, yet the atmosphere within the lodging remained unchanged, if not more so. Seated side by side, Pietro and Gerard worked, though their spirits diverged sharply. Pietro attacked his canvas with erratic bursts of energy; he would produce a marvel in minutes, only to abandon it in a fit of rage, muttering curses against the medium before resuming his labor with renewed fury, ending each cycle with a heavy groan. In stark contrast, the shrewd Dutchman worked with a calm, unyielding steadiness, a quiet smile playing upon his lips as he guided his brush. To be blunt, Gerard had already tamed his companion; the friendship was, in truth, a partnership where Gerard held the reins, guiding Pietro's raw talent through the harness of discipline. Their craft was simple but effective: they painted playing cards to "boil the pot."

Once finished, the indignant master seized his creation to begin his daily rounds in search of a buyer. Gerard beseeched him to take the deck as well, urging him to try selling them. At first, Pietro recoiled, his expression contorted in a look of pure disdain, resembling a rattlesnake poised to strike. Yet, the warmth of the clime and the weight of Gerard's plea softened him. Eventually, embracing Gerard in the traditional Italian fashion, he agreed, first drying the freshly completed cards in the fierce sunlight that bathed their happy corner of the world.

Left alone, Gerard sketched a few Greek letters, then repaired a tear in his hose. The landlady observed this humble industry and, with an ironic lift of her brow, inquired whether there were truly no women in the house. "When you have finished that," she declared, "descend and speak with Teresa, my friend—of whom I have spoken. She possesses a husband who claims acquaintance with the great, though he is far from being such himself."

Gerard descended, and lo, standing before him was the very Roman matron he sought. "Ah, Madam," he greeted her warmly, "you? The good lady gave me no word of your presence. And the little fair-haired boy—is he well? Did the voyage in that strange vessel harm him?"

"He is well," the matron replied simply.

"Why, what are you two discussing?" the landlady interjected, staring alternately at Gerard and Teresa with wide eyes. "And why do you tremble so, *mia Teresa*?"

"He saved my child's life," Teresa answered, struggling to compose herself.

"What!" the landlady exclaimed. "My lodger? And he never uttered a word of this to me? Are you not ashamed to look me in the face?"

"Alas, speak not harshly to him," the matron pleaded gently. Turning to her friend, she poured forth a glowing account of Gerard's conduct, describing the selfless care he had shown. As she spoke, Gerard stood crimson, blushing like a schoolgirl, scarcely recognizing his own actions until gratitude painted them anew in her retelling.

"And to think you asked me to serve your lodger," Teresa lamented, addressing the landlady, "of whom I knew naught save that you held him in high regard. That was enough for me. My dear youth, in serving you, I serve myself."

An eager dialogue ensued between the two women, outlining a strategy to breach the formidable wall of fees, commissions, and legal chicanery that separated patrons of art from unknown talents in the Eternal City. Teresa, however, smiled with a sadness that touched Gerard's heart as she reflected on his naive approach to presenting his work at the doors of the powerful.

"What!" she cried, her voice sharp with reproach. "Without promising a share to the servants? Without offering even a coin to those who might introduce your wares to the Signori? It is as if you had cast them into the Tiber!"

"Well-a-day!" sighed Gerard, his shoulders slumping. "Then how shall an artist find a patron? For artists are poor, not rich."

"By seeking a city nobler, less greedy than this," Teresa advised wisely. "'La corte Romana non vuol' pecora senza lana.'"

She fell into contemplation, declaring she would return the next day. The landlady, sensing a shift in the wind, felicitated Gerard. "Teresa has something in her mind," she whispered, "something stirring."

Scarcely had Teresa departed when Pietro returned, carrying his picture, his countenance black as thunder. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him upstairs to offer comfort. "What, have they refused to accept your masterpiece?" Gerard asked.

"As heretofore," Pietro grumbled.

"More fools they, then."

"That is not the worse."

"Why, what troubles you?"

"They have bought the cards!" Pietro yelped, hammering the empty air furiously with his fists. "They were enraptured with them! They tried to hide their longing, but they could not. I saw it, I feigned ignorance, and I pillaged them! Curse those boobies!"

With dramatic flair, he flung a dozen small silver coins onto the floor, leaped upon them, dancing with basilisk-like eyes, kicking them assiduously until they spun and scattered across the room. Down knelt Gerard, following the maltreated innocents directly, transferring them tenderly back into his purse.

"You should have smiled at their ignorance and put it to profit," Gerard suggested softly.

"And so I will," Pietro vowed, his face a mask of concentrated indignation. "Those brutes! We shall paint a pack a day; we shall set the whole city gambling and ruining itself, living like princes upon their vices and stupidity." He paused, his gaze softening as he pointed to a specific card. "There was one queen, though—I had fain kept back. You limned her, brother. She possessed lovely red-brown hair, sapphire eyes, and above all, a soul."

"Pietro," Gerard said softly, "I painted that one from my heart."

The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling with a sudden understanding. "You love her so well, yet you leave her behind."

"Pietro," Gerard continued, his voice heavy with emotion, "it is precisely because I love her so dearly that I have wandered this weary road."

This poignant conversation was abruptly cut short by the landlady's cry from below: "Come down, you are wanted." Gerard obeyed, descending to find Teresa waiting once more.

"Come with me, Ser Gerard," she invited.
