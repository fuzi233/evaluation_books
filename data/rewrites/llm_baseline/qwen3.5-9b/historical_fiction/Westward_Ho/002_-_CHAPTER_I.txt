"The hollow oak our palace is, Our heritage the sea."

Any traveler who has wandered through the lush landscapes of North Devon knows the little white town of Bideford. It climbs gently upward from its wide tidal river, paved with golden sands, crossing the ancient, multi-arched bridge where salmon await the autumn floods. Beyond lies the pleasant upland to the west. Above the town, hills close in, cushioned with deep oak forests, through which jagged crags of fern-fringed slate protrude. Below, the terrain lowers into softly rounded knolls and fertile patches of red and green, sinking finally into the vast, hazy expanse of salt marshes and rolling sandhills where the Torridge meets the Taw. Together, they flow quietly toward the great surf of the bar and the eternal thunder of the Atlantic swell.

Pleasantly, the old town stands there beneath its soft, Italian-like sky, fanned day and night by the fresh ocean breeze. This wind keeps away the biting winter frosts and the fierce heat of the Midlands. It has stood thus for perhaps eight hundred years, since the first Grenville, a cousin of the Conqueror, returned from conquering South Wales. He gathered around him loyal Saxon serfs, free Norse rovers with their golden hair, and dark Silurian Britons from the shores of Swansea. Their mingled blood still grants the coastal people of the neighboring counties their strength, intellect, and even today, their distinctive beauty of face and form.

Yet, at the time of which I write, Bideford was more than merely a pleasant country town with a quiet quay. It was one of England's chief ports. It supplied seven ships to fight the Armada. Even more than a century later, chroniclers noted that "it sent more vessels to the northern trade than any port in England, saving (a strange juxtaposition!) London and Topsham." It was the center of a local civilization and enterprise, modest perhaps compared to the massive efforts of our own day, but who dares despise the day of small things if it proves to be the dawn of mighty ones?

England owes the foundation of her naval and commercial glory to the sea-life and labor of Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, Plymouth (then a mere village), and many other little western towns. It was the men of Devon—the Drakes, Hawkins, Gilberts, Raleighs, Grenvilles, and Oxenhams—a host of "forgotten worthies" whom we shall one day honor—that she owes her commerce, her colonies, and indeed her very existence. Had they not first crippled the ill-gotten resources of the Spaniard through their West Indian raids, and then crushed his final, huge effort in Britain's Salamis during the glorious fight of 1588, what would we be today but a papal appendage of a world tyranny as cruel as heathen Rome, and far more diabolical?

I write this book in memory of these men—their voyages, battles, faith, valor, heroic lives, and no less heroic deaths. Should I occasionally seem to warm into a style somewhat too stiff and pompous, please excuse me for the sake of my subject. It fits better to be sung than spoken, and proclaimed to all true English hearts not as a novel, but as an epic (which some man may yet undertake to write). It carries the same great message that the songs of Troy, the Persian Wars, and the trophies of Marathon and Salamis spoke to the hearts of all true Greeks of old.

One bright summer afternoon in the year of grace 1575, a tall and handsome boy lingered along the Bideford quay. Clad in a scholar's gown, carrying a satchel and slate, he watched wistfully the shipping and the sailors. Just as he passed the bottom of the High Street, he found himself opposite one of the many taverns overlooking the river. Inside an open bay window sat merchants and gentlemen discussing their afternoon drink of sack. Outside the door, a group of sailors had gathered, listening intently to someone standing in the midst of them.

Alive with interest in any news of the sea, the boy stepped up to join the sailor-lads peeping and whispering under the arms of the men. Thus, he caught the following speech, delivered in a loud, bold voice with a strong Devonshire accent and a fair sprinkling of oaths.

"If you don't believe me, go and see, or stay here and turn blue with mold. I tell you, as I am a gentleman, I saw it with these very eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo there through a window in the lower room. We measured the heap, as I am a christened man, seventy feet long, ten feet broad, and twelve feet high—silver bars! Each bar weighed between thirty and forty pounds."

"And what did Captain Drake say?"

"He said, 'There, my lads of Devon, I've brought you to the mouth of the world's treasure-house, and it's your own fault now if you don't sweep it out as empty as a stockfish.'"

"Why didn't you bring some of it home, then, Mr. Oxenham?"

"Why weren't you there to help carry it? We would have brought it away safely, and Young Drake and I had already broken the outer door. But Captain Drake went off in a dead faint. When we looked, he had a wound in his leg you could fit three fingers into, and his boots were full of blood, soaked for an hour or more. Yet his heart was set on fighting; he never knew until he collapsed. Then his brother and I carried him to the boats, him kicking and struggling, bidding us let him go on with the fight, though every step he took sank into a pool of blood. So we escaped. Tell me, ye sons of shotten herrings, wasn't it worth more to save him than the dirty silver? Silver we can get again, brave boys. There's more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave all the streets of the west country. But of such captains as Franky Drake, Heaven never makes but one at a time. If we lose him, goodbye to England's luck!"

The speaker was a tall, sturdy figure with a florid, black-bearded face and bold, restless dark eyes. Leaning against the wall with crossed legs and arms akimbo, he appeared to the schoolboy a true *magnifico*, some prince or duke at least. His dress defied all sumptuary laws of the time: a suit of crimson velvet, slightly worn; a long Spanish rapier and a pair of daggers with gaudy hilts at his side; fingers sparkling with rings; several gold chains about his neck; and large earrings in his ears, behind one of which a red rose was tucked jauntily among his glossy black curls. On his head sat a broad velvet Spanish hat, lacking a feather but fastened with a great gold clasp holding a whole Quetzal bird. Its gorgeous plumage of fretted golden-green shone like a single precious stone.

As he finished speaking, he removed the hat and looked at the bird.

"Look ye, my lads, have you ever seen such a fowl before? That is the bird the old Indian kings of Mexico allowed no one to wear but themselves. Therefore I wear it—I, John Oxenham of South Tawton—as a sign to all brave lads of Devon that, as the Spaniards are masters of the Indians, we are the masters of the Spaniards!" He replaced his hat.

A murmur of applause followed, though one voice hinted that the Spaniards were too numerous for them.

"Too many? How many men did we take Nombre de Dios with? Seventy-three, and no more when we sailed out of Plymouth Sound. Before we saw the Spanish Main, half were *gastados*—used up, as the Dons say—with scurvy. In Port Pheasant, Captain Rawse of Cowes fell in with us, adding some thirty hands. With that handful, my lads, only fifty-three in all, we picked the lock of the New World! And whom did we lose but our trumpeter, who stood braying like an ass in the middle of the square instead of tending his neck like a Christian? I tell you, those Spaniards are rank cowards, as all bullies are. They pray to a woman, the idolatrous rascals! No wonder they fight like women."

"You're right, captain," shouted a tall, gaunt fellow standing close by. "One West Countryman can fight two Easterlings, and an Easterling can beat three Dons any day. Eh, my lads of Devon?"

"For O! it's the herrings and the good brown beef, And the cider and the cream so white; O! they are the making of the jolly Devon lads, For to play, and eke to fight."

"Come," said Oxenham. "Come along! Who lists? Who lists? Who'll make his fortune?"
