For days, the sea had raged against us like an angry beast. Six times, darkness swallowed the sky in thunder and lightning, each dawn bringing no relief—only fiercer winds and waves that crashed over the deck with growing fury. By the seventh day, all hope seemed lost.

We were tossed far from our course, drifting without knowing where we were. The crew had given up. Their strength was gone after endless hours of struggling to keep the ship afloat. Masts snapped and fell into the churning water; leaks sprang open everywhere, and seawater poured in faster than we could bail it out.

No more curses now—only desperate cries to God for mercy, mixed with wild promises made in fear: “If we survive, I’ll give up drinking!” or “I’ll never curse again!” Every man on board turned to prayer, begging for life while also trying to think of how to save himself.

My heart sank as I looked at my family amid this chaos. Our four sons trembled with fear. “Dear children,” I said, “if the Lord wills it, He can save us even now. If not, let us give our lives peacefully into His hands. For those who love one another, even death is not too bitter, because we will be together forever in heaven.”

At these words, my wife lifted her face from her hands, tears still streaming down it, but her eyes bright with courage. As the boys gathered close, she began to comfort them with quiet strength. I felt proud of her bravery—even though my own chest ached with worry.

Together, we knelt and prayed. Each of us spoke from the heart, full of fear yet trusting in God. Fritz, especially, prayed first for his parents and brothers, forgetting himself entirely. In that moment, the storm outside seemed less terrifying. “Ah,” I thought, “the Lord hears us. He will help.”

Then, through the roar of the waves, came a cry: “Land! Land!” At the same time, the ship shuddered violently—a terrible crash sent everyone sprawling. The vessel groaned and split apart. Water surged in from every side.

Above the chaos, the captain shouted: “Lower the boats! We’re doomed!”

“Doomed?” I echoed, and the word pierced me like a knife. But seeing my children’s terror rise again, I forced myself to smile. “Courage, my boys! We’re still above water. Look—land isn’t far. We must do what we can. Stay with your mother. I’ll go see what’s to be done.”

I climbed onto the deck. A wave threw me down. Another followed. Then another. I fought to stand. The ship was shattered—broken on both sides, with a gaping hole in the hull.

The sailors rushed to the lifeboats, ignoring passengers. The last man cut the ropes and launched the final boat into the storm.

My horror deepened when I saw it float away—every sailor aboard, leaving us behind. I called out, pleaded, but my voice was lost in the wind. Even if they’d wanted to return, the waves were mountains high. No boat could come back.

I scanned the wreckage, then slowly realized something: the stern of the ship, where our cabin was, was jammed between two large rocks. It stayed partly above the waves, while the front was being torn apart by the surf. As the rain cleared, I caught sight of a rocky coastline—rough, yes, but land nonetheless. My heart leapt. Help was near.

Still, loneliness weighed on me. I returned to my family and tried to sound calm. “Don’t fear,” I said with a smile. “Though our ship is gone, our cabin is safe. If the storm calms tomorrow, we can reach shore.”

These words lifted their spirits. My children trusted me—and suddenly, the danger didn’t feel so absolute. They laughed, joked, even enjoyed the fact that the ship wasn’t rolling anymore.

But my wife saw through my mask. She knew I was afraid. I told her the truth—how we were abandoned, how the wreck might break apart at any moment. Yet she did not waver. Her faith held firm. Seeing that, I found strength again.

“We need food,” she said. “We must eat well tonight. We’ll need all our strength tomorrow.”

Night fell fast. The storm raged on. Crashes echoed—more parts of the ship giving way. We thought of the lifeboats. Were they all lost?

“God will help us soon, won’t He, Father?” asked my youngest son.

“Silly little thing,” said Fritz sharply. “We don’t get to decide what God will do. We must wait patiently.”

“That’s wise,” I replied gently. “But you speak too harshly to your brothers, even if you don’t mean to.”

Supper was ready. The boys ate hungrily, then slept deeply. Fritz stayed awake with us. After a long silence, he said, “Father, could we make swimming belts for Mother and the others? You and I can swim. With floats, we might all make it to land.”

“It’s a brilliant idea,” I said. “I’ll begin right away.”

We searched the ship. We found empty flasks and tin cans—just enough to link together into buoyant floats. We tied two together, forming pairs strong enough to support a person. My wife and the younger boys each wore one. I packed matches, dry tinder, knives, rope, and anything portable—just in case the wreck broke apart before morning.

Fritz and his brothers fell asleep quickly. All night, my wife and I kept watch, praying and listening for signs of collapse. Every groan of the ship made us tense.

Finally, dawn broke. The storm softened. Blue sky appeared. The sunrise painted the horizon in golden light.

I woke the boys. We gathered on the deck—and froze.

“No one else is here,” said Fritz. “Where are the sailors? Did they take the boats? Why did they leave us?”

“Children,” I said firmly, “we must not despair. Those we relied on have left us. But God has not. He is with us still. Let’s act. Who has an idea?”

“The sea will calm soon,” said Fritz. “We could swim.”

“But only you can swim!” cried Ernest. “What about Mother and the rest? Why not build a raft and all go together?”

“A raft might be hard to make,” I said. “But we must try. First, find everything useful.”

We scattered. I checked supplies—food, water, tools. My wife took Franz to care for the animals, who had been neglected and were suffering.

Fritz went to the arms chest. Ernest hunted for tools. Jack opened the captain’s cabin—and two large dogs burst out, leaping and barking so wildly they knocked him over.

Jack didn’t panic. He recovered, and the dogs licked his face as if asking forgiveness. He grabbed the bigger one by the ears, climbed on its back, and rode toward me like a hero. I laughed. “Good job, Jack—but remember, hungry dogs can be dangerous.”

When we met again, we showed off our finds.

Fritz had guns, powder, bullets, and a shot belt.

Ernest brought nails, scissors, an axe, hammer, pincers, chisels—tools sticking from every pocket.

Even little Franz held a box. “Look!” he said proudly. “Nice sharp little hooks!”

His brothers snickered.

“Well done, Franz!” I said. “Those fishhooks may save us. Fritz and Ernest, you chose well.”

“What about me?” asked my wife. “I have no treasures—but good news. Some animals are alive: a donkey, goats, sheep, a ram, a cow, and a pregnant sow. I just fed them. I milked the goats—but I don’t know how to keep the milk fresh.”

“Wonderful!” I said. “And Jack—your dogs are hungry and useless.”

“Oh, no!” said Jack. “They’ll help us hunt!”

“Maybe,” I said. “But how do we get ashore?”

“Why not use big tubs?” Jack suggested. “I’ve floated in them at home!”

“My child,” I said, “you’ve struck gold! That’s worth trying. Ernest—bring your tools. Everyone, gather tubs!”

We found four large casks—strong, iron-bound—floating in the hold. We pulled them up and placed them on the lower deck. I sawed each in half. It was hard work. We paused to drink goat’s milk, wine, and biscuits.

Now we had eight tubs, lined up near the edge of the wreck. I studied them with satisfaction—until I noticed my wife’s face.

“I’ll never get into one of those,” she said.

“Wait until you see what I build,” I said. “You’ll prefer it to this sinking ship.”

Next, I found a long plank and bent its ends upward to form a keel. I nailed two planks along the sides, shaping them to a point at each end. Together, they formed a narrow boat divided into eight sections. It looked like a small craft—stable in calm water.

But when we tried to move it—nothing. Too heavy. Too clumsy. Even all of us pushing couldn’t budge it.

“I need a lever!” I shouted. “Bring the capstan bar!”

Fritz ran and fetched it. I cut a spar into rollers and raised the front of the boat with the bar. My sons placed a roller under it.

“How does this work, Father?” asked Ernest. “How can one man move more than all of us?”

I explained Archimedes’ principle—how leverage multiplies force. He grinned. “So if I had a long enough lever, I could move the world?”

“Exactly,” I said. “We’ll talk mechanics when we’re safe.”

I tied a long rope to the stern and anchored it to a beam. We added more rollers and pushed again—this time, success! The boat slid smoothly into the water. Had the rope not held, it would have drifted away.

The boys wanted to jump in—but the boat leaned heavily to one side.

We threw in heavy items. Gradually, it balanced. The boys scrambled in, laughing, eager to sit in the tubs.

But I saw it needed more. I added outriggers—long poles across the bow and stern, with empty brandy casks fixed at the ends—to keep it steady.

Then I stepped in. I turned the raft toward the open side of the wreck, clearing debris to make a path. The boys brought oars.

But it was too late to leave. We had to spend another night.

We sat down to supper—biscuits, wine, milk—after a long day of work. We prepared for sleep, better rested than the night before. Still, I warned everyone to wear their float belts again.

I urged my wife to wear a sailor’s outfit. “It will be easier,” I said. “More practical.”

She hesitated—then agreed. She returned in a smart blue uniform, found in a midshipman’s chest. She blushed, but we all admired her. Soon, she forgot her embarrassment.

We lay down in our hammocks. Sleep came easily—ready for the next day.

We rose early. Hope lightens sleep. After prayer, I said, “With God’s help, we escape today. Feed the animals well. Leave plenty of food. We may return. Now, gather everything useful.”

The boys obeyed joyfully. I chose from their haul: canvas for a tent, carpenter’s tools, guns, pistols, powder, fishing gear, an iron pot, soup cakes, biscuit.

We loaded the raft. I added ballast—useful things instead of stones. Still, we had to leave some behind.

We prayed again. Then, we took our places—one in each tub.

Just then, chickens clucked and roosters crowed—as if accusing us.

“Why not bring the fowls?” I said. “If we can’t feed them, they’ll feed us.” Ten hens and two roosters were placed in one tub, covered with wire mesh.

The ducks and geese swam free. Pigeons flew toward the shore.

My wife came last, carrying a bag as big as a pillow. “This is mine,” she said, tossing it to Franz. I thought it was a cushion.

We cast off. My brave wife sat in the first tub. Next, Franz—sweet, gentle, nearly six. Then Fritz—fourteen, bold and quick-witted. The middle tubs held our supplies. Then Jack—ten, daring and impulsive. Next, Ernest—twelve, thoughtful but lazy. I stood at the stern, guiding the raft.

Everyone wore float belts. Each had tools nearby.

The tide helped us. We slipped out of the wreck and into the open sea. All eyes strained for land. The boys rowed hard—but the raft spun in circles.

I adjusted the steering. Then we moved forward—swiftly now!

We had left the dogs, Turk and Juno, on the wreck. But when they saw us go, they howled and jumped into the sea.

I felt sorry. The distance was great. But they swam after us—resting their paws on the outriggers, keeping pace.

Jack wanted to push them away.

“No,” I said. “That would be cruel. Remember: the kind man cares for his beasts. God gave dogs to be our friends.”

Our journey was slow, but safe. As we neared the shore, the rocks looked barren and bleak.

But around us, casks and boxes floated. Fritz and I towed two hogsheads—maybe they held food.

Then, beyond the cliffs, we saw green grass and trees. Tall palms swayed in the breeze. Ernest hoped they were coconut trees—dreaming of sweet milk.

“I wish I’d brought the captain’s telescope,” I sighed.

“Here, Father!” Jack said, pulling out a tiny spyglass.

Through it, I saw the coast to the left—lusher, greener. But a current carried us toward the jagged rocks.

Then I noticed a stream flowing into the sea. Our geese and ducks were heading there.

I steered toward it. We entered a quiet inlet—calm water, shallow depth. The ground sloped gently upward, and the cliffs curved back, forming a small plain.

We landed—everyone except Franz, who had to be lifted out of his tub.

The dogs raced ahead, barking wildly, wagging tails. Chickens, geese, ducks—noisy as ever. Flamingoes and penguins screamed nearby. The noise was loud—but welcome. I smiled. Good dinners awaited.

As soon as we stood on solid ground, we knelt together—thankful, humbled. We praised God for saving us. Then we set to work.

We unloaded the raft. How rich we felt! The poultry wandered free. We found a spot for our tent—using a spar and pole as a frame, stretching the sailcloth over it. We pegged it down and piled chests along the edges. Hooks secured the entrance.

The boys gathered moss and grass for beds. I built a firepit near the brook, using flat stones. Dry twigs and seaweed burned brightly. I filled the iron pot with water. My wife took the portable soup cakes.

Franz watched, curious. “Is Papa making glue?”

“No, my dear,” she said. “This is soup.”

“But it looks like glue!”

“And I wouldn’t want glue soup,” he said. “Do you want beef or mutton, Mama?”

We laughed. And as the fire crackled, I knew—we were safe. For now. And tomorrow? Tomorrow, we would begin our new life.
