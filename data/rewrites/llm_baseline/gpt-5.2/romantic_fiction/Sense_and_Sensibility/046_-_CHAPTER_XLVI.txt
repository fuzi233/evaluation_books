Marianne’s illness, though it had wasted her in a way that frightened even those accustomed to see the young recover, had not held long enough to make convalescence a laborious season. Youth, a constitution naturally sound, and—more potent than either—the steady nearness of her mother, drew her back by degrees that were almost daily measurable. Within four days of Mrs. Dashwood’s arrival, Marianne could be moved from the sick-room into Mrs. Palmer’s dressing-room, where the air was kinder, the light less cruel, and the household’s small, ordinary sounds—water poured, linen shaken, a footfall softened at the threshold—could re-enter her life without assaulting it.

From that refuge she sent, by her own earnest request, for Colonel Brandon. She was impatient, she said, to thank him for fetching her mother; and to Marianne, gratitude was not a coin to be paid at leisure, but a dish best served hot, while the heart still tasted the need that had called it forth.

When he came in, the room altered with him. He had the look of a man who had braced himself for a sight and yet had not been braced enough. At the first view of her changed face—the hollowness beneath the eye, the thinness over cheekbone, the helpless angle of her reclined posture—his composure wavered like a flame in a draught. Marianne, pale but resolved, held out her hand at once, as if to spare him ceremony and herself delay. He took it, and for a moment seemed unable to trust his voice.

Elinor, watching with that quiet exactness which missed neither word nor silence, believed his emotion rose from more than the affection he had long felt and the consciousness that others guessed it. There was a deeper tide in him, pulled by an old memory. That resemblance between Marianne and the unhappy Eliza—once acknowledged, often feared—was now sharpened by sickness into a cruel fidelity. The very signs of weakness that made Marianne pitiable to a stranger made her dangerous to his recollection: the sickly skin, the shadowed eyes, the languor that forced her to receive attentions instead of offering them. His melancholy look and shifting colour spoke of scenes revived against his will.

Mrs. Dashwood saw differently. Her mind, governed by feeling rather than inference, read nothing in the Colonel’s behaviour but the simplest tenderness and alarm; and in Marianne’s softened manner—those earnest thanks, that gentle steadiness—she fancied she could already glimpse something dawning beyond obligation.

A day or two more, and Marianne gathered strength as if each twelve hours had brought its own small harvest. With that improvement, Mrs. Dashwood—pressed at once by necessity and longing—began to speak of returning to Barton. On her decision hung the movements of their friends. Mrs. Jennings, generous to the point of being restless when she could not be useful, would not leave Cleveland while the Dashwoods remained; and Colonel Brandon, drawn into their circle by care as much as by affection, was soon urged—by both mother and benefactress—to consider his own staying as fixed, if not as indispensable.

Then came those exchanges of kindness that, like household cookery, depend upon the graceful give-and-take of offering and accepting. At Mrs. Jennings’s and the Colonel’s united request, Mrs. Dashwood was at last persuaded to take his carriage for the journey, that Marianne might travel with less jar and fatigue. And in return—pressed again by invitation, and pleased to be included as more than a provider of conveniences—Colonel Brandon promised to redeem that service by visiting them at the cottage within a few weeks. Mrs. Jennings, whose hospitable zeal seemed capable of feeding not only her own appetites but those of everyone around her, engaged him with a warmth that made refusal impossible.

The day of separation arrived, and with it the peculiar sorrow of leaving a place that had been, in one breath, a theatre of danger and a scene of salvation. Marianne’s leave-taking of Mrs. Jennings was long and particular: grateful, respectful, tender to the edge of self-reproach, as if she tasted now—with a sharpened palate for kindness—how much she had once overlooked. Her farewell to Colonel Brandon held the cordiality of a friend, and yet Elinor could not help noticing that the Colonel, in assisting her, seemed anxious she should occupy at least half the carriage, as though space itself could be offered in protection.

Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed; and then Mrs. Jennings and the Colonel were left together to talk of the travellers and feel the dullness that followed usefulness. Soon Mrs. Jennings was called away to her chaise, to find comfort—of a kind suited to her—among the gossip of her maid; and Colonel Brandon went, solitary, toward Delaford, with the road before him sounding, perhaps, louder than it ought in an empty day.

The Dashwoods were two days upon the road. Marianne bore both without essential fatigue. Everything that love and vigilance could contrive was done for her: cushions arranged, stops shortened, a shawl tucked closer at the first change in wind. Even the very sound within the carriage was managed for her comfort; her mother’s voice kept low and even, Elinor’s movements measured, as though quiet were a broth simmered patiently to restore a weakened body. In those small attentions each watcher found reward—in Marianne’s bodily ease, and, more precious still to Elinor, in her calmness of spirit.

For Elinor, who had witnessed week after week the same suffering—anguish that had neither courage to speak nor fortitude to conceal—this new composure was like seeing a storm-damaged garden begin, at last, to put out clean shoots. If it sprang from serious reflection, as Elinor hoped, it must lead in time to content and cheerfulness.

As they drew near Barton, however, and the familiar fields began to appear—each hedge and tree carrying its own private sting—Marianne grew silent. She turned her face from their notice and sat gazing out, intent as if listening not only with her eyes but with her whole skin to what the landscape remembered. When Elinor helped her down at the cottage door, she saw that Marianne had been crying; yet the tears had fallen without display, and that unobtrusiveness made them seem not less sorrowful but more to be respected.

Within, Marianne’s manner showed a mind forcing itself into new discipline. No sooner had they entered their common sitting-room than she looked around it with a resolute firmness, as if determined to accustom herself at once to every object that might summon Willoughby. She spoke little; but each sentence aimed at cheerfulness, and if a sigh escaped, it did not pass without the atonement of a smile—like someone tasting a bitter infusion and insisting, by sheer will, on finding the warmth it still carried.

After dinner she declared she would try her pianoforte. She went to it; but the music that lay uppermost was an opera Willoughby had procured for her, containing favourite duets, and upon the outer leaf her own name written in his hand. That would not do. She shook her head, put it aside, and ran her fingers over the keys for a minute. The notes—so quick to fill a room, so impossible to call back once struck—seemed to startle her more than silence could. She complained of feebleness in her hands and closed the instrument; yet even in withdrawing she declared, firmly, that she would practise much in future.

The next morning brought no abatement of these signs. Strengthened by rest, mind and body together, she spoke with more genuine spirit. She anticipated Margaret’s return and talked of the dear family party that would then be restored—of mutual pursuits and cheerful society—as the only happiness worth wishing.

“When the weather is settled, and I have recovered my strength,” she said, “we will take long walks every day. We will walk to the farm at the edge of the down, and see how the children go on; we will walk to Sir John’s new plantations at Barton Cross, and the Abbeyland; and we will go often to the old ruins of the Priory, and try to trace the foundations as far as we are told they once reached. I know we shall be happy. I know the summer will pass happily away. I mean never to be later in rising than six, and from that time till dinner I shall divide every moment between music and reading. I have formed my plan, and am determined to enter on a course of serious study. Our own library is too well known to me to be resorted to for anything beyond mere amusement. But there are many works worth reading at the Park; and there are others of more modern production which I know I can borrow of Colonel Brandon. By reading only six hours a day, I shall gain, in the course of a twelve-month, a great deal of instruction which I now feel myself to want.”

Elinor honoured her for a plan so nobly rooted; yet she could not help a slight smile at seeing the same eager fancy which had lately driven Marianne toward languid indolence and selfish repining now at work in threatening excess in a scheme of rational employment and virtuous control. The smile faded into a sigh when she remembered her promise to Willoughby—still unfulfilled—and feared that what she must communicate might unsettle Marianne again and overturn this fair prospect of busy tranquillity. She resolved, therefore, to wait until Marianne’s health was more secure.

It was a resolution made only to be broken.

Marianne had been two or three days at home before the weather turned fine enough for an invalid to venture out; but at last there came a morning soft and genial, with air that felt newly washed, and a quietness in the lane as if the world itself were trying to tread lightly. It tempted Marianne’s longing and justified her mother’s confidence. Leaning on Elinor’s arm, she was permitted to walk as long as she could without fatigue in the lane before the house.

They moved slowly, for Marianne’s weakness made the very act of walking—so natural once, so taken for granted—an experiment. They had not gone far beyond the cottage, only far enough to command a full view of the hill behind, when Marianne paused. Her eyes turned toward it, and she spoke with a calm that was more affecting than any passion.

“There—exactly there,” she said, pointing to the projecting mound, “there I fell; and there I first saw Willoughby.”

Her voice sank on his name; then, as if she refused to let that sinking be the last motion of her spirit, she added, “I am thankful to find that I can look with so little pain on the spot. Shall we ever talk on that subject, Elinor?” It was said with hesitation. “Or will it be wrong? I can talk of it now, I hope, as I ought to do.”

Elinor, tender and steady, invited her to speak openly.

“As for regret,” Marianne continued, “I have done with that, as far as he is concerned. I do not mean to talk to you of what my feelings have been for him, but what they are now. At present, if I could be satisfied on one point—if I could be allowed to think that he was not always acting a part, not always deceiving me; but above all, if I could be assured that he never was so very wicked as my fears have sometimes fancied him, since the story of that unfortunate girl—”

She stopped. Elinor stored the words, grateful for what they promised.

“If you could be assured of that,” Elinor said, “you think you should be easy.”

“Yes. My peace of mind is doubly involved in it; for not only is it horrible to suspect a person, who has been what he has been to me, of such designs—but what must it make me appear to myself? What, in a situation like mine, but a most shamefully unguarded affection could expose me to—”

“How then,” Elinor asked, “would you account for his behaviour?”

“I would suppose him—Oh, how gladly would I suppose him—only fickle, very, very fickle.”

Elinor said no more. Within herself she debated whether to begin her narration at once or postpone it until Marianne was stronger. They went on a few minutes in silence, their steps small upon the lane, the hedgerows rustling faintly with that restrained sound of rural life which heals precisely because it does not insist.

“I am not wishing him too much good,” Marianne said at last, with a sigh, “when I wish his secret reflections may be no more unpleasant than my own. He will suffer enough in them.”

“Do you compare your conduct with his?” Elinor asked.

“No. I compare it with what it ought to have been; I compare it with yours.”

“Our situations have borne little resemblance.”

“They have borne more than our conduct. Do not, my dearest Elinor, let your kindness defend what I know your judgment must censure. My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was recovered enough to talk, I was perfectly able to reflect.

“I considered the past: I saw in my own behaviour, since the beginning of our acquaintance with him last autumn, nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself, and want of kindness to others. I saw that my own feelings had prepared my sufferings, and that my want of fortitude under them had almost led me to the grave.

“My illness, I well knew, had been entirely brought on by myself—by such negligence of my own health as I had felt even at the time to be wrong. Had I died, it would have been self-destruction. I did not know my danger till the danger was removed; but with such feelings as these reflections gave me, I wonder at my recovery—wonder that the very eagerness of my desire to live, to have time for atonement to my God, and to you all, did not kill me at once.

“Had I died, in what peculiar misery should I have left you—my nurse, my friend, my sister! You, who had seen all the fretful selfishness of my latter days; who had known all the murmurings of my heart! How should I have lived in your remembrance! My mother too! How could you have consoled her! I cannot express my own abhorrence of myself. Whenever I looked towards the past, I saw some duty neglected, or some failing indulged. Everybody seemed injured by me.

“The kindness—the unceasing kindness—of Mrs. Jennings I had repaid with ungrateful contempt. To the Middletons, to the Palmers, the Steeles—to every common acquaintance even—I had been insolent and unjust; with a heart hardened against their merits, and a temper irritated by their very attention. To John, to Fanny—yes, even to them, little as they deserve—I had given less than their due.

“But you—you above all, above my mother—had been wronged by me. I, and only I, knew your heart and its sorrows; yet to what did it influence me? Not to any compassion that could benefit you or myself. Your example was before me; but to what avail? Was I more considerate of you and your comfort? Did I imitate your forbearance, or lessen your restraints by taking any part in those offices of general complaisance or particular gratitude which you had hitherto been left to discharge alone? No; not less when I knew you to be unhappy, than when I believed you at ease, did I turn away from every exertion of duty or friendship—scarcely allowing sorrow to exist but with me, regretting only that heart which had deserted and wronged me, and leaving you, for whom I professed an unbounded affection, to be miserable for my sake.”

The rapid current of her self-reproach ceased. Elinor, longing to soothe yet too honest for flattery, gave her at once the praise her frankness and contrition deserved, and the support she needed to bear them.

Marianne pressed her hand. “You are very good,” she said. “The future must be my proof. I have laid down my plan; and if I am capable of adhering to it, my feelings shall be governed and my temper improved. They shall no longer worry others, nor torture myself.

“I shall now live solely for my family. You, my mother, and Margaret must henceforth be all the world to me; you will share my affections entirely between you. From you—from my home—I shall never again have the smallest incitement to move; and if I do mix in other society, it will be only to show that my spirit is humbled, my heart amended, and that I can practise the civilities, the lesser duties of life, with gentleness and forbearance.

“As for Willoughby—to say that I shall soon or that I shall ever forget him would be idle. His remembrance can be overcome by no change of circumstances or opinions. But it shall be regulated; it shall be checked by religion, by reason, by constant employment.”

She paused, and then, in a voice lowered as if she feared even the air might repeat it back too loudly, added, “If I could but know his heart, everything would become easy.”

Elinor had been turning the question of propriety over and over, like a careful cook uncertain when to add the sharp ingredient that will either perfect a dish or spoil it. Reflection did not bring her nearer to decision; and seeing that it would never do so, she understood that resolution must act where deliberation failed. Marianne’s last words decided her. She led, gently but steadily, to the facts.

As she related—simply and honestly—the chief points of Willoughby’s apology, she prepared Marianne with caution; she did justice to his repentance, and softened only those protestations of present regard which might have been more dangerous than consoling. Marianne did not interrupt with a single word. She trembled; her eyes fixed on the ground; her lips turned whiter than sickness had left them. Questions rose in her like breath held too long, yet she did not dare to shape one. She caught every syllable with a panting eagerness, her hand—without her knowing it—closing hard upon Elinor’s, while tears slid down her cheeks.

Elinor, fearing fatigue, drew her quietly toward home. Until they reached the cottage door she spoke of nothing but Willoughby and the conversation she had had with him, and, guessing the curiosity that Marianne would not voice, she was minute in every particular of speech and look where minuteness could be safely indulged—those small turns of expression, those pauses that carry meaning no dominant vocabulary can fully command.

Once inside, Marianne kissed her with a gratitude that shook through her whole frame. Only two words came, just articulate through tears: “Tell mama.” Then she withdrew from Elinor and walked slowly upstairs, seeking a solitude that was not flight but effort—a private chamber in which to attempt to translate what she had heard into something her heart could bear.

Elinor did not follow. She would not disturb a retreat so reasonable. With her mind anxiously arranging what might come of this disclosure, and with a firm intent to revive the subject again should Marianne fail to do so, she turned into the parlour to fulfil her sister’s request.
