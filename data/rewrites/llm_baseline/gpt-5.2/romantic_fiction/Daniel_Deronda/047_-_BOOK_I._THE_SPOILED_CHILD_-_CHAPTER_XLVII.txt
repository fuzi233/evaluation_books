It would have been easy—almost wickedly easy—to envy Daniel for the simple luxury of buying another man comfort, and then lingering over the fancy of it as if he were arranging a still life: the fine gray flannel shirts, newly aired, folded so their softness showed; the brown dressing-gown chosen for its humble likeness to a Franciscan frock; and, above that sober cloth, Mordecai’s worn, beautiful head and the long, fragile line of throat which seemed made for speech and prayer rather than for swallowing and breath.

Half Daniel’s pleasure lay in imagining Mirah’s eyes upon her brother—those eyes that could turn poverty itself into a kind of consecration—and in trying, by these preparations, to secure her joy from any jarring shock. Yet when the parcels were opened and the garments laid ready, doubt came like a thin cold draught under a door. Was he mistaking her? Was he putting the lower effect for the higher, as if tenderness must be bribed by cleanliness? Might she not feel her brother’s distinction more deeply if it came to her still wrapped in the visible memorials of hardship? The mind, after all, can be nourished by what the palate would reject; and Mirah’s reverence had never been dainty.

But there were also the Meyricks—good hearts, ready hands, and that quick recoil of taste which a lifetime of careful domestic sweetness can breed in those who have not been taught to read sanctity through alien signs. Daniel detected in himself a little prickle of pride: he wanted to get out of sight whatever might feed their ready repugnance toward this “too Judaic” brother. He wanted, too, to take away from Hans any cheap target for mockery. The air of a room, like the seasoning of a dish, can decide whether a guest will be met with appetite or aversion; and Daniel, with his own peculiar sensitiveness, was trying to quiet the harsh note before it could sound.

If he had been present late the same evening, he would have heard how loudly that note was already vibrating around Mrs. Meyrick’s fire, with all the familiar domestic sounds that made their little parlor a refuge: the quick purr and snap of coals as Mrs. Meyrick poked them into a blaze, the soft scrape of a chair-leg pulled closer, the hush that comes when a candle is snuffed and the fire becomes the chief light, and, beyond that, the steady, far-off city murmur—wheels and footsteps and distant calls—like a sea that never quite lets you forget it is there.

“Now, Kate, put out your candle,” Mrs. Meyrick said, briskly, as if coziness were an order to be obeyed, “and all come round the fire cosily. Hans, dear, do leave off laughing at those poems for the ninety-ninth time, and come too. I have something wonderful to tell.”

“As if I didn’t know that, ma,” said Kate, letting her impatience show itself in a composure that was only the prettier for being vexed. “I have seen it in the corner of your eye ever so long, and in your pretense of errands.”

The girls came up and set their feet on the fender, turning themselves toward the heat as creatures turn toward sustenance. Hans pushed his chair near and sat astride it, resting his fists and chin on the back, already braced for amusement.

“Well, then—if you are so wise,” Mrs. Meyrick said, with her clearest articulation, as if announcing news at the market, “perhaps you know that Mirah’s brother is found!”

“Oh, confound it!” Hans burst out, as if the words had been a sudden clatter in his ear.

“Hans, that is wicked,” Mab cried, pained and indignant at once. “Suppose we had lost you?”

“I cannot help being rather sorry,” Kate said, with that frankness which in her was less cruelty than an instinct for keeping life in proportion. “And her mother?—where is she?”

“Her mother is dead,” said Mrs. Meyrick, and the fire made a small sigh as a coal fell in.

“I hope the brother is not a bad man,” Amy said, in her quiet way, always anxious to keep kindness safe.

“Nor a fellow all smiles and jewelry—a Crystal Palace Assyrian with a hat on,” Hans added, in the worst humor, as if his imagination could only defend itself by ridicule.

“Were there ever such unfeeling children?” Mrs. Meyrick said, gathering her maternal dignity like a shawl about her shoulders. “You don’t think the least bit of Mirah’s joy in the matter.”

“You know, ma, Mirah hardly remembers her brother,” Kate insisted. “People who are lost for twelve years should never come back again.”

“They are always in the way,” Hans said, with the brutal exactness of a boy kicking at something he cannot name.

“Hans!” Mrs. Meyrick’s reproach had in it the tremor of fear—fear that he would grow into a man who could not be taught.

“If you had lost me for twenty years, I should have thought—”

“I said twelve years,” Hans broke in, as if the number mattered to his argument. “Anywhere about twelve years is the time at which lost relations should keep out of the way.”

“Well, but it’s nice finding people—there is something to tell,” Mab put in, clasping her knees and leaning forward, eager for story as for sweetmeat. “Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?”

Then Mrs. Meyrick, neat as ever in her narrative—never wasting words, never letting emotion blur the outline—told all she knew: how Mr. Deronda had discovered him, how strange and beautiful and grave he seemed, how Mirah was to be told. She ended with what struck her most.

“Mr. Deronda has the highest admiration for him—seems quite to look up to him. And he says Mirah is just the sister to understand this brother.”

“Deronda is getting perfectly preposterous about those Jews,” Hans said, with disgust. He rose, set his chair away with a bang, and the sound rang harshly in the little room, like a note that does not belong to the tune. “He wants to do everything he can to encourage Mirah in her prejudices.”

“Oh, for shame, Hans—to speak in that way of Mr. Deronda,” Mab cried; and Mrs. Meyrick’s face showed something like an under-current of feeling—pride, gratitude, and a little resentment—held down by her habit of quiet.

“And now we shall never be all together,” Hans went on, pacing with his hands thrust into the pockets of his brown velveteen coat, as if movement might shake off what he feared. “We must have this prophet Elijah to tea with us, and Mirah will think of nothing but sitting on the ruins of Jerusalem. She will be spoiled as an artist—mind that—she will get as narrow as a nun. Everything will be spoiled—our home and everything. I shall take to drinking.”

“Oh, really, Hans,” Kate said, sharp with the impatience that comes when one’s own nerves are tired of being pulled. “I do think men are the most contemptible animals in all creation. Every one of them must have everything to his mind, else he is unbearable.”

“Oh, oh, oh, it’s very dreadful!” Mab cried. “I feel as if ancient Nineveh were come again.”

“I should like to know what is the good of having gone to the university and knowing everything, if you are so childish, Hans,” Amy said, with a plainness that was like salt—necessary, a little stinging, but wholesome. “You ought to put up with a man that Providence sends you to be kind to. We shall have to put up with him.”

“I hope you will all of you like the new Lamentations of Jeremiah—‘to be continued in our next’—that’s all,” Hans said, seizing his wide-awake. “It’s no use being one thing more than another if one has to endure the company of those men with a fixed idea, staring blankly at you, and requiring all your remarks to be small foot-notes to their text. If you’re to be under a petrifying wall, you’d better be an old boot. I don’t feel myself an old boot.”

Then abruptly, as if the boy in him still wanted absolution from the woman who had borne him, he bent and kissed his mother’s brow in a hasty, desperate manner. “Good night, little mother.” And, condescendingly, on his way to the door, “Good-night, girls.”

“Suppose Mirah knew how you are behaving,” Kate called after him.

But the answer was only a slam—loud, final, and out of tune with the room’s soft fire-speech.

“I should like to see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her,” Kate went on, turning back to her mother as if to restore the severed thread. “I know she will look so beautiful.”

Yet Daniel did not tell her. On second thoughts, he wrote instead, and the letter came to Mrs. Meyrick the next morning, asking her to make the revelation without waiting for him. He did not confess the deeper reason—that he shrank from again going through a narrative in which he seemed to place himself at the center, dressing his actions in the showy garments of general beneficence. He said only that he wished to remain with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah on what should be understood as a visit, so that there might be a little interval before the change of abode which he expected Mirah herself would propose.

Still, under Daniel’s considerate arranging there lay an anxious curiosity, as persistent as the city’s low continuous roar. He wondered how far Mordecai—after years of solitude, his mind running in one deep channel cut by conviction and worn deeper by weakness—would be able to let his sister be dear to him simply as sister, not only as a pious duty reclaimed. Mordecai’s affection for the Cohens, and especially for little Jacob, had shown itself active, habitual, as if love too could become a daily bread; but those had been near him for years, kneaded into his routine. Mirah was a name, a prayer, a past.

It struck Daniel as noticeable that Mordecai asked no new questions about her. He maintained an unusual silence on all subjects, and seemed to submit to the changes gathering around him as a sick man submits to weather, not resisting, only feeling. He put on the new clothes obediently, as if clothing were merely another circumstance—yet afterward he said, with a faint smile that had tenderness in it, “I must keep my old garments by me for a remembrance.” The words were simple, but they had the weight of a people’s habit: to keep what is worn, what holds the body’s history, as if cloth itself could preserve a vanished voice.

When they were seated waiting for Mirah, Mordecai uttered no word. His eyelids were closed, but his hands betrayed him—restless, lightly opening and shutting, and his face had that nervous changefulness which comes when the mind, long driven forward in one direction, is suddenly compelled into a new, reopened channel. There are people who dread such meetings—imperious revivals of the past—as they dread a threatening illness. Joy may be there, but joy too can be terrible, like music suddenly made too loud for a weakened ear.

Daniel felt the infection of that excitement. When he heard the ring at the door—sharp in the stairwell, followed by the muffled footfalls—he went out, not knowing exactly why, only that he wanted to see and greet Mirah beforehand, to offer her some quietness before the storm of recognition.

He was startled to find her in the hat and cloak in which he had first seen her—the cloak that had once been wetted for a winding-sheet. It seemed an omen carried into ordinary daylight, as if memory could not help but dress itself. She had come down-stairs equipped in that way; and when Mrs. Meyrick, in a tone of gentle question, had said, “You like to go in that dress, dear?” Mirah had answered, “My brother is poor, and I want to look as much like him as I can, else he may feel distant from me”—imagining she should meet him still in a workman’s dress.

Daniel could find no remark that would not sound like self-justification. He felt, with a sudden shame, how his own fastidious arrangements might seem—like trying to sweeten a truth so it would be swallowed more easily by other people’s tastes.

They shook hands silently. Mirah looked pale and awed, as if her body had grown light with anticipation. When Daniel opened the door for her, Mordecai had risen. He stood with his eyes turned toward the entrance, an eager gaze fixed as if he were listening with his whole face.

Mirah took only two or three steps and then stood still.

They looked at each other motionless. It was less their own presence they felt than another’s: a mother’s call lingering in the air, the old language of the household that had once been theirs, sounds that carried a whole vanished room with them. They were meeting first in memories, compared with which touch was no union.

Mirah broke the silence first, from where she stood, her voice exactly as when she had once repeated their mother’s call, as if she were afraid to alter even a syllable.

“Ezra.”

Mordecai moved suddenly forward and laid his hand on her shoulders. He was a head taller, and he looked down at her with a tenderness that seemed to soften the sharpness of his illness.

“That was our mother’s voice,” he said. “You remember her calling me?”

“Yes,” Mirah answered, and her words came quickly now, warmed by the return of something long kept cold. “And how you answered her—‘Mother!’—and I knew you loved her.”

Then she flung her arms round his neck, clasped her little hands behind it, drew down his face, and kissed it with a childlike lavishness that had no thought but love. Her hat slipped backward and fell, and all her curls were suddenly free, dark and shining as if they held the light.

“Ah, the dear head, the dear head!” Mordecai murmured, low, loving, laying his thin hand gently on the curls as if blessing them.

“You are very ill, Ezra,” Mirah said then, sorrow entering with clearer sight. She looked at him now not only with memory but with observation.

“Yes, dear child, I shall not be long with you in the body,” he answered quietly, as if he were stating a known season.

“Oh, I will love you and we will talk to each other,” Mirah said, her words pouring out sweetly, spontaneous as bird-notes heard at dawn. “I will tell you everything, and you will teach me—you will teach me to be a good Jewess—what she would have liked me to be. I shall always be with you when I am not working. For I work now. I shall get money to keep us. Oh, I have had such good friends.”

Only then did she seem to remember there were others in the room. She turned with the prettiest unconscious grace, keeping one hand on her brother’s arm, and looked at Mrs. Meyrick and Daniel as if presenting them, as if gratitude itself were something that must be spoken aloud.

“See this dear lady!” she said. “I was a stranger, a poor wanderer, and she believed in me, and has treated me as a daughter. Please give my brother your hand,” she added beseechingly—taking Mrs. Meyrick’s hand and putting it into Mordecai’s, then pressing them both with her own and lifting them to her lips, as if sealing a covenant.

“The Eternal Goodness has been with you,” Mordecai said to Mrs. Meyrick. “You have helped to fulfill our mother’s prayer.”

Mrs. Meyrick’s happy emotion, in witnessing this meeting, had already won her. Mordecai’s face—so refined in its suffering, so gentle in its authority—seemed to her to have more dignity than she had felt obliged to believe possible from Daniel’s account. She could not answer much; her eyes said what her careful speech could not.

“I think we will go now, shall we?—and return later,” Daniel said, laying a gentle pressure on Mrs. Meyrick’s arm, and she immediately complied.

He was afraid—unreasonably, yet irresistibly—of any reference Mordecai might make to facts about himself which Daniel had kept back. And yet, as he led Mrs. Meyrick away, the uneasiness that had haunted him softened: he felt no fear now in leaving brother and sister alone together. Whatever was untranslatable between them—whatever words had died on their tongues over twelve years of separation—there was already a language in the room older than explanation, spoken in a voice that had once said, “Ezra,” and answered, “Mother.”
