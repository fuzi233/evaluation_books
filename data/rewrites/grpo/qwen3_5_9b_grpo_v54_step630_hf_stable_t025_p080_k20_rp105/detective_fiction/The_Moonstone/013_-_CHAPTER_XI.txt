The final carriage wheels had long since vanished into the twilight when I retreated to the inner hall, finding Samuel already stationed at the side-table, diligently dispensing brandy and soda. My Lady and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, trailed by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a glass, but Mr. Franklin declined, sinking into a chair with the exhaustion of a man drained dry. The conversation of the evening, I presumed, had been too taxing for his constitution.

My Lady turned to bid them goodnight, her gaze lingering with a sharp, calculating intensity on the wicked Colonel's legacy, now glittering in her daughter's dress.

"Rachel," she asked, "where do you intend to deposit the Diamond tonight?"

Miss Rachel was in a buoyant, giddy mood, prone to the kind of nonsensical chatter that often passes for sense in young women when they are at the height of excitement. First, she claimed ignorance of where to place it. Then, she suggested her dressing-table, alongside her other trinkets. Suddenly, she recalled the Diamond's terrifying, moon-like luminescence in the dark, which might frighten her in the dead of night. Next, she conceived the brilliant idea of placing the Indian diamond in the Indian cabinet in her sitting-room, allowing two beautiful native treasures to admire one another.

"My dear!" My Lady interjected, cutting off her flow of nonsense. "Your Indian cabinet has no lock."

"Good heavens, Mamma!" cried Miss Rachel, feigning horror. "Is this an hotel? Are there thieves in the house?"

Ignoring this theatrical display, My Lady wished the gentlemen goodnight and then approached Miss Rachel, kissing her forehead. "Why not let *me* keep the Diamond for you tonight?" she proposed.

Miss Rachel received this offer with the same reluctance a child might show toward being parted from a beloved doll. My Lady saw there was no reasoning with her that night. "Come into my room, Rachel, the first thing tomorrow morning," she said softly. "I shall have something to say to you." With those words, she departed slowly, her countenance suggesting a deep, unspoken dissatisfaction with the path her thoughts were leading her.

Miss Rachel was the next to bid farewell. She shook hands first with Mr. Godfrey, who stood at the far end of the hall gazing at a picture, before turning to Mr. Franklin. He remained seated in a corner, weary and silent.

I cannot recount the words exchanged between them. But standing near the old oak frame holding our large mirror, I saw her reflection slip the locket Mr. Franklin had given her from the bosom of her dress. She held it up to him, a smile playing on her lips that held a meaning far deeper than usual, before she tripped off to bed. This incident shook my confidence in my own judgment. I began to wonder if Penelope might be right regarding the state of her young lady's affections after all.

As soon as Miss Rachel left, Mr. Franklin noticed me. His variable humor, which shifts constantly, had already shifted onto the topic of the Indians.

"Betteredge," he said, "I'm half inclined to think I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously during our talk in the shrubbery. I wonder if he's been trying any of his traveler's tales on us? Do you really mean to let the dogs loose?"

"I'll relieve them of their collars, sir," I answered, "and leave them free to take a turn in the night if they smell a reason for it."

"All right," said Mr. Franklin. "We'll see what is to be done tomorrow. I am not at all disposed to alarm my aunt without a pressing reason. Good-night."

He looked so worn and pale as he nodded and took his candle that I ventured to suggest a drop of brandy-and-water as a nightcap. Mr. Godfrey, walking toward us from the other end of the hall, backed me up, urging Mr. Franklin to take something before retiring.

I noted these trifling details because, after all I had witnessed that day, it pleased me to observe that our two gentlemen were on as good terms as ever. Their verbal warfare, heard by Penelope in the drawing-room, and their rivalry for Miss Rachel's favor seemed to have bred no serious discord. They were both good-tempered and men of the world; there is certainly a merit in people of station that they are not nearly so quarrelsome as those of no station.

Mr. Franklin declined the drink initially but, on the landing, either persuaded by his cousin or simply changing his mind as he often did, he called down, "Perhaps I may want it in the night. Send up some brandy-and-water into my room."

I sent Samuel up with the drink and then went out to unbuckle the dogs' collars. They lost their heads with astonishment at being set loose so late, jumping upon me like puppies. However, the rain soon cooled them down; they lapped a drop of water each and crept back into their kennels. As I entered the house, I noticed signs in the sky promising a break in the weather. For the moment, it still poured heavily, and the ground was a perfect soup.

Samuel and I went through the house, securing everything as usual. I examined everything myself, trusting nothing to my deputy on this occasion. All was safe and fast when I rested my old bones in bed between midnight and one in the morning.

The worries of the day had been too much for me. I suffered a touch of Mr. Franklin's malady that night. It was sunrise before I finally fell asleep. Throughout the time I lay awake, the house was as quiet as the grave, disturbed only by the splash of rain and the sighing of the wind among the trees as a breeze sprang up with the morning.

About half-past seven I woke and opened my window to a fine, sunshiny day. The clock had struck eight, and I was just going out to chain the dogs again when I heard a sudden whisking of petticoats on the stairs behind me.

I turned, and there was Penelope flying down after me like mad. "Father!" she screamed. "Come upstairs, for God's sake! The Diamond is gone!"

"Are you out of your mind?" I asked.

"Gone!" said Penelope. "Gone, nobody knows how! Come up and see."

She dragged me into our young lady's sitting-room, which opened into her bedroom. There, on the threshold of her bedroom door, stood Miss Rachel, almost as white as the white dressing-gown she wore. Beside her stood the two doors of the Indian cabinet, wide open. One of the drawers inside was pulled out as far as it would go.

"Look!" said Penelope. "I myself saw Miss Rachel put the Diamond into that drawer last night." I went to the cabinet. The drawer was empty.

"Is this true, miss?" I asked.

With a look that was not like herself and a voice that was not her own, Miss Rachel answered as my daughter had: "The Diamond is gone!"

Having said this, she withdrew into her bedroom and shut and locked the door.

Before we knew which way to turn, My Lady came in, hearing my voice and wondering what had happened. The news of the loss seemed to petrify her. She went straight to Miss Rachel's bedroom and insisted on being admitted. Miss Rachel let her in.

The alarm ran through the house like fire, catching the two gentlemen next. Mr. Godfrey was the first to emerge from his room, holding up his hands in a state of bewilderment that did little credit to his natural strength of mind. Mr. Franklin, whose clear head I had confidently relied upon, seemed as helpless as his cousin. For a wonder, he had had a good night's rest, and the unaccustomed luxury of sleep had apparently stupefied him. However, after swallowing his cup of coffee, his brains brightened. The clear-headed side turned up, and he took the matter in hand, resolutely and cleverly.

He first sent for the servants, instructing them to leave all lower doors and windows exactly as they had been left when we locked up. He next proposed to his cousin and me to ensure the Diamond had not accidentally dropped somewhere out of sight—say, at the back of the cabinet or behind the table. Having searched both places and found nothing, and having questioned Penelope to no avail, Mr. Franklin suggested extending inquiries to Miss Rachel and sent Penelope to knock at her bedroom door.

My Lady answered the knock and closed the door behind her. The moment after, we heard it locked inside by Miss Rachel. My mistress came out among us, looking sorely puzzled and distressed. "The loss of the Diamond seems to have quite overwhelmed Rachel," she said. "She shrinks, in the strangest manner, from speaking of it, even to *me*. It is impossible you can see her for the present."

Adding to our perplexities, My Lady, after a little effort, recovered her usual composure and acted with her usual decision. "I suppose there is no help for it?" she said quietly. "I suppose I have no alternative but to send for the police?"

"And the first thing for the police to do," added Mr. Franklin, catching her up, "is to lay hands on the Indian jugglers who performed here last night."

My Lady and Mr. Godfrey both started, looking surprised.

"I can't stop to explain myself now," Mr. Franklin went on. "I can only tell you that the Indians have certainly stolen the Diamond. Give me a letter of introduction to one of the magistrates at Frizinghall—merely telling him that I represent your interests—and let me ride off with it instantly. Our chance of catching the thieves may depend on our not wasting one unnecessary minute."

Whether it was the French side or the English, the right side of Mr. Franklin seemed uppermost now. The only question was how long it would last.

He put pen, ink, and paper before his aunt, who wrote the letter he wanted a little unwillingly. If it had been possible to overlook such an event as the loss of a jewel worth twenty thousand pounds, I believe—with My Lady's opinion of her late brother and her distrust of his birthday gift—it would have been privately a relief to her to let the thieves get off with the Moonstone scot-free.

I went out with Mr. Franklin to the stables and asked him how the Indians, whom I suspected as shrewdly as he did, could possibly have entered the house.

"One of them might have slipped into the hall in the confusion when the dinner party was leaving," said Mr. Franklin. "The fellow may have been under the sofa while my aunt and Rachel were talking about where the Diamond was to be put. He would only have to wait till the house was quiet, and there it would be in the cabinet, to be had for the taking." With those words, he called to the groom to open the gate and galloped off.

This seemed certainly the only rational explanation. But how had the thief contrived to escape? I had found the front door locked and bolted as I had left it. The other doors and windows were still safe and fast. The dogs, too? Suppose the thief had escaped by dropping from an upper window; how had he evaded them? Had he been provided with drugged meat? As the doubt crossed my mind, the dogs themselves came galloping at me round a corner, rolling over on the wet grass in such lively health and spirits that it was with no small difficulty I brought them to reason and chained them up again. The more I turned it over in my mind, the less satisfactory Mr. Franklin's explanation appeared.

We had our breakfasts—whatever happens in a house, robbery or murder, you must have your breakfast. When we had done, My Lady sent for me, and I found myself compelled to tell her all that I had hitherto concealed regarding the Indians and their plot. Being a woman of high courage, she soon got over the first startling effect of my communication. Her mind seemed far more perturbed about her daughter than about the heathen rogues and their conspiracy. "You know how odd Rachel is," My Lady said. "But I have never, in all my experience, seen her so strange and reserved. The loss of her jewel seems almost to have turned her brain. Who would have thought that horrible Diamond could have laid such a hold on her in so short a time?"

It was certainly strange. Taking toys and trinkets in general, Miss Rachel was nothing like so mad after them as most young girls. Yet there she was, still locked up inconsolably in her bedroom. It is fair to add that she was not the only one thrown out of the regular groove. Mr. Godfrey, though professionally a consoler-general, seemed at a loss where to look for his own resources. Having no company to amuse him and no chance to comfort Miss Rachel, he wandered hither and thither about the house and gardens in an aimless, uneasy way. He was in two different minds about whether to relieve the family of his presence as a guest or stay on to offer humble services. He decided ultimately that staying was perhaps the most customary and considerate course. Circumstances try the metal a man is really made of. Mr. Godfrey, tried by circumstances, showed himself of weaker metal than I had thought him. As for the women-servants, excepting Rosanna Spearman who kept to herself, they took to whispering in corners and staring suspiciously, as is the manner of that weaker half of the human family when anything extraordinary happens. I myself acknowledged to have been fidgety and ill-tempered. The cursed Moonstone had turned us all upside down.

A little before eleven, Mr. Franklin came back. The resolute side of him had, to all appearance, given way under the stress. He had left us at a gallop; he came back at a walk. When he went away, he was made of iron. When he returned, he was stuffed with cotton, as limp as limp could be.

"Well," said My Lady, "are the police coming?"

"Yes," said Mr. Franklin. "They said they would follow me in a fly. Superintendent Seegrave of your local police force and two of his men. A mere form! The case is hopeless."

"What! Have the Indians escaped, sir?" I asked.
