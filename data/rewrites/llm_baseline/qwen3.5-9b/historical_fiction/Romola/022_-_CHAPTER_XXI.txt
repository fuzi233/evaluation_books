It was November 17th, 1494. More than eighteen months had passed since Tito and Romola were last united in the joyous light of Easter, a day marked by a shower of comfits thrown in the ancient Greek style, signaling that heaven itself would sprinkle sweetness upon their shared life. Since that springtime, however, the landscape of Florence had shifted dramatically. Just as every blossom and fruit depends on the lifeblood flowing through the roots of the tree, the destinies of Tito and Romola were now tethered to the sweeping political tides reshaping Italy.

Only a week prior, the spirit of antiquity had surged back into the hearts of the Florentines. The great bell of the palace tower had shattered the silence with an alarm call, summoning the populace to arm themselves with rusted weapons, farming tools, and makeshift clubs to expel the Medici. The Gate of San Gallo had slammed shut on Piero de' Medici, fleeing toward Bologna with his mercenaries in panic, while his younger brother, the Cardinal, escaped disguised as a Franciscan friar, with a bounty placed on both their heads. In the aftermath, houses were sacked according to old custom; the shameful portraits of former conspirators were scrubbed from public walls; and the exiled enemies of the Medici were welcomed home. The fledgling tyrants had been evicted from their golden nest in the Via Larga, and the Republic had regained its voice.

Yet, a mere week later, the same grand palace in the Via Larga was being transformed to receive a new occupant. If the colorful drapes covering the streets, the banners streaming from windows, and the carpets stretching across every step were proof of celebration, then Florence was indeed rejoicing in anticipation of its distinguished guest. A vibrant river of color flowed from the palace, winding past the Cathedral, through the great Piazza della Signoria, and across the Ponte Vecchio to the Porta San Frediano, the gateway facing Pisa. Near this gate, a platform and canopy had been erected for the ruling Signoria.

Messer Luca Corsini, a doctor of law, felt his heart flutter with the weight of a Latin oration awaiting him. Every senior elder of Florence was preparing for the procession, smoothing their chins and adjusting their fine silk *luccos*. Young nobles inspected their new tunics, styled after the French fashion, designed to impress the stranger with their unique elegance. Meanwhile, a vast assembly of the clergy—from the Archbishop in his radiant vestments to trains of black, white, and grey-robed monks—conferred early in the morning. They debated how best to arrange their relics, sacred banners, and consecrated jewels to honor the arrival of the illustrious visitor, scheduled for three o'clock in the afternoon.

This was an unprecedented guest. He had crossed the Alpine passes leading an army Italy had never witnessed: thousands of formidable Swiss mercenaries, hardened to fight for love, hate, or coin; a host of proud cavaliers; and an infantry where one in a hundred carried an arquebus. Worse still, he brought bronze cannons that fired iron balls instead of stones, pulled by horses rather than oxen, capable of breaching walls before a city could repair the damage.

Some hailed the newcomer as Charlemagne, the legendary restorer of Florence and conqueror of degenerate kings; others compared him to Cyrus, the liberator of the chosen people restoring the Temple. His ambitions were equally grandiose: to march through Italy amid jubilant crowds, resolve conflicts in Rome, seize the Kingdom of Naples by hereditary right and force, and launch a crusade against the Turks, aiming to slaughter or convert them to Christianity. This plan seemed fitting for the "Most Christian King," the son of the cunning Louis XI, whose France had risen to become the preeminent Christian monarchy. This figure, an antitype of Cyrus and Charlemagne, was none other than Charles VIII of France.

On the surface, the event seemed destined to stir the collective memory of humanity's greatest epochs. Indeed, a widespread conviction held that the arrival of the French king and his army was a miracle of Providence—a moment when marble statues might sweat, phantom warriors might fight in the sky, and beasts might birth monsters. This belief rested less on the sheer scale of the invasion and more on the moral atmosphere of the times, giving rise to profound presentiments. These emotions found their most striking expression in the voice of a single man: Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco.

On a September morning, as news of the French advance rang in everyone's ears, Savonarola preached in the Florence Cathedral from the text, "Behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth." He believed this was divine guidance, interpreting the "flood" as a symbol of both avenging wrath and purifying mercy, specifically pointing to the French army. His audience, comprising some of the era's most cultivated minds, listened with shuddering awe. Savonarola possessed a rare power to imprint his convictions upon others, swaying varied souls alike. Four years prior, he had proclaimed from Florence's main pulpit that a scourge was descending upon Italy to purge the Church. He appeared to hold a mission akin to the Hebrew prophets, casting the Florentines as a second chosen people.

While prophecies were not unknown in that age—often resembling a dim candlelight flickering in small corners of fate—Savonarola stood apart as a monumental exception. Where others offered vague whispers of the future, his vision burned like a mighty beacon guiding and warning humanity. To the soberest observers, the supernatural quality of his foresight gained credibility from the peculiar stagnation of the age. By late 1492, following the death of Lorenzo de' Medici and Tito Melema's arrival, Italy enjoyed a deceptive peace. There was no fear of famine, as harvests were bountiful; new palaces rose in every city, and villas dotted the hills. Those with excess lived without fear of the poor, whose armor had rusted and who had grown tame, paying for mercenary armies as easily as they bought goods from Smyrna. Even the threat of the Turk had faded, with the Pope preferring bribes for potential poisoning over plans for war or conversion.

The world seemed a comfortable establishment for the fortunate few, a realm where lust, obscenity, treachery, and murder were not only tolerated but managed effectively. Adorned with the patronage of learning and art, flattery was available in exquisite Latin, and artists painted both the holy and the profane with equal skill. The Church, though reportedly disgraced in its leadership and lacking spiritual vitality, prospered more than ever. The heavens smiled above, and the earth remained still. Yet, amidst this festive facade, a man named Savonarola had spent over two years preaching of an impending scourge, declaring that the world was unfit for hypocrites, libertines, and oppressors.

From beneath these smiling skies, he had seen a sword hanging—the sword of God's justice, soon to descend with purifying punishment. Born in Ferrara seventeen years earlier, the stark contradiction between men's actions and their professed beliefs had nearly destroyed his appetite for worldly life, driving him to the cloister at twenty-three. He believed God entrusted the Church with the sacred lamp of truth, only to see it corrupted into a tomb hiding that light. As scandals multiplied and hypocrisy turned to impudence, he asked: Had the world lost its righteous ruler? Was the Church abandoned? His answer was firm: No. The Sacred Book revealed that when the wickedness of the chosen people cried out, God's judgment inevitably followed. Reason itself demanded vengeance to break the cycle of evil. Unless the Church was reclaimed, how could the promise of converting the heathens and uniting the world under one law be fulfilled?

Savonarola's faith was fueled by visions, but its true strength lay in his burning indignation at injustice and his fervent trust in an Unseen Justice that would restore purity. To his ambitious soul, which craved great ends achieved through willpower, faith in a righteous ruler merged seamlessly with faith in immediate divine intervention.

Beneath the glittering mask of ecclesiastical and secular dignity, tensions simmered across Italy. Ludovico Sforza of Milan, a patron of Leonardo da Vinci, coveted the ducal crown for himself rather than letting it rest on a weak nephew vulnerable to poisoning. He feared the Spanish-born King Ferdinand and his son Alfonso of Naples, who despised the treachery required to depose relatives for a Lombard usurper. The Neapolitan royals, in turn, feared their suzerain, Pope Alexander Borgia. All watched Florence nervously, fearing its strategic position might tip the balance. Everyone, including the cautious and powerful Venice, eyed the shifting alliances.

Lorenzo de' Medici had previously maintained a delicate equilibrium, balancing alliances between Naples, the Pope, and Milan. However, his son Piero's rash vanity shattered this stability. Suspecting a conspiracy, Ludovico Sforza decided to invite the French king to intervene, leveraging Charles VIII's claim to Naples as an heir of the House of Anjou. Ambassadors, termed "orators" in those rhetorical ages, traveled constantly. A recalcitrant cardinal, refusing to recognize a pope elected by bribery, joined the chorus, urging the French king with fiery rhetoric. The young king listened willingly.
