The Major and Captain Costigan were veterans of the road, men who had stared down enemies without flinching, so their composure remained unshaken. Yet, the atmosphere in Cos's sitting-room shifted palpably with Pendennis's sudden appearance. Miss Fotheringay's heart, usually a steady drum, began to stutter; a flush, bright and unfeigned, climbed her cheeks as Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks fixed a squinting glare upon her. Meanwhile, the tiny, bent figure perched in the window-seat—the theater composer who had abandoned his pen to watch the fencing match—lifted his gaze with renewed vigor as the Major, sporting impeccably blacked boots, swept into the room with a cascade of bows that would have done justice to a courtier.

"My daughter—my friend, Mr. Bows—my gallant young pupil and companion, I trust, Sir Derby Oaks!" boomed Costigan, waving a hand that seemed to conduct an orchestra of introductions. "In a trice, Major, I am at your service!" With this, the old soldier vanished into his adjoining chamber. In a blur of motion that defied physics, he twisted his lank hair with an ancient brush, ripped off a threadbare stock, swapped it for a pristine new one crafted by Emily, tucked in a crisp collar, and donned the brand-new coat ordered specifically for Miss Fotheringay's benefit night. All this metamorphosis occurred within sixty seconds.

Sir Derby followed close behind, emerging moments later encased in his 'shell-jacket,' a garment that hugged his large frame with uncomfortable tightness—a look, Cos suspected, that Milly, Emily, and perhaps Pen, found utterly adorable.

While the actress and the newcomer exchanged pleasantries regarding the weather, Costigan re-emerged, fully transformed into his new suit. "Need I apologize to ye, Major, for receiving ye in shirt-sleeves?" he asked, his voice dripping with a courtesy that masked a lifetime of rough trade.

"An old soldier finds no nobler employment than instructing a youth in the art of the sword," the Major replied, standing tall. "I recall hearing tales of your prowess, Captain Costigan."

"Why, you've heard of Jack Costigan, Major?" the Captain exclaimed, feigning deep offense.

The Major had, in fact, pried details from his nephew regarding this Irish officer. Whether he truly remembered the man or simply wanted to sound authoritative, he proceeded with absolute conviction. "I distinctly recollect meeting Mr. Costigan at Sir Richard Strachan's table during the Walcheren campaign. A fine singer, I'm told."

At this, Miss Fotheringay looked bewildered. "Perhaps we shall discuss these matters later," the Major suggested smoothly, shifting his focus back to the actress. "I sought you today to pay my respects." He executed another bow, so elegant it would have graced a duchess, causing Milly to rise immediately. She retrieved an unripped satin gown from a nearby seat, carried it over, and presented the empty chair to the Major with a curtsy so sharp it nearly sliced the air.

"You speak highly of my performances from my nephew," the Major noted, settling into the chair. "But Arthur is but a boy, a wild spirit whose enthusiasm often outpaces reality. I wished to judge for myself. Permit me to say, your performance astounded me. I have seen the greatest stars, and yet, you surpass them all. You possess a majesty akin to Mrs. Siddons."

"Faith, I always did say so!" Costigan winked at his daughter, gesturing for the Major to sit. "Milly, draw a chair." As soon as the hint fell, Milly produced the seat.

"You are equally pathos-filled," the Major continued, "reminiscent of Miss O'Neill. Those songs echoed Mrs. Jordan in her prime. And your bearing? It called to mind Mars. Have you ever seen the God of War, Miss Fotheringay?"

"There were two Mahers in Crow Street," Emily chimed in. "Fanny was passable, but Biddy..."

"The Major speaks of the deity, Milly, my dear," Costigan interjected quickly.

"It is not that Mars I refer to, though Venus might forgive thoughts of him," the Major smiled, directing his gaze squarely at Sir Derby Oaks, who had returned in his jacket. However, the compliment failed to soothe the young officer. Sir Derby, who clearly did not grasp the nuance of classical mythology, received the remark with a stiff, sullen silence, scowling at Miss Fotheringay as if to ask, *What devilry is this?*

Pendennis, however, was not perturbed. The ill-humor delighted him. "Ah," he mused, "a rival enters the arena." He silently vowed that Sir Derby might win this contest, whatever the cost to Pen and Emily.

"I fear I interrupted your fencing lesson," the Major said lightly. "My stay at Chatteris is brief, but I needed to introduce myself to Captain Costigan and see a lady who charmed me from afar. Last night, Miss Fotheringay, many hearts were captivated. A reverend friend of mine returned home in a state of ecstasy over Ophelia, and I saw Sir Derby Oaks toss a bouquet—an offering no actress deserves less." He pointed to the flowers in a glass of water on the mantel. "Had I known what awaited me, I would have brought one. Are not these the same blooms?"

"I adore flowers," Miss Fotheringay murmured, casting a languid glance at Sir Derby, who remained sullen.

"Sweets to the sweet," Pendennis quoted, striving for warmth. "'Pon my life, I haven't a clue," Sir Derby muttered. "Not a literary man."

"Do you not share your father's love for letters?" the Major asked, feigning shock. "He was a remarkable scholar."

"He saved my life," the Major added solemnly.

"Oh, really?" Miss Fotheringay gasped, rolling her eyes first toward the Major, then at Sir Derby with gratitude. But the young officer was immune to her glances. Far from being pleased that his father, the apothecary, had spared the Major's life, Sir Derby looked as though he wished the accident had gone differently.

"My father was a decent doctor," Sir Derby replied coldly. "I'm not in medicine. Good morning, sir. Cos, goodbye. Miss Fotheringay, good morning." Despite the young lady's imploring smiles, the dragoon bowed stiffly and marched out, his sabre clattering down the creaking stairs. His voice rang out in anger as he cursed little Tom Creed, kicking a spinning top into the street with an oath.

The Major did not smile, despite the amusement the scene provoked. "A monstrously handsome youth," he told Costigan. "One of the finest-looking soldiers I have ever seen."

"A credit to the army and humanity itself," Costigan agreed. "Refined manners, princely fortune, sixteen stone of muscle."

"A perfect champion," the Major laughed. "All the ladies must admire him."

"He's pleasant enough, considering his weight," Milly admitted dryly, "but he lacks conversation."

"He shines best on horseback," Mr. Bows observed. "Third in the steeplechase on a horse named Tareaways."

The Major nodded slowly, realizing Milly's lack of wit was matched by her acting genius.

With typical Irish hospitality, Costigan pressed refreshments upon his guest. The Major, though not hungry after a hypothetical feast, feigned a craving for biscuits and Madeira, knowing that accepting small kindnesses flattered the donor and softened hearts. "Some of the old Madeira, Milly," Cos whispered.

The girl nodded intelligently, slipped out, and summoned her little messenger, Tommy Creed, downstairs. She handed him coins and sent him running to the Grapes for a pint of wine and sixpence-worth of biscuits, instructing him to hurry back.

Downstairs, Miss Costigan sat with Mrs. Creed, whispering about the Major upstairs—"a soft-spoken gentle giant"—and how Sir Derby had stormed out in jealous rage.

"She keeps the cellar key, Major," Costigan announced as the girl departed.

"By Jove, you have a magnificent butler!" Pendennis replied gallantly. "Plainer women served us well in our day, Captain."

"And lucky is the man who secures her," Costigan insisted, turning to Mr. Bows. "Ask my friend here if Miss Fotheringay's mind is not superior to her beauty, if she lacks a cultivated intellect and an amiable disposition."

"Bah," Mr. Bows murmured drily. "Here comes Hebe, blushing from the cellar."

"Don't you think it's time to go to rehearsal, Miss Hebe?" Bows asked, gathering his papers. "A fine late arrival costs a penalty." He shot her a look suggesting they should depart, leaving the elders alone.

Miss Fotheringay, looking uncommonly pretty and good-natured, obeyed. Bows shuffled after her, retrieving his hat and cane.

"Must you go?" the Major asked, stepping forward. "Before you leave, allow an old fellow to shake your hand. I am proud to have made your acquaintance and earnestly desire to become your friend."
