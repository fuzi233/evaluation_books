My lady’s household was where the trouble began—or rather, where it ended, though none of us knew it then. The Diamond’s disappearance would never have touched us if it hadn’t been for my lady’s daughter, and my lady’s daughter wouldn’t have existed at all if not for my lady herself. So, if we’re to make sense of this business, we must begin with her.

You’ve likely heard of the three Miss Herncastles—Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia—the finest trio of sisters to grace London society. Julia, the youngest, was the best of them, though I may be partial, having served in their father’s house as a boy. The old lord was a man of endless words and short temper, but we needn’t dwell on him. My place was with the sisters, and there I stayed until Miss Julia married Sir John Verinder.

Sir John was a good man, if a bit in need of steering—a task my lady took on with relish. Under her care, he prospered, grew stout, and lived content until his last breath. When my lady became a widow, she kept me in her service, and I followed her to this house, where I took up the duties of bailiff. I’d been a farmer’s son, so the work suited me.

Years passed. My lady, ever perceptive, noted my advancing age and one winter’s evening presented me with a woolen waistcoat—her own handiwork—before proposing I retire from outdoor labor and take up the steadier role of house-steward. I resisted, of course, but she had a way of getting her way. After some deliberation (aided by my trusty *Robinson Crusoe*), I relented.

But I digress. The heart of the matter is the Diamond, and I’ve strayed far from it. My daughter Penelope, peering over my shoulder, reminds me sharply of this fact. So let me set myself right: the Diamond came to us through Miss Rachel, my lady’s daughter, and with it came the first whispers of misfortune. But that, as they say, is another chapter.
