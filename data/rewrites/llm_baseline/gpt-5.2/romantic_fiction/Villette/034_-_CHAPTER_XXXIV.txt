Madame Beck sent for me on Thursday afternoon, and, with that quiet promptitude in her eye which always made a request wear the air of an order, asked whether I had anything to hinder me from going into town to execute some little commissions at the shops.

I had none. To be useful—especially in matters requiring neatness and exactitude—was at least to have a place, and a purpose; and so I accepted, and was presently furnished with a list—wools in such a shade as would not “bleed” in washing, silks to match certain patterns, skeins of embroidering-thread, tapes, slides, tassels: all the fussy, finger-staining furnishings by which young girls learn patience, and women learn economy.

I dressed for a day whose sky hung low, sultry and leaden—air thick as if it had been boiled down to a syrup, and then forgotten on the fire. I had drawn the spring-bolt of the street-door, my hand already on the latch to go out, when Madame’s voice recalled me to the salle-à-manger.

“Pardon, Meess Lucie!” she cried, as if a thought had darted upon her at the very instant. “I recollect one more errand for you, if your good-nature will not deem itself over-burdened?”

I hastened to protest—there are forms of refusal which women like her do not pardon; besides, I was indeed disengaged.

She ran into the little salon and came back with a basket—pretty, light, and contrived for a lady’s hand rather than a servant’s—filled with hothouse fruit, such fruit as seems less grown than fabricated: skins polished to a blush, fragrance forced to a sweetness almost too full, leaves around them dark-green and wax-like, with pale, starry blossoms of some foreign plant pinned in for show. The scent rose warm and faintly cloying—summer coaxed out of glass and fire, as if Nature had been bribed.

“There,” said she, placing it on my arm with an air of kindness that kept its edge of command. “It is not heavy; it will not shame your neat toilette as if it were a household detail. Do me the favour to leave this little basket at the house of Madame Walravens, with my felicitations on her fête. She lives down in the old town, Numéro 3, Rue des Mages. The walk is rather long—but you have the afternoon; do not hurry. If you are not back in time for dinner, I will order a portion to be saved, or Goton—who is fond of you—will toss up some trifle for your especial benefit: an omelette, perhaps, or some soup quickly strengthened with herbs. You shall not be forgotten, ma bonne Meess. And oh! please—” she called me back once more as I moved away—“be sure to insist on seeing Madame Walravens herself, and give the basket into her own hands. She is punctilious. Adieu! Au revoir!”

So at last I got away.

In the shops my list consumed more time than my steps. To choose wools is to handle half the flocks of the country—each skein with its own rough breath of dye and lanolin; to match silks is to strain the eye upon gleam and undertone; and embroidering-thread, fine as a spider’s cable, entangles itself like thought. I stood among drawers and trays, amid the whisper of measuring-tapes and the dry music of paper unrolled and smoothed; the city outside sounded muffled, as if the air, already heavy with storm, had begun to thicken the very noise. At length, however, the patterns for slippers and bell-ropes and cabas were chosen, the tassels and slides selected; all that little “tripotage” was off my mind. Nothing remained but the fruit and the felicitations.

I liked the prospect of the long walk into the grim Basse-Ville; and I liked it no worse because the evening sky, over the city, was settling into a mass like black-blue metal heated at the rim and slowly inflaming to a dull, labouring red. There are weathers that command exertion—high wind, for instance, that wrestles with one’s limbs and calls out strength I do not possess without pain. But rain—rain asks only resignation. It takes you as you are; it drenches garment and hair; and in return it purifies the town. It sweeps the broad streets as clean as a scullery-maid’s pail; it empties the pavements; it hushes wheels and cries; it petrifies the living city into a silent, wet monument. Let then the floods descend—only I must first rid myself of this basket of fruit, this burden of warm, perfumed sweetness, which in such a storm seemed almost absurdly delicate.

An unknown clock from an unknown tower—Jean Baptiste’s voice was now too distant to be heard—was tolling the third quarter past five when I reached the street and house Madame Beck had named. It was hardly a street; rather a piece of a square. Grass grew between broad grey flags; the houses were large, old, with an air of shut-up pride; behind them rose the dark suggestion of trees—gardens, perhaps, where former owners had walked in leisure. Antiquity brooded there; trade and bustle were banished. Rich men had once held that quarter, and greatness had sat there; but wealth had long since stretched its gilded wings and flown, leaving its old nests—either to shelter penury, or to stand empty and mouldering through winters.

Across this deserted place the first great drops were beginning to fall—slow, heavy drops, darkening the stones like little bruises. In all that open space I saw no sign of life except an infirm old priest, bending and propped on a staff—the very emblem of decay. He had issued from the house I sought; and when I paused before the door, just closed after him, and rang, he turned to look at me. He did not soon avert his gaze. Perhaps I, with my basket of forcing-house fruit, and my youth—youth without authority—appeared incongruous in that solemn, forsaken square.

The door was opened, not by a plump, ruddy bonne, but by a very old woman in an antique peasant costume—cap at once hideous and costly, with long flaps of native lace; jacket and petticoat of stout cloth; sabots like little boats. The dress suited the place; it matched the grey flags and the ancient doors. Her face, however, did not soothe as her costume did. I had seldom seen anything more sourly cantankerous. She would scarcely reply to my inquiry for Madame Walravens; and I believe she would have snatched the basket from my arm—fruit and all—had not the priest, hobbling back, checked her.

His deafness, or seeming deafness, made my task awkward. I repeated, more than once, that I must see Madame Walravens herself; that my orders were to place the basket in her own hands. At last he understood. He addressed the aged bonne—not in French, but in the aboriginal tongue of Labassecour: the very sound of it struck my ear with a strange power, like an old song half remembered. It was thicker, earthier than French, with consonants that seemed to have grown out of fields and barns. The bonne, who had treated my French as if it were impertinence, listened to that dialect as to law; her whole manner softened—no, not softened, but yielded. Language, I saw, is command; and the tongue of the soil holds dominion where the polished speech of cities may knock and be left on the doorstep.

I was at last admitted. The priest himself escorted me up-stairs and ushered me into a sort of salon, and there left me.

The room was large, with a fine old ceiling and windows of coloured glass, almost church-like; but it was bare and desolate, and, under the thick shadow of the coming storm, looked lowering—like a hall waiting for a funeral that had been delayed. A smaller room opened within; there the blind of the single casement was closed. In the deep gloom I could scarcely distinguish furniture; only outlines—an upright chair, a little table, something like a framed picture on the wall.

By-and-by that picture seemed to shift. To my bewilderment, it shook, sank, rolled back, and vanished, leaving an arched opening—an arched passage beyond, and a winding stair, cold stone, uncarpeted, unpainted, like a stair in a donjon. Down those steps came a tap, tap—as of a stick. Then a shadow fell, and last of all a presence.

Was it a body? It seemed less a body than an obstruction—something that darkened the arch. It drew nearer, and I saw it well, and comprehension came with a chill.

Well might this be named the quarter of the Magi; well might those turrets overlooking it take for godfathers three mystic sages of dead and dark art. Here hoar enchantment prevailed. That vanishing picture, that stone passage, that winding stair—these were stage-properties of a fairy tale; and the chief figure was its malevolent fairy.

She might have been three feet high; yet she had no shape—only angles and hollows, a heap of brocade and jewels arranged upon deformity. Her skinny hands rested on each other, pressing the gold knob of a wand-like ivory staff. Her face—large, set not on shoulders but before her breast, as if she had no neck—was scored with a century; her eyes seemed older still: malign, unfriendly, thick grey brows above, livid lids around. They looked at me as if my existence were an offence.

She wore a gown of bright blue brocade, full-tinted as a gentian, with satin foliage in a large pattern; over it a costly shawl, gorgeously bordered, so large that its many-coloured fringe swept the floor. But her chief splendour was her jewels: long clear earrings with a lustre no glass could borrow; rings on her skeleton hands, thick gold hoops set with stones—purple, green, blood-red. Hunchbacked, dwarfish, doting—she was adorned like a barbarian queen. A silver beard bristled her chin.

“Que me voulez-vous?” she demanded, hoarsely—the voice rather of male than female old age.

I delivered the basket and Madame Beck’s message.

“Is that all?” she asked.

“It is all,” I answered.

“Truly—it was well worth while,” she returned. “Go back to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy fruit when I want it; et quant à ses félicitations, je m’en moque!”

And she turned her back.

At that instant thunder broke, and lightning blazed broad over salon and inner room. It was as if the tale insisted upon its accompaniment: the wanderer decoyed into the enchanted house, the spell-wakened tempest rising outside. The air had its own soundscape now—no longer the distant commerce of streets, but the great, blunt percussion of heaven and the white tearing hiss of rain. Modern noise is a needless cruelty; but the storm’s uproar is primitive and, in its way, cleansing—an orchestra older than any city, yet capable of drowning it like a basin held under a tap.

What, in all this, was I to think of Madame Beck? She owned strange acquaintances. She sent gifts to an unpropitious shrine, and the uncouth being she honoured received both offering and congratulation with scorn. There went that sullen Sidonia, tapping her ivory staff on the mosaic parquet, muttering venomously as she disappeared.

The rain now fell in sheets. The sky, ruddy a while ago, had turned, through all its blackness, deadly pale, as if terrified by its own violence. Forked bolts lanced through vertical torrents; red zigzags interlaced a descent blanched like white metal; and all burst from a firmament swollen and dark.

Notwithstanding my late boast that I did not fear a shower, I did not like to go out under this waterspout. I left the inhospitable salon and sought the cold staircase; on the landing there was a seat. There I waited, listening: the rain’s continuous drumming, the thunder’s nearer crashes, the wind’s sudden shoves against shutters—each sound had its place, its pitch, its threat. In such moments the ear becomes the surest organ; it reads danger before the eye can see it.

Somebody glided along the gallery above. It was the old priest.

“Indeed, Mademoiselle shall not sit there,” he said. “It would displease our benefactor if he knew a stranger was so treated in this house.”

He begged me, with such earnest courtesy, to return to the salon, that without discourtesy I could not refuse. But instead of the bare larger room, he led me into the smaller inner chamber. He partially withdrew the blind, and the place revealed itself—less a boudoir than an oratory: solemn, close, dedicated rather to remembrance than comfort. It had that particular smell of old devotion—wax, dry wood, time itself—like linen laid away with lavender in a chest no one opens.

He sat down as if to keep me company; but he did not talk. He took out a book, fixed his eyes on the page, and moved his lips in whispering—prayer, litany. A yellow electric light from the storm-sky gilded his bald head while his figure remained in deep purple shade. He sat still as sculpture. Only when a fiercer bolt or harsher, closer rattle spoke of nearing danger did he raise his eyes—less in fear than in awe.

I, not being crushed by terror, could observe. To speak truth, I began to fancy that this old priest resembled Père Silas, before whom I had once knelt in the church of the Béguinage. The thought was vague—my confessor I had seen only in dusk and in profile—yet there seemed a likeness; I thought too I recognized the voice. As I watched him, he betrayed by a lifted look that he felt my scrutiny; and I turned my gaze to the room.

Beside a cross of curiously carved old ivory, yellow with age, above a dark-red prie-dieu furnished with a rich missal and ebon rosary, hung the picture whose dim outline had drawn my eyes before—the picture which had moved and rolled away with the wall. In the gloom I had taken it for a Madonna. Now, in clearer light, it proved a woman’s portrait in a nun’s dress. The face was pale, young, touched with the dejection of grief or ill health. Not beautiful; not even intellectual. Its amiability was the amiability of weakness—of a frame too frail for passion, of habits trained to acquiescence. Yet I looked long, and could not choose but look.

The priest, without lifting his head, as if his eyes never left the page, perceived where my attention rested. In a slow, distinct voice he let fall four observations, each like a bead told off:

“She was much beloved.

“She gave herself to God.

“She died young.

“She is still remembered—still wept.”

“By that aged lady—Madame Walravens?” I asked, thinking to find, in bereavement, a key to the old woman’s desperate ill-humour.

He shook his head, half smiling.

“No, no,” said he. “A grand-dame’s affection for her children’s children may be great, and her sorrow lively; but it is only the affianced lover—whom Fate, Faith, and Death have trebly denied the bliss of union—who mourns what he has lost, as Justine Marie is still mourned.”

He seemed to wish me to question him. I asked who had lost, and who still mourned, Justine Marie. He answered with a little narrative, not without romance, told while the storm, as if weary of its own violence, began to subside. He spoke with that French art of sentiment which pulls emotion into fine threads; I confess I should have found the truth more impressive had it been told with less handling and more simplicity. Yet the old man, though pious and gentle, had in him a certain shrewdness: when he lifted his eyes, he looked at me out of their corners with a subtlety one would hardly have expected to survive seventy years. Still, I believe he was a good man.

His tale concerned a former pupil—his benefactor—who had loved this pale Justine Marie, daughter of rich parents, when his own worldly prospects warranted the hope of a well-dowered hand. Then disaster came. The pupil’s father—once a rich banker—failed, died, and left debts and destitution. The son was forbidden to think of Marie. Above all, the hunchbacked grand-dame—Madame Walravens—opposed the match with a violence which deformity seemed to have sharpened into something demoniac.

Justine Marie, mild and weak, had neither the treachery to be false nor the force to be steadfast. She gave up her first suitor; but refusing to accept a second with a heavier purse, she withdrew to a convent and died there in her noviciate.

Lasting anguish, the priest said, had taken possession of the faithful heart that had worshipped her; and the truth of that love and grief showed itself afterward in deeds that, even to me, listening, were touching.

Years after Justine Marie’s death, ruin came upon her house also. Her father—a jeweller by nominal calling, but one who dealt on the Bourse—was entangled in financial transactions that brought exposure and ruinous fines. He died of grief for the loss and shame for the infamy. His old mother—this very Madame Walravens—and his bereaved wife were left penniless, and might have died of want; but the once-despised suitor, hearing of their condition, came with singular devotedness to the rescue. He took on their insolent pride the revenge of pure charity—housing them, caring for them, befriending them as tenderly and efficiently as a son.

The mother—on the whole a good woman—died blessing him. The strange, loveless, misanthrope grandmother lived on, supported entirely by this self-sacrificing man. Her, who had blighted his hope and denied him domestic happiness, awarding him long mourning and cheerless solitude, he treated with a respect a good son might offer a kind mother.

He had brought her to this house; and, said the priest, tears rising frankly to his eyes, “here, too, he shelters me—his old tutor—and Agnes, a superannuated servant of his father’s family. To our sustenance, and to other charities, I know he devotes three parts of his income, keeping only the fourth to provide himself with bread and the most modest accommodation. By this arrangement he has rendered it impossible ever to marry. He has given himself to God and to his angel-bride as much as if he were a priest, like me.”

He wiped his tears before those last words; and when he pronounced them he lifted his eyes to mine for an instant. Though veiled, the glance shot a meaning that struck me—like a pin passed through cloth to fasten something down.

Certain people—Romanists, especially—are strange beings. Such a one, whom you know no more than the last Inca, knows you and all your concerns; and says to you so and so, not because the impulse happens to seize him, but because a plan has been laid. An errand suddenly remembered; a basket of fruit; an address in an ancient square; the old priest “accidentally” crossing as I arrived; his intervention with the bonne; his reappearance on the stairs; my introduction to this oratory; the portrait; the narrative volunteered—each incident, taken alone, might seem chance, like loose beads in a palm. But I had seen the rosary on the prie-dieu. I felt the string somewhere. I could not yet find the knot.

Perhaps my abstraction looked suspicious. He interrupted gently.

“Mademoiselle,” said he, “I trust you have not far to go through these inundated streets?”

“More than half a league.”

“You live—?”

“In the Rue Fossette.”

“Not—” he spoke with sudden animation—“not at the pensionnat of Madame Beck?”

“The same.”

“Donc,” he cried, clapping his hands. “Donc, vous devez connaître mon noble élève—mon Paul?”

“Monsieur Paul Emanuel, Professor of Literature?”

“He—and none other.”

A brief silence followed. The hidden clasp of the necklace seemed suddenly to give under pressure; I felt the connection become palpable.

“Was it of M. Paul you have been speaking?” I asked. “Was he your pupil—and the benefactor of Madame Walravens?”

“Yes; and of Agnes, the old servant. And moreover”—he laid an emphasis as if to seal the sentence—“he was and is the lover, true, constant, eternal, of that saint in heaven—Justine Marie.”

“And who, father, are you?” I continued. The question was scarcely needed; I had prepared myself for the answer.

“I, daughter, am Père Silas,” he said; “that unworthy son of Holy Church whom you once honoured with a noble and touching confidence—showing me the core of a heart, and the inner shrine of a mind whereof, in solemn truth, I coveted the direction, in behalf of the only true faith. Nor have I for a day lost sight of you; nor for an hour failed to take in you a rooted interest. Passed under the discipline of Rome, moulded by her high training, inoculated with her salutary doctrines, inspired by the zeal she alone gives—I realize what then might be your spiritual rank, your practical value; and I envy Heresy her prey.”

I half realized myself, for an instant, as if already in that condition—disciplined, moulded, inoculated. “Not so,” thought I; but I restrained any protest and sat quiet.

“I suppose M. Paul does not live here?” I asked, turning to what seemed to me the only matter worth pursuing.

“No. He comes occasionally—to worship his beloved saint, to make his confession to me, and to pay his respects to her he calls his mother. His own lodging consists but of two rooms. He has no servant. And yet he will not suffer Madame Walravens to dispose of those splendid jewels with which you see her adorned, and in which she takes a puerile pride—the ornaments of her youth, and the last relics of her son the jeweller’s wealth.”

How often, I said to myself, has this man—this M. Emanuel—seemed to me to lack magnanimity in trifles; and yet how great he is in great things!

I did not count, among his greatness, either confession or saint-worship. But self-denial, carried out in bread and rent and daily living—self-denial that keeps other people warm while it leaves oneself poorly housed—that I could understand. It is easy to offer one’s feelings; it is harder to portion out one’s income like a loaf and give away the larger share.

“How long is it since that lady died?” I asked, looking again at Justine Marie.

“Twenty years. She was somewhat older than M. Emanuel. He was then very young; for he is not much beyond forty.”

“Does he yet weep her?”

“His heart will weep her always,” said Père Silas. “The essence of Emanuel’s nature is—constancy.”

He spoke the word as if it were doctrine, and also warning.

Now the tempest had spent itself. The rain still fell, but without rage; the sun broke out pale and watery through a cleft in the hot firmament, as if exhausted by its own lightnings. A longer delay would leave me no daylight for the return. I rose, thanked the father for his hospitality and his tale, and was answered benignantly with a “pax vobiscum,” which I could accept, for it seemed uttered with true benevolence—though I liked less the mystic phrases that hung about him like incense.

“Daughter, you shall be what you shall be!” he added.

It was an oracle that made me shrug as soon as I had got outside the door. Few of us know certainly what we shall come to; but for all that had happened, I had good hope of living and dying a sober-minded Protestant. There is a hollowness within, and a flourish without, in that “Holy Church” which tempts me but moderately.

I went on my way through wet, darkening streets, pondering many things. Whatever Romanism may be, there are good Romanists. This man, Emanuel, seemed of the best—touched with superstition, influenced by priestcraft, yet wondrous for fond faith, for pious devotion, for sacrifice of self, for charity unbounded. It remained to see how Rome, by her agents, handled such qualities—whether she cherished them for their own sake and for God’s, or put them out to usury, and made booty of the interest.
