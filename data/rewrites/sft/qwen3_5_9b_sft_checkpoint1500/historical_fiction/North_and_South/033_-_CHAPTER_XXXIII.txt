The silence within the Hale household felt unnatural, a heavy, suffocating void left in the wake of such terrifying commotion. Mr. Hale, having ensured every necessity for his daughter's return, sat once more in his customary chair, sinking into one of those somber, wakeful reveries that often plagued him. Dixon had enlisted Mary Higgins to manage the kitchen, directing her with a low, fierce whisper; her rebukes carried an energy born of reverence, for speaking loudly would have seemed sacrilegious while a corpse lay in the house. Margaret resolved not to speak of the final, crowning terror to her father. To discuss it would serve no purpose; it had ended, albeit tragically. Now, her greatest fear was not the death itself, but that Leonard would somehow secure funds to follow Frederick to London and hunt him down. Yet, the odds were immense against any such scheme succeeding. Margaret determined not to torment herself with thoughts of what she could not prevent. Frederick would be as vigilant as she could make him, and within a day or two, he would be safely out of England.

"I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow," Margaret said softly.

"Yes," replied her father, his voice flat. "I suppose so."

"If he can come, he will be here by evening, I should think."

"If he cannot, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral. I cannot go alone. I think I would break down utterly."

"Don't ask Mr. Thornton, Papa. Let me go with you," Margaret implored, her tone sudden and earnest.

"You? My dear, ladies do not generally attend funerals."

"No!" she interrupted, her eyes flashing. "Because they cannot control themselves. Women of our class do not go because we lack the power to govern our emotions, yet we are ashamed to display them. Poor women go, and they do not care if they are seen overcome with grief. But I promise you, Papa, if you allow me, I will cause you no trouble. Do not send a stranger and leave me out. If Mr. Bell cannot come, I *must* go. I will not press this wish against your will if he does arrive."

Mr. Bell could not come. A severe attack of gout kept him confined. Yet, his letter was profoundly affectionate, brimming with genuine sorrow for his absence. He expressed hope to visit them soon, though his Milton estate required immediate attention, and his agent insisted his presence was mandatory. Or perhaps, he had avoided Milton as long as possible, and now the sole solace for this unavoidable journey was the faint hope that seeing his old friend might offer him some comfort.

Margaret found it nearly impossible to dissuade her father from inviting Mr. Thornton. An indescribable repulsion rose within her at the prospect. On the eve of the funeral, a stately note arrived from Mrs. Thornton to Miss Hale. Written at her son's request, it declared that their carriage would attend the ceremony, provided it did not offend the family. Margaret tossed the note onto her father's lap.

"Oh, let us avoid these formalities," she pleaded. "Let us go alone, Papa. You and I. They do not care for us, or else he would have offered to walk himself, rather than propose sending an empty carriage."

"I thought you were so extremely averse to his company, Margaret," Mr. Hale said, surprised.

"And so I am," she admitted, her voice trembling. "I do not want him to come at all; I should especially dislike the humiliation of asking him. But this... it feels like a mockery of mourning, and I did not expect such courtesy from him." Suddenly, she burst into tears. Having been so subdued in her grief, so considerate of others, so gentle and patient, her father could not comprehend her sudden agitation. She seemed restless, unable to sit still, and despite the tenderness he lavished upon her, her crying only grew louder.

That night proved disastrous. Margaret slept fitfully, unprepared for the fresh anxiety brought by a letter from Frederick. Mr. Lennox was out of town, returning only by Tuesday, perhaps Monday. Concluding it prudent to wait, Frederick decided to remain in London for a couple of extra days. He had considered coming down to Milton again; the temptation had been overwhelming. However, the lingering presence of Mr. Bell in his father's house, coupled with the alarming incident at the railway station moments prior, had hardened his resolve to stay put. Margaret could assure herself that he took every precaution against being tracked by Leonard. Relief washed over her as she realized her father was absent in his mother's room; if he had been present, she would have been forced to read the letter aloud. It would have triggered a state of nervous alarm in him that she would have struggled to quell.

There was more than the mere fact of Frederick's detention that troubled her. The letter contained allusions to the recognition at Milton and the possibility of a pursuit, chilling her blood to the bone. How would this affect her father? Regret gnawed at her. Many times she reproached herself for urging the plan to consult Mr. Lennox. At the time, it had seemed to involve so little delay, adding so little to the already slim chances of detection. Yet, every subsequent event had proven it undeniably undesirable. Margaret battled fiercely against this regret, against the sting of self-reproach for saying what then seemed wise but now appeared foolhardy. But her father, his mind and body too depressed to rise above the darkness, would succumb to these seeds of morbid reflection. Margaret summoned every ounce of strength to aid him.

Her father seemed to have forgotten they awaited news from Frederick. He was consumed by a single, crushing thought: the last visible token of his wife's presence was about to be removed from him, hidden forever from his sight. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker arranged the black crepe drapery around him. He looked wistfully at Margaret, and when finally released, he tottered toward her, his voice a broken murmur. "Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it, truly I do. I know it is God's will. But I cannot understand why she died. Pray for me, Margaret, that I may find faith to pray. It is a great strait, my child."

Sitting beside him in the carriage, Margaret supported him almost entirely in her arms. She repeated every noble verse of holy comfort, every text of faithful resignation she could recall. Her voice never faltered, and strangely, she drew strength from the act of offering it. Her father's lips moved along with hers, echoing the familiar texts as her words suggested them. It was terrible to witness the patient, struggling effort to achieve a resignation he lacked the inner strength to accept as part of himself.

Margaret's fortitude nearly shattered when Dixon, with a subtle gesture, pointed out Nicholas Higgins and his daughter standing apart, watching the ceremony with deep, silent attention. Nicholas wore his usual fustian, save for a scrap of black cloth sewn around his hat—a mark of mourning he had never worn for his daughter, Bessy. But Mr. Hale saw nothing. He continued to repeat the funeral service mechanically in his mind, sighing twice or thrice as the clergyman finished, before placing a hand on Margaret's arm. He silently entreated to be led away, acting as if blind, trusting her as his faithful guide.

Dixon sobbed aloud, covering her face with a handkerchief, so lost in her own grief that she failed to notice the crowd dispersing until someone spoke to her nearby. It was Mr. Thornton. He had stood there the entire time, bowed head, hidden behind a cluster of mourners, unrecognized.

"I beg your pardon," he said, his voice steady. "Can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale?"

"Of course, sir," Dixon replied, wiping her eyes. "They are as one might expect. Master is terribly broke down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely."

Mr. Thornton wished, with a cruel selfishness, to hear that she suffered the natural sorrow of loss. First, there was enough pride in him to derive a twisted pleasure from the notion that his great love might come to comfort her—a sensation akin to a mother's joy when a drooping infant nestles close, dependent and safe. This delicious vision of what might have been, in which he had indulged only days ago despite her repulsions, was miserably shattered by the recollection of what he had seen near Outwood Station. "Miserably disturbed" was not strong enough. He was haunted by the image of the handsome young man, standing with her in an attitude of such familiar, easy confidence. The memory shot through him like an agony, forcing him to clench his hands tightly to subdue the pain.

At that late hour, so far from home! It took a monumental moral effort to revive his trust—once so perfect—in Margaret's purity and innocence. As soon as that effort ceased, the trust collapsed, falling dead and powerless. Wild, frantic fantasies chased one another through his mind. Here was a piece of miserable, gnawing confirmation. "She bore up better than likely." She must have some hope to look to, something so bright it could lighten the dark hours for a daughter newly bereaved. Yes! He knew how she would love. He had not loved her without gaining that instinctive knowledge of her capabilities. Her soul would walk in glorious sunlight if any man, worthy by his power to love, could win it back.
