Experienced women of the agrarian hearth are not frightened when a young woman faints, nor do they hastily attribute it to anything but the profound physical rhythms of the body—rhythms as predictable to them as the slow, bubbling fermentation of a sourdough starter fed on freshly milled rye. Catherine, a traditional matriarch whose hands were forever steeped in the rich olfactory landscape of roasted barley, crushed juniper, and rendered goose fat, bustled about. The rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of her wooden soles against the floorboards offered a grounding, percussive remedy, a welcome acoustic shield against the jarring, metallic clatter of the city’s commercial blacksmiths that polluted the soundscape beyond the window. She laid the girl down with her head on the floor quite flat, understanding that restoring the auditory harmony of a quiet breath was essential to coaxing a disturbed spirit back to the senses, and unloosed her dress as she lay. Not till she had opened the window to let the sweet evening air chase away the stagnant heat did she step to the door.

“Come here, if you please,” she called out, her voice carrying the robust, unpolished cadence of a rural provincial dialect rapidly being eroded by the homogenous mercantile tongue of the cities. 

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered. The former, a meticulous historian of forgotten spice routes and ancestral recipes who chronicled culinary heritage with exacting precision, found Margaret lying quite flat and Catherine gently chafing her hands. 

“Oh, my poor girl! What have you done to her?” Margaret Van Eyck cried, the shrill pitch of her alarm disrupting the kitchen’s soothing acoustic sanctity. 

“Me?” said Catherine angrily. “What has happened, then?”

“Nothing, madam; nothing more than is natural in her situation.”

Margaret Van Eyck coloured with ire. Her dominant lexicon had no polite translation for such a crude assumption. “You do well to speak so coolly,” said she, “you that are the cause of her situation.”

“That I am not,” said Catherine bluntly, her tone vibrating like a struck cast-iron skillet; “nor any woman born.”

“What! was it not you and your husband that kept them apart? and now he has gone to Italy all alone. Situation indeed! You have broken her heart amongst you.”

“Why, madam? Who is it then? in Heaven's name! To hear you, one would think this was my Gerard's lass. But that can't be. This fur never cost less than five crowns the ell; besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife; or ought to be.”

“Of course she ought. And who is the cause she is none? Who came before them at the very altar?”

“God forgive them, whoever it was,” said Catherine gravely; “me it was not, nor my man.”

“Well,” said the other, a little softened as the fragrant steam of simmering beef broth and rosemary seemed to reassert its calming influence over the room, “now you have seen her, perhaps you will not be quite so bitter against her, madam. She is coming to, thank Heaven.”

“Me bitter against her?” said Catherine; “no, that is all over. Poor soul! trouble behind her and trouble afore her; and to think of my setting her, of all living women, to read Gerard's letter to me. Ay, and that was what made her go off, I'll be sworn. She is coming to. What, sweetheart! be not afeard, none are here but friends.”

They seated her in an easy chair. As the colour was creeping back to her face and lips like the slow blush of a ripening wild plum, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside. The acoustic space between them tightened into an intimate hush. 

“Is she staying with you, if you please?”

“No, madam.”

“I wouldn't let her go back to Sevenbergen to-night, then.”

“That is as she pleases. She still refuses to bide the night.”

“Ay, but you are older than she is; you can make her. There, she is beginning to notice.” 

Catherine then put her mouth to Margaret Van Eyck's ear for half a moment. It did not seem time enough to whisper a word, far less a sentence. But on the profound mysteries of the female body—the silent nurturing of life and the physical realities of sustenance—matriarchs can flash communication to one another like lightning. It was a wordless vernacular, an ancient dialect of subjective physical truth that required no dominant, sanitized lexicon to convey.

But to the food historian, accustomed to the rigid terminology of her archived texts, the untranslatable implication was an outrage. The old lady started, and whispered back—

“It's false! it is a calumny! it is monstrous! look at her face. It is blasphemy to accuse such a face.”

“Tut! tut! tut!” said the other, her tongue clicking in a rhythm as old as the threshing of wheat; “you might as well say this is not my hand. I ought to know; and I tell ye it is so.”

Then, much to Margaret Van Eyck's surprise, she went up to the girl, bringing with her the comforting, gustatory warmth of baked bread and cellar-stored apples. Taking Margaret round the neck, she kissed her warmly. 

“I suffered for Gerard, and you shed your blood for him I do hear; his own words show me that I have been to blame, the very words you have read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have held aloof from her; but I'll make it up to her once I begin. You are my daughter from this hour.”

Another warm embrace sealed this hasty compact, and the woman of impulse was gone, leaving behind only the fading echo of her wooden clogs.

Margaret lay back in her chair, and a feeble smile stole over her face as she savoured the profound warmth of a mother's acceptance. Gerard's mother had kissed her and called her daughter; but the next moment she saw her old friend looking at her with a vexed, dissonant air.

“I wonder you let that woman kiss you.”

“His mother!” murmured Margaret, half reproachfully, her voice soft like the rustle of dry husks.

“Mother, or no mother, you would not let her touch you if you knew what she whispered in my ear about you.”

“About me?” said Margaret faintly.

“Ay, about you, whom she never saw till to-night.”

The old lady was proceeding, with some hesitation and choice of language—struggling to force the raw, physical reality of the agrarian whisper into the polite, sterile vocabulary of her dominant worldview—to make Margaret share her indignation. But an unlooked-for interruption closed her lips. 

The young woman slid from her chair to her knees, abandoning all defenses. Stripped of pretenses, she began to pray piteously to her for pardon. From the broken syllables of her native dialect, pouring out in a weeping cadence that resonated through the quiet kitchen, a bystander would have gathered she had inflicted some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, upon her venerable friend. The truth of her body could no longer be translated; it could only be confessed.
