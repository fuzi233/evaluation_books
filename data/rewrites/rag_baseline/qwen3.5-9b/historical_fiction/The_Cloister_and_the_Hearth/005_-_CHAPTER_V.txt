Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man of calculated artistry, a strategist who understood that the most effective trap is often disguised as an offer. He approached the novice with a bait that deliberately missed its mark, casting a line far wider than the hook he truly sought to set.

"The town records," he began, his voice smooth as polished oak, "are crabbedly written, the ink rusted by the slow corrosion of centuries." With a gesture that feigned benevolence, he extended the honor of transcription to Gerard. "You shall make them fair."

Gerard, ever pragmatic, inquired immediately of the wage. Ghysbrecht named a sum so meager it covered merely the cost of pens, ink, and parchment.

"But, Burgomaster," Gerard pressed, his tone sharpening, "what of my labor? Consider this a year's work."

"Your labor?" Ghysbrecht scoffed, a dismissive curl touching his lip. "Do you call the mere marking of parchment 'labor'? A little sweat goes to that, I trow."

"'Tis labor," Gerard countered, his stance firm despite the weight of the older man's presence, "and skilled labor to boot. In every craft, skill is valued higher than brute toil, regardless of the sweat shed. Furthermore, there is the matter of my time."

"Your time?" Ghysbrecht laughed softly, the sound devoid of warmth. "Why, what is time to you, at twenty-two?"

Fixing his eyes upon Gerard with a keen, predatory intensity designed to gauge the tremor in the boy's resolve, he delivered the blow. "Say, rather, you have grown idle. You are in love. Your body remains among these chanting monks, but your heart belongs to Peter Brandt and his red-haired girl."

"I know no Peter Brandt."

The denial only hardened Ghysbrecht's suspicion that the exorcist was playing a deeper, more dangerous game than he admitted.

"Ye lie!" Ghysbrecht roared, the mask of civility shattering. "Did I not find you at her elbow on the road to Rotterdam?"

"Aha!"

"And you were seen at Sevenbergen but the other day."

"Was I?"

"Aha! And at Peter's house."

"At Sevenbergen?"

"Aye, at Sevenbergen."

In the lexicon of modern intrigue, this maneuver constitutes a bold draw—a guess presented as undeniable fact, intended to force the subject's hand and reveal the truth through their reaction. The result of this psychological stratagem surpassed even the crafty Burgomaster's expectations.

Gerard sprang to his feet, seized by a strange, electric state of nervous excitation.

"Burgomaster," he said, his voice trembling not with fear, but with a fierce, defiant clarity, "I have not been to Sevenbergen these three years. I know not the names of those you claim to have seen me with, nor where they dwell. But since my time is precious—even if you choose to squander it—I bid you good day."

With a sudden, darting motion, he turned and fled, his eyes sparkling with a light that suggested not defeat, but revelation.

Ghysbrecht surged upward in a fit of huge, blinding ire, only to sink slowly back into his chair, the rage cooling into a colder, more calculating dread.

"He fears me not," he muttered, tracing the rim of his cup. "He knows something. If not everything, certainly enough to unsettle me."

Then, summoning his trusty servant with a hurried command, he almost dragged the man to the window.

"Do you see yon man?" Ghysbrecht cried, pointing a shaking finger toward the street below. "Haste! Follow him! But ensure he sees nothing of you. He is young, yes, but old in craft. Keep him in sight all day. Tell me whither he goes, and what he does."

Night had fallen when the servant returned, breathless and urgent.

"Well? Well?" Ghysbrecht demanded, leaning forward eagerly.

"Master," the servant replied, his voice low, "the young man went from here... straight to Sevenbergen."

Ghysbrecht let out a long, mournful groan that echoed in the silent room.

"To the house of Peter the Magician."
