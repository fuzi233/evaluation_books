The fastest route from Lady Verinder’s sitting room out to the gardens cuts straight through that familiar shrubbery path—Mr. Franklin Blake’s favourite walk, the spot we always found him loitering if he’d vanished from the rest of the grounds. Now, I’m an obstinate old man, I’ll own it: the more Sergeant Cuff clamped his thoughts shut tight against me, the harder I pried to peek inside his head. We’d barely turned onto the path before I tried another trick to draw him out.
“At this point,” I said, “if I was in your boots, I’d be completely at my wits’ end.”
“If you were in my boots,” the Sergeant corrected, not even pausing in his steady trudge, “you’d already have settled on your theory—and every last lingering doubt about those conclusions would be long gone. Don’t pester me for details just yet, Mr. Betteredge. I didn’t drag you out here to be badgered like a cornered badger. I brought you out for information. We could’ve talked inside, sure, but doors and curious listeners have a nasty habit of pairing up. In my line of work, we develop a very healthy taste for fresh open air.”
Who could outmanoeuvre a man like that? I surrendered, and waited as patiently as I could for whatever he’d say next.
“We won’t waste time unpacking Miss Verinder’s strange reasons for refusing to help this investigation,” he went on. “All that matters is that her refusal makes this whole job twice as hard as it needs to be. We’ve got to solve the mystery of that paint smear on the door—and take my word for it, solving that smear means solving the Moonstone mystery right along with it—another way. I’ve decided interviewing the servants, digging into their recent actions and moods, will serve me better than rummaging through all their wardrobes. Before I start, though, I’ve got a few questions. You’re a sharp observer, did you notice anything odd about any of the staff, allowing for all the natural panic and shock after the Diamond went missing? Any fights between them? Anyone far off their usual temperament—suddenly snappish, or suddenly taken ill out of nowhere?”
I’d just barely registered that he was describing Rosanna Spearman’s sudden, mysterious illness at dinner the night before, and was about to say so, when Sergeant Cuff’s head snapped sideways toward the bushes. He muttered a soft, under-his-breath “Hullo!”
“What’s wrong?” I asked.
“Twinge of rheumatism in my back,” he replied, loud enough that anyone hidden nearby would clearly hear. “We’re due for a change in the weather soon, mark my words.”
A few more steps brought us to the corner of the house. We turned sharp right onto the terrace, descended the central steps, and stepped out onto the open lawn, where we could see every inch of the surrounding grounds clear as day. Cuff stopped there.
“About that young maid, Rosanna Spearman,” he said. “She’s not what you’d call a pretty girl, so it’s not very likely she’s got a sweetheart hanging around. But for the girl’s own sake, I’ve got to ask you straight: has she set her heart on any boy around the place, like all the other servants do eventually?”
I stared at him, completely bewildered. What on earth made him ask that kind of question at a time like this?
“I saw Rosanna hiding in the shrubbery as we walked past,” he explained.
“When you said ‘Hullo’?”
“That’s right. If she was there lying in wait for a sweetheart, her hiding’s no harm done. But if there’s no sweetheart in the picture? With all the weird business going on in this house? That hiding is a deeply suspicious circumstance. It’d be my painful duty to act on it accordingly.”
I was trapped. I knew perfectly well this shrubbery was Mr. Franklin’s favourite walk. I knew he’d most likely head straight there when he got back from the station. I knew my daughter Penelope had caught Rosanna lingering around that path a dozen times, swearing up and down the quiet little maid was foolishly trying to catch Mr. Franklin’s eye. If Penelope was right, Rosanna had just been lying in wait for him when the Sergeant spotted her. I could either repeat Penelope’s silly sounding gossip and clear her name, or leave the poor unfortunate girl to face the full weight of Sergeant Cuff’s suspicion. Out of pure, unadulterated pity for her soul, I told him the whole truth: Rosanna Spearman had been mad enough to fall hopelessly in love with Mr. Franklin Blake.
Sergeant Cuff never laughed. The only sign he was amused, on the rare occasions something caught his fancy, was a tiny little curl at the corner of his mouth. He curled that corner now.
“Might as well call it what it is,” he said dryly. “Ugly, low-ranking servant girl pining for a gentleman. Falling for a man as charming and handsome as Mr. Franklin Blake isn’t the mad part of her behaviour, far from it. I’m glad we got that sorted out, anyway. It’s a relief to clear up a loose end. And I’ll keep your secret, Mr. Betteredge. I’m always tender to human foolishness—though I don’t get many chances to exercise that kind of charity in my line of work. You’re sure Mr. Franklin hasn’t the faintest clue the girl’s got a crush on him? Ah. He would’ve figured it out fast enough if she’d been pretty. Ugly women get a terrible deal in this life. Let’s hope they get rewarded for it in the next. Nice garden you’ve got here, perfectly kept lawn. Look how much better the roses look growing in grass instead of gravel. No, thank you, I won’t pick one. Hurting a flower that’s perfectly happy on its stem pains me. Just like it pains you to see something going very wrong in the servants’ hall, right? You didn’t notice anything you couldn’t explain in any of the servants the moment they first found out the Diamond was gone?”
Up until that second, I’d got on splendidly with Sergeant Cuff. But that sly, seamless way he slipped that question right back in made my hackles go up. I did not relish the thought of helping him sneak around prying into my fellow staff’s private business like a snake in the grass.
“I saw nothing,” I said, keeping my face as steady as I could. “We all lost our heads that night, me included.”
“Oh,” he said, his deadpan eyes pinning me right to the spot. “That’s all you’ve got to tell me, is it?”
“That’s all.”
“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, holding out his hand. “Would you oblige me by shaking hands? I’ve taken an extraordinary liking to you.”
I can’t for the life of me explain why he chose the exact second I was lying through my teeth to give me that mark of approval. I was downright proud of myself! I really thought I’d finally outwitted the famous Sergeant Cuff.
We headed back to the house. The Sergeant asked me to set aside a private room for him, then send all the indoor servants in to see him one by one, ordered by their rank, from highest to lowest. I showed him straight into my own quarters, then called the whole staff to assemble in the hall. Rosanna was there, looking as calm and ordinary as could be. I suspected she’d overheard him talking to me about interviewing the staff right before he spotted her hiding in the bushes, because she acted like she’d never set foot in that shrubbery in her whole life.
I sent them in one after another, just as he’d asked.
The cook went first, stayed barely five minutes, and came out reporting that Sergeant Cuff was a gloomy sort, but a perfect gentleman.
Lady Verinder’s own maid went next, stayed far longer, and stormed out huffing that if the Sergeant didn’t believe a respectable woman, he could at least keep his rude opinions to himself.
Penelope popped in after her, stayed only a moment, and whispered to me on her way out that the poor man must have been cruelly jilted in his youth, no other explanation for that permanent sour face.
The first housemaid went in after Penelope, stayed almost as long as Lady Verinder’s maid, and came out fuming that she hadn’t taken a job in this household to be openly doubted by some lowly police officer.
Rosanna Spearman went in next. She stayed longer than all the rest put together. When she walked back out, she didn’t say a single word. Her lips were pale as ash.
Samuel the footman followed her, stayed a minute or two, and snarked that whoever polished Sergeant Cuff’s boots ought to be ashamed of themselves for doing such a bad job.
Little Nancy the kitchen maid went last, stayed just a couple minutes, and came out grinning that Sergeant Cuff had a heart, unlike everyone else in the house, he never made cruel jokes at poor overworked girls’ expense.
When all the interviews were done, I walked into my room to see if he had any further orders for me, and found Sergeant Cuff at his old familiar trick: staring out the window, whistling *The Last Rose of Summer* under his breath.
“Any discoveries yet, sir?” I asked.
“If Rosanna Spearman asks permission to go out for a walk,” he said, “let the poor girl go. But come tell me first, before you say yes.”
I might as well have kept my mouth shut about Rosanna and Mr. Franklin! It was obvious the unfortunate girl was already deep under Sergeant Cuff’s suspicion, no matter how hard I’d tried to protect her.
“I hope you don’t believe Rosanna had any hand in stealing the Moonstone?” I ventured to say.
The corners of his sad, droopy mouth curled up again, and he stared me dead in the face the same way he had in the garden.
“I think I’d better not tell you, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “You might lose your head, you know, for the second time.”
Suddenly I wasn’t nearly so sure I’d outsmarted the great Sergeant Cuff after all. A knock at the door interrupted us. It was the cook’s messenger, come to say Rosanna had just asked to go out, complaining of a headache and saying she needed fresh air. I told her yes, following Cuff’s nod.
“Which way is the staff exit out of the house?” he asked once the messenger had left. I showed him. “Lock the door to your room,” he ordered. “And if anyone asks where I am, tell them I’m inside meditating to calm my mind.” He gave that tiny, secret curl of a smile again, and slipped out the door.
Left alone, my curiosity was eating me alive. It was obvious the Sergeant’s suspicions about Rosanna had come from something he’d heard during those interviews. The only two people besides Rosanna who’d stayed in that room longer than a few minutes were Lady Verinder’s maid and the first housemaid—the two spiteful women who’d made Rosanna’s life a misery ever since she arrived at the house. I strolled casually into the servants’ hall where the pair were sitting down to tea, and invited myself to join them. Everyone knows a good cup of tea to a woman’s tongue is what a drop of oil is to a sputtering lamp. It didn’t fail me. In half an hour, I knew everything Sergeant Cuff knew.
The two maids never for a second bought Rosanna’s story about being sick in her room all afternoon the day the Diamond vanished. They’d snuck up to her room at intervals, found her door locked, knocked and got no answer, pressed their ears to the wood and heard no sound. When she’d come down later for tea, still claiming she was ill, and gone back up to bed, they’d checked her door again. It was still locked. The keyhole was stuffed full of paper. They’d seen a light glinting under her door at midnight, and heard a fire crackling in her room at four o’clock in the morning—for heaven’s sake, in the middle of June, when no soul lights a bedroom fire! They’d spilled the whole story to Sergeant Cuff, who’d stared at them both with such sour, distrustful looks that they were absolutely convinced he didn’t believe a word of it. That’s why they’d all come out of the interviews so angry and offended, and why their tongues were loose enough to spill every last detail to me over teacups. The Sergeant had pretended to disbelieve them on purpose, of course, to keep their overinflated egos from running their mouths and tipping Rosanna off that they’d ratted on her.
I wandered out into the soft summer afternoon, miserable for the poor girl, and deeply uneasy about where this whole mess was heading. A little later, drifting toward the shrubbery, I ran straight into Mr. Franklin. He’d just got back from dropping Rachel off at the station, and had spent a long closed-door talk with Lady Verinder. She’d told him all about Rachel’s inexplicable refusal to let anyone search her wardrobe, and he was so low-spirited he could barely bring himself to talk about it. For the first time in all the months I’d known him, that brooding, stubborn family temper was written all over his face.
“Well, Betteredge,” he said, glum. “How do you like living surrounded by this whole atmosphere of mystery and suspicion? Remember the morning I first turned up here with the Moonstone? I wish to God we’d hurled it straight into the Frizinghall quicksand the second we set eyes on it.”
He didn’t speak again for a minute or two after that, until he’d calmed himself down. We walked side by side in silence for a little while, then he asked where Sergeant Cuff had got to. I couldn’t very well lie and tell him the Sergeant was locked in my room meditating. I told him exactly what had happened, every detail, including the two maids’ story about Rosanna’s locked room and the midnight fire.
Mr. Franklin’s sharp mind clicked straight to the answer in a blink.
“Didn’t you tell me this morning,” he said, “that one of the local tradesmen swore he’d met Rosanna on the path to Frizinghall that same day, when we all thought she was sick in bed?”
“Yes, sir.”
“If those two maids are telling the truth, then the tradesman definitely saw her. That illness of hers was just a ruse to sneak out of the house. She had a guilty reason to go to town secretly. The paint-stained nightgown the Sergeant is hunting for is hers. That fire they heard crackling at four a.m. was her burning it to get rid of the evidence. Rosanna Spearman stole the Moonstone. I’m going straight in to tell my aunt what we’ve worked out.”
“Not just yet, if you please, sir,” a sad quiet voice said right behind us.
We both spun around. Sergeant Cuff was standing there, looking as calm and unflappable as ever.
“Why not just yet?” Mr. Franklin demanded, his temper flaring instantly.
“Because if you tell Lady Verinder,” Cuff said evenly, “Lady Verinder will tell Miss Verinder. And that’s the last thing we can afford to have happen right now.”
Mr. Franklin stepped close to the Sergeant, face blazing with anger. “Suppose she does tell her. What then?”
“Do you think it’s wise, sir,” Cuff said, not moving an inch, “to ask me that kind of question at a time like this?”
There was a long, heavy silence between them. They stared each other straight in the eye. Mr. Franklin dropped his voice all at once, suddenly sharp and careful.
“I suppose you know, Mr. Cuff,” he said, “that you are treading on very delicate ground here?”
“Wouldn’t be the first time, not by many hundreds,” Cuff replied, as unshakable as a stone. “You need to understand this: if you tell Lady Verinder, or anyone else, what we’ve worked out before I give you permission, I throw up the case immediately and walk off the job.”
There was no arguing with that. Mr. Franklin had no choice but to submit. He turned on his heel, stormed off in a fury, and left the two of us standing there. I was trembling all over, completely confused, no idea who to suspect or what to believe next. But two things were perfectly clear to me: first, that Miss Rachel was somehow, in some unaccountable way, at the very root of that sharp, unspoken standoff between them, and second, that they’d understood each other perfectly without saying a single word of extra explanation out loud.
“Mr. Betteredge,” Cuff said, taking me by the arm, “you’ve done a very foolish thing while I was gone. You’ve been doing a little amateur detective work of your own. From now on, be so kind as to do your detective work right alongside me.”
I shook his arm off and stopped short. Thief or no thief, I pitied Rosanna Spearman, and I wasn’t about to help him set traps for her. “What do you want from me?” I asked.
“Just a little information about the local countryside,” he said. I couldn’t very well refuse to give him directions. “Is there a footpath from this house that leads straight down to the sea beach?” He pointed toward the fir plantation that ran straight to the Shivering Sand.
“Yes,” I said. “There’s a path.”
“Show it to me.”
Side by side, as the grey of the summer evening crept over the grounds, Sergeant Cuff and I set off toward the Shivering Sand.
