I descended slowly, quietly, into the endless dark. Each step was cautious, deliberate—every movement a battle against the crushing weight of night that pressed in on me like a living thing. This darkness was not mere absence of light; it was thick, suffocating, as if it had seeped into my bones and clouded my mind. I felt unmoored, adrift in a world without form or sense. At times, I walked with no awareness of ground beneath me, as though I were drifting into nothingness, stepping beyond the edge of reality.

But reason would snap me back. A sudden kick against an unseen rock. A stumble over a boulder hidden in shadow. These shocks reminded me—I was still on solid earth. I was real. The world was real. Yet the illusion clung to me like frost: that I might walk forever through this void, never touching land again.

And so I kept descending. That was my only guide—the slope itself, pulling me downward. But progress was agonizingly slow. In the utter blackness, I could barely manage a mile per hour, sometimes less. My frustration grew with each passing moment, each failed stride. I longed to move freely, to run, to leap—but here, even walking was an act of endurance.

Then came an idea. I would use the Diskos. Not often, not recklessly—but when I needed it. I spun the disc, its strange, pale glow cutting through the abyss for a few yards ahead. I studied the terrain by its faint radiance, memorizing contours, rocks, gaps. Then I extinguished it, letting the dark reclaim me, trusting memory to carry me forward. When doubt crept in, or my feet tripped once more, I’d summon the light again—just enough to reorient myself, then shut it away.

The glow seemed impossibly bright, not because it was strong, but because the surrounding dark was so absolute. It was like holding a candle in the heart of a tomb. And yet, I used it sparingly. I knew the power of the Diskos was finite. To waste it would be madness.

For six long hours, I repeated this ritual—step, pause, glimpse, remember, advance. I had not slept since the previous day, and exhaustion gnawed at me. By the twentieth hour since last rest, I sat upon the slope, numb and weary. I ate two tablets, mixed water, and drank slowly, tasting only the cold metal of the cup and the grit of dust on my lips. I wrapped my cloak around me, placed my pack and pouch beneath my head, and held the Diskos close. Sleep came swiftly, but not without dreams—fragments of Naani’s face, her voice echoing from some distant past.

I woke suddenly, jolted upright in the dark. Something had touched me—or nearly. I gripped the Diskos instinctively, listening. Silence. No sound. No breath. Nothing. After a while, I calmed. I sat up, reached for my supplies, and ate again, fumbling in the dark. When done, I gathered my gear and resumed my crawl.

All that day, I carried a quiet dread. My spirit whispered that something followed me—close, silent, always just behind. But my ears heard nothing. Only the wind, the shifting stones, the rhythm of my own breath. Still, I stopped often to listen. And every time, I moved forward again, deeper into the night.

Near the seventh hour, I misstepped. My foot caught in a hollow, and I fell hard against a jagged stone. Pain shot through my side. I lay still, stunned. The armor saved me from worse injury, but the fall left me shaken. When I regained strength, I made a decision: I would no longer walk. I would crawl.

On hands and knees, I could feel the ground beneath me. I could sense changes in elevation, avoid sudden drops. The Diskos became less necessary. I relied more on touch than sight. For the next eighteen hours, I crept forward, inch by inch. I ate three times, rested briefly, and continued. When I finally stopped, I searched the dark for a level spot, clearing small rocks until I found one suitable. I ate, drank, and prepared to sleep. But the unease lingered—this feeling of being watched. Twice during the night, I woke abruptly, gripping the Diskos, straining to hear. Nothing. Still, I did not sleep deeply. Even in slumber, I listened.

Six hours later, I woke again—startled, alert. Same sensation. Something near. But again, silence. No threat. No proof. Only fear.

The next day brought new danger. Near the eighth hour, I nearly plunged into a vast pit. My chest struck the edge, halting my fall. I pulled back, trembling, and crawled around the chasm in the dark. I emerged on the other side, shaken, more afraid than before. How many such traps lay ahead? Were they natural? Or made by something else?

That night, I devised a plan. I needed a line—a way to probe ahead. But I had no rope. Then I remembered: the straps of my pouch. One was long, thin, strong. I fastened a stone to one end, tied it securely, and began casting it forward as I crawled. Holding the other end, I could feel the tension—if the line slackened, there was a drop. If it snapped taut, there was a ledge. It was clumsy, slow—but it worked. For the first time since beginning my descent, I moved with greater confidence.

At the eighteenth hour, I slept. But I woke early—again, abruptly—as if stirred by a presence. The pattern repeated. I could not explain it. But no harm came. Still, I could not shake the belief that something was near—something unseen, unspoken, but ever-present.

Three days passed in this manner. I crawled down the slope, using the line, the Diskos only when needed. Six full days now I had spent descending into the eternal night. I had no idea where I was going—only that I must go further. The air had changed. It was warmer, heavier. It pressed on my chest, thick and dense. I realized then: the atmosphere was different here. Lower, richer. Unlike the thin, biting air of the Night Land above.

This led me to reflect on the people of the Pyramid. They had broad chests, stronger lungs. Was it because they lived in higher altitudes, where air was thinner? Or was it simply adaptation? I thought of the Upper Cities—those who breathed rarefied air all their lives. Their bodies had grown to match their environment. And perhaps, too, the Road-Makers of ancient times had flown—until the air grew too weak to sustain flight. Maybe they forgot how. Or feared falling into the deep below.

I wondered why they hadn’t flown down into the valley. Why build a road instead? Perhaps because they believed they would never rise again. Or because the lower world was filled with monsters—creatures unknown, alien, born from the chaos after the world split. The great rift had been cataclysmic—oceans rushing in, fire roaring, storms tearing the sky. It looked like the end. But it wasn’t. It was the beginning. From death came life. From destruction, renewal.

And so, too, had humanity rebuilt. First with roads, then with cities, then with airships. But eventually, even those were lost. Progress is not linear. It circles. We rise, we fall, we rise again.

Now, after six days, I stood at the edge of something new. The air was thick, rich. When I mixed my water, it fizzed violently, boiling over the cup, splashing onto my hand. I knew then: I had entered a deeper layer of the world. The pressure, the density—it was unlike anything above.

And then—light.

Far off, in the blackness, a flicker. Weak, uncertain. I froze. Could it be real? I stood up, staring. Yes—there it was. A glimmer, growing fainter, then stronger. Hope surged through me—then collapsed. Was it illusion? A trick of the mind? But then it returned, clear and undeniable.

I ran. Not carefully. Not cautiously. I charged down the slope, heart pounding. I stumbled, nearly broke my ankle, but held on. Teeth clenched, breath ragged, I forced myself forward.

When I stopped, I crawled again. Slowly. Carefully. The light grew brighter, but still distant. I walked for hours—six, then three more—before I came near. And still, it seemed far.

Then I paused. I stood, looking. And I heard it—a sound. A piping. Strange, mournful, rising from the depths. I dropped to my hands and knees, hiding among the rocks. The sound grew louder. Not mechanical. Not human. It was wild, elemental—like the earth itself singing.

And then I saw it.

I had reached the mouth of a massive gorge. To my left, a wall of stone stretched endlessly. To my right, a mountain rose into the night, its peak lost in darkness. Between them, the gorge opened wide.

And in the distance—fires. Faint, shimmering, scattered across the valley floor.

But what truly amazed me was the source of the light.

From the earth, a great blue flame erupted—towering, roaring, dancing wildly. It surged upward, a thousand feet high, then dipped low, swirling like a storm in reverse. It lit the canyon walls, revealed the jagged cliffs, cast shadows that danced like spirits.

It was not magic. Not supernatural. It was gas—natural gas—igniting in the fissures of the land. But still, it was breathtaking. A wonder. A miracle of nature, raw and untamed.

And I knew then: I had reached the bottom of the Mighty Slope.

Not the end. But the threshold.

Beyond lay the unknown.

And the fire burned on.
