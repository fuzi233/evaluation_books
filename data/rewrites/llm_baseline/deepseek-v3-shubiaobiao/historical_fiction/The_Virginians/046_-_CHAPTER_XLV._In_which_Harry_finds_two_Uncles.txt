The world is full of men who wear their virtues like a cloak, yet beneath the pious fabric hides a heart untouched by faith. We have read of such creatures in books—the sanctimonious preacher who thunders damnation while his own soul rots, the widow who weeps over scripture yet counts her coins with greedy fingers. But in truth, I have met few of these monstrous hypocrites in life. The common sinner is far simpler: the maid dreaming of her sweetheart during prayers, the merchant tallying debts as the psalm is sung, the schoolboy fidgeting through the sermon, his mind fixed on supper. None are saints, yet none pretend to be. Their religion is a quiet thing, kept like a remedy in a cupboard—brought out in illness, recommended to others, but seldom tasted in health.

Lady Warrington was such a dispenser of moral physic. Convinced of her own righteousness, she dosed her household with unrelenting virtue. Her daughters—Flora the Beauty and Dora the Genius—were paragons, polished by maternal exhortation until they shone. To Harry, fresh from the riot of London’s pleasures, their household was a haven of order. Sir Miles boomed with jollity, clapping him on the back; Lady Warrington beamed with maternal solicitude, pressing hymns into his hands. The girls, dutiful and bright, entertained him—Flora with her practiced smiles, Dora with earnest lectures on self-improvement.

Yet beneath this tranquil surface, Harry’s fortunes had crumbled. His luck at the tables had deserted him. Castlewood, his own kin, had stripped him clean. Even the trinkets set aside for poor Sampson had vanished into the pawnbroker’s clutches. Now bailiffs lurked in the shadows, and Harry, still playing the prosperous gentleman, sat in Hill Street, sipping watered wine while his aunt extolled his virtues.

*"We must reclaim you, dear Harry!"* Lady Warrington sighed, gazing heavenward. *"You have strayed, but love will guide you back."*

Love, perhaps—or necessity. For that very night, as Harry departed, full of cousinly kisses and paternal blessings, the law seized him. A grimy hand clapped his shoulder; a voice declared him prisoner. The scion of Virginia, who had dined with lords and charmed countesses, was hauled to a spunging-house in Cursitor Street.

There, in the stench of stale drink and the jeers of hardened debtors, Harry Warrington faced his ruin. No velvet, no lace, no adoring kin to shield him now. Only the dark, the rats, and the relentless tally of his folly.

Let him lie there, this prodigal. Let him gnash his teeth on the bitter bread of consequence. For youth must learn, and the school of shame has hard masters.
