Leaving Bretton was like stepping out of a dream. I had spent six months there, never imagining I would never return—never again walk its quiet streets or feel the comfort of its familiar embrace. Home called me back, and though one might assume I was eager to reunite with my family, I let the assumption stand unchallenged. It was easier that way.

For eight years, I might have appeared like a boat adrift on tranquil waters—lulled by calm seas, the helmsman drowsing under a benevolent sky. Many women live such placid lives; why not I? Picture me then: content, idle, warmed by unbroken sunshine. But the truth, as always, is more complicated. Storms find every vessel eventually.

I remember the cold. The fear. The long, unrelenting struggle. Even now, nightmares drag me beneath icy waves, salt burning my throat, pressure crushing my lungs. There had been a tempest—not fleeting, but a siege of days and nights without sun or stars. We had thrown our own hopes overboard. In the end, the ship was lost, the crew with it.

I did not complain. Who was left to hear me? Mrs. Bretton had long since vanished from my life, severed by circumstance. Time had not been kind to her either; fortune had dwindled, and Graham, I heard, had taken up a profession in London. There was no one to lean on. I had only myself.

So when Miss Marchmont—a reclusive, eccentric woman of means—sent for me, I went. Perhaps she had a task for me, some purpose to fill the emptiness.

Her house was grand, but she was a prisoner within it, crippled by rheumatism for twenty years. Confined to her rooms, she was a figure of stern solitude, her face lined with years of suffering. Her former companion was leaving to marry, and she thought I might take her place.

"It won’t be an easy life," she warned me plainly that evening by the fire. "I require much attention, and you’ll be confined. Still, compared to what you’ve endured, it may be tolerable."

I considered it. Tolerable? Perhaps. But the thought of spending my youth in those dim rooms, shackled to another’s pain, was a bleak prospect. My heart faltered—then steadied. I was no stranger to hardship.

"My doubt is whether I have the strength," I admitted.

She studied me. "You look worn thin," she said. And I did—my reflection showed a ghost in mourning. Yet I still felt life thrumming beneath the weariness.

"Have you other prospects?" she asked.

"Nothing certain. But I may yet find something."

"Then try," she said. "If fortune fails you, return. The offer stands for three months."

Her kindness surprised me. Before I could thank her properly, pain seized her. I tended to her as best I could, and in that moment, an understanding passed between us.

She was not an easy woman—sharp-tongued, exacting—but there was strength in her, a clarity of will that commanded respect. Even her reprimands lacked cruelty; they were the impatient scolds of a mother, not a mistress. And beneath her severity, reason always prevailed.

Days passed. I returned each morning, and with time, the idea of staying took root. Within a week, I had agreed. Her world became mine: two close rooms, the hum of the fire, the rhythm of her needs. I forgot the outside—the fields, the sky—because she filled the space with her presence. Her pain was my burden; her relief, my solace.

She was not soft, not amiable, but she was true. Her passions ran deep, her love—what remained of it—was steadfast. For that, I would have stayed forever.

But fate had other plans.

One February night, the wind howled like a grieving spirit. I sat sewing by the fire, unease prickling my skin. There was a voice in that storm—a sound I knew. Three times in my life, I had heard it, and each time, death followed.

Miss Marchmont slept as the tempest raged. Then, abruptly, silence fell. Frost glittered beyond the window. When I turned, she was awake, her eyes alight with an energy I had never seen.

"It’s a fine night," she said. "I feel young again."

She spoke of the past as if it lived before her—memories vivid, love undimmed. Frank, her lost betrothed, was with her again in spirit. She had loved him fiercely, and his death had left her adrift for thirty years.

"I never understood why he was taken," she murmured. "But tonight, I can say—*Thy will be done.* And I believe, at last, that death will reunite us."

She spoke of their last night together—the snow, the waiting, the terrible moment when his horse returned without him. She had held him as he died, his final words a whisper of paradise.

Her voice softened. "I’ve been selfish in my grief. But tomorrow, I’ll do better. Starting with you, Lucy. I’ll find a way to help you—after I’m gone."

I said nothing. Words failed me.

She smiled faintly. "Go to bed. It’s late."

I obeyed. By morning, she was gone—peacefully, as if she had slipped into a dream. Her restless spirit had finally found its rest.

And I was alone again.
