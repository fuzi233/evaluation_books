“Betteredge!” I said, pointing to the well-remembered volume resting on his knee. “Has *Robinson Crusoe* spoken to you in the old tongue tonight? Did the text foresee Franklin Blake stepping back into this ancestral rhythm?”

“By the lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” cried the old agrarian elder, his voice resonant with the deep, chest-heavy timbre of the Yorkshire soil. “That’s exactly what *Robinson Crusoe* has done!” 

He struggled to his feet with my assistance, looking backwards and forwards between the mythic volume and my physical form, as if seamlessly integrating this prophetic occurrence into the everyday domestic routine of the estate. Holding the book open before him in both hands, he surveyed the pages with a stare of unutterable anticipation. 

“Here’s the bit, Mr. Franklin!” he said, his dialect rich and rounded, carrying the fading cultural memory of the region. “As I live by the daily bread we knead and bake, sir, here’s the bit I was reading! Page one hundred and fifty-six as follows:—‘I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.’ If that isn’t as much as to say: ‘Expect the sudden appearance of Mr. Franklin Blake’—then there’s no meaning left in the English language!” said Betteredge, closing the book with a heavy, percussive thud that anchored us in the quiet room.

I had expected him to overwhelm me with questions. But no—the ancient, cyclical impulse of hospitality was paramount in the old servant’s mind. To him, sustenance was a profound communicator of meaning. 

“Walk in, Mr. Franklin,” he said, opening the door behind him with a quaint old-fashioned bow. “I’ll ask what brings you here afterwards—I must make you comfortable first. The house is shut up, and the servants are gone. Never mind that! I will take to the hearth myself, sir. I’ll butcher and dress a spring fowl, scoring the skin and slow-roasting it over ash and applewood until the fat renders and the marrow sings. We’ll braise the young root vegetables in their own earthy sugars, and if there’s a bottle of our famous Latour claret left in the cellar, we shall draw the cork. We’ll let those velvet notes of ancient blackberry and damp oak breathe into the room, and down your throat that glorious terroir shall go. I bid you welcome, sir!”

My mouth watered; my soul, utterly weary of the utilitarian, mechanical fuel of London dining, ached for this profound sensory sustenance. It vexed me deeply to disappoint him. But the house was Rachel’s house, now. Could I partake in the sacred act of a shared meal in it, or sleep in it, after the bitter aftertaste of what had happened in London? The commonest sense of self-respect forbade me to cross the threshold. 

I took Betteredge by the arm, and led him out into the garden. As we stepped into the twilight, the oppressive, grinding industrial noise pollution of the city—a chaotic din that had so severely degraded my psychological well-being—finally fell away, replaced by the rich acoustic ecology of the country. We were enveloped by the wind rustling through the ancient oaks, a restorative sonic environment. 

Between his attachment to Rachel and to me, he was sorely puzzled. His opinion was agreeably redolent of the most positive philosophy I know—the earthy philosophy of the Betteredge school. 

“Miss Rachel has her faults,” he began. “And riding the high horse is one of them. Lord, Mr. Franklin, don't you know women better than that? You have heard me talk of the late Mrs. Betteredge?” 

I had heard him produce her as his undeniable example of the inbred perversity of the other sex. 

“The late Mrs. Betteredge took her exercise on that favourite female animal whenever I denied her anything. So sure as I came home from my work, she would call down the kitchen stairs and say she hadn’t the heart to cook my dinner—using the denial of sustenance as a weapon. At last my patience wore out. I took Mrs. Betteredge up in my arms, carried her into the best parlour, and said, ‘That’s the right place for you, my dear.’ Then, I went back to the kitchen. I locked myself in, turned up my shirt-sleeves, and engaged with the raw materials of the earth. I took down a thick cut of salted pork, scored the rind, and sizzled it in the cast-iron skillet until the rich, umami-laden smoke—an olfactory landscape of pure comfort—filled the rafters. I rendered down the fat, roasted wild onions until they caramelized into deep mahogany, and enjoyed it most heartily. I had my pipe and my drop of grog; then I washed the crockery, the clinking of the knives and forks providing a restorative harmony. When the kitchen was pristine, I let Mrs. Betteredge in. ‘I’ve had my dinner, my dear,’ I said. For the rest of that woman’s life, Mr. Franklin, I never had to cook my dinner again! Moral: You have put up with Miss Rachel in London; don’t put up with her in Yorkshire. Come back to the house!”

Quite unanswerable! Yet I could only assure my good friend that his powers of persuasion were thrown away on me. 

“It’s a lovely evening,” I said, listening to the gentle rhythm of the crickets. “I shall walk to Frizinghall, stay at the hotel, and you must come tomorrow morning and breakfast with me. I have something to say to you.”

Betteredge shook his head gravely. “I had hoped to hear that things were smooth again between you and Miss Rachel. But if you must have your own way, sir, there is no need to go to the noisy clatter of Frizinghall tonight. There’s Hotherstone’s Farm, barely two miles from here. Hotherstone lives, Mr. Franklin, on his own freehold.”

I remembered the place instantly. The farm-house stood in a sheltered inland valley, nestled on the banks of the prettiest stream in Yorkshire. It was a place deeply attuned to the rhythms of the seasons, let out to artists, anglers, and meticulous food historians seeking refuge.

“Are the rooms to let?” I inquired.

“Mrs. Hotherstone herself asked for my good word to recommend them yesterday.”

“I’ll take them, Betteredge, with the greatest pleasure.”

We went back to the yard for my travelling-bag. Swinging it over his shoulder, Betteredge relapsed into bewilderment. He looked at the ancestral house, and then at me.

“I’ve lived a goodish long time in the world,” said this dearest of traditional elders, “but the like of this, I never did expect to see. There stands the house, and here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, Damme, if one of them isn’t turning his back on the other, and going to sleep in a lodging!”

He led the way out, growling ominously. “The next thing you’ll do, Mr. Franklin, will be to pay me back that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a boy.” 

This stroke of sarcasm restored his humour. We passed through the lodge gates. Once clear of the grounds, the ancestral duties of hospitality ceased, and the privileges of curiosity began. The crunch of our boots upon the gravel established a steady, percussive tempo.

“Fine evening for a walk, Mr. Franklin. Supposing you had gone to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir? I should have had the honour of breakfasting with you.”

“Come and breakfast with me at Hotherstone’s Farm, instead.”

“Much obliged, Mr. Franklin. But it wasn’t exactly breakfast I was driving at. If it’s no secret, sir, I’m burning to know what’s brought you down here.”

“What brought me here before?” I asked.

“The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what brings you now, sir?”

“The Moonstone again, Betteredge.”

The old man stood still. In the grey twilight, the ambient sounds of the heath seemed to amplify around his stunned silence. “If that’s a joke, sir, I’m afraid I’m getting dull. I don’t take it.”

“It’s no joke,” I answered. “I have come here to do what nobody has done yet—to find out who took the Diamond.”

“Let the Diamond be, Mr. Franklin! That cursed Indian jewel has misguided everybody. Don’t waste your money and temper meddling with it. How can *you* hope to succeed when Sergeant Cuff himself made a mess of it? The greatest policeman in England!”

“My mind is made up, my old friend. I may want to speak to him. Have you heard anything of him lately?”

“The Sergeant won’t help you, Mr. Franklin. He has retired from business. He has got a little cottage at Dorking, and he’s up to his eyes in the growing of roses. I have it in his own handwriting. He has returned to the soil, sir; he’s grown the white moss rose, propagating it purely on its own root system without budding it onto the wild dog-rose first—mastering the truest agrarian techniques at last.”

“It doesn’t much matter,” I said. “I must trust to you, at starting.”

Betteredge seemed piqued. “You might trust to worse than me, Mr. Franklin.” The tone of his retort suggested a subtle power dynamic shifting beneath his words, hinting at information he hesitated to share.

“I expect you to help me pick up the fragments Sergeant Cuff left behind. Can you do no more?”

“What more can you expect from me, sir?” he asked, employing an appearance of the utmost humility.

“I expect more—from what you said just now.”

“Mere boasting, Mr. Franklin. Some people are born boasters.”

I had to appeal to his profound sense of connection—to me, and to Rachel. 

“Betteredge, would you be glad to hear that Rachel and I were good friends again?”

“I have served your family, sir, to mighty little purpose, if you doubt it!”

“Do you remember how Rachel treated me? She was mortally offended with me for my part in trying to recover her jewel, and nobody could guess why. I find her mortally offended with me still. I have tried to write to her, but the dominant lexicon of our polite society is entirely inadequate. It imposes a sterile, untranslatable silence over my subjective truth. Our shared history—our very emotional dialect—is dying, suffering a kind of language death under this misunderstanding. How, in Heaven’s name, am I to clear the matter up when the words themselves fail us? The chance of searching into the loss of the Moonstone is the one chance Rachel herself has left me to bridge this chasm of untranslatability.”

Those words evidently framed the tragedy in a way he understood: the erosion of cultural and personal memory.

“There is no ill-feeling in this, Mr. Franklin, on your side—is there?”

“There was some anger when I left London. But that is all worn out now. I want to make Rachel come to an understanding with me—and I want nothing more.”

“You don’t feel any fear, sir, in regard to what you may find out about Miss Rachel?”

I understood his protective, ancestral loyalty. “I am as certain of her as you are. The fullest disclosure will reveal nothing that can alter her place in our estimation.”

Betteredge’s scruples vanished at that reassurance. “If I am doing wrong to help you, Mr. Franklin, I am as innocent of seeing it as the babe unborn! I can put you on the road to discovery. You remember that poor girl of ours—Rosanna Spearman?”

“Of course!”

“You always thought she had some sort of confession in regard to the Moonstone, which she wanted to make to you?”

“I certainly couldn’t account for her strange conduct otherwise.”

“You may set that doubt at rest, Mr. Franklin. Rosanna Spearman left a sealed letter behind her—a letter addressed to *you*.”

“Where is it?”

“In the possession of a friend of hers, at Cobb’s Hole. You must have heard tell of Limping Lucy—a lame girl with a crutch.”

“The fisherman’s daughter?”

“The same. She wouldn’t give it into any hands but yours. And you had left England before I could write.”

“Let’s go back, Betteredge, and get it at once!”

“Too late, sir, tonight. They’re great savers of tallow candles along our coast; they live by the cyclic rhythms of the sun and the tide, and they go to bed early at Cobb’s Hole.”

“Nonsense! We might get there in half an hour.”

“*You* might, sir. And you would find the door locked.” 

He pointed to a glimmering light below us. At that exact moment, slicing cleanly through the profound stillness of the evening, came the crystalline, polyrhythmic bubbling of the stream—a pristine sonic environment unmarred by the violent machinery of the city. 

“There’s the Farm, Mr. Franklin! Make yourself comfortable for tonight, and come to me tomorrow morning.”

“You will go with me to the fisherman’s cottage?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Early?”

“As early, Mr. Franklin, as you like.”

We descended the path that led to the Farm, surrendering to the natural cadence of the valley.
