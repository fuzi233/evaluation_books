The air in the drawing room at Tunbridge Wells was thick with the scent of stale lavender water and suppressed gossip. To the old ladies gathered there, particularly Madame de Bernstein, Harry Warrington was not a nobleman of promise but a cautionary tale, a young Englishman already rotting from the inside out. Madame de Bernstein, a creature of ancient Roman stoicism who believed her nephew should emulate the vices of Rome itself, found comfort in his infamy. Her vigilance over Lady Maria had slackened, replaced by a weary indifference. Dragons, she knew, grew soft with age; their scales dulled, their claws became clumsy props, and their venom lost its lethality, reducing to a mere annoyance. She watched them trail their languid tails back to their caves, unaware that the danger had simply shifted forms.

While the Baroness dismissed the threat, the atmosphere at Oakhurst remained tense. Rumors of Harry's dissipation traveled faster than the post coaches. When the little dancer returned to London, the narrative was fixed: she had been abandoned by a heartless lover. The whispers at the Wells were a cacophony of speculation, mixing women of fashion with those of dubious repute, all united in their pursuit of health or distraction. This collective delusion soothed the Baroness's conscience. She could now speak freely of finding Harry a suitable wife, a transactional arrangement to secure his future, dismissing his attachment to Maria as a childish phase.

Mr. Sampson, the chaplain, observed the scene with a mixture of amusement and calculation. He possessed the natural wit and animal spirits of a man who had navigated the treacherous waters of the world since his days as a servitor in college. He was a professional "toad-eater," a term that carried a weightier connotation in those days than today. Back then, a gentleman's toad-eater did not pretend to equality; he bowed low, called his patron "Sir" aloud, and swallowed his own dignity to advance the interests of the powerful. Sampson wore this role with pride, acting as Harry's aide-de-camp, managing his tradesmen, and collecting percentages for his introductions. Yet, beneath the obsequious exterior lay a genuine, if cynical, affection for the young Virginian. He admired Harry's blushing cheeks, his bright eyes, and his shyness—a stark contrast to the cynicism Sampson himself cultivated.

Their intimacy deepened quickly. Sampson, who claimed to follow the maxim *Video meliora, deteriora sequor* ("I see better things, but choose worse"), offered Harry a shield against the harsh realities of adult life. He spoke of his own rough past, of keeping a "decent tongue" for his boarding school sister, and positioned himself as a mentor who would guide Harry away from the "savages" of his homeland and the Methodists of his mother. He was eager to marry Harry off, perhaps to the widow Patcham or Mrs. Hooper, viewing these unions as strategic victories in his own career.

One afternoon, returning from the coffee-house, Sampson burst into the room with news that nearly ignited Harry's temper. "What a reputation, sir, they have made for you!" Sampson exclaimed, his voice rising. "Monsieur de Richelieu was nothing to you!"

Harry, confused by the reference to a French duke he had never met, asked what he meant. Sampson explained the local gossip: the widow Patcham's desperation, the Alderman fetching his wife away from the scandal, the entire town buzzing with accusations.

"Who says so?" Harry cried, his face flushing with indignation. "I should like to meet the man who dares say so, and confound the villain!"

"I should not like to show him to you," Sampson laughed, a dry, nervous sound. "It might be the worse for him."

"It's a shame," Harry continued, pacing the room, his hands clenched. "To speak with such levity about the character of ladies or gentlemen."

Sampson nodded gravely, though his eyes sparkled with satisfaction. "So I told them."

"It's a shame," Harry insisted, his voice trembling with righteous fury. "Do you know, in our country, a fellow's ears would not be safe. Before I left home, three brothers shot down a man for having spoken ill of their sister."

"Serve the villain right!" Sampson cheered.

"They have started this calumny here, Sampson," Harry said, his composure cracking. "About me and the poor little French dancing-girl."

"Wicked, wasn't it?"

"Abominable."

"They said the very same thing about my Lord March. Isn't it shameful?"

"Indeed it is," Sampson replied, maintaining a mask of solemn concern.

"If these stories reach my mother's ears..." Harry trailed off, imagining the devastation. "It would break her heart. Why, only a few days ago, Mr. Wolfe told me how horrible lies were circulating. What do they think a gentleman of my name and country can be? A seducer of women? They might as well say I was a horse-stealer or a housebreaker. I vow, if I hear any man say so, I'll have his ears!"

Sampson chuckled, shaking powder from his wig. "I have read, sir, that the Grand Seignior of Turkey receives bushels of ears sometimes. If you collected all those who had heard scandal against you, what basketsful you would fill!"

"And so I would," Harry declared, his voice ringing with a dangerous intensity. "Any fellow who speaks against a lady or a gentleman of honour!"

"If you go down to the Well, you'll find a harvest of them," Sampson mused, gesturing vaguely toward the window. "It was the high tide of Scandal. Detraction was at its peak. You may see the *nymphas discentes* and the *aures satyrorum acutas*."

"That may be as you say, Sampson," Harry replied, stopping his pace. He looked at the chaplain, his expression hardening into something final. "But if ever I hear any man speak against my character, I'll punish him. Mark that."
