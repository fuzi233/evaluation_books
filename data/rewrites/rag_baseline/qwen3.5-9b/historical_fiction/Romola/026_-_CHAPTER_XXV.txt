Outside the Duomo, while Baldassarre remained paralyzed by the psychic echo of Savonarola's voice, a third party had slipped through the rear archway. Piero di Cosimo stood in the periphery, his gaze fixed not on the pulpit but on the man who had just emerged—a former prisoner of the Frate's fervor. As the congregation succumbed to the aftershocks of the sermon, a collective, wordless catharsis washing over them, the newcomer moved forward. He placed a hand on Baldassarre's shoulder. The painter looked back, tears tracking slow paths through the dust on his cheeks, exhaling a ragged breath that signaled the end of his emotional collapse.

Piero lowered his voice. "Shall I sever your bonds? I've heard the account of your capture."

Baldassarre hesitated, his eyes narrowing with a defensive suspicion toward this intruder. Finally, he conceded. "If you wish."

"Let us move outside," Piero suggested.

Suspicion flickered again in Baldassarre's stare. Seizing the moment, Piero smiled, drew a blade, and sliced the ropes binding the man's wrists. He observed the prisoner's face, noting a peculiar intensity that hinted at madness rather than simple despair. *'If he causes trouble,'* Piero reasoned, *'he'll be re-caged quickly enough. Let him breathe for a moment.'*

"You fear me," Piero whispered, pressing closer. "You hesitate to share your story."

"As you prefer," Piero replied, shrugging. "Though consider this: Florentines are notoriously hospitable to travelers with torn coats and hollow bellies. There is a hospital for the destitute beyond the city gates. I could arrange passage. Your French guard has been dispatched; there is no threat."

Baldassarre nodded silently, accepting the lifeline, and the two men departed the church together.

"You would not wish to sit for a portrait?" Piero asked as they walked along the Via dell'Oriuolo toward the Gate of Santa Croce. "I am a painter. I would pay you handsomely."

The doubt returned to Baldassarre's gaze. "No."

"Ah!" Piero dismissed the request curtly. "Then proceed directly to the Porta Santa Croce. The hospital lies just outside. Do you refuse my further aid?"

"I thank you for your kindness thus far. I require nothing more."

"It is settled," Piero muttered, turning away.

*Mysterious old tiger,* the artist mused internally. *Truly a subject worthy of canvas. Ugly, carved deep by time, as if the plowshare and harrow had ravaged his very heart. A stark, brutal counterpoint to my own bland, smiling Messer Greco—the Baccus Triumphans who wedded the fair Antigone against all historical precedent. Ah! The scholar's blood curdles at the sight of that ancient claw.*

Upon returning to the Piazza del Duomo, the tide of humanity surged outward from every portal. The frantic pace of the dispersing crowd testified to the gravity of the sermon, which had stolen hours from their daily labors. Piero leaned against the sharp angle of the Baptistery, watching the exodus with an appreciative eye. He delighted in the kaleidoscope of attire and the piercing individuality of the faces—features reminiscent of Masaccio's work from half a century prior, or those of Ghirlandaio, who had not yet abandoned such vivid realism.

This morning marked a unique convergence. The assembly before Fra Girolamo represented the full spectrum of Florentine society and its fracturing politics. Among them were noblemen of high station, freshly emboldened as enemies of the Medici and champions of popular rule, devout Piagnoni who viewed San Marco as a modern-day sanctuary of Samuel. Figures like Francesco Valori stood out—men of commanding presence and fiery tempers, intoxicated by the immediate divine mandate to liberate Florence in their own image. Beside them stood figures like Soderini, possessing less of the zealous fanatic and more of the seasoned statesman.

Others included men of family, such as Piero Capponi, brave defenders of sober republicanism who preferred the sword to debate, rejecting any ideology that excluded the Medici or stifled public spirit. At their sides walked doctors of law, whose study of Accursius had not extinguished their spiritual fire; Messer Luca Corsini, for instance, a man destined soon to hurl stones in the streets for the cause of religion and the Frate.

Amidst the dignitaries draped in black luccio mantles or fur-lined cloaks, there were those with contemplative, sensitive visages: scholars bearing names like Strozzi and Acciajoli, already inclined toward the monastic habit; artists inflamed by Savonarola's teachings, including a young painter who had recently surpassed himself in a fresco of the Divine Child within the Frate's bare cell—unaware that he too would soon don the tonsure and become Fra Bartolommeo. There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, rushing perhaps to deliver news of the beloved Frate's imminent arrival to his friend Pico della Mirandola, a man who would never see another sunrise.

Well-born women appeared, clad in such rigorous simplicity that their refined grace served only to distinguish them from their lesser peers. Predominant among the ranks were the genuine popolani of the Major and Minor Arts, acutely aware of purses drained by war taxes. Most striking, however, was the long stream of poorer tradesmen and artisans. Their faith ranged from a crude, undifferentiated trust in the Frate as the champion of the poor against the oppressive rich, to an intense, sedentary absorption in biblical subtleties that illuminated the dim corners of their workshops with a pale, divine flame.

Scattered throughout this motley host were those who were neither disciples nor opponents. Some were Mediceans, displaying feigned deference born of fear and strategy. Others were sincere advocates of liberty who viewed Savonarola merely as an ambitious monk—a half-witted, half-fanatical tool wielded by the masses, a social fact to be endured. Even bitter enemies lingered: members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean faction seeking to reclaim control for specific families, or licentious youths who despised the Frate as Florence's ultimate killjoy.

The sermons in the Duomo had evolved into political spectacles, drawing the ears of curiosity and malice alongside faith. Intellectuals like young Niccolò Machiavelli attended to observe and report to friends in the countryside; gluttons like Dolfo Spini came to hunt the Frate as a public nuisance depleting game supplies, lying in wait for accusations.

Perhaps no preacher ever exerted such massive influence as Savonarola, yet none ever worked with such heterogeneous raw material. The secret of his power lay in the complexity of his message. Baldassarre, driven to ecstatic self-martyring vengeance, represented only an extreme edge of the audience's fragmented sympathies. Savonarola's preaching contained strains that appealed to the highest susceptibilities of human nature, balanced by elements that gratified low egoism, piqued gossiping curiosity, and fascinated timorous superstition. His need for personal dominance, his labyrinthine allegorical readings of Scripture, his enigmatic visions, and his false certainty regarding Divine intent were, in his own vast soul, continually elevated by fervid piety, a passionate sense of the infinite, active sympathy, and a clear-eyed demand for subordinating selfish interests to the general good—traits shared by the greatest minds of mankind.

Yet for the mass of his listeners, the potency of his words rested solely on his bold assertions of supernatural claim, his denunciatory visions, and the false certainty that transformed his sermons into urgent political bulletins. Having mastered the audience, it became essential to his nature—and ostensibly to their welfare—to maintain that mastery. The result was inevitable: no leader struggles to retain power over a mixed multitude without corruption; his standards inevitably descend to meet their lowest needs rather than rising to his own highest insights.

The complexities of human character were rarely displayed with greater efficacy than in Girolamo Savonarola. Yet, if one regards his life as a drama marked by profound internal shifts accompanying external changes, a reverence emerges that requires no closing of the eyes to fact. Up to this juncture, when his direct intervention in political affairs had only just commenced, his imperious need for ascendancy likely burned undiscernibly within the fierce flame of his zeal for God and man.

It was customary in antiquity, when an ox was led to sacrifice for Jupiter, to chalk over its dark spots, presenting a false facade of unblemished whiteness. Let us discard the chalk. Boldly state it: the victim is spotted, yet it remains no less valid that his mighty heart is laid upon the altar of men's highest hopes.
