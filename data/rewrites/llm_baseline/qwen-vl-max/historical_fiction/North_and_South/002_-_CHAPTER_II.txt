“By the soft green light in the woody glade,
On the banks of moss where thy childhood played,
By the household tree, thro’ which thine eye
First looked in love to the summer sky.”

Margaret Hale sat quietly beside her father in the railway carriage, dressed simply for the journey home. The wedding at Harley Street had ended, and now they were returning to Helstone parsonage, where peace and familiar routines awaited them. Her mother had stayed behind, citing a host of vague excuses—none of which anyone truly understood, except Mr. Hale himself, who knew full well that his wife had refused to wear the grey satin gown he’d suggested, halfway between new and old, because she lacked the means for a fresh wardrobe. And so, with no new dress to wear on such a special occasion, Mrs. Hale had chosen not to attend at all.

If Mrs. Shaw had known the real reason, she might have sent gowns by the dozen. But it had been nearly twenty years since Mrs. Shaw was the pretty Miss Beresford, and time had softened her memory of past hardships. Now, her only lingering complaint was the sorrow of marrying someone older—a grievance she could talk about for hours. She remembered how her sister Maria had married the man of her dreams, just eight years her senior, with a gentle nature and those rare, dark-blue eyes. Mr. Hale, too, was one of the most moving preachers she’d ever heard, a model of pastoral devotion. So when she reflected upon her sister’s life, she concluded: “Married for love—what more could dearest Maria possibly desire?”

Mrs. Hale, if she spoke honestly, might have replied: “A silver-grey glacé silk, a white chip bonnet, dozens of things for the wedding, hundreds for the house.” But Margaret didn’t know the truth behind her mother’s absence—only that she hadn’t come, and she wasn’t sorry. It was better to greet her parents in the quiet comfort of their home than amid the chaos of London, where she’d spent the last two days playing hostess, running from room to room, managing guests and schedules, until her body ached and her mind felt strained.

Now, as the train rolled through the countryside, she let go of the past. The farewells had been swift, the goodbyes bittersweet. The people she’d lived with for so long—friends, servants, neighbours—were slipping away, and she felt a deep ache for what was lost. Not because those times had been perfect, but because they were gone forever. Yet, as the night drew near, she pulled herself back from melancholy, focusing instead on the future—on the warmth of home, the green woods, the quiet joy of being free again.

She watched her father, asleep in the corner seat. His once-dark hair was now threaded with grey, thinning over his brow. The bones of his face stood out clearly—sharp, almost severe, yet softened by the grace of fine features. His expression was still, but not at rest. There was a weariness beneath the calm, an undercurrent of worry that settled into the lines around his mouth and eyes. Margaret sighed softly. *Poor Frederick,* she thought. *If only he had become a clergyman instead of joining the navy…* She wished she knew the truth of what had happened—the story Aunt Shaw never told, only hinted at. Why couldn’t Frederick come home? What terrible affair had severed him from them?

Her heart tightened at the sight of her father’s quiet suffering. She wanted nothing more than to be there for him, to ease his burden, to bring light into their home. When he stirred, she smiled brightly—no trace of fatigue in her eyes. He returned her smile, but faintly, as though it cost him effort. Then, slowly, his face slipped back into its usual look of concern. He had a habit of half-opening his mouth as if to speak, then stopping—leaving his lips uncertain, his thoughts unspoken.

But his eyes—large, soft, slow-moving—were hers. They held depth and dignity, framed by delicate, translucent lids. Margaret bore more resemblance to her father than to her mother. Some said she wasn’t beautiful—too plain, too serious—but others saw something stronger: a presence that commanded attention without pretence. Her mouth was wide, not delicate like a rosebud, but full of life, curved in rich red lips. Her skin, though not pale, had a smooth, ivory-like quality. When she spoke to her father, her face lit up—dimples appearing, laughter sparkling in her eyes. She was young, yes, but already carried within her a quiet strength, a hopeful spirit.

It was late July when she arrived home. The forest stood thick and dark, the ferns glowing under slanting sunlight. The air was still, heavy with heat. Margaret used to walk beside her father, crushing the ferns beneath her feet with a kind of joyful cruelty, inhaling their earthy scent as they released it. She loved these walks—out across the broad commons, where wild creatures roamed freely, basking in the sun, feasting on flowers and herbs. This life—these walks—were everything she’d dreamed of. The forest was hers. Its people were hers. She made friends with them, learned their ways, spoke their language. She visited cottages tucked in the shade, nursed sick children, read aloud to the elderly, brought food to the ill. She even planned to teach at the village school—though her father often found himself distracted, drawn away from his duties to check on some friend or family in need.

Outside, her life was perfect. Inside, however, shadows lingered.

She felt a childlike shame when she realized how sharply she noticed the cracks in their domestic peace. Her mother—so tender, so loving—was often discontented. She complained that the bishop neglected his duties, failing to give Mr. Hale a better living. She gently reproached her husband for not seeking advancement, for not speaking up for himself. And every time he sighed and said, “If I can do what I ought here, I am thankful,” she pressed harder.

Each plea seemed to deepen his silence. Margaret tried to mediate, to remind her mother of the beauty of Helstone, of the simplicity that could be cherished. But her mother insisted the place was unhealthy—too many trees, too much damp. Margaret would coax her out onto the sun-dappled common, hoping the open air would lift her spirits. It helped for a while. But as autumn approached, the weather turned unpredictable, and her mother grew more restless. She resented the fact that her husband, more learned than Mr. Hume, more devoted than Mr. Houldsworth, had not received the recognition they both deserved.

This constant discontent—this quiet erosion of peace—was what Margaret hadn’t expected. She’d imagined giving up luxuries as a form of freedom, not as a source of sorrow. In London, she’d enjoyed comforts, but also felt constrained by them. Here, she could breathe, walk, think—yet the weight of her parents’ unhappiness pressed down on her.

There had been murmurs before—small complaints, fleeting regrets—but in the glow of happy memories, she’d forgotten them. Now, they returned, louder and clearer.

By mid-September, the rains came. Margaret was forced to stay indoors more often. Helstone was isolated, far from any refined society. “It is undoubtedly one of the most out-of-the-way places in England,” her mother said, sighing. “Papa has no one to associate with. He’s wasted here, seeing only farmers and labourers week after week.”

“If we lived on the other side of the parish,” Margaret offered, “we’d be near the Stansfields. The Gormons would be within walking distance.”

“The Gormons?” her mother asked, raising an eyebrow. “Those merchants from Southampton?”

“Oh, I’m glad we don’t visit them,” Margaret said firmly. “I don’t care for shopkeepers. I’d rather know only honest workers, people without pretension.”

“You mustn’t be so fastidious, dear,” her mother replied, thinking of a handsome young Mr. Gormon she’d once met.

“I like people who work the land,” Margaret insisted. “Soldiers, sailors, scholars—I admire them. But I don’t want you to think I should praise butchers and candlestick-makers.”

“But the Gormons are coach-builders,” her mother pointed out.

“Still a trade,” Margaret said. “And a far less useful one than baking bread or slaughtering meat. Oh, I used to hate riding in Aunt Shaw’s carriage! I longed to walk.”

And walk she did—despite the rain. She moved through the wind and mist as if carried by the breeze itself, light as a leaf swept along by autumn’s breath.

But evenings were harder. After tea, her father retreated to his small library. Margaret and her mother were left alone. Mrs. Hale never read much—she’d discouraged her husband early in their marriage from reading aloud while she worked. They’d tried backgammon once, but as Mr. Hale became more involved in his parish, his duties began to clash with her expectations. She saw interruptions as burdens, not as part of his calling. So he withdrew, finding solace in philosophy and metaphysics, books that few others would touch.

When Margaret had come home before, she’d brought a trunk full of books—recommended by teachers, assigned by governesses—and spent her summer racing through them. Now, the drawing-room shelves held only a few well-bound English classics: Thomson’s *Seasons*, Hayley’s *Cowper*, Middleton’s *Cicero*. These were the lightest, newest, most enjoyable among them. But they weren’t enough.

So Margaret told her mother about London—every detail, every person, every moment. Her mother listened, sometimes amused, sometimes wistful, comparing her sister’s comfortable life to their own modest circumstances. On such nights, Margaret would fall silent, listening to the steady *drip-drip* of rain on the lead roof. Once or twice, she found herself counting the drops, wondering if she dared ask—just once—about Frederick.

Where was he now? What was he doing? How long had it been since they’d heard from him?

But she hesitated. She knew the mutiny—the incident that had taken Frederick away—had marked a turning point. Her mother’s health issues, her dislike of Helstone, all began then. And the full story had never been told. It seemed buried, like a secret too painful to unearth.

When she was with her mother, she thought her father might answer. When she was with him, she believed she could approach her mother. But each time, she stopped short. Perhaps there was nothing new to hear. In a letter from before she left London, her father had written that they’d heard from Frederick—he was still in Rio, healthy, sending his love. But that was cold comfort. It wasn’t the living news she craved.

Frederick was always spoken of as “Poor Frederick.” His room remained untouched—cleaned weekly by Dixon, the maid, who treated it with reverence. Dixon had once served Lady Beresford, and she still carried the memory of the young Miss Beresfords, the belles of Rutlandshire. To her, Mr. Hale was the ruin of her mistress’s fate. If Miss Beresford hadn’t rushed into marriage with a poor country priest, who knew what she might have become? Yet Dixon stayed, loyal, protective—even if she saw herself as the fairy battling the wicked giant, Mr. Hale.

Master Frederick had been her favourite. Every week, she entered his room with quiet care, arranging it as if he might return that very evening.

Margaret suspected there was news—something her mother didn’t know—that weighed heavily on her father. But Mrs. Hale seemed unaware of any change. Her husband’s spirits were gentle, easily touched by sorrow—depressed after a deathbed, shaken by a crime. But now, Margaret noticed a new absence—an emptiness, as if his mind were occupied by something beyond daily tasks.

He didn’t go out among the villagers as much. He stayed in his study, waiting anxiously for the postman—whose arrival was signaled by a knock on the kitchen shutter. Once, this call was ignored, forgotten. Now, Mr. Hale lingered in the garden or stood by the window, watching the postman pass, nodding half-respectfully, half-confidentially, before retreating inside with a heavy heart.

Yet Margaret was young. And youth, when faced with uncertainty, often finds escape in sunshine. When October brought fourteen brilliant, clear days, her worries lifted like thistledown. She thought only of the forest—the golden leaves, the crisp air, the open glades now accessible after the rain.

The fern harvest was over, but the woods still held wonders. She had studied drawing with Edith, and during the rainy weeks, she’d regretted not capturing the beauty of the woods while she could. Now, determined to make up for lost time, she prepared her sketchboard one morning.

Just then, Sarah, the housemaid, flung open the drawing-room door and announced, “Mr. Henry Lennox.”
