Three days passed without a single word from Willoughby, leaving Elinor certain that her decision to seek her mother's counsel had been wise. It was near the end of this quiet interval that they were invited to accompany Lady Middleton to a gathering, an event from which Mrs. Jennings was absent due to the illness of her youngest child. For Marianne, who had fallen into a deep despondency, the prospect offered no joy. She dressed with a careless indifference, neither hoping for nor fearing the night ahead, and upon returning to the drawing room after tea, she sat listlessly by the fire. She remained frozen in her posture until the announcement of Lady Middleton's arrival, starting only when it became necessary to leave her seat, as if suddenly remembering that guests were expected.

Upon reaching the venue, they navigated through a line of carriages, ascended the stairs, and stepped into a room aglow with lights, crowded with people, and stiflingly warm. After offering their customary courtesies to the hostess, they mingled in the throng, sharing the discomfort and heat that their presence inevitably added to the atmosphere. For some time, they said little and accomplished nothing, until Lady Middleton seated herself at Cassino. Since Marianne lacked the spirit to move about, Elinor arranged for them both to sit close to the table, finding relief in the proximity.

It was not long before Elinor spotted Willoughby standing a few yards away, engaged in earnest conversation with a highly fashionable young woman. He soon noticed her gaze, bowing politely, yet he made no attempt to speak to either her or Marianne, despite clearly seeing them, and continued his discourse with the same lady. Turning involuntarily to Marianne, Elinor checked to see if her sister had noticed his coldness. In that same instant, Marianne perceived him. A flush of sudden, overwhelming delight spread across her face; she would have rushed toward him immediately had not her sister seized her arm.

"Good heavens!" Marianne cried out, her voice trembling. "There he is! There he is! Why does he not look at me? Why cannot I speak to him?"

"Elinor whispered urgently, "Please compose yourself. Do not let everyone here see what you feel. Perhaps he has not yet seen you."

But this reassurance was futile; Marianne could not believe it, nor did she possess the strength to remain calm. She sat in an agony of impatience that twisted every feature of her face. Finally, Willoughby turned back and looked at them both. Marianne sprang to her feet, calling his name with an air of affection, and extended her hand. He approached, directing most of his attention to Elinor as if wishing to avoid her gaze, and deliberately ignoring her gesture. With hurried politeness, he inquired after Mrs. Dashwood and asked how long they had been in town.

The directness of his address stripped Elinor of her composure, leaving her speechless. Yet, Marianne's emotions spilled forth instantly. Her cheeks burned crimson, and she exclaimed with profound distress, "Good God, Willoughby! What is the meaning of this? Have you not received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?"

Forced by her urgency, he took her hand, but the contact seemed painful to him, and he released it almost immediately. Throughout the exchange, he appeared to struggle desperately to maintain control. Elinor watched his countenance closely, noting a gradual shift toward tranquility. After a brief pause, he spoke calmly.

"I called at Berkeley Street last Tuesday," he said, "but I deeply regretted not finding you or Mrs. Jennings at home. I trust I have not lost my card."

"But have you not received my notes?" Marianne shrieked, consumed by wild anxiety. "There must be some terrible mistake. What could possibly explain this? Tell me, Willoughby—for heaven's sake, tell me what is wrong!"

He offered no reply. His complexion drained, and his embarrassment returned in full force. However, upon catching the eye of the fashionable young woman with whom he had been conversing, he felt compelled to summon instant resolve. Recovering himself, he remarked, "Yes, I had the pleasure of receiving notice of your arrival in town, which you were so kind as to send," gave a slight bow, and hastened away to join his friend.

Marianne, now looking ghastly pale and unable to stand, sank back into her chair. Elinor, fearing she might faint, shielded her from the eyes of others and revived her with lavender water. As soon as Marianne could speak, she cried, "Go to him, Elinor! Force him to come to me. I must see him again—I must speak to him instantly. I cannot rest; I shall know no peace until this is explained. Some dreadful misunderstanding must exist. Oh, go to him at once!"

"How can that be done?" Elinor pleaded gently. "No, my dear Marianne, you must wait. This is not the proper place for explanations. Wait until tomorrow."

Yet, it required immense effort to keep Marianne from pursuing him herself. Persuading her to curb her agitation, to wait with the appearance of composure so that she might confront him later with greater privacy and effect, proved impossible. Marianne continued to surrender repeatedly to the misery of her feelings, muttering exclamations of wretchedness. Shortly thereafter, Elinor saw Willoughby exit the room via the staircase. Telling Marianne that he had departed, she used this fact to urge her further toward calmness, arguing that it was impossible to speak to him again that evening. Instantly, Marianne beseeched her sister to beg Lady Middleton to take them home, declaring she was too miserable to endure another minute.

Though Lady Middleton was in the midst of a game, she was too polite to object for a moment to Marianne's request. Handing over her cards to a friend, the sisters departed as soon as a carriage could be secured. The journey back to Berkeley Street was marked by silence. Marianne sat in a silent agony, so overwhelmed by grief that she could scarcely weep. Fortunately, Mrs. Jennings had not yet returned, allowing them to proceed straight to their room, where hartshorn eventually helped restore Marianne to some semblance of herself. Quickly undressed and tucked into bed, she expressed a desire to be left alone, prompting Elinor to depart, leaving ample time to reflect on the day's events while waiting for her mother.

Elinor knew beyond doubt that some form of engagement had once existed between Willoughby and Marianne, and that Willoughby had grown weary of it. Regardless of how Marianne might cling to her hopes, Elinor could not attribute such behavior to mere mistake or misunderstanding. Only a complete transformation of sentiment could account for it. Her indignation might have been even fiercer had she not witnessed the evident embarrassment that betrayed a consciousness of his own misconduct; this prevented her from believing he was capable of playing with her sister's affections from the start without a design worthy of scrutiny. While absence might have weakened his regard, and convenience might have driven him to suppress it, she could not bring herself to doubt that such a regard had truly existed.

As for Marianne, reflecting on the pain of such an unhappy meeting, and the far worse consequences that lay ahead, filled Elinor with the deepest concern. Her own situation seemed comparatively secure; while she might remain divided from Edward in the future, her mind could always find support in her esteem for him. But every circumstance served to intensify Marianne's misery, sealing her fate in a final, irreconcilable separation from Willoughby.
