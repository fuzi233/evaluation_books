An hour after Henleigh Grandcourt had ridden away from Offendene, the news of Gwendolen’s engagement had traveled all the way to the rectory, and by sunset Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne and quiet Anna had walked over to crowd the little drawing room, their faces bright with the kind of eager celebration that clings to a favorite cousin’s triumph.

“My dear girl, let me be the first to congratulate you on having inspired such a deep, steady attachment,” the rector said, patting her shoulder gently. “You look serious, and no wonder: a life-long union is no trivial promise. But from all Mr. Grandcourt has said and done, we can already see that our recent hard luck has turned to good. It gave him the chance to show you how delicate, how generously he means to care for all the people you love.”

He was referring, of course, to Grandcourt’s quiet assurance that he would make ample provision for Mrs. Davilow—every word of which Gwendolen had repeated perfectly to her mother earlier that day.

“I’ve no doubt he would have offered just as handsomely even if you’d never run off to Germany,” Mrs. Gascoigne put in, her tone firm with the weight of well-meaning auntly duty. “You could have been engaged to him more than a month ago, after all. But there’s no room for silly caprice now, and I’m sure you’ve no taste for it. A woman owes a great debt of gratitude to a man who persists in laying a fortune and his whole heart at her feet. I’m certain you feel exactly how much this means.”

“I’m not at all sure I do, aunt,” Gwendolen replied, her tone light with saucy gravity. “No one ever sat me down to list all the things a proper engaged lady is supposed to feel.”

The rector laughed, and patted her shoulder again, writing it off as harmless, endearing naughtiness. Anna leaned in to kiss her, her eyes already glistening, and whispered “I hope you’ll be so, so happy” before she slipped back to the edge of the group, pressing a handkerchief to her eyes to hide the tears. For weeks she’d been nurturing a soft, quiet little romance of her own: if Rex’s heart still ached for Gwendolen, surely his old crush might soften her cousin enough that in time, when their fortunes improved, the two of them might marry. Now all that daydream was dust, and she was fighting to find the grace to rejoice for Gwendolen instead of grieving for her brother.

Even Miss Merry and the four little girls—Alice with the high, proud shoulders, Bertha and Fanny who always whispered in corners, quiet little Isabel who listened to every word—were clustered around the fringes of the room, all of them agreeing that real life was turning out far more thrilling than the pages of *Sir Charles Grandison*. The rest of the evening passed in the rector’s calm, decisive remarks, as the two elder ladies speculated over what their new life would look like. The rector insisted there was no need to fuss over legal settlements yet: everything would be left safely, generously, in Grandcourt’s hands.

“I do wish I knew exactly what Ryelands and Gadsmere are like,” Mrs. Davilow said, her voice soft with wonder.

“Gadsmere’s just a secondary, smaller estate,” Mr. Gascoigne told her. “But Ryelands? It’s one of the finest country seats in the whole county. Thousands of acres of parkland, woods so valuable they’re worth a fortune all on their own. The house was built by Inigo Jones, ceilings painted in the Italian style, just like the royal palaces. They say the estate brings in twelve thousand a year, and the Grandcourts have the gift of two church livings, one a rectory, to give away. There might be small old debts on the land, but Grandcourt was an only child—there’s no one else to claim a share.”

“Imagine if he inherits the Lord Stannery peerage on top of all that!” Mrs. Gascoigne gushed, ticking the advantages off on her fingers. “The Grandcourt estate, the Mallinger estate, the baronetcy, and a seat in the House of Lords—only they say the peerage doesn’t come with extra land.” She frowned, finding no fifth finger left to mark another grand prize, and sighed at the small disappointment.

“The peerage is still a long shot,” the rector said judiciously. “There are two cousins between the current lord and Grandcourt, after all. It’s quite remarkable how chance deaths can pile whole fortunes onto one man’s shoulders. But a man with that much wealth and station, with the name Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger, has a gift more than enough for any man to manage well. Let’s hope he uses it kindly.”

“And what a position for you, Gwendolen!” Mrs. Gascoigne turned to her, eyes sparkling. “It’s a great honor, and a great responsibility. Henry, you must write to Mrs. Mompert at once—your excuse of an engagement will save you from the governess post you were dreading. She’s a very proud, high-tempered woman, and we don’t want her offended.”

Gwendolen almost smiled. The name Mompert had come to feel like some bogeyman’s name to her, and now that horror was gone forever. She barely spoke the rest of the evening, and that night she could not sleep a wink in her little white bed. It was almost unheard of for her, strong, bright, unshakable as she’d always been, to lie wide awake at midnight. She took extra care not to wake her mother, not to let anyone see her restlessness: she who had always been so sure of herself, so ready to twist everyone around her finger, had just taken a step she’d once sworn she would never, ever take. There was no turning back now. She loved almost everything she could see stretching out before her: the fine houses, the horses, the freedom from shabby genteel poverty, the thrill of being the lady of a great estate. But the shadow of that old, firm, gut-deep refusal of Grandcourt hung over her still.

Staring at the pitch black around her curtains, the kind of darkness the blind can “see” all too clearly, she felt a cold terror creep over her. She was about to do the very thing she’d run away from in Leubronn, the thing she’d recoiled from with all her might. For the first time in her life, a question of right and wrong was spurring real fear in her: before, every small misstep she’d made had been fixed with a soft apology or a pretty new present. Now a new, sharp consciousness was waking in her chest. She was on the edge of making that bitter, reckless line she’d spat out in her despair months ago her whole life’s rule: nothing matters, just amuse yourself as best you can. That lawlessness, that throwing away of every standard of right, terrified her. Behind it loomed all the vague, awful calamities she’d once thought were only words, all the half-remembered lines from old sermons she’d never paid attention to, wrapping her in a vague, heavy sense that some avenging power was watching her. The brilliant future she’d dreamed of, the freedom she was sure she would carve out for herself in marriage, the escape from the dull smallness of her girlhood—all of it was right there, within reach, but it felt like food that had been touched by sacrilege, something she’d have to snatch at while her hands shook with fear.

In the dark quiet, her usual boldness couldn’t fight off that first wave of dread. The face of that unhappy woman Mrs. Glasher, and her little children, kept replaying behind her eyes, clinging to her memory like a spot of disgrace, until every other thought faded away, leaving only the knowledge that she was letting those messy, ugly secrets become part of her own life. Her long wakefulness felt like a half-delirium, until a thin, pale streak of light seeped past the curtains, and the room grew chill. She could not bear it another second.

“Mamma!” she called, her voice small.

“Yes, my love,” Mrs. Davilow answered immediately, already awake.

“Let me come to you.”

She stumbled across the hall, and was asleep in minutes, curled up on her mother’s shoulder, and slept late until she dreamed she was waltzing in a glittering ballroom, and opened her eyes to see her mother standing by the bed, holding a small wrapped package.

“I’m sorry to wake you, darling, but I thought you should have this at once. Grandcourt’s groom brought Criterion—he rode a second horse, and says he’s to stay here for you.”

Gwendolen sat up, tearing open the paper. Inside lay a delicate enameled casket, holding a brilliant diamond betrothal ring, and a short note folded around a slip of colored paper:
*Wear this ring when I come at noon today, as a sign we are promised to each other. I’ve enclosed a check made out to Mr. Gascoigne to cover all your immediate expenses. Of course Mrs. Davilow will stay on at Offendene, at least for the foreseeable future. I hope when I arrive you will have picked an early date for us to marry, so you may begin to command me from a much shorter distance.
Yours, devotedly,
H. M. Grandcourt*

The check was made out for five hundred pounds. Gwendolen held it out to her mother alongside the letter.

“How very kind, how thoughtful,” Mrs. Davilow said, her voice warm with feeling. “But I truly would rather not be dependent on a son-in-law. I and the girls could get along very comfortably on our own.”

“Mamma, if you say that one more time, I won’t marry him,” Gwendolen snapped, her eyes flashing.

“My dear child, I do hope you aren’t only marrying for my sake,” Mrs. Davilow said, soft and apologetic.

Gwendolen tossed her head away on the pillow, leaving the ring lying on the sheets. She was furious that her mother was trying to take her reason for this marriage away from her, and deep down, she knew she wasn’t only marrying to save her family from poverty: she was pulled to this marriage by all sorts of urges far stronger than that, urges no small protest from her mother could stop. She woke to the full, bright light of day, and knew there was no going back. All the ugly visions, all the midnight alarms, would look far weaker when the sun was high.

“All I want is your happiness, my love,” Mrs. Davilow said gently. “I won’t say anything to vex you. Won’t you put the ring on?”

Gwendolen was quiet for a few minutes, turning the thought over in her head. Finally she sat up, the same fierce determination she used to spur herself on when she mounted a skittish horse, no matter what wild fancies were running through her head.

“I always thought the lover was supposed to slip the betrothal ring on himself,” she said, laughing, as she slid the brilliant onto her finger and tilted her head to admire the sparkle. “I know why he sent it ahead,” she added, nodding at her mother, conspiratorial.

“Why?”

“He’d rather I put it on myself than beg me for permission to do it. Aha! He’s very proud. But so am I. We’ll match each other perfectly. I’d hate a man who groveled on his knees, fawning all over me. He really isn’t disgusting at all.”

“That’s very faint praise for your future husband, Gwen.”

“No, it’s very high praise for a man,” Gwendolen said gaily. “Now I’m getting up to dress. Come do my hair for me, mamma dear,” she went on, pulling her mother’s face down to kiss her cheeks, “and stop being so silly about living in poverty. You have to let me make you comfortable, even if you don’t like it. Mr. Grandcourt is behaving perfectly, isn’t he?”

“Certainly he is,” Mrs. Davilow said, encouraged, fully convinced now that Gwendolen was truly fond of her betrothed. She’d always thought a suitor had to be judged by his station and his standing in polite society; it was so hard to know much else about a man, after all. All her anxiety was for Gwendolen’s mood, not Grandcourt’s character.

That mood shifted fast once she was awake and moving around. Halfway through doing her riding habit up, she was pulling every scrap of common sense she had together to justify her choice. The thing she kept coming back to, over and over, was the firm promise to herself: once she was Grandcourt’s wife, she would make him be as generous as possible to Mrs. Glasher’s children.

“What good would it do her if I refused to marry him? He could have married her years ago if he’d wanted to. He didn’t. Maybe she’s the one to blame for that. There’s so much about her I don’t know. He must have been good to her, in his way, otherwise she’d never have wanted to marry him.”

But that argument felt flimsy almost as soon as she thought it. Of course Mrs. Glasher didn’t want another woman’s children standing between her own son and the inheritance. That made sense. So Gwendolen found another way to make it all fair: “Maybe Grandcourt and I will never have children. I hope we never do. Then he can leave the whole estate to her pretty little boy. My uncle said Mr. Grandcourt can do whatever he likes with the land anyway, and once Sir Hugo Mallinger dies, there’ll be more than enough money for everyone.”

Suddenly Mrs. Glasher seemed unreasonable for demanding her son be the sole heir. Gwendolen Harleth, with all her sharp pride and her good intentions, would never wrong her. Gwendolen had gone her whole life thinking she was almost blameless, that all the fault in the world belonged to other people.

It was remarkable how fast her midnight revulsion at Grandcourt’s past faded, how small that old repugnance had become next to her urgent need to tell herself she was doing no harm at all. The midnight terror that she was crossing some invisible line into wickedness had dulled all her outrage at his choices. She thought of him, no matter what secrets he held, as a man she would soon hold nearly unlimited power over. Loving him had never been part of her plan, so any charm he had was just a nice bonus. Gwendolen had no idea that marriage could hold forces no one could manage, no matter how clever you were: she saw it as a game she would easily win, a role where she got to reign. She even found herself making excuses for him, telling herself he was no worse than any other man of his class, and making little plans to learn just what sort of behavior was considered normal and acceptable for gentlemen of his station.

Her hair was soon pinned perfectly for riding, and she stepped out in her habit, eager to get on horseback. She wanted the wind whipping through her hair, the thrill of a hard gallop, the old reckless daring she’d always carried with her back. The weight on her chest felt lighter already: in the bright daylight, moving fast, doubting her choice was far less oppressive than the alternative, that shabby, small life of servitude and insignificance she’d been trapped in before.

“Go back up and make yourself look like a duchess, mamma,” she said, turning on the stairs suddenly. “Put your best point-lace shawl over your head. I won’t have you looking meek and humble, not when you’re the mother of the lady of Ryelands.”

When Grandcourt arrived, he lifted her left hand gently, his eyes fixed on the diamond on her finger. “It was so good of you to think of everything and send that packet,” Gwendolen said, her tone soft and serious.

“Will you tell me if there’s anything I ever forget?” he said, holding her hand softly in his. “I will do absolutely anything you wish.”

“But I’m very unreasonable when it comes to what I want,” Gwendolen smiled.

“Yes. I expect that. All women are.”

“Then I won’t be unreasonable at all,” she said, pulling her hand away and tossing her head saucily. “I won’t be lumped in with every other woman on earth.”

“I didn’t say that,” Grandcourt replied, his usual quiet gravity softening a little as he looked at her. “You are no other woman. You are the only one who matters.”

“And what is that supposed to mean, exactly?” Gwendolen asked, stepping a few feet away from him, a playful little challenging glint in her eye.

Grandcourt paused, for a long, deliberate beat, before he spoke. “You are the woman I love.”

“Oh, what lovely, smooth speeches!” Gwendolen laughed. The sharp, odd twinge she’d felt before, the thought that he’d once said those very same words to another woman under far stranger circumstances, was already fading, growing familiar.

“Give me a nice speech back,” he said. “Tell me when we’re to be married.”

“Not yet. Not until we’ve had a full gallop over the downs. I’m so thirsty for that ride, I can’t think of anything else. I wish the hunting season would start. Sunday the twentieth, twenty-seventh, Monday, Tuesday…” Gwendolen counted off the days on her fingers, nodding prettily as she looked up at him, before sweeping one palm over the other triumphantly. “It starts in ten days!”

“Then let’s be married in ten days,” Grandcourt said. “Then we won’t have to fuss over the stables, or the hunt schedules, at all.”

“What do all women say when a man asks them that?” Gwendolen teased, mischievous.

“They say yes,” Grandcourt replied, caught a little off guard.

“Then I say no,” Gwendolen laughed, pulling her gauntlets on, her eyes bright with fun.

The moment was perfect for both of them. A cruder, more overeager lover would have ruined it all, grabbing for clumsy, unwanted kisses, spoiling the quiet, delicious tension of their little game. But Grandcourt loved the drama of it, the dance, and Gwendolen was free to play at being his queen, her spirits rising higher by the minute.
