Though Mrs. Jennings spent nearly three-quarters of every year roving between the homes of her married children and dearest friends, chattering her way through dinners and card parties from Devonshire to Derbyshire, she still kept a settled, much-loved home of her own. After her husband, a shrewd, good-natured tradesman, passed away, she’d settled there for every winter: a warm, chintz-draped house tucked into a quiet street just off Portman Square, scented year-round with dried lavender and spiced orange peel, stuffed full of the wedding favours and baby gifts she’d hoarded from all her grandchildren’s celebrations.

As the first frosts of January crisped the hedgerows around Barton Park, her thoughts turned stubbornly back to that London house, and one grey afternoon over tea, she blurted out an invitation to the two eldest Dashwood sisters so abruptly it left them both blinking over their teacups. Elinor, not yet noticing the way Marianne’s cheeks flushed bright pink, or the bright, restless spark in her eyes that had been dim ever since Willoughby left Devonshire, gave a gentle but firm refusal on both their behalves, certain she spoke for their shared wish not to leave their mother all alone in the depth of winter.

Mrs. Jennings blinked at the reply, then leaned forward, patting Elinor’s arm earnestly. “Oh, Lord, my dear, your mother will scarce miss you! I’ve set my whole heart on having you both. You won’t be the slightest inconvenience, I swear it – I won’t put myself out of sorts one bit. We three can ride up in my chaise, no fuss, and once we’re in town, if you tire of my silly old rounds, you can go off with either of my girls whenever you please. I’ve raised three children of my own, after all – your mother will know I’m more than fit to look after you two, and mark my words, I’ll make sure every handsome young bachelor in London hears what sweet, clever girls you are. If I don’t get at least one of you well married before the season’s out, it won’t be for lack of me speaking up!”

Sir John, who’d been slouched in his chair nodding along, boomed out his agreement. “I say! Miss Marianne wouldn’t turn it down if Miss Dashwood here wasn’t being so cautious. Hard on the girl, isn’t it, to miss out on all the fun just because her sister’s not in the mood? I’d advise you two to sneak off with Mrs. Jennings when you next get bored of Barton, and not say a word to Elinor about it!”

“Nonsense, I’ll be over the moon to have Marianne whether Elinor comes or not!” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “More the merrier, that’s what I say. If you two get sick of my old rambling, you can huddle in the corner and whisper and laugh at my odd ways all you like, I won’t mind a bit. But I must have at least one of you with me – I’m used to having Charlotte at my side every minute, and I can’t bear the thought of poking around that big London house all by myself. Come on, Marianne, shake on it! And if Elinor changes her mind later, all the better!”

Marianne’s whole face lit up as she leaned forward, voice warm and earnest. “Thank you, ma’am, from the very bottom of my heart. Your invitation is the kindest thing anyone has offered me in months, and I can scarce think of anything that would make me happier than to come – but my mother, my dearest, dearest mother… Elinor is right, if our absence made her the tiniest bit less happy or comfortable, I could never forgive myself. Nothing would ever tempt me to leave her if that was the cost.”

Mrs. Jennings promised her over and over that Mrs. Dashwood would be perfectly well off with Margaret for company, and by then Elinor had seen the truth clear as day: Marianne, who so often bristled at Mrs. Jennings’ loud, unfiltered manners, who would snap if the older woman teased her too freely about young men, cared nothing for any of that. Her longing to see Willoughby again was so strong it washed out every other annoyance, every usual irritation, every scruple she’d ever held. It was proof of how deep her love ran, so fierce Elinor could scarce bring herself to oppose it any longer. She referred the final decision to their mother, even though she knew Mrs. Dashwood would never say no to anything that made Marianne’s eyes shine that bright – and Elinor dared not voice her own reason for dreading London, the tangled secret of Edward Ferrars and Lucy Steele that she’d been carrying alone for months.

When they brought the invitation home to their cottage, Mrs. Dashwood did not hesitate a second. She could see Marianne’s whole heart was set on the trip, and she was certain a few weeks in London would do both girls a world of good. “I am delighted!” she cried, beaming. “This is exactly what I’ve been hoping for! Margaret and I will have the coziest, quietest time here without you two – we’ll pore over new books, practice our duets, you’ll come home and find Margaret can play the whole of her piano sonata without a single mistake! I’ve got a little plan to redecorate your bedrooms too, which we can do without disturbing you at all. It’s right that you two see London, meet other young people, learn the ways of the world. And you’ll get to see your brother John, too – no matter what silly disagreements he and I have had, I don’t want my girls estranged from their own family.”

Elinor smiled faintly. “You’ve thought of every joy, mama, but there is one objection I don’t think you’ve accounted for. Mrs. Jennings is very kind, but her company can scarcely be called a pleasure, and being under her protection won’t lend us any of the respectability you might wish for us.”

Marianne’s face fell for a second, before she leaned forward, eager to argue. “If that’s all that’s stopping you, Elinor, it need not stop me. I don’t care a fig for any of that. I can put up with every small inconvenience, every silly comment, every tedious hour, without the smallest effort.”

Elinor bit back a smile, thinking of all the times she’d had to remind Marianne to be polite to Mrs. Jennings when she’d snapped over some harmless tease. She resolved in that moment that if Marianne was set on going, she would go too – she could not let her impulsive, passion-led sister loose in London alone, with no one to steady her when her heart ran away with her. She felt a small flicker of relief, too: Lucy had told her Edward Ferrars would not be arriving in London until February. If they kept their visit short, they could be back at Barton long before he ever set foot in the city.

“I will hear no more of these silly objections,” Mrs. Dashwood said firmly. “You will both go, and you will both have a wonderful time. And Elinor, if you would only let yourself look forward to it, you might find far more joy there than you expect – perhaps even a pleasant surprise from Mr. Ferrars, or his family.”

Elinor’s chest tightened. She had long hoped to gently soften her mother’s certainty that Edward would one day marry her, to make the coming hurt less sharp when the truth of his secret engagement came out. She spoke as steadily as she could. “I like Edward very well, and I will always be glad to see him. But as for the rest of his family, I do not care at all if I ever make their acquaintance.”

Mrs. Dashwood smiled, and said no more. Marianne stared at Elinor in astonishment, and Elinor realized she might as well have kept quiet.

It was settled soon after that they would accept the invitation fully. Mrs. Jennings was over the moon, chattering all the way home about the new gowns she’d help the girls pick out in town. Sir John was equally delighted – for a man who hated nothing more than being bored and alone, adding two more people to the London party was a triumph. Even Lady Middleton made the effort to look pleased, giddy at the thought of showing off her new silk embroidery to new company. And the Miss Steeles, especially Lucy, could not have been happier: now Elinor would be in London, too, close at hand where Lucy could keep an eye on her.

Elinor found herself far less reluctant to go than she’d expected. When she saw her mother humming happily about the house making plans, and Marianne bouncing around the cottage, her old animation fully restored, singing the old ballads Willoughby used to recite and tucking the little locket with a lock of his hair into her travelling bag, she could not bring herself to feel unhappy about the trip. Marianne’s joy was so bright it felt almost like magic, and she could scarce bring herself to doubt it would turn out well.

The only time Marianne’s giddiness faded was the day of their departure, in the first week of January. She clung to her mother, sobbing into her shoulder, as if she feared she would never see her again. Mrs. Dashwood was scarcely less tearful. Only Elinor, standing quietly beside them, felt a little twist of foreboding, wondering what secrets and joys London held in store for them all.

The Middletons would follow them to town a week later. The Miss Steeles would stay on at Barton Park until the whole party left, and no one guessed that the quiet streets of Portman Square would hold more heartbreak, and more romance, than any of them could imagine.
