While the residents of Fairoaks drifted through their daily routine in a state of sleepy lethargy, the great house perched atop the hill across the River Brawl was waking up. After sleeping soundly through the lives of two generations of masters, it was suddenly shaking off its slumber, radiating signs of vigorous renewal.

This transformation coincided perfectly with a minor mishap involving Pen, who was so consumed by his own sorrow that he failed to notice the seismic shift occurring around him. Meanwhile, a rumor was sweeping the countryside like wildfire. Provincial newspapers had announced that Clavering Park was to be inhabited again. The news traveled fast: from Clavering Market to Cackleby Fair, from Chatteris Sessions to the roadside encounters between the squire's carriage and the vicar's humble conveyance. Whether at Tinkleton Church gate amidst the tolling bells and the procession of white smocks and scarlet cloaks, or in countless village gatherings, the word was unanimous: *Clavering Park is coming alive.*

To understand the magnitude of this event, one must look back five years. At that time, the county papers reported the marriage in Florence, at the British Legation, of Francis Clavering, Esq., the only son of Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., of Clavering Park, to Jemima Augusta, daughter of Samuel Snell of Calcutta and widow of the late J. Amory, Esq. The prevailing gossip suggested that the bankrupt Clavering had finally secured a wealthy widow from India. A select few travelers, including the Kickleburys in Italy, glimpsed the newlyweds. They were spotted residing opulently in the Poggi Palace, hosting lavish parties, yet strangely unable—or unwilling—to return to England.

Later, young Peregrine of Cackleby encountered them at Schloss Schinkenstein on Lake Mummel during his vacation. Rumors of the couple drifted through Rome, Lucca, Nice, and the gambling dens of the Rhine and Belgium, reaching Clavering Park in sporadic gusts. Their final known location was Paris, where, following the death of Samuel Snell in Calcutta, they reportedly lived in immense style and splendor.

As for Sir Francis Clavering's background, it offered little material for flattering biographies. Born to an outlaw hiding in a grim chateau near Bruges, he attempted a career in the dragoons but collapsed almost immediately. Gambling debts quickly ruined him; after two years in the army, he was forced to sell his commission, spent time in the Fleet Prison, and eventually sailed to Ostend to join his gout-ridden father in exile. For years, this decayed prodigal could be found lurking in billiard rooms and watering holes across Belgium, France, and Germany, punting at casinos, dancing at boarding-house balls, and riding steeple-chases on borrowed horses.

It was at a boarding house in Lausanne that Francis Clavering executed what he termed his "lucky coup" by marrying the recently returned Widow Amory. Upon her father's death, she inherited a fortune rumored to be staggering, and her step-father, Mr. Snell, doubled her allowance before passing away shortly thereafter, leaving her and her children a magnificent legacy.

However, prior to this windfall, Lady Clavering's reputation was anything but pristine. The respectable English expatriates avoided her; her manners were considered crude, and her origins dubious. Retired East Indians scoffed at her father, a disreputable lawyer and indigo smuggler, and her first husband, a ship's mate who had never set foot in Calcutta's society. Even Old Sir Jasper Rogers, the former Chief Justice, claimed to possess a scandalous secret about her first marriage but refused to share it, much to the disappointment of his daughters.

Yet, once the family moved to Paris and established themselves at the Hôtel Bouilli in the Rue Grenelle, Lady Clavering transformed into a beacon of generosity. The Faubourg Saint-Germain embraced her. Viscount Bagwig, the Ambassador, courted her attentively; princes flocked to her salons. Even the most austere figures of London society—Lady Elderbury, Lady Rockminster, and the Countess of Southdown—publicly endorsed her. Ten thousand pounds a year seemed to have miraculously purified her character. Her charitable contributions were boundless: funding schools and convents for French pious ladies, supporting the Armenian Patriarch, Father Barbarossa, Baptist missions, and even the Orthodox settlement in the cannibalistic islands of Feefawfoo. Legend had it that on the same day she donated five Napoleons to aid persecuted Jesuits, she also funded the Reverend J. Ramshorn's visionary project to convert the Pope. Beyond charity, she hosted the finest dinners and balls in Parisian society.

During this era, she seemingly resolved her husband's English debts. Suddenly, Sir Francis reappeared in his homeland, free from arrest, announced in the *Morning Post* and local papers as a resident of Mivart's Hotel. One day, the anxious housekeeper at Clavering House witnessed a carriage and four horses ascending the long avenue, stopping before the moss-covered steps of the vast, melancholy portico.

Inside the open carriage sat three gentlemen. On the rear bench was Mr. Tatham of Chatteris, a familiar face. Beside him, occupying seats of honor, sat a handsome, portly gentleman draped in mustaches, whiskers, fur collars, and elaborate braiding. Next to him reclined a pale, languid man who stepped out weakly, while the lawyer and the man in fur leaped out with nimble agility.

They ascended the mossy steps toward the hall door. A foreign attendant, wearing earrings and a gold-laced cap, vigorously pulled the handle of the cracked, sculptured gate. The bell rang loudly, echoing through the gloomy mansion. Footsteps echoed on the marble pavement inside, and the doors swung open. Finally, Mrs. Blenkinsop, the housekeeper, Polly, her aide-de-camp, and Smart, the keeper, emerged, bowing humbly.

Smart, pulling at his wispy, hay-colored hair, kicked his left heel as if warding off imaginary dogs, and bowed deeply. Old Mrs. Blenkinsop curtsied. Little Polly performed a series of rapid bows. Then, with great emotion, Mrs. Blenkinsop quavered, "Welcome to Clavering, Sir Francis. It does my poor eyes good to see one of the family once more."

These words were directed at the grand gentleman in fur and braiding, who wore his hat jauntily to one side and twirled his mustaches regally. However, he burst into laughter. "You've saddled the wrong horse, old lady! I am not Sir Francis Clavering returning to revisit the halls of my ancestors!" He gestured dramatically. "Friends and vassals! Behold your rightful lord!"

He pointed to the pale, languid gentleman, who retorted, "Don't be an ass, Ned."

"Yes, Mrs. Blenkinsop, I am Sir Francis Clavering," the Baronet continued, ignoring the interruption. "I remember you quite well. Did I forget? How do?" He took the old lady's trembling hand, nodding kindly in her astonished face.

Mrs. Blenkinsop insisted on her conscience that she would recognize Sir Francis anywhere; he was the very image of his father and grandfather.

"Oh yes—thanky—of course—very much obliged—and that sort of thing," Sir Francis said, gazing vacantly around the hall. "Dismal old place, isn't it, Ned? I never saw it but once, when my governor quarreled with grandfather in 'twenty-three."

"Dismal? Beautiful! The Castle of Otranto! The Mysteries of Udolpho, by Jove!" exclaimed the man addressed as Ned. "Look at that fireplace! You could roast an elephant in it! Splendid carved gallery! Inigo Jones, I swear! I'd lay five to two it's Inigo Jones."

"The upper section by Inigo Jones; the lower was altered by the eminent Dutch architect, Vanderputty, during the reign of George the First," corrected the housekeeper.

"Oh indeed," said the Baronet. "Gad, Ned, you know everything."

"I know a few things, Frank," Ned replied. "That isn't a Snyders over the mantelpiece—bet you three to one it's a copy. We'll restore it, my boy. A lick of varnish, and it will shine wonderfully. That old fellow in the red gown is presumably Sir Richard."

"Sheriff of the county and Member of Parliament in Queen Anne's reign," explained the housekeeper, marveling at the stranger's knowledge. "That on the right is Theodosia, wife of Harbottle, second baronet, painted by Lely as Venus... her son Gregory, the third baronet, beside her as Cupid... and that on the next panel is Sir Rupert, knighted by Charles the First, whose property was confiscated by Cromwell."

"Thank you—no need to continue, Mrs. Blenkinsop," interrupted the Baronet. "We'll explore the place ourselves. Frosch, give me a cigar. Would you care for one, Mr. Tatham?"
