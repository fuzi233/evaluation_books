I woke one morning from a sleep so deep it felt like I’d been dragged half-submerged across the threshold of another world, a sharp throbbing fire lancing up from my left hand that sent me jolting upright. The back of it was swollen to twice its usual size, stretched tight and shiny, and dead center of the inflamed flesh sat a neat triangular puncture, oozing a faint bead of iridescent blood exactly the shape of a leech’s bite. The swelling ebbed as the hours dragged on, and by dusk the wound was little more than a faint silver scar, almost fully healed. I turned over every moss-caked loose stone in the cave that night, probed every crevice in the rock walls, but found nothing that could have possibly sunk its fangs into me.

Days bled into one another, slow and soft as the warm mineral mist curling off the cave’s running hot stream. The body I guarded never moved, never stirred a single eyelid, but it could not be dead. Not a hint of rot clung to the air around it; the sharp jutting angles of its cheekbones and ribcage were softening, the parchment dryness of its old stretched skin smoothing into something plump and faintly pearlescent. Life had to be there, thrumming slow under that yielding skin – the tide that had ebbed so far out toward the edge of nothingness had finally turned, creeping back to fill the empty shape beneath the white linen I’d draped over her. Twenty times a day I knelt to check for the smallest sign of that return, twenty times I doubted, twenty times I almost despaired… until I pictured her the way I’d found her, crumpled in the moon-dappled forest, and hope would spark bright in my chest again.

Several weeks had slipped by that way, when one night I lay wakeful for hours, the cave’s warm air cloying and heavy on my skin, until I finally rose to step outside for a cooler breeze. The full moon hung low over the treeline, pouring silver light through the cave mouth to dapple the floor, and I paused for one last lingering look at my treasure before I left. “Bliss eternal,” I gasped out loud, my voice cracking, “am I seeing her eyes?”

Great dark orbs, black as the void between stars, luminous not with reflected light but with the excess of their own endless dark, seemed to glow out of the pale, moon-silvered curve of her face. I crept closer, my heart thudding so hard I was sure the noise of it would rouse her, and bent over her – only to feel my stomach drop straight through the floor of the cave. Her lids were still sealed tight. Hope and my own starved, lonely imagination had woven the illusion between them. I collapsed on the cold stone, and wept until my throat was raw.

Then a thought hit me. That tiny, awful crack in her lids I’d spotted the first few days after I’d carried her here, the little gap that had let nothingness peer through? It was gone. She must have opened them for a heartbeat, then fallen back asleep. Or maybe she was awake right now, holding them closed on purpose. Either way, it was life that had shut them, not death. My dread ebbed, and I dropped back onto my pallet, fast asleep in seconds.

That night I was bitten again, and woke burning with a thirst so fierce my tongue felt glued to the roof of my mouth. I searched every inch of the cave the next morning, even probing the depths of the hot stream’s shallows with a stick, and still found nothing. The bite was identical to the first, and by evening it was nearly gone. I concluded some giant, uncanny leech native to the hot waters was sneaking in to feed at night. “But if it’s only after blood,” I told myself, “so long as I’m here, I’ll give it mine before it ever gets near her.”

That same morning, when I peeled a plump sun-ripened grape, plucked out all the seeds, and brushed it against her lips, they twitched in a tiny, clear movement of reception. I knew then, beyond all doubt, that she lived. My hope swelled so big I started planning: she had to have clothes, something soft and easy to slip on, so the moment she was strong enough to stand, she could do it without delay.

I headed out into the forest that afternoon, hunting for a suitable fabric, and soon spotted the tough, fibrous skeletons left behind by fallen prickly pear leaves, woven into fine, strong reticulated mats. I gathered an armful, spread them out in the sun to dry, pulled apart their fine interwoven layers, and started stitching together two loose, flowing garments: one a skirt that hung from the waist, the other a tunic that draped soft over her shoulders, sewn through three layers of the fiber with an aloe spine needle and twisted vine filament.

For a week after that, she showed no new signs of waking, but every day she took the grapes more easily, her form growing rounder, her skin fairer and clearer. But a horrid, creeping doubt would nudge at me sometimes: was this growth not life, but some kind of uncanny fungal bloom? The few grapes I fed her could never account for how fast she was filling out.

Then I was bitten again. The thing began to visit me every three days, without fail. It would sink its fangs into my neck or my upper arm, always just one clean bite, always while I slept, never once venturing out in the daylight even when I napped on purpose. I lay awake for hours on the watch, ears straining, eyes wide in the dark, but I never heard it approach, never saw a glint of it, never even felt the moment its teeth broke my skin. Eventually I stopped trying to catch it. I could feel my strength slipping, feel the blood draining out of me at a dangerous rate, but I didn’t care. Death was retreating from her, life was growing brighter by the day, and soon we would leave this cave together, and I’d recover fast enough once the leech was gone.

I finished the garments, then wove layers of the same fiber into soft, flexible sandals for her. One night I jolted awake, breathless and faint, my head spinning so bad I had to crawl toward the cave mouth to get air – when a soft rustle from her leaf couch froze me mid-movement.

“I caught the vile thing,” said a faint, clear voice, speaking my own mother tongue, so familiar I almost cried out. “I caught it in the very act.”

She was alive. She spoke. I clamped down on my wild, overwhelming joy before it could make me stammer and startle her. “What creature?” I breathed, barely pushing the sound past my lips.

“The thing that was biting you.”

“What was it?”

“A great white leech.”

“How big?” I asked, forcing every muscle in my body to stay still and calm.

“Nearly six feet long, I think,” she answered.

“You may have saved my life! But how could you touch that horrid thing? That was so brave of you!” I cried.

“I did it,” was all she said, and I heard a faint shudder in her tone.

“Where is it? What could you do with a monster that big?”

“I threw it in the river.”

“Then it’ll come back, I’m afraid!”

“I don’t think I could have killed it even if I knew how,” she said. “I heard you moaning, got up to see what was hurting you, and saw that awful thing fastened to your neck. I wrenched it off. But I couldn’t hold it, I could barely heave it away from me. All I heard was it splash into the rushing water.”

“We’ll kill it next time it comes,” I said – but the words barely left my mouth before the world swam, my vision went white, and I pitched forward toward the cave mouth.

I woke at sunrise, the golden light spilling over me. She stood a few paces away, clad in the rough, clumsy fiber clothes I’d stitched, and even in that crude garment, she was grand as a marble caryatid, graceful as a wind-blown pine. I’d seen those glorious eyes the night before, I realized. They’d shone in the dark hours, dark as the primeval void, and now in the sun they glowed brighter than daylight itself. She stood straight as a carved column, gazing at me, her pale face showing no softness, only a quiet, unyielding question.

I scrambled to my feet. “We have to leave,” I said. “The white leech –”

I broke off. A strange, cold little smile flickered across her beautiful face.

“Did you find me there?” she asked, pointing at the pallet where I’d laid her these past months.

“No,” I said. “I carried you there.”

“You carried me?”

“Yes.”

“From where?”

“From the deep forest.”

“What did you do with my clothes? And my jewels?”

“You had none when I found you. Nothing but skin and bone, and your hair.”

“Then why didn’t you leave me there?”

“Because I hoped you weren’t dead.”

“Why would you care?”

“Because I was so lonely. I wanted you to wake, so I wouldn’t be alone anymore.”

“You kept me trapped, enchanted, just to hoard my beauty for yourself,” she said, sharp with proud, icy scorn.

Her words stung, and indignation flared hot in my chest. “There was no beauty left to hoard when I found you,” I said.

“Then why didn’t you just leave me to die?”

“Because you were my own kind.”

“Your kind?” she cried, her tone dripping with utter contempt. “I thought that once, too – but I see I was very much mistaken.”

“I pitied you, that was all.”

“No woman ever deserved more pity, and less of every other feeling,” I shot back.

A look of unutterable pain, mortification, and blazing anger crossed her face. She turned away from me, and went silent. The dark gulfs of her eyes held the depth of a starless night; the light in them had died, snuffed out by her hate for the kindness I’d shown.

“If you’d never roused me, what would you have done?” she asked suddenly, not turning around.

“I would have buried you.”

“It? What ‘it’? You would have buried *this*?” she shrieked, spinning around on me in a white, blinding fury, her arms flung wide, her eyes darting forks of cold, unearthly lightning.

“No, not you,” I said, steady, unflinching – there was no point in lying to a woman like this. “What we’ve brought back to life, these weeks of watching and tending. But if I’d spotted the smallest sign of rot on you, I would have buried you at once, no hesitation.”

“Foolish dog!” she snarled. “I was only in a trance! Samoil, what a cursed fate! Go fetch that savage woman you stole this hideous rag of a dress from.”

“I wove it for you myself,” I said. “It’s ugly, I know, but it was the best I could do.”

She drew herself up to her full, towering height. “How long was I unconscious?” she demanded. “A woman couldn’t sew that much in a single day.”

“Not in twenty, hardly even thirty,” I told her.

“Ha! And how long do you claim I lay there, senseless? Answer me now.”

“I don’t know how long you’d been lying there before I found you, but when I picked you up, you were nothing but skin stretched over bone. That was more than three months ago. Your hair was the only beautiful thing left on you. I cared for it as best I could.”

“My poor hair,” she murmured, and she drew a great, heavy fall of it over her shoulder from behind her. “It’ll take far more than three months of care to make me forgive you for dragging me back to life. I suppose I have to thank you, though I can’t say I feel grateful.”

“There’s no need for thanks, madam,” I said. “I would have done the same for any woman, or any man, for that matter.”

“Why isn’t my hair tangled?” she asked, running her fingers through the silken strands.

“I washed it for you every morning in the hot river’s current. I never could have brought you back to life without those daily baths.”

She flinched hard, a shudder of raw disgust running through her, and stood for a long minute staring at the rushing, warm water. Then she turned back to me, her face set like stone.

“We are going to understand each other clearly,” she said. “You have done me the two worst wrongs anyone could inflict. You forced me back to life, and you shamed me. Neither of those wrongs can I ever pardon.”

She lifted her left hand, and flung it out in a sharp, repelling motion. Something ice-cold, colder than the heart of a frozen glacier, slammed into my forehead. The world went black. When I woke, I was sprawled on the wet cave stone, soaked through, shivering so hard my teeth chattered, alone.
