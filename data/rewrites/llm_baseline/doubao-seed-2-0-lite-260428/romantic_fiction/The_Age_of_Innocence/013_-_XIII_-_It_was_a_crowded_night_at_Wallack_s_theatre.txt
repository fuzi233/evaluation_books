The velvet-draped stalls of Wallack’s Theatre hummed with the kind of warm, packed energy that turns a regular show into a shared, unforgettable ritual that night. Dion Boucicault’s *The Shaughraun* was well into its peak popularity, every seat filled to the rafters: gallery audiences whooped and cheered unreservedly at every dramatic beat, while the society crowds in the boxes and stalls smiled knowingly at the familiar, overwrought lines and beloved tropes, and lost themselves in the story all the same.
There was one scene above all others that held the whole house breathless, floor to ceiling. It was the quiet parting between Harry Montague and Ada Dyas, where after a sparse, almost wordless exchange of goodbyes, the young lover turned for the door. Ada Dyas, standing framed by the stage mantel, her face tilted down toward the fake hearth fire, wore a plain gray cashmere gown no fashion plate would have praised—untrimmed, unlooped, moulded to her tall frame, the hem pooling soft at her feet. A narrow black velvet ribbon circled her throat, its two ends slipping loose down her back. When her suitor turned away, she braced her arms on the mantel shelf and buried her face in her hands. He paused on the threshold, staring back at her, then stole silently across the room, lifted one trailing end of that velvet ribbon, pressed a soft kiss to it, and slipped out the door before she could hear him move. The curtain fell on that still, silent frame, and the whole theatre exhaled as one.
Newland Archer had come to *The Shaughraun* half a dozen times just for that scene. He thought the pair’s wordless grief finer than any grand, overacted performance he’d ever seen in Paris or London, the quiet reticence of it stabbing deeper than the most thunderous theatrical monologue.
That night, though, the scene hit different. For no clear reason he could name, it pulled his thoughts straight back to his private meeting with Madame Olenska, ten days prior. There was no logical resemblance between the two situations: he was no handsome romantic stage lead, Ellen Olenska was nothing like the tall, pale, stately redhead playing Ada Dyas, and the two of them had not been star-crossed lovers parting—they had been a lawyer and his client, going over the terrible, unvarnished details of her petition for divorce.
But Ellen Olenska carried a strange, unnameable weight with her, a quiet aura of all the tragic, moving possibilities that lay far outside the tidy, predictable bounds of New York society. She never spoke a word that would draw attention to it, but it clung to her, as if she had been plucked straight from the roiling maelstrom of a scandalous European life so full of drama she had long ago stopped being surprised by its cruelties. Archer had always believed people drew fate to themselves far more than fate chose them, and he had sensed that quiet tendency in Ellen from the first moment they met: no matter how hard she shrank from conflict, no matter how carefully she tried to hide, trouble would find her.
When he had left her that day, he’d come away certain that Count Olenski’s cruel accusation was not unfounded: the “secretary” who had helped her escape her husband’s cruel household had likely been more to her than a rescuer. What could be more natural, after all, than for a young, terrified, desperate woman to be grateful to the person who pulled her from a life too awful to name? The tragedy was that that very gratitude, in the eyes of New York law and New York society, put her on the exact same moral level as the monstrous husband she had fled.
He had been bound to tell her the truth, that the simple, kindhearted New York family she’d counted on for mercy would never indulge her, that a divorce would drag every last private, messy secret of hers out for the world to gawk at. To watch her accept that fact with quiet, unprotesting resignation had gutted him. He felt drawn to her by a tangled mess of pity and sharp, unnameable jealousy, as if that small, humbling secret she’d laid at his feet had made her soft and vulnerable, impossible to turn away from. He’d told the whole family she’d dropped her divorce plans of her own accord, no messy scandal for them to endure, and they had all sighed in relief: Mrs. Welland bragged endlessly about her clever future son-in-law, and old Mrs. Mingott had cackled and called Ellen a silly little goose for wanting to pretend she was a plain old maid instead of a married countess.
By the time the curtain fell that night, Newland’s eyes were stinging with tears, and he stood at once to leave, to carry that tender, silent parting image home with him before it faded. As he turned to make his way up the aisle, he glanced back toward the boxes, and his breath caught. There she was, Ellen Olenska, seated in the Beaufort box surrounded by Lawrence Lefferts, Sillerton Jackson, and a handful of other familiar faces.
They had not spoken alone since their meeting, and he had been deliberately avoiding her in public, but their eyes locked the second he turned, and Mrs. Beaufort gave him that lazy, perfect little gesture of invitation she reserved for her favorite guests. There was no polite way to refuse. He squeezed past Beaufort and Lefferts to take a seat directly behind Ellen, while Jackson murmured a long, confidential story to Mrs. Beaufort about Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s recent scandalous Sunday reception, where gossip swore guests had dared to dance. No one was paying them the slightest mind.
Ellen turned, her voice low and soft enough that only he could hear it, and nodded toward the dark, empty stage. “Do you think he’ll send her a bunch of yellow roses tomorrow morning?”
Newland’s face burned, his heart leaping straight into his throat. He had sent her two unmarked boxes of yellow roses, no card included, after each of his first two visits to her home. He’d been fully certain she’d never guessed they were from him. Now she had named the flowers, tied that small, secret gift to the tender, wordless kiss the actor had pressed to the velvet ribbon, and he was flooded with a giddy, agitated joy he could not name.
“I was just thinking that exact thing,” he said. “I was about to leave right then to carry that picture away with me before it faded.”
A soft, dusky blush rose to her cheeks, almost as if she was fighting not to let it show. She twisted the mother-of-pearl opera glass in her smoothly gloved hands, and paused for a long beat before asking, “What do you do, while May is away?”
He felt a faint, irrational prickle of annoyance, but explained: the Wellands had left town the week prior for St. Augustine, per the unshakable annual ritual built around Mr. Welland’s “delicate bronchial tubes.” The quiet, mild-mannered patriarch could not find his own hairbrushes or stamp his own letters without his wife’s guidance, and the whole adoring family would never dream of letting him travel south alone, even though May’s two lawyer brothers could not leave New York in the winter to join him till Easter. The engagement had been announced earlier than planned, but no one would ever dare disrupt Mr. Welland’s routine. Newland had daydreamed of joining them, of lazy weeks boating in the Florida sunshine with his betrothed, but he knew the entire Mingott clan would label him frivolous if he asked for a mid-winter holiday, and he had already accepted that quiet, unprotesting resignation would be the backbone of his married life.
Ellen looked up at him from beneath lowered lids, her voice soft and abrupt. “I did what you advised. I dropped the idea.”
“Ah— I’m so glad,” he fumbled, embarrassed that she would bring up such a private, heavy subject in a crowded theatre box.
“You were right,” she went on, her breath catching a little. “But sometimes life is so hard. So perplexing.”
“I know,” he said.
“I wanted you to know that I’m grateful. That I see you were right,” she finished, lifting her opera glass quickly to her eyes just as Beaufort’s loud, resonant laugh boomed across the box, breaking the quiet bubble around them.
Newland stood up at once, and left the box, left the theatre entirely, the sharp cold New York night air stinging his hot cheeks. Only the day before, he had received a letter from May, so bright and candid, begging him to be kind to Ellen while they were away. She had written that Ellen admired him more than anyone, that none of the family saw how lonely she was, that Granny and the uncles all thought she craved nothing but fancy dinners and new gowns, and did not understand she missed the music, art, and clever conversation she had been surrounded by in Europe, the exact kind of things Newland loved too. He had thought he would ignore that request, determined as an engaged man not to draw too much attention to Ellen, convinced she had more than enough protectors in Beaufort, the van der Luydens, and a dozen other fawning society men. But every time he spoke to her, he realized May’s sweet naivety held a kind of quiet divination.
Ellen Olenska was lonely. She was unhappy. And no one else in all of New York seemed to see it.
