In the hushed hours before sundown, the scent of Deleglise's renowned Sunday suppers wafted through the Georgian townhouse in Queen Square, an aromatic harbinger of the feast to come. The old gentleman's passion for cooking was a force to be reckoned with, and as he moved with purposeful ease about his gleaming stove, the anticipation grew thicker than the rich sauces simmering in the pots. Behind him, his guests, a veritable Who's Who of the English-speaking world, waited patiently, their eyes fixed on the master chef as he worked his culinary magic.

Madeline, his elderly, white-capped Bonne, flitted about the table, her slender fingers arranging the cut glass and silverware with the precision of a conductor leading an orchestra. As the savory aromas danced through the air, the conversation, hushed and muted, began to stir, a gentle hum of anticipation building to a crescendo.

The talk, like the swell of the Moonlight Sonata, would burst forth once more, a torrent of wit and wisdom, of stories and anecdotes, from artists, musicians, actors, writers, and scholars, whose names were household words. Yet, to those who listened with fresh ears, their epigrams might seem less brilliant, their jests less laughable, for humor and understanding, like the tides, are ever-changing.

As I reflect on those enchanted afternoons, I recall the quiet rituals that governed our preparations. On Sundays, Dan and I would take turns critiquing each other's work. I would sample his creations, offering constructive criticism, while he would scrutinize my housekeeping. "Too much sugar," I would say, savoring the flavor.

Dan would chuckle good-naturedly, his eyes twinkling with amusement. "You silly Juggins, that's the point – it's a sugar cake!" But I remained firm, insisting that a balance of flavors was essential. We would debate, our banter a delicate dance of give-and-take, until we reached a compromise.

His cookery was Dan's domain, and he guarded it jealously. Even when his creations failed, he would devour them with a show of enthusiasm, feigning delight. I've seen him finish a sponge cake, its center a dry, crumbly disaster, declaring it a masterpiece. Later, he would retreat to his study, a glass of brandy in hand, lost in thought.
