The revelations concerning the Clavering household, gathered by Major Pendennis through the diligence of Mr. Strong and his own direct interventions, served as a heavy anchor for the old gentleman's ambitions. To betroth his nephew, Arthur, to a lady burdened by fathers-in-law of such dubious repute—the "guileless" and ill-starred Lady Clavering and her companion, the notorious convict Altamont—was a recipe for disaster. While these two fathers-in-law might theoretically cancel each other out, the practical reality was far less diplomatic. Any public sighting of Altamont would trigger his immediate flight and subsequent execution; a rope was his inevitable fate under British law. Yet, no guardian wished to secure a wife whose lineage required the disposal of a parent via hanging. Furthermore, the Major's strategy relied on the threat of exposure to keep Sir Francis Clavering in check, turning the Baronet into a willing slave to anyone who held the fatal secret of Altamont's return.

However, the financial calculus presented a grim alternative. If the Begum, driven by love, liquidated her entire fortune to bail out this "irreclaimable reprobate," her heirs would inherit an empty treasury. Worse still, Miss Amory would arrive at her husband's side not with a dowry or a parliamentary seat, but with her person alone and a pedigree tarnished by the infamous legal abbreviation *sus*.

Yet, amidst these dire prospects, the Major's scheming mind identified a final avenue. This opportunity lay within a conversation that was about to unfold between the Major and the Honourable Baronet, the Member for Clavering.

When a man finds himself drowning in pecuniary difficulties, he vanishes from the flock of his former peers, diving into strange, distant nooks to catch his breath. One knows a gentleman of fashion who, vanished from the clubs and parks, resurfaced happy and affable at a shabby tavern in Billingsgate; another, a man of learning, sent his address from a cellar in the Strand when outrunning a constable. Under such misfortunes, a man may have a house, but he is rarely in it; he possesses an address for letters, but only fools expect to find him there. Only the faithful possess the key to his hiding place.

Thus, despite the disputes with his wife and the resulting chaos, finding Sir Francis Clavering at home proved impossible. "Since I last fetched him for my book—which cost fourteen pounds—he doesn't return until three o'clock," Mr. Lightfoot remarked to his friend Morgan. "Then he pretends to sleep when I bring his water in the morning and slips out when I'm downstairs." Announcing his intention to become Butler to My Lady and marry his old woman, Lightfoot described the Baronet's current state. Similarly, following his altercations with Strong, Clavering had fled to other haunts, escaping the Chevalier's reproaches and, perhaps, the reach of his own conscience.

Though the elder Pendennis, bent on seeing his country neighbour and parliamentary representative, was determined to engage the shy Baronet, it required considerable effort. Since being called in as a family friend, Clavering had treated the Major like a plague, avoiding him whenever accounts needed rendering or business discussed—a behavior mirrored by all lawyers and agents when a bill was due. The Major's attempts to lure the bird failed repeatedly: an innocent dinner invitation at Greenwich resulted in the Baronet suspecting a trap and leaving the Major to eat his whitebait alone; a request for ten minutes' talk at Bays's was acknowledged with precise punctuality ("four o'clock... precisely"), yet Clavering never materialized. The Major realized that seeking a man was easy, but finding him was another matter entirely.

Just before the close of the day, a critical transaction occurred. Sir Francis Clavering, having sworn to his wife's advisers to accept no further bills and to subsist solely on her allowance, had nonetheless signed his name to a piece of stamped paper. Carried off by his friend, Mr. Moss Abrams, it promised to be "done" by a party intimately connected with Abrams. Meanwhile, Mr. Santiago's cigar-shop, located near St. James's and Bury Streets, served as a hive of activity. Strong, constantly frequenting the back-parlour, heard of this transaction.

"He is at his old work again," Mr. Santiago informed a customer. "He and Moss Abrams were in my parlour. Moss sent my boy for a stamp. It must have been a bill for fifty pounds. The Baronet told Moss to date it two months back. He will claim it is an old bill, forgotten during his recent settlement with his wife. I dare say they will grant him more funds now that he is 'clear.'" A man who habitually signs "promises to pay" at six months enjoys the satisfaction of knowing his affairs are scrutinized and his signature circulated among London's worst knaves.

As the sun set, Major Pendennis walked daintily toward his lodgings in Bury Street, only to encounter Mr. Strong, the Chevalier, striding along the same pavement, burning with wrath and smelling faintly of Havana tobacco.

"Confound these young men," the Major thought, wrinkling his nose. "How they poison everything with smoke. Here comes a fellow with mustachios and a cigar. Every fellow who smokes and wears mustachios is a low fellow." Realizing who it was, he offered a dignified bow. "I hope you are well, Mr. Strong?"

Strong stopped, extending his hand. Old Pendennis offered a couple of ungracious fingers, maintaining a patronising air. "What is your good news?" he asked, condescending to address the other as if he were merely a common acquaintance. "Still in town, Mr. Strong? I hope I see you well."

"My news is bad news, sir," Strong replied gravely. "It concerns our friends at Tunbridge Wells, and I should like to talk to you about it. Clavering is at his old tricks again, Major Pendennis."

"Indeed!" The Major's interest awakened immediately. "Pray do me the favour to come into my lodging."

Inside the drawing-room, Strong unburdened himself of his indignation. "No promises will bind him, sir," Strong declared. "Remember when we met with my lady's lawyer? He wouldn't be satisfied with giving his honour; he wanted to take his oath on his knees, ring for a Bible, and swear perdition on his soul if he ever gave another bill. Yet, he signed one today, sir. He will sign as many more as you please for ready money. He will deceive anybody—his wife, his child, or his old friend who has backed him a hundred times. Why, there's a bill of his and mine due next week."

"I thought we had paid all."

"Not that one," Strong admitted, blushing slightly. "He asked me not to mention it, and—and I had half the money for that, Major. And they will be down on me. But I don't care; I'm used to it. It's Lady Clavering that riles me. It's a shame that that good-natured woman, who has paid him out of gaol a score of times, should be ruined by his heartlessness. A parcel of bill-stealing boxers and rascals get his money, and he don't scruple to throw an honest fellow over. Would you believe it, sir, he took money of Altamont—you know whom I mean."

"Indeed?" The Major feigned an impenetrable countenance. "Of that singular man, who I think came tipsy once to Sir Francis's house? Who is Altamont, Mr. Strong?"

"I am sure I don't know, if you don't know," Strong answered, his expression shifting to one of surprise and suspicion.

"To tell you frankly," the Major said, adopting a tone of grave wisdom, "I have my suspicions—I suppose, mind, I only suppose—that in our friend Clavering's life, who, between you and me, Captain Strong, is about as loose a fish as any in my acquaintance, there are queer secrets and stories he would not like known. Very likely this fellow, Altamont, knows some story against Clavering and holds him to ransom. I know some of the best men in England paying through the nose in that way. But their private affairs are no business of mine, Mr. Strong. And it is not to be supposed that because I dine with a man, I pry into his secrets or am answerable for his past life. As for Clavering, I am most interested for his wife's sake, and her daughter, who is a most charming creature. When her ladyship asked me, I looked into her affairs and tried to set them straight. And if I am called upon—and, by the way, this Mr. Altamont, Mr. Strong? How is he? Is he in town?"

"I don't know that I am called upon to know where he is, Major Pendennis," Strong said, rising and taking up his hat in dudgeon. The Major's patronising caution and impertinent inquiries had offended the honest gentleman.
