Waiting for a letter from one you love can twist the heart into strange shapes. Margaret, writing to her dying father, poured out all her hope and belief—whether in vision or illusion, she could not say. When the words were written, and Luke had gone on his journey, she stood back from herself, startled by how easily she had let faith carry her away. A pang of guilt followed—what had she done? Had she given false comfort? And then Catherine came, sharp as ever, to scold her for clinging to dreams that might never come true. The rebuke was just, but it left Margaret broken. She had soared too high, only to crash down into deeper sorrow.

It was then that Joan Ketel, a woman of quiet strength and kind heart, found her sitting alone, wrapped in silence and despair. Joan came radiant with an idea. “Margaret,” she said, “I know what will heal thee. There’s a holy hermit in Gouda—a man who sees things others cannot. When he’s in the mood, he knows what lies ahead.”

“Aye,” Margaret replied, weary and doubtful. “I’ve heard of him.”

Joan, though gentle, would not be denied. With some coaxing, she persuaded Margaret to walk to Gouda, bringing with them a basket of butter and eggs as a gift. It was a long way, but they went, side by side, talking little, each lost in their own thoughts.

They reached the hermit’s cave at dusk. It was carved into the rock, dark and silent, with a small opening where offerings were placed. They set down their basket, and Margaret stepped forward, placing her lips to the aperture.

“Holy hermit,” she whispered, “we bring thee butter and eggs, the best we have. I am a poor girl, deserted, wife yet not wife, mother to the sweetest babe. I come to ask—alive or dead? True to his vows, or false?”

A faint voice answered, thin and weak: “Trouble me not with matters of earth. Send me a holy friar—I am dying.”

“Oh, no!” Margaret cried, her heart sinking. “Poor soul! Then let us come in, to help thee.”

“Saints forbid!” came the reply. “Thine is a woman’s voice. Send me a holy friar.”

They turned and walked back, silent, the wind tugging at their cloaks. Joan could not hold her tongue. “Are women so evil, then, that even a dying man must not see them?”

But Margaret did not answer. She was too far gone in grief.

Joan tried to console her, gently at first, then more firmly. “And Jorian—he’ll speak soon. He’s close to bursting. He’s grateful, truly. You saved him when no doctor could.”

“Seeing is believing,” Margaret said quietly, almost bitterly.

“I know,” Joan pressed on. “He says he’d have thought feverfew a sorry herb if it hadn’t been for you. ‘Why,’ he said, ‘if there’s a poor thing like that, I’d pull mine up.’ But then I told him remedies aren’t better for being rare. You and your herb cured him when grand medicines failed. So I said, ‘You may go down on your knees to feverfew.’ But yes—he’s grateful. All he talks about is you. Only last night—”

“What?” Margaret asked, her voice softening.

“He made me swear not to tell you.”

“Then tell me now.”

“Well,” Joan hesitated, “he said: ‘If I speak, it won’t bring him back—but it will stir up trouble. I wish I had more sense. I’m sorely troubled. But least said is soonest mended.’ That’s his favourite saying. He comes back to it from a mile off.”

Margaret shook her head. “We are wading through deep waters, my child and I.”

It was Saturday night. Still no word from Luke.

“Poor Luke,” she murmured. “It was kind of him to go on such a journey.”

“He is one in a hundred,” Catherine said warmly.

“Mother,” Margaret asked softly, “do you think he would be kind to little Gerard?”

“I am sure he would. So be kinder to him when he returns. Will you?”

“Aye.”

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, took a shortcut along the riverbank, avoiding a bend, and marched swiftly toward the coast. He reached a point closer to Rotterdam than where Luke waited for Gerard Eliassoen. The next morning, he rose early and arrived in the city by noon.

Without delay, he made for the port, eager not to keep Jerome waiting. He saw several monks of his order standing on the quay. He approached them.

“Have you seen Brother Jerome?” he asked.

“Surely, brother,” came the reply. “Prithee, where is he?”

“Where? Why, there!” one monk pointed, gesturing toward a ship already under full sail.

Clement looked up—and saw it. A vessel cutting through the water, sails billowing, leaving the shore behind.

“What? Gone without me? Oh, Jerome! Jerome!” he called out, his voice cracking with pain.

The friars turned, surprised. One stepped forward. “You must be Brother Clement,” he said. Then they greeted him warmly, kissed his hand, and handed him a letter Jerome had entrusted them to deliver.

It was hastily written, cold, and distant.

*“A ship sails for England. I do not wish to waste time. You may follow if you choose, but you would serve God better here. Preach to your own people. You have a gift—Dutch hearts respond to you. But the English are haughty. You are too tender for them.*

*I met one on the road who sought you by the name you bore in the world. Be wary. Let not the world ensnare you again with its silken nets.*

Clement read it twice. His face grew pale. He felt betrayed—not merely by a friend, but by a spiritual brother. Here was Jerome, whom he had loved and admired, whom he had shared hardship with, who had once been his pupil, his penitent, his son in Christ—now casting him aside with indifference.

Had he done something wrong? Was he unworthy? Or had Jerome forgotten the very love that should bind brothers?

“You have no heart,” Clement whispered to himself. “You fly from me, man without mercy. I will not follow. I will go to Sevenbergen and pray over a grave.”

Three hours later, he passed Peter’s cottage. Children laughed and ran around it; the house had been repaired, a new shed built. He turned his head away quickly, not wanting to disturb the memory of what once was.

He walked instead to the churchyard. Among the stones, he searched for Margaret’s tomb. He could not find it. He searched again, every corner, every row.

“Ah, poverty,” he sighed. “Stern poverty. Poor soul—you were like me. No one remained to love you when Gerard was gone.”

He entered the church, knelt before the altar, and kissed the steps. Then he prayed long and deeply for the soul of the woman whose resting place he could not locate.

As he stepped out, he saw an old man leaning over the churchyard gate, peering in.

“Do you live here?” Clement asked.

“Four score and twelve years, man and boy,” the old man replied. “And I come here every day lately. This is where I expect to rest myself soon.”

“My son,” Clement said gently, “can you tell me where Margaret Brandt lies?”

“Margaret? There are many Margarets here.”

“Margaret Brandト. Daughter of a learned physician.”

“As if I didn’t know,” the old man snapped, then softened. “But she doesn’t lie here. Bless you, they left this place long ago. Gone in a moment. House empty. What? Is she dead? Margaret, Peter’s daughter, dead? Now, think on it. Likely enough. These great towns—they don’t suit country folk.”

“What great towns?” Clement asked.

“Rotterdam, they said. Or Amsterdam? I think it was Rotterdam. And gone there to die!”

Clement sighed.

“It wasn’t in her face when I saw her. I can usually tell. Alas, she was a blooming flower—cut down too soon. And here I stand, old weed like me, still breathing.”

“She was a May rose,” Clement said softly.

“Dear heart,” the old man added, “what a smile she had—”

“God bless you, my son,” Clement said, turning away. “Farewell.”

He walked on, reaching the convent at sunset. He stayed in the chapel, watching and praying—for Jerome, for Margaret—until long after midnight, until his soul had cooled, and the fire of grief had settled into quiet ash.
