After a fortnight, Lady Palmer recovered sufficiently for her mother to reduce her visits to once or twice daily, allowing Mrs. Jennings to return to Berkeley Street and her accustomed routine. Finding the Miss Dashwoods eager to resume their former hospitality, the household settled comfortably into this new rhythm.

It was about the fourth morning following this resettlement that Mrs. Jennings burst into the drawing-room, where Elinor sat alone. Her countenance bore an air of hurried importance that instantly primed Elinor for news. With barely a breath taken, Mrs. Jennings launched into her tale.

"Lord!" she exclaimed, "my dear Miss Dashwood, have you heard the news?"

"No, ma'am. Pray, what is it?"

"Oh, something so strange! But you shall hear the whole. When I visited Mr. Palmer, I found Charlotte quite flustered regarding the infant. It was crying, fretting, covered in pimples, looking terribly ill. I examined it immediately and assured them, 'Lord!' it was nothing at all, merely the red gum,' and the nurse agreed. Yet Charlotte refused to be consoled. Consequently, Dr. Donovan was summoned. Fortunately, he had just stepped in from Harley Street, and upon seeing the child, he echoed our diagnosis—that it was merely the red gum—and Charlotte was instantly relieved. However, just as he departed, a thought struck me—I confess I cannot explain how it occurred—to inquire if there were any further developments. Immediately, his expression shifted; he smirked, simpered, and adopted a grave demeanor, suggesting he knew something significant. Finally, he whispered, 'To prevent any unpleasant reports reaching the young ladies under your care concerning their sister's indisposition, I believe there is no cause for alarm. I hope Mrs. Dashwood will recover fully.'"

"What!" Elinor gasped, "Is Fanny ill?"

"That is precisely what I asked," Mrs. Jennings continued, her voice rising with excitement. "'Lord!' I said, 'is Mrs. Dashwood ill?' And then the truth spilled out. Here is the summary, as far as I could ascertain: Mr. Edward Ferrars—the very young gentleman I used to jest about with you, though happily, as fate would have it, those jokes were empty air—appears to have been secretly engaged to my cousin, Lucy, for over twelve months! Think of it, my dear! Not a soul knew of this arrangement except Nancy. Could anyone have imagined such a thing? Their mutual affection was not surprising, but that matters should advance to this stage without a whisper of suspicion is truly baffling. I never saw them together, or I am certain I would have discovered it sooner.

Well, this secret was guarded jealously, presumably to avoid offending Mrs. Ferrars, and neither she, your brother, nor your sister suspected a word until this very morning. Poor Nancy, a well-meaning but not particularly astute individual, inadvertently revealed everything. Thinking, 'They are so fond of Lucy, surely they will accept her,' she ran straight to your sister. Little did she know that your sister, Eliza, was sitting alone at her carpet-work, only moments ago telling your brother she intended to match Edward with some Lord's daughter. Imagine the blow to her vanity and pride! She immediately fell into violent hysterics, screaming so loudly that your brother, who was downstairs composing a letter to his country steward, heard her. He rushed upstairs, and a terrible scene ensued. Lucy had arrived by then, unsuspecting of the turmoil, and I truly pity her, though I must say she was treated harshly. Your sister scolded like a fury, driving Lucy into a fainting fit. Nancy knelt and wept bitterly, while your brother paced the room in confusion.

Mrs. Dashwood declared they must leave immediately, forcing your brother to kneel and plead for permission to let them pack their belongings. Then, the hysterics resumed, and so terrified was he that he called for Dr. Donovan, finding the house in absolute uproar. The carriage stood ready, and as he arrived, Lucy was in such a fragile state she could scarcely walk, while Nancy was nearly equally ill. I confess, I have no patience with your sister's behavior, and I fervently hope the union will happen despite her objections. What a shock poor Mr. Edward will receive! To have his love so scornfully rejected! They say he adores her, as he well might. I daresay he will be in a state of great passion. Dr. Donovan shares my view. He has returned to Harley Street to await news, for Mrs. Ferrars was summoned immediately upon the departure of the cousins, and your sister was certain *she* would succumb to hysteria. Let her do so, for I care not. I have no pity for either of them. I cannot comprehend why people make such a fuss over money and social standing. There is no earthly reason why Mr. Edward and Lucy should not marry; I am sure Mrs. Ferrars can provide handsomely for her son. Though Lucy possesses little, she knows how to stretch resources to perfection. If Mrs. Ferrars granted him five hundred a year, she could maintain as respectable an appearance as anyone else with double that sum. Imagine how snug they might live in a cottage like yours, slightly larger, with two maids and two men; I believe I could even arrange for a housemaid from my Betty's sister, who would fit them perfectly."

Mrs. Jennings paused, and having allowed Elinor sufficient time to compose her thoughts, she responded with observations that appeared natural and impartial. Elinor was doubly relieved: first, that Mrs. Jennings no longer suspected her of any extraordinary attachment to Edward—a hope she had often nurtured lately—and second, that Marianne was absent, sparing her the immediate embarrassment of discussing the affair without her sister present.

Though she struggled to define her own expectations of the outcome, Elinor earnestly endeavored to dismiss the possibility of any result other than the marriage of Edward and Lucy. Curiosity burned within her regarding Mrs. Ferrars's reaction, but her anxiety centered on Edward's conduct. For him, she felt profound compassion; for Lucy, very little—which she found difficult to muster; for the remainder of the party, none at all.

Since Mrs. Jennings could converse on no other topic, Elinor quickly realized the necessity of preparing Marianne for the revelation. Time was of the essence to undeceive her, acquaint her with the truth, and train her to discuss the matter without betraying her own unease for her sister or resentment toward Edward.

Elinor's task was painful. She intended to remove what she believed to be Marianne's chief consolation, to disclose particulars of Edward that might permanently ruin his image in Marianne's eyes, and to force Marianne to relive her own disappointment through a striking resemblance of their situations. Despite the unwelcome nature of this duty, Elinor hastened to fulfill it.

She made no effort to dwell on her own feelings or feign excessive suffering, relying instead on the self-command she had practiced since learning of Edward's engagement. Her narration was clear and simple. Although delivered with emotion, it lacked violent agitation or impetuous grief. Such emotions belonged to the listener, for Marianne listened with horror and wept excessively. Elinor resolved to be the comforter of others in their distresses as much as in her own, offering reassurances of her composure and a vigorous vindication of Edward from every charge save imprudence.

Yet for some time, Marianne refused to accept either explanation. Edward seemed a second Willoughby. Acknowledging, as Elinor did, that she had loved him most sincerely, could Marianne feel less than herself? As for Lucy Steele, she viewed her as utterly unamiable and incapable of captivating a sensible man, rendering her unable to believe, let alone pardon, any former affection Edward might have held. She would not even admit it natural, and Elinor was left to convince her through a deeper knowledge of human nature.

Initially, Elinor's communication limited itself to stating the fact of the engagement and its duration. Marianne's emotions then overwhelmed the orderly delivery of details, shifting the focus to soothing her distress, lessening her alarms, and combating her resentment. The first question Marianne posed, prompting further particulars, was, "How long has this been known to you, Elinor? Has he written to you?"

"I have known it for four months," Elinor replied. "When Lucy first came to Barton Park last November, she confided her engagement to me."

At these words, Marianne's eyes expressed astonishment beyond what her lips could utter. After a pause of wonder, she exclaimed, "Four months! Have you known of this for four months?"

Elinor confirmed it.

"While attending me in all my misery, has this burden lain heavy on your heart? And I have reproached you for being happy!"

"It was not fitting that you should know then how much I was the reverse!"

"Four months!" cried Marianne again. "So calm! So cheerful! How have you been sustained?"

"By the conviction that I was fulfilling my duty. My promise to Lucy obligated me to secrecy. I owed it to her to avoid giving any hint of the truth, and I owed it to my family and friends not to create a solicitude about me that I could not satisfy."

Marianne seemed deeply struck.

"I have often wished to undeceive you and my mother," Elinor added. "Once or twice I attempted it; but without betraying my trust, I could never have convinced you."

"Four months! And yet you loved him!"
