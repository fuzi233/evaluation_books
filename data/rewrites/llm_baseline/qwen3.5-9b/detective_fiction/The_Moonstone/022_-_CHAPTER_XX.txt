Those ahead of us had already carried the news. By the time we arrived, the household was in a state of absolute panic. As we approached my mistress's door, it flew open violently from within. My lady emerged amidst the chaos, accompanied by Mr. Franklin, who strove in vain to compose her agitation. She was utterly beside herself, consumed by the sheer horror of the discovery.

"You are answerable for this!" she shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the Sergeant with wild intensity. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money—and release me from the sight of him!"

The Sergeant was the only soul among us capable of holding his ground, being the only one entirely composed. "I am no more responsible for this distressing calamity, my lady, than you are," he replied. His tone was respectful yet unyieldingly firm. "Should you still insist upon my departure from this house in half an hour, I shall accept your ladyship's dismissal, but certainly not your ladyship's money."

The words struck home, affecting both my mistress and myself. She allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside, closing the door behind them. Once they were gone, the Sergeant cast an observant glance around the group of female servants. While the others trembled in fear, he noted that Penelope was weeping openly.

"When your father has finished changing his wet clothes," the Sergeant instructed her gently, "come and speak to us in your father's room."

Before the half-hour elapsed, I had dressed in dry garments and provided the Sergeant with the change of attire he required. Penelope entered to hear what he sought. Never before had I felt so acutely the strength of my bond with my daughter. I drew her onto my lap, clasped her close, and prayed silently for her protection. She buried her face in my bosom, wrapping her arms tightly around my neck, and we remained in silence for a moment. In my heart, I believed the poor deceased girl must have been the catalyst for this tragedy, alongside my daughter and myself.

The Sergeant moved to the window, gazing outward. I felt compelled to express my gratitude for his consideration of our situation, and I did so. There is a peculiar luxury reserved for those of high station: the freedom to indulge their emotions. For those of lower standing, necessity offers no such reprieve. Hardship spares the privileged, yet shows no mercy to us. We are taught to suppress our feelings, to internalize them, and to endure our duties with patient stoicism. I make no complaint of this condition; I merely observe it.

Soon, both Penelope and I were prepared to receive the Sergeant. When questioned regarding what might have driven her fellow-servant to such a desperate act, my daughter answered—as one might expect—that it was due to her affection for Mr. Franklin Blake. Next, asked if she had shared this suspicion with anyone else, she replied, "I have not mentioned it, for Rosanna's sake."

I deemed it necessary to interject. "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear, as well," I added softly. "If Rosanna indeed perished because of her love for him, it was neither known nor caused by him. Should he depart the house today, let him do so without the unnecessary pain of bearing this truth."

Sergeant Cuff nodded approvingly, "Quite right," before falling silent again. To me, it appeared he was weighing Penelope's theory against some private hypothesis of his own, keeping the latter strictly to himself.

At the conclusion of the half-hour, my mistress's bell rang. En route to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin exiting his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was eager to confer with Sergeant Cuff, in my presence as previously arranged, though he wished to address the Sergeant privately first.

On our return journey to my chamber, he paused to examine the railway timetable hanging in the hall. "Are you truly intending to leave us, sir?" I asked. "Miss Rachel will surely recover if you simply grant her time?"

"She will recover," Mr. Franklin responded, "once she realizes I have departed and that she will never see me again."

I initially interpreted his words as resentment stemming from how my young lady had treated him. However, this was not the case. From the moment the police arrived, my mistress had observed that even the mere mention of his name ignited Miss Rachel's temper. Having loved his cousin too deeply to admit it to himself until reality forced the truth upon him, he had retreated to his aunt's residence. Now, with his eyes opened to the cruel reality—a transformation familiar to those who know him—Mr. Franklin had made his decision. It was the single resolution a man of spirit could possibly take: to leave the house.

What he intended to tell the Sergeant was spoken in my hearing. He conveyed that her ladyship was willing to acknowledge her previous haste. He then asked if the Sergeant would agree, under these circumstances, to accept his fee and withdraw from the matter of the Diamond as it currently stood.

"No, sir," the Sergeant replied. "My fee is compensation for performing my duty. I decline to accept payment until that duty is fulfilled."

"I do not understand you," Mr. Franklin said.

"I shall explain, sir," the Sergeant continued. "Upon arriving here, I undertook to shed necessary light on the disappearance of the Diamond. I am now ready and waiting to honor that pledge. Once I have presented the current state of affairs to Lady Verinder and clearly outlined the course of action required to recover the Moonstone, the burden of responsibility will shift to her shoulders. Only then, after she decides whether to permit me to proceed further, will I consider my task complete. At that point, I shall have done exactly what I promised, and I will claim my fee."

In those words, Sergeant Cuff reminded us that even within the Detective Police, a man possesses a reputation to safeguard. His stance was undeniably correct, leaving no room for further argument.

As I rose to escort him to my lady's room, he inquired if Mr. Franklin wished to remain. Mr. Franklin answered, "Unless Lady Verinder desires it." Then, lowering his voice so only I could hear, he whispered, "I know what that man intends to say about Rachel; and I am too fond of her to listen and maintain my composure. Leave me alone."

I departed, finding him leaning miserably against the window sill, his face concealed in his hands, while Penelope peered through the doorway, yearning to offer comfort. Had I been in his position, I would have summoned her immediately. When wronged by one woman, there is profound solace in confiding in another, for nine times out of ten, the listener will stand by your side. Perhaps, while my back was turned, he did call her in? If so, it is only fair to declare that my daughter would stop at nothing to console Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady's room. During our last consultation, we had found her reluctant to look up from the book resting on the table. This time, however, there was a marked improvement. She met the Sergeant's gaze with a steadiness equal to his own. A fierce family spirit illuminated every line of her face, and I knew that Sergeant Cuff would find a worthy adversary in a woman like my mistress, especially when she was primed to hear the worst he could possibly say.
