I had only been hacking away at the giants’ assigned labor for a handful of minutes when I heard high, tinkling voices drift through the trees, soft as wind chimes strung with pine resin. Moments later, the Little Ones—as they soon told me they named themselves—came creeping out of copses of knee-high miniature trees that sprung like brushwood between the colossal, gnarled old-growth trunks. Within a minute, scores of them swarmed around my boots, their hair the hue of moss and wildflower petals, no bigger than the length of my thumb.

I made urgent signs that the giants had only just left me, and could not be far off, but they laughed outright, telling me the wind was clean of their stench now. “They are too blind to see us,” they trilled, their laughter pealing like a hundred tiny sheep bells ringing all at once.

“D’you like that rope tied round your ankles?” one little boy asked, tilting his head up at me.
“I want them to think I can’t tear it loose,” I replied.
He cackled so hard he nearly toppled over. “They can scarcely see their own stumpy feet! Walk with tiny shuffling steps, and they’ll never notice the rope’s gone slack.”

The tallest of their girls, the quiet, sharp-eyed one who would soon reveal herself as their de facto matriarch, knelt down to the thick, hemp knot around my ankles. I smiled, assuming her dainty needle-thin fingers could never work the clumsy tangle—but it slipped free in two seconds flat. They bade me sit down on the moss, and fed me fruits so sweet they burst like honey on my tongue, tiny plums and wild berries no bigger than my fingernail. The smallest of them immediately began to play with me in the wildest, giddiest fashion, clambering over my lap, across my shoulders, tugging my hair and playing tag across my forearms, so that I could not possibly have returned to my chopping work if I tried. When the first set of children grew tired, another took their places, the game stretching on until the sun bled crimson over the treetops, and heavy, thudding steps shook the ground under my feet.

The Little Ones scattered in an instant, and I fumbled to re-wrap the loose rope around my ankles, retying it to look as if I had never touched it. “We have to be careful,” the girl who had freed me whispered, the last to linger. “One misstep from one of their horrid fat feet could crush a dozen of us before we even feel it!”
“Can they not perceive you at all?” I asked.
“They might catch a flicker of movement. If they’d found all of us piled on top of you ten minutes ago, they’d have stamped every last one flat. They hate every living thing that isn’t as big and dull as they are—half the time they’re not even properly alive, themselves!” She trilled a note sharp and bright as a wren’s call, and the next blink not a single Little One remained in sight, no sound, no trace, as if they had seeped straight into the moss.

My giant “master,” as he thought of himself, had arrived to drag me home. He wrenched my ankles hard to yank me across the dirt, threw me on the ground outside his hut’s door, retied my feet, gave me a brutal kick to the ribs, and trundled back inside. I could have slipped the rope the second he left, run for the woods and escaped for good—but I did not. I had friends now, bright and winsome and full of such soft, glowing joy that I could not bear to leave them behind, not until I knew every secret they held. “Tomorrow,” I thought, giddy in the dark, “I will see them again.” And from the moment the last snore rumbled from the huts until I fell asleep, I heard their tiny whispers brushing my cheeks, tucking stray leaves out of my hair, watching over me. They never left me entirely alone after that, not for an hour.

I never came to know the giants at all. There was scarcely anything to know. They never grew the least bit friendly, but they were far too stupid to invent new cruelties: most of the time, when they went to kick me, I just caught their calloused foot, twisted it, sent them sprawling in the dirt, and they never renewed their attempt that same day. But the Little Ones? Every day they said or did something that made my chest feel light, something that surprised me. I grew more and more loath to leave them. While I worked, they came and went in constant little bursts, amusing me, chasing away every scrap of the misery and most of the weariness of my monotonous toil. Very soon I loved them more than I could ever put into words. They did not know the book-learning of my own world, but they were wise in the way old trees and hidden streams are wise, and seemed capable of learning anything they set their minds to. I had no bed but bare dirt, but almost every morning I woke curled in a nest of children: one tucked in the crook of my arm, one resting on my chest, the smallest snuggled against my neck, trading turns through the night so no one got left out. I never suffered from the night’s bitter cold, not even a little.

The first thing they did at dawn, and the last before sunset, was bring me more of their delicious wild fruit. One morning, I woke to find myself entirely alone. As my senses cleared, I heard soft, muffled sounds of excitement, and the tall, grave girl their whole community called their mother stepped out from the wood, followed by a whole throng of the Little Ones, all glowing with silent jubilation so they would not wake the giant dozing ten feet from where I lay. She carried a boy-baby swaddled in dandelion down in her arms, the youngest they had ever found. Lona—for that was her name—laid the tiny baby in my arms. He blinked one eye open to stare at me, then closed them again and fell fast asleep.

“He loves you already!” Lona said, her eyes sparkling.
“Where did you find him?” I asked.
“Out in the wood, of course,” she laughed. “That’s where we always find them. Isn’t he a beauty? We’ve been out all night looking for him. Sometimes it takes hours to track them down!”
“How do you know there’s a new baby to find in the first place?”
“I can’t tell,” she said. “Everyone rushes to tell everyone else, but no one ever remembers who said it first. Sometimes I think someone murmurs it in their sleep, and another hears it half-awake. When there’s a baby waiting in the wood, no one stops to ask questions—by the time we think to wonder, we’re already running to find it.”

The more questions I asked, the more odd, gentle truths I pried loose. They did not count their numbers, she told me, it would not “feel smooth,” they would not want to know the exact tally of their small, soft community. Babies never came from anywhere but the wood, fully finished, no messy unspoken bits no one could explain. She squeezed a drop of plum juice to the baby’s lips, and he sucked it down without ever waking, and she glowed with such gentle pride she looked like a tiny sun herself.

But there was one secret they hated to speak of. Some of the Little Ones, Lona said, after they thought they had finished growing, began to get bigger anyway. It was a frightful thing. It meant they were turning into giants. It happened when a Little One stopped caring about sharing, when they got greedy, then lazy, then dull, then cruel. They ate the big sour apples the giants cultivated, the fruit no Little One was ever supposed to touch, and they grew faster every day, until they forgot all the songs, forgot all their friends, forgot how to laugh like sheep bells. They woke one morning as full giants, and never remembered they had ever been small. Blunty, the moody little boy I had seen hoarding an apple nearly as big as his own head, was the latest one. It was already too late for him, Lona said, they had tried everything, but he would not listen.

A few days later, word spread that Blunty had vanished. There were a few grave faces among the older Little Ones, but no one mourned him for long. The next dawn, Lona crept to my side and whispered, “Look—by that quince tree. That’s the giant that was Blunty. Would you have known him?”
“Never,” I said, staring at the lumbering, blank-faced man shoveling apples into his mouth. “But now you say it, I can almost see the little boy through a fog. He looks so stupid.”
“That’s what happens when Little Ones refuse to stay little,” she sighed.

A cold, horrid realization settled over me then. Back in my own world, we called that growing up. We called chasing more bigness, more things, more weight we hoarded for ourselves—wealth—success. I had spent my whole life chasing that sort of bigness, never noticing I was slowly turning myself into a giant, too. I had been given the chance to stay small, stay soft, learn to laugh like the Little Ones forever, and I had almost thrown it all away. Blunty did not even know he had lost something—but I had just begun to understand mine.
