The June sun beat down on the square, turning the red bricks of the old house into a furnace of light. A crowd had gathered, drawn by something unseen but felt—like a breath of cold air in the heat. They stood close together, faces turned upward toward the windows of the house, its walls scarred with faded insurance marks and its paling lined with wooden "To Let" signs that hung like gallows warnings.

Two constables stood at the broken gate of the narrow alley leading to the house, arms crossed, blocking the way. The women lingered at the edges of the throng, whispering as they shifted their positions, trying to catch sight of the red blinds drawn tight across the upper floor. The children were inside, behind locked doors, safe from whatever this was.

A long-nosed man with a face like dried leather had gathered a small group around him. He repeated his story again and again, each time with more conviction: “I was standing there—where Constable Saunders is now—or rather, I was passing about my business—when they came out. There was no deceiving me. Oh, no deceiving *me*! I saw her face…”

“What was it like, Mr. Barrett?” someone asked.

“Like hers whom our Lord said, ‘Woman, doth any man accuse thee?’—white as paper, and no mistake! Don’t tell *me*!” He paused, letting the silence stretch. “So I walked straight across to Mrs. Barrett and said, ‘Jane, this must stop, and stop at once. We are commanded to avoid evil.’ And she said—‘John,’ she said, ‘it’s four-and-sixpence a week’—that was her exact words.”

“Then I said, ‘Jane, if it was forty-six thousand pounds, it should stop!’” He clenched his jaw. “And from that day to this, she hasn’t set foot inside that gate.”

A hush fell over the listeners. Then, from the back, a voice whispered: “Did Mrs. Barrett ever… *see* anything?”

Barrett turned sharply. “What Mrs. Barrett saw—and what she did not—shall not pass these lips. Even as it is written: keep thy tongue from speaking evil.”

Another man stepped forward. “He was pretty near canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, wasn’t he, Jim?”

“Yes,” replied the second man, “he hadn’t half copped it. Not standing treat much, neither. He was in the bar, all on his own.”

“So he was,” nodded the first. “We talked about it.”

But the fat man with wide, frightened eyes tried again. “She got the key off me—she had the number—came into my shop Tuesday evening…”

No one listened. A heavy labourer grunted, “Shut your heads. She hasn’t been found yet. Here come the inspectors. We’ll know more soon.”

Two inspectors arrived, their boots crunching on gravel as they approached the gate. The fat man rushed forward, breathless. “She bought the key from me! I remember the number—three ones and three threes—11133!”

An inspector waved him aside. “Nobody’s been in?” he asked the constable.

“No, sir.”

“Then you, Brackley—come with us. Smith, keep the gate. A squad’s on its way.”

They entered the alley, passed through the broken gate, and climbed the wide, carved staircase. One inspector kicked aside a pile of dead leaves and crumpled paper outside a door marked with no name. “This don’t look like he’s been out much lately,” he muttered.

“I don’t think we need knock,” said the other. “Break a pane, Brackley.”

The door had two glazed panels. Glass shattered. Brackley shoved his elbow through the hole, reached for the latch, and pulled.

“Faugh!” choked one of the inspectors as they stepped inside. “Let some light and air in, quick—it stinks like a hearse.”

Outside, the crowd watched as the red blinds were raised and the windows flung open. Sunlight poured in, blinding the figure on the bed.

“This is better,” said the inspector, leaning out. “That seems to be the bedroom—Simms, go in while I check the rest.”

The blind had been drawn up. On the bed lay a man—waxy-white, hollow-eyed, emaciated. He held his hands up like shutters against the glare. His mind reeled. Two policemen bent over him, asking where “she” was.

“She?” he croaked. “Who?”

“This woman Bengough—goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough. Where is she?”

He shook his head. “I don’t know… I don’t know…”

“No good, Brackley,” said the inspector. “Get him up. Careful with him. I’ll just stick my head out.”

The other inspector searched the study—empty. Then the kitchen. Dead flowers covered the floor, ankle-deep. He kicked them aside, and noticed tracks—shuffling, deliberate—leading to a cupboard in the corner.

The cupboard door had a small square panel, sliding on runners. The inspector reached for the knob and slid it open.

He froze.

Framed in the aperture, slightly tilted forward before jamming, was something wrapped in faded brownish-red frieze—a lumpy mass like a pudding, but heavier, darker. It pulsed faintly.

“Ah,” he breathed.

He didn’t want to touch it. To close the hatch, he’d have to push it back with his hand. Instead, he turned the handle of the cupboard itself.

It resisted. He pulled. It opened only a few inches. He peered inside—then pushed with his shoulder to close it.

As he did, a triangle of black-and-white check skirt protruded from beneath the door, sticking out just above the floor.

He went into the hall. “All right!” he called.

Oleron had been dressed. He still shielded his eyes, confused. The mess—the dead flowers everywhere—the police—the stretcher being fetched—he couldn’t make sense of it. Why was there so much decay? Why was he being moved?

“What’s the matter?” he murmured.

Then he heard the murmur from the square. A four-wheeler stopped outside. An officer leaned in. Oleron whispered something, and the man snapped back a string of words—something about evidence.

They lifted him to his feet. He stumbled, dazed. Down the stairs. Through the alley. The crowd roared—angry voices, shouts, demands for justice. Someone wanted to lynch someone.

Oleron’s attention caught on the fat man again—talking, trembling, telling his story into a notebook.

“I’d seen her with him… they were often together… she came into my shop… said it was for him… I thought it was all right… 111333…”

The people were furious. Police formed a line. But one inspector’s voice cut through—calm, almost gentle. He told someone to get another person into the cab before something else was brought out.

Oleron saw the cab. He saw the hooded stretcher behind it.

They lifted him in. The inspector stood between him and the stretcher—too late. Oleron saw it.

The door remained open. He glimpsed the “To Let” boards among the privet trees. One read: *Key at Number Six.*

Then—silence.

Shuffling footsteps echoed down the alley. The second inspector appeared at the cab door.

“Right away,” he said to the driver.

He climbed in, closed the door, and blocked the window with his back.

Between the two inspectors, Oleron slept.

The cab rolled out of the square. The other vehicle climbed the hill.

The mortuary lay that way.
