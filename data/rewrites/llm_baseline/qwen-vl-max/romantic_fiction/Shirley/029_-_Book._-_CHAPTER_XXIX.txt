Louis Moore was accustomed to quiet—quiet in thought, in habit, in the very rhythm of his days. He was not one to seek noise or company; rather, he found comfort in stillness, as though silence were a companion he could trust. And yet, tonight, solitude felt like a weight upon his chest. The house at Fieldhead lay hushed, all but him. Miss Keeldar, the Sympsons, even Henry—all had gone to Nunnely, summoned by Sir Philip’s kind invitation to meet his mother and sisters. The baronet had extended the same courtesy to the tutor, but Moore would have preferred meeting ghosts beneath the ancient oaks of Nunnely Forest than facing that gathering. A phantom abbess? A spectral nun wandering among the ruins? That would have been preferable. But not this—this polite, expectant world of manners and duty.

And so, he was left alone.

The evening was restless. Though the storm had passed, the sky still stirred with the breath of the equinox. The clouds broke apart slowly, revealing a moon that rode high and bold, unafraid of the wind that howled through the trees. It was a moon that loved the tempest, gliding through the gale as if it welcomed the touch of the wild air. No shepherd watched from the hills tonight, no Endymion waited for his goddess. There was no audience for such beauty—only Moore, standing in the schoolroom, listening to the wind lash the gables, feeling the loneliness press in.

“All the parlours are empty,” he murmured. “I am sick at heart of this cell.”

He rose and walked away from the sheltered end of the house, drawn toward the larger windows where the night stretched wide and clear. No candle was needed—the moon poured silver light across every surface, painting shadows and lifting corners into soft brilliance. He moved from room to room, as if following an unseen presence. Then he stopped—before the oak chamber.

It was warm here. The fire burned low but steady, casting flickering gold on the walls. Near the hearth stood a small worktable, a chair beside it, a desk open, keys still in the lock. On the table: a delicate glove, a silver pen, a seal, a few ripe berries resting on a green leaf, and a tiny satin bag hanging from the back of the chair.

Moore paused. His breath caught.

She had been here. Not long ago. In haste, perhaps. Forgotten things—trifles, yes—but each one spoke of her. Careless, yet refined. Absent-minded, yet full of grace. Her mark was everywhere. And in that moment, Moore did not feel alone.

“She has been here,” he whispered. “Careless, lovely thing. Left behind a little piece of herself. Why does she leave such traces? How can she be so heedless and never seem wrong? There is always something to chide in her—and yet, when I think of reprimanding her, the words turn into a sigh, and the reprimand melts into a wish to hold her close. Better spend half an hour scolding her than a day admiring any other woman alive.”

He hesitated. Was he speaking aloud? He looked around. Yes—he was alone. Yet, he knew better than to speak too freely. So he stopped.

Then he made himself comfortable. He drew the curtains shut, blocking out the moon and its court of stars. He stoked the fire, lit a candle, placed another chair opposite the one she had sat in. And then he sat down.

From his pocket, he took a small, thick book of blank paper and a pencil. He began to write—not for anyone else, but for himself, for the truth he could not say aloud.

“It is nine o'clock. The carriage will not return before eleven. I am free until then. Free to sit in her room, to rest my elbow on her table, to breathe in the air she breathed.

I once liked solitude. I thought of it as a quiet nymph, a mountain spirit descending to me in mist and chill. I used to find peace in her silence. But since that evening—since she came to me in the schoolroom, opened her heart, asked for help, leaned on me—I have come to hate solitude. It is cold. It is dead. It is the sister of death.

Now, I find joy in writing about what is near—what is real. This book is mine. No one can take it from me. Through these words, I can say what I dare not speak, what I cannot even think without trembling.

We have scarcely met since that night. Once, I was searching for a book in the drawing-room when she entered, dressed for a concert. She saw me, nodded slightly, and passed to the window. Her shyness—oh, her shyness!—it was not awkwardness, but a kind of sacred reserve. I have heard much about maiden modesty, but never truly understood it until now. When she turned away, her cheek flushed, I called her nothing but ‘stainless virgin’ in my mind. There was a glow about her—a quiet radiance. And I, plain and dull as I am, felt myself lifted by her presence.

I know I sound foolish. Dreaming. Romantic. But what can I do? She has awakened something in me—something I never knew existed. I see her now—looking up at me, pleading, almost desperate, asking me not to let them smother her, begging for a strong narcotic. I see the tear fall silently from her eyelash. I see her confessing that she is not as strong as people believe. And I remember thinking—yes, I thought it—she is childish. But I did not despise her. Oh, no. I felt something deeper—something tender. I felt the right to protect her, to stand beside her, to hold her when she trembles.

I worship her perfections. But it is her flaws—her little weaknesses—that draw me closer. They are the steps I climb to reach her. If she were perfect, polished, untouched by human frailty, I would not know how to love her. It is the uneven ground—the mossy slope, the hollows—that invites ascent. It is the natural hill, not the artificial mound.

Let me be honest. I delight in seeing her. I would recognize her anywhere. If I were a king and she a servant sweeping my stairs, I would still see her worth. If I were a gentleman and she served me at my table, I could not help liking her. Take away her dress, her education, her wealth—leave only her form, her smile, her warmth—and I would still want to stay. I would linger. I would regret leaving.

How careless she is—leaving her desk open, her keys hanging in the lock, a purse spilling from a satin bag. Robert would be furious. He would call her reckless. But I—my irritation is sweet. I enjoy finding fault with her. I would welcome her imperfections if she lived under my roof. She would give me reasons to lecture her—just as I sometimes do with Henry, though with him, a word suffices. With her… I would talk. I would argue. I would scold—yet never truly mean it.

When her eyes meet mine, when her mood turns teasing, mischievous—then I am most drawn to her. She is never more beautiful than when she returns from a ride, wind-tousled, fiery-eyed. I have waited in the courtyard, just to see her dismount. To catch her in my arms. And I have noticed—only to this page, never to another—that she allows no man but me to help her from the saddle. Even Sir Philip, whom she treats with gentle kindness, she politely declines. Sam Wynne, too—she refuses him with quiet dignity.

And I know—my heart knows—it is not reluctance. It is choice. She gives herself to me willingly. And I, I am not her slave. But my thoughts, my strength, my calm—they gather around her like genii drawn to a lamp. All my faculties wait for her command. And when she speaks, they leap to serve.

She is careless—but never vulgar. Her carelessness reveals refinement, not disorder. I have seen her belongings scattered—her gloves, her pens, her bags—and never found anything sordid. Everything bears her elegance. Look at this glove—pure kid, unsullied. The satin bag—fresh, clean. She is careless in manner, but meticulous in soul.

There is a difference between her and Caroline. Caroline is precise, exact, faultless. She would suit Robert—perfectly. But what could I do with someone so flawless? She is like a lily—pure, innocent, needing no change. But I—my heart desires the rose. Sweet, yes—but guarded by thorns. My future wife must stir me, challenge me, awaken my patience. I was not born to live with a lamb. I would rather tend a young lioness.

I like sweetness that is also sharp. Light that burns. Beauty that fights back when teased. Fascination that threatens to become fierce. I fear I would tire of innocence that never stirs. But I would rejoice in taming a wild spirit—in guiding a restless merlin.

O Shirley! O Peri! Too rebellious for heaven, too pure for hell. I shall never possess you. I can only watch, adore, and desire.

Yet I know—I know—I could make you happy. Will it be my fate to see you given to one who cannot?

Beware, Sir Philip Nunnely. I have seen you at her side. I have seen her lips tighten, her brow furrow, her body recoil slightly—as if enduring something she does9t admire, something she tolerates only because you are good in some other way. I have seen her face grow grave, her eyes flash with quiet resistance. And I think of Semele—but reversed.

Not the daughter of Cadmus longing to see Zeus in glory. But a priest of Juno, watching alone in the temple. For years, he has dreamed of her. He loves the idol. He prays for her favor. And she hears. She will be kind.

All Argos sleeps. The temple doors are closed. The priest waits at the altar.

Then—a shock. Not felt by the city, but by him. Silence. Then—light. From the heavens, through the shattered roof, pours a blaze. Stars fall. A god descends.

He has what he asked for. But he cannot look. He is blinded. He hears a sound—unbearable. He sees a glory—too terrible. Gods, have mercy!

In the morning, a pious man enters to offer prayer. Thunder struck last night. The shrine is broken. The marble split. Saturnia’s statue stands unharmed. At her feet—ashes. No priest remains. The watcher is gone.

* * *

There—there is the carriage!

I must lock the desk. Pocket the keys. She will come looking for them tomorrow. She will have to come to me.

I hear her voice—clear, hesitant, ashamed—‘Mr. Moore, have you seen my keys?’

Yes. That is how she will ask. Twenty times already. And I will keep her waiting. I will tease her. I will make her beg. And when I return them—only after a lecture. The bag. The purse. The glove. The pen. The seal. Each one, slowly, with confession, with entreaty.

I will make every touch a privilege. Every glance, every smile, every movement—mine to cherish. If I must be her slave, I will not lose my freedom. I will claim every moment, every detail, every breath she takes—for myself.”

He locked the desk. He gathered the items. He tucked them into his coat. Then he left the room, closing the door softly behind him, as if he had never been there at all.
