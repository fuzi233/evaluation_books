Lona and I kept the lower path where the grass still held the river’s breath, and the Little Ones had gone up into the green rafters of the taller trees as if the boughs were ladders made for their feet. For a while there was only the steady hush of water and leaf—sound so clean it seemed to rinse the inside of the ear—until a shout broke overhead, not harsh but round and young, like a call across a farmyard at supper-time.

They began dropping out of the foliage, one after another, as if the branches were shaking them loose with laughter. Their cheeks were bright with wind and sap-smell.

“We saw it!” they cried. “We climbed the tallest tree—taller than all—and far across the plain, on the side of the lonely mountain, there’s a curious something!”

“The mountain rises and rises,” one added, squinting as though the sky were still in his eyes, “until the sky gathers thick to keep it down, and knocks its top off!”

“It may be a city,” said another, “but it’s not at all like Bulika.”

The word “city” came to me with a taste, as if I had bitten an old name and found it had kept its spice. I went up to see for myself. From that height the plain spread like a table laid in pale greens and browns, and beyond it the solitary mountain lifted its shoulder into a blue that did not know where rock ended and air began. Upon its flank, climbing into the clouds, stood indeed a city—ascending, dissolving, reappearing—so that mountain and sky and palace and precipice mingled in broken shine and shadow, as if the place were being continually built by weather.

I came down. The Little Ones were already on the move, and Lona and I with them. They sped as those do who have never learned to look back for loss; their merriment ran ahead like a bright instrument in an orchestra, and we followed the tune of their feet.

The river, too, grew lovelier as we went, until I understood with a kind of shameful wonder that I had never seen real water before. What I had called water in my own world had been only its likeness—useful, measured, often bruised by pipes and stones and noise. This was water that held silence inside it. It carried the sound of itself—lap, glide, lift—not a clatter, not a harshness. Even its scent had a body: cold sweetness, green-rooted, with the faintest mineral edge as of clean flint struck in the dark.

By and by we could see the city from the plain, lodged among blue clouds. But other clouds had begun to gather around a lofty tower—or was it a rock?—that stood above the city nearer the crest. They were gray, then dark gray, then purple, writhing in cross-grained motions, tossing up a vaporous foam; and in places the cloud thickened into whorls that spun like water in a basin.

Then a flash broke from it—dazzling, swift—and for one instant it seemed to play about the Little Ones in front of us like a blessing that could not keep still. After it came a blinding darkness, but no thunder—no brutal clap such as shakes a city’s metal roofs. Only a deepened hush, as if the air listened.

In that hush I heard the children, their voices lowered with delight, like people speaking in a kitchen when something rare is on the table and no one wants to frighten the fragrance away.

“Did you see?”

“I saw.”

“What did you see?”

“The beautifullest man.”

“I heard him speak!”

“I didn’t—what did he say?”

And Luva, the smallest voice, baby-rough with joy, answered as one repeating a phrase in a home-dialect that holds more than the dictionary can carry:

“He said, ‘’Ou’s all mine’s, ‘ickle ones: come along!’”

I had seen the lightning; I had heard no words. Lona, walking close beside me, had seen and heard with them, as if her soul were tuned to that register of sound where meaning rides the breath. It struck me—sharp as the scent of crushed leaf—that what is spoken may be the same and yet not the same, depending on whose ear receives it; and that the Little Ones’ clipped, dear grammar held a truth no polished sentence could improve. Their speech was not broken; it was rooted.

A second flash came, and my eyes, though not my ears, were opened in a way I cannot translate without loss. The great quivering light was compact of angel-faces—faces within faces, each briefly distinct as a flame seen through rain—lighting themselves visible and vanishing as if their very being were a kind of music too fine to stay.

A third flash came; its substance and radiance were human.

“I see my mother!” I cried, and the cry rose out of me like a hunger suddenly given its name.

“I see lots o’ mothers!” said Luva, as if mothers were a plentiful harvest here, and grief had been only a misunderstanding of the seasons.

Once more the cloud flashed, and in that single bright draught all kinds of creatures were there: horses and elephants, lions and dogs—beasts more themselves than any beast I had known; and birds—oh, such birds! Great ones whose wings gleamed singly every colour gathered in sunset or rainbow; little ones whose feathers sparked as if the earth’s hoarded stones had taken flight—silvery cranes, red flamingoes, opal pigeons, peacocks gorgeous in gold and green and blue, jewelled humming-birds. There were great-winged butterflies; and lithe, volumed creeping things, bright as wet skins under starlight, all together in one heavenly flash.

“I see that serpents grow birds here,” said Lona, “as caterpillars used to grow butterflies!”

“I saw my white pony,” I said—my throat thick with the remembered warmth of its neck under my palm—“the pony that died when I was a child. I needn’t have been so sorry; I should just have waited!”

Still no thunder. Instead there came a sweet rain, and the whole air changed its taste: coolness that caressed, a freshness that soothed the heated places of the mind. We breathed deep. Each breath felt like drinking without swallowing. The drops fell thickening, and as they fell they flashed colours as if every gem of the earth had wakened and turned its face upward. A mighty rainbow sprang from the rain and spanned the city.

The blue clouds gathered thicker; the rain fell in torrents; the children exulted and ran until it was all we could do to keep them in sight. Yet even in that rush the soundscape stayed gentle—no harsh drumming, only the soft multitudinous tapping of water on leaf and skin, the river’s larger voice running beneath like a bass line. It made me think of my own world’s incessant mechanical roar—wheels and engines and hollow music sold too loudly—and I felt, with sudden anger and sorrow, how noise can steal a people’s inward language, until even their prayers come out in borrowed tones.

With silent, radiant roll, the river swept onward, filling to the margin its smooth, yielding channel. It did not scrape over rock or shingle or sand; it flowed over grass, and in the grass grew primroses and daisies, crocuses and narcissi, pimpernels and anemones—a starry multitude, large and bright under the brilliant water. The water did not cloud with the rain; it took no yellow, no brown, no muddied memory. It shone with a pale berylline gleam that rose from its deep, dainty bed; and the scent of it was like sliced cucumber and cold stone and something faintly floral, as if the flowers beneath were steeping the current like herbs in a clear broth.

As we drew nearer to the mountain, we saw the river came from its very peak and rushed in full volume through the main street of the city. It descended to the gate by a stair of deep and wide steps, mingled of porphyry and serpentine, which continued down to the foot of the mountain. There, on both banks, shallower steps rose to the gate and along the ascending street.

Without the briefest halt the Little Ones ran straight up the stair to the open gate. Outside, on the landing, sat the portress—a woman-angel of dark visage—leaning her shadowed brow on her idle hand, as if she had been listening for a sound that had not yet arrived. The children rushed upon her and covered her with caresses; before she could gather herself to understand, they had taken heaven by surprise and were already in the city, still mounting beside the descending torrent.

A great angel, attended by a company of shining ones, came down to meet and receive them. But the Little Ones, merry as sparks, evaded them all and ran on. Then a group of woman-angels descended in a kind of dance—movement so ordered it made no noise at all—and in a moment the children were fettered in heavenly arms, laughing as if capture were merely another game with a sweeter end. The radiants carried them away, and I saw them no more.

“Ah!” said the mighty angel, continuing his descent to meet us, who were now almost at the gate and within hearing. “This is well! These are soldiers to take heaven itself by storm! I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers: these will make short work with such!”

Then he saw the horse and the elephants clambering up behind us, their hides wet with rain, their breath steaming faintly in the cool air.

“Take those animals to the royal stables,” he added; “there tend them; then turn them into the king’s forest.”

He turned to us and bent low with the sweetest smile.

“Welcome home,” he said.

The words struck like bread placed warm into the hands—simple, sustaining, impossible to argue with. Immediately he turned again and led the way higher. The scales of his armour flashed like flakes of lightning, but without glare; their brightness belonged to the air as naturally as the river’s voice. Thought cannot form itself to tell what I felt, thus received by the officers of heaven. It was as if every hunger I had carried unnamed—every appetite of heart and sense I had dulled with habit—had been recognized and answered.

We stood for a moment at the gate whence issued roaring the radiant river. I know not whence came the stones that fashioned it, but among them I saw the prototypes of all the gems I had loved on earth—far more beautiful than those, for these were living stones. In them I saw not the intent alone, but the intender too; not the idea alone, but the imbodier present—the operant outsender. Nothing in this kingdom was dead; nothing was mere; nothing only a thing.

We went up through the city and passed out. There was no wall on the upper side, but a huge pile of broken rocks, upsloping like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Through openings between the rocks the river came billowing out, as if the mountain itself were a great vessel forever being filled and poured. On the top I could dimly discern what seemed three or four great steps of a stair, disappearing in a cloud white as snow; and above the steps I saw—only with my mind’s eye, as one tastes a remembered dish without having it on the tongue—as it were a grand old chair: the throne of the Ancient of Days.

Over and under and between those steps issued, plenteously, unceasingly new-born, the river of the water of life.

The great angel could guide us no farther.

“Those rocks,” he seemed to say without saying, “you must ascend alone.”

My heart beat hard with hope and desire. I held faster the hand of my Lona, and we began to climb; but soon we let each other go, to use hands as well as feet in the toilsome ascent of the huge stones. The rock was cold and wet, smelling of clean iron and rain, and our breathing made a small honest noise in the folds of that immense quiet.

At length we drew near the cloud, which hung down the steps like the borders of a garment. We passed through the fringe and entered the deep folds, and everything went white and muffled, as if sound itself had been wrapped in wool.

Then a hand—warm and strong—laid hold of mine. It drew me to a little door with a golden lock. The door opened. The hand let mine go and pushed me gently through.

I turned quickly.

The board of a large book was in the act of closing behind me.

I stood alone in my library.
