Colonel Henry Esmond, a veteran who had worn the uniform of a regiment under Queen Anne, found himself, at the close of her reign, entangled in the turbulent schemes for restoring the Stuart line to the throne. While the nation wisely preferred the House of Hanover, a handful of loyalists fled across the seas. Among them was Colonel Esmond, advised by friends to seek exile abroad. Though he deeply regretted his political involvement, and though the new monarch, Prince George, proved a sovereign of remarkable patience, Esmond’s friends eventually brokered a reconciliation, allowing him to return to a quieter life.

It is recorded that Esmond hailed from the ancient English line of Castlewood, in Hampshire. Rumor had it that King James II and his son had once offered him the title of Marquis, along with an Irish peerage inherited by his family, but Esmond, weary of court intrigues and domestic strife, had declined to regularize the matter through formal channels. Instead, tired of the European struggle, he turned his gaze to Virginia, where he held a substantial estate originally granted by King Charles I to his ancestor. There, amidst the rolling fields of the New World, his daughter was born, followed by twin grandsons, while his wife passed away.

She had been the widow of the Colonel’s cousin, the unfortunate Viscount Castlewood, who met his end in a duel with Lord Mohun at the conclusion of King William's reign. Esmond named his American home "Castlewood," modeling every aspect of its life upon the customs of his homeland. It was a fiercely loyal colony, where the Virginians proudly claimed that King Charles II had ruled over their soil before he ever ascended the throne in England. Both the English Crown and the Church were revered with singular devotion. The local gentry, allied with the best bloodlines of Albion, stood head and shoulders above the Dutch merchants of New York and the Puritan traders of Pennsylvania. Indeed, the spirit of republicanism was nearly absent in this great province, which would later lead the charge against the British Crown.

Life on a Virginia plantation was conducted with a patriarchal grandeur. Each estate commanded a multitude of hands—purchased and indentured servants—who lived under the absolute sway of their masters. The land provided food, livestock, and game in abundance; the great rivers teemed with fish, offering a clear path home. Ships loaded tobacco from private wharves on the Potomac and James Rivers, carrying it to London or Bristol in exchange for manufactured goods. Hospitality was boundless; no stranger was ever turned away. The gentry traveled and received one another in a manner that bordered on the feudal.

Yet, the institution of slavery, though not yet fully articulated as a moral crisis, was accepted without hesitation. To own black servants caused no shock among the Virginian gentlemen, nor was the dominion they exercised typically described as savage. Provisions were plentiful, and the enslaved population, largely indolent yet content, rarely suffered. One might have preached abolition to Madam Esmond of Castlewood with the same futility as telling her to release her horses from their stalls; she viewed the lash and the corn-bag as necessities for both beasts and men.

Her father, however, possessed a skeptical mind. While he harbored doubts, he never voiced them in open defiance, appearing more disaffected than rebellious. Once, in his youth, he had sought glory in public life, but in later years, he seemed indifferent to its rewards. Some unseen tragedy had left a permanent melancholy shadow over his soul. He remained kind and affectionate to those around him, obsequious toward the women of his family, rarely contradicting them, yet his heart appeared bankrupt, and he never truly enjoyed living. He merely submitted to life, waiting for its final curtain.

Upon his wife's death, his daughter assumed control of the Colonel's affairs, to which he readily surrendered his books and peace. When guests arrived, he entertained them with cheerful sarcasm, deriving genuine pleasure from their eventual departure. "My love, I shall not be sorry to go myself," he once remarked to his daughter, "and you, though the most affectionate of daughters, will console yourself after a while. Why should I, who am so old, be romantic? You may, who are still a young creature." He spoke thus, knowing full well that his daughter, a pragmatic and matter-of-fact soul, possessed little capacity for romance.

Fifteen years after settling in Virginia, the estate prospered so greatly that the Colonel acquiesced to his daughter's ambitious plan to replace his plain wooden dwelling with a mansion of enduring grandeur, fitting for the nobility of their name. While neighbors like the Warringtons erected stately homes fueled by ambition, the Colonel himself cared little for architecture or lineage. Yet, his daughter, possessing a towering sense of her ancestry, pressed forward. Her father, now serene and good-natured in his twilight years, amused himself by accommodating her peculiarities. With his extensive antiquarian knowledge and skill as a painter, he assisted her in constructing a genealogical tree. Having studied extensively in Europe, Miss Esmond returned with volumes on heraldry and documents proving the Esmonds' descent from Norman warriors and native kings. Her father painted two magnificent trees: one tracing the line from Emperor Charlemagne, depicted in plate armor with an imperial diadem, and the other from Queen Boudicca, portrayed in light drapery with a golden crown and fur mantle, her skin beautifully tattooed with blue designs. From these illustrious roots, the family tree bloomed until it converged in the thirteenth century upon a fortunate ancestor who claimed both lines.

Regarding the Warrington family into which she had married, the virtuous Madam Rachel paid scant attention. She signed her letters "Esmond-Warrington" but was universally known as Madam Esmond of Castlewood. After her father's passing, she ruled the domain, though her temper was occasionally soured by disputes over precedence within colonial society. Although her father had burned a Marquis's patent from King James—which she disowned—she often acted as if it remained valid, considering the English Esmonds inferior to her own branch and asserting her supremacy over the entire colonial aristocracy. Consequently, her notes record frequent quarrels, angry words, and occasional scuffles at Governor's assemblies in Jamestown. Yet, why recall these petty squabbles? The participants are gone, and the Republic has since dismantled these social hierarchies. Before independence, Virginia was perhaps the most aristocratic region on earth, and its inhabitants, including the young twins of Castlewood, were bred to hold the institutions of home in the highest reverence.

When the boys' grandfather died, their mother proclaimed her eldest son, George, the sole heir, instructing Harry, his younger brother by half an hour, to show him respect. The entire household, from the large and happy family of negroes to the indentured servants from Europe, was taught to honor George. Only two figures remained defiant: Madam Mountain, a faithful friend to the mistress, and Harry's foster-mother, a devoted black woman who could not comprehend why her child, whom she deemed handsomer, stronger, and cleverer, should not be placed first despite the twins' physical similarity.

In temperament, however, the brothers were stark opposites. George was peaceful, studious, and silent; Harry was warlike and noisy. Harry learned quickly once started but struggled to begin tasks, whereas George aided his brother willingly. Harry possessed a strong military bent, drilling the little negroes on the estate and cane-handling them like a corporal, engaging in boxing matches without holding grudges. George, conversely, was gentle and sparing of blows. In accordance with custom, each boy had a personal attendant. It became notorious that George, finding his small black servant asleep on his bed, sat beside him to brush flies away with a feather fan. This act horrified Old Gumbo, the child's father, and enraged Madam Esmond, who ordered the boy to the overseer for a whipping.

George pleaded passionately, bursting into tears and begging for mercy. Despite his entreaties, his mother remained unmoved regarding the punishment. The little negro departed, imploring his young master not to weep. A fierce conflict erupted between mother and son. Refusing to be pacified, the boy declared the punishment a shameful affront, insisting that as master, he alone had authority over his servant, and no one—not even his mother—could lay a hand upon him. He vowed that upon reaching maturity, he would free young Gumbo. He visited the slave quarters and presented the boy with one of his own toys.

The young black child was an impudent, lazy, and saucy spirit who, the Colonel presumably believed, would benefit far more from a whipping than kindness. He acquiesced to the punishment when Madam Esmond insisted, only laughing in his benevolent fashion when his indignant grandson shouted, "You let Mamma rule you in everything, Grandpapa!"

"Why, so I do," admitted Grandpapa. "Rachel, my love, how obvious my petticoat-ridden state is that even this baby has discovered it."

"Then why don't you stand up like a man?" queried little Harry, eager to support his brother.

Grandpapa looked bewildered. "Because I like sitting down best, my dear," he explained gently. "I am an old gentleman, and standing fatigues me."
