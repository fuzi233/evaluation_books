The evening air was thick with the promise of rain, heavy and threatening, as Romola returned from the hospital of San Matteo. Beside her walked Maso, holding a lantern that cast a wavering pool of light upon the cobblestones. Just as they emerged from the shadows of the street, her husband, Tito, stepped forth from the monastery of San Marco. He had barely spent the day with her, having retreated into the solitude of the Via de' Bardi moments after his arrival. Seeing her, he immediately dismissed Maso, his impatience with the servant's slow gait evident, and offered to escort Romola home himself.

This was a performance Tito reserved for occasions when his role demanded it; an official courtesy rather than a sign of intimacy. Between them, there existed no friction, no heated debate. They were two souls drifting in parallel currents, hopelessly alienated in their inner lives, rendering any true contest for agreement impossible. Instead, they navigated their shared existence with a careful, mechanical adherence to a pre-arranged script. If Tito desired a supper prepared in the old library, now transformed into a banquet hall, Romola complied without question, ensuring every detail was perfect. In return, Tito granted her absolute autonomy over her daily rhythms, accepting her assistance with his manuscripts and her supplies for charity, all while maintaining a rigid distance. He avoided her gaze, feigning ignorance of her presence within the house, and playfully attributed her withdrawal to a preference for solitude.

In the early days of her return, after she had abandoned her plan to flee, Romola had made timid attempts to restore a frank relationship. She believed that only through open dialogue regarding their differences could they find a moral understanding. Tito, however, required something far more elusive: a return to her effusive tenderness, a love that would erase the memory of their discord. He desired no explanation; he deemed it impossible. He wished for her affection, but she found tenderness impossible to fabricate. She could be submissive, gentle, and suppress any sign of repulsion, but she could not pretend. Helpless before the reality of their estrangement, she watched as her husband drifted further away.

This distance made her acutely sensitive to secrets he would never willingly share. Regarding his political maneuvers, Tito assured her that the Medici cause was lost and that he served the popular government with genuine conviction—a sentiment she shared warmly. Yet, subtle impressions, like faint odors, gnawed at her peace of mind concerning his dealings with San Marco. She was torn between the terror of discovering evidence that would confirm her deepest suspicions and the urgent impulse to watch, lest some harm befall the city that she might have prevented.

As they walked, Tito spoke of unfinished business. "The day is not yet done for me," he said, hurrying her toward their door. "I must fulfill another commission, perhaps taking an hour, before I can return to rest, which I sorely need." He then chatted lightly of his visit to Pisa, guiding her until they reached a quiet loggia where a lamp burned before a picture of the Virgin. Suddenly, the street, previously empty, filled with the sound of many approaching footsteps and confused voices.

"We shall not reach home dry unless we take shelter here," Tito said, pulling Romola hastily up the steps of the loggia with a start.

"Surely it is futile to wait for such a drizzle," Romola remarked, surprised.

"No," Tito replied, his eyes scanning the gloom with the hyper-vigilance of a mind accustomed to caution. "The rain is growing heavier. Wait a moment." He had spotted the leader of the approaching group: a red feather and a glittering sword-hilt marked him as Dolfo Spini, the captain of the Compagnacci. It was the last person Tito wished to encounter with Romola beside him. Earlier that day, he had met Spini, and now he faced a second, more critical interview. Tito had been entrusted with forged letters—carefully crafted documents bearing the signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, a powerful ally of Savonarola. These letters claimed the Cardinal, unwilling to enter Florence directly, intended to meet the Frate at San Casciano the following morning, disguised as a simple priest. The plot was clear: Spini was to ambush the Frate on the lonely road, seize him, and hand him over to Milanese horsemen waiting near San Casciano to be taken into Roman territory.

Tito had already spoken with Savonarola, presenting the letters with consummate art. He had led the Frate to believe they referred to a rendezvous outside the gates, urging him to request an armed guard from the Signoria, offering his own services to carry the request privately. Savonarola had refused, stating that an armed guard contradicted the privacy required for such a meeting. His flashing eyes revealed his intent to accept the risk. Tito cared little for the outcome; his acute mind saw that regardless of whether the trap succeeded or failed, he would emerge richer and more powerful. If Savonarola fell, Tito gained papal favor and diamonds; if he survived, Tito's agency strengthened his position with both the Frate and Spini.

However, Spini was an unpredictable variable. Cunning enough to devise plots, he lacked the self-command to keep them secret. He often drank heavily, and despite their agreement to maintain a cool demeanor in public, there was always the danger that a drunken Spini might blur the lines of their partnership with sudden, affectionate familiarity. Tito's greatest fear was that Spini had already seen him. In the shadow of the loggia, Tito had been illuminated for an instant by the lamp's rays, marking him as clearly as Spini himself. Romola, cloaked in black, had gone unnoticed, standing behind her husband's shoulder.

"Ha! My carrier-pigeon!" Spini's harsh voice grated out, though he meant it as a whisper, his hand clamping onto Tito's shoulder. "Why did you run to hide? You didn't know it was comrades coming. It's good I caught you; it saves time. What of the chase tomorrow? Will the bald-headed game rise? Are the falcons ready?"

If Tito possessed a temper, rage would have flared against this bull-faced accomplice, unfit to lead or follow. His lips turned white, his excitement born of the desperate need to choose a safe path. To hush Spini would only deepen Romola's suspicion; she was a woman who, once alarmed, could not be silenced. To repel Spini angrily might provoke the wine-soaked Compagnaccio into savagery. Tito chose a third course, one that demonstrated Romola still held a terrifying power over him: the power of dread.

He pressed Romola's hand, feigning a signal to her, and spoke in a tone of jovial camaraderie. "Yes, my Dolfo, you may prepare in all security. But take no trumpets with you."

"Don't be afraid," Spini retorted, slightly piqued. "No need to play Ser Saccente with me. I know where the devil keeps his tail as well as you. What! He swallowed the bait whole? The prophetic nose didn't scent the hook?"

"The brute won't be satisfied until he has emptied the bag," Tito thought, but aloud he said, "Swallowed it as easily as you swallow a cup of Trebbiano. Ha! I see torches; there must be a dead body coming. The pestilence has been spreading, I hear."

"Santiddio! I hate the sight of those biers. Good-night," Spini muttered, hastening away.

The torches were indeed approaching, preceding a church dignitary returning home. Tito bowed with due reverence, but Romola saw none of the outward ceremony. If defeating this treachery—which she felt with the force of long prescience—required her to spring upon her husband and hurl them both down a precipice, she felt she could do it. Union with this man was intolerable. The self-discipline of two years evaporated; she felt only the chasm between them.

Darkness fell again, leaving them to see only each other's faces dimly.

"Tell me the truth, Tito—this time tell me the truth," Romola whispered, her voice trembling. "It will be safer for you."

"Why should I desire to tell you anything else, my angry saint?" Tito replied with a touch of contempt, venting his annoyance. "Since the truth is precisely what you have most reason to rejoice in: that my knowledge of Spini's plot enables me to secure the Frate from falling victim to it."

"What is the plot?"

"That I decline to tell," Tito said. "It is enough that the Frate's safety will be secured."

"It is a plot to draw him outside the gates so Spini may murder him."

"There has been no intention of murder," Tito corrected smoothly. "It is simply a plot to compel him to obey the Pope's summons to Rome. But since I serve the popular government and believe the Frate's presence here is vital to its maintenance, I choose to prevent his departure. You may go to sleep with an easy mind tonight."

For a moment, Romola was silent. Then, in a voice of anguish, she said, "Tito, it is of no use: I have no belief in you."

She could just discern his shrug, the spreading of his palms in silent dismissal. A cold dislike, the anger of unimpassioned beings, hardened within him.

"If the Frate leaves the city—if any harm comes to him," Romola said, her tone shifting to one of indignant resolve, "I will declare what I have heard to the Signoria, and you will be disgraced. What if I am your wife?" she continued, impetuous. "I will be disgraced with you. If we are united, I am that part of you that will save you from crime. Others shall not be betrayed."

"I am quite aware of what you would likely do, *anima mia*," Tito said in his coolest, liquid tones. "Therefore, if you possess a small amount of reasoning, consider that if you believe me in nothing else, you may believe me when I say I will take care of myself, and not put it in your power to ruin me."

"Then you assure me that the Frate is warned—he will not go beyond the gates?"

"He shall not go beyond the gates."

A moment of pause followed, but distrust remained unexpelled.

"I will go back to San Marco now and find out," Romola said, making a move forward.

"You shall not!" Tito whispered bitterly, seizing her wrists with all his masculine strength. "I am master of you. You shall not set yourself in opposition to me."

Passersby were approaching; Tito had heard them, which was why he spoke in a whisper. Romola, too conscious of being mastered to struggle, and aware that witnesses were near, yielded instantly to his movement to lead her from the loggia.

They walked on in silence under the small, drizzling rain. The initial rush of indignation and alarm in Romola began to give way to more complex feelings, rendering speech and action difficult. In that simpler state of vehemence, open opposition to a husband whose soul she felt revolting against had seemed the easiest course. But now, habits of self-questioning, memories of subdued impulses, and the proud reserve left by her discipline began to emerge from the flood of passion. The grasp of her wrists, asserting her husband's physical dominance, did not arouse a new fierceness in her. Instead, it gave her a momentary shuddering horror at this form of contest. It was the first time they had been in declared hostility since her flight and return, and the check given to her ardent resolution then retained the power to arrest her now.

In this altered state, her mind dwelt on probabilities that would save her from a desperate course. Tito would not risk betrayal by her; whatever his original intention, he must now be determined by the fact that she knew of the plot. She was not bound to do anything else than hang over him with the certainty that if he deceived her, her lips would not be closed. And then, it was possible—yes, she must cling to that possibility until disproved—that Tito had never meant to aid in the betrayal of the Frate.

Tito, meanwhile, was busy with his own thoughts, remaining silent until they were near home. Then he said, "Well, Romola, have you now had time to recover calmness? If so, you can supply your want of belief in me by a little rational inference: you can see, I presume, that if I had any intention of furthering Spini's plot, I should now be aware that the possession of a fair Piagnone for my wife, who knows the secret of the plot, would be a serious obstacle in my way."

Tito assumed the tone most comfortable to him, guessing that persuasive deprecation would fall on deaf ears in her current mood.

"Yes, Tito," she said in a low voice, "I think you believe that I would guard the Republic from further treachery. You are right to believe it: if the Frate is betrayed, I will denounce you." She paused, then added with an effort, "But it was not so. I have perhaps spoken too hastily—you never meant it. Only, why will you seem to be that man's comrade?"

"Such relations are inevitable to practical men, my Romola," Tito said, gratified by discerning the struggle within her. "You fair creatures live in the clouds. Pray go to rest with an easy heart," he added, opening the door for her.
