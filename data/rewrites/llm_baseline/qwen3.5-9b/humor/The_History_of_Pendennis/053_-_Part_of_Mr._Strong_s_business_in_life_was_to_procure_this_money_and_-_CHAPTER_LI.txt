Let's revisit the charming little town of Clavering, that same spot where Pen's ancestral home, Fairoaks, stands nearby. We've mentioned it before, mostly because the local society wasn't exactly known for its wit or charm, so our previous reports were brief. However, things get interesting when we introduce Mr. Samuel Huxter. Remember him? The lively fellow we met at Vauxhall. During his vacations, Sam was the lifeblood of Clavering. He'd drag his friends into his mother's parlors, regaling them with the sharp repartee of Bartholomew's Club and the latest gossip from London's high-society circles.

Then there was Mr. Hobnell, the young scoundrel Pen once thrashed over the Fotheringay incident. While a student at the Grammar School, Hobnell was Mrs. Huxter's favorite guest. He knew exactly how to sneak past the surgery door to raid the tamarind pots and could scent his handkerchiefs with rosewater faster than anyone else. It was during this golden era that he fell for Miss Sophy Huxter. After her father passed, he married her and moved them to "The Warren," a few miles out of town.

The Hobnells used to be simple yeomen, but Hobnell Sr. decided to upgrade. He tore down the old farmhouse and erected a glaring white mansion complete with massive stables, a grand piano in the drawing room, and a pack of hunting hounds. With those additions, they officially became County Gentry. When the elder Hobnell died, his son inherited the title of Squire. Meanwhile, Sam Huxter, back in London, loved nothing more than bragging to his mates at Bartholomew's about his brother-in-law's hounds, horses, and endless hospitality.

Every year, usually when Mrs. Hobnell was too busy with her nursery duties to travel, Squire Hobnell would jet up to London for a spree. He'd check into the Tavistock Hotel, and he and Sam would tear through the city—Ascot, the theaters, Vauxhall, and the raucous taverns around Covent Garden. True to his motto, "do as London does," the Squire would indulge in every vice the city offered. Once he returned to the countryside, he'd gift Mrs. Hobnell a new bonnet and shawl, ready to trade urban elegance for rural sports for the next eleven months.

Sam and Hobnell maintained a perfect balance: letters full of metropolitan gossip exchanged for baskets of fresh hares, partridges, and clotted cream sent from the Warren. To the locals, Sam was the brightest star. When he arrived home, he was the center of attention. His songs, jokes, and pranks turned the Warren upside down. Legend even claimed he once saved their eldest child by fishing a bone out of her throat. Simply put, he was the delight of the village.

Unfortunately for Arthur Pendennis, fate had other plans. Just three days after their awkward encounter at Vauxhall, Pen bumped into Mr. Huxter again. Sticking to his vow, Pen hadn't visited little Fanny. He was desperately trying to drown his thoughts in work and distraction. He slaved away in his rooms, writing savage critiques for the *Pall Mall Gazette* on poems and romances that clearly deserved to die. Afterward, he retreated to the lonely Polyanthus Club, where the empty halls only made him moodier. He attended plays hoping for relief, but instead found himself bored by an "ignoble farce" that made him miserable. Seeing Pen's gloomy face would have dampened the spirits of any actor on stage.

Feeling adrift, Pen decided to visit the Back Kitchen, his old haunt with Warrington. He wasn't tired, exactly, but he needed comfort. Thinking of Bows, the pianist, gave him hope. Sure enough, Bows was there, presiding over the keys while the room erupted in laughter at some ridiculous comic songs. To Pen, it felt surreal. Inside what he claimed was an "extinct volcano" of emotion, he suddenly felt a fierce flame ignite. Two days of indulgence had started it; two days of abstinence had fueled it to madness.

As he sat there, nursing his drinks, Arthur noticed Mr. Huxter enter with two or three friends. Huxter had also been to the theater. He whispered something to his companions that annoyed Pen, then sauntered across the room, nodded familiarly, and extended a dirty hand for a handshake. Pen, feeling unnecessarily harsh after their last meeting, shook it. Huxter, however, was in a jovial mood. The slight disagreement at Vauxhall was a mere trifling joke to him; he couldn't imagine being disagreeable to anyone.

Deciding to make conversation, Huxter's friends suggested a topic that guaranteed disaster: the Vauxhall incident.
"A jolly night at Vauxhall, wasn't it?" Huxter winked knowingly.
"I'm glad you liked it," Pen groaned internally.
"I was terribly upset," Huxter continued, eyeing Pen's arm. "Been dining with some chaps at Greenwich. That was a pretty bit of muslin hanging on your arm—who was she?"

That question broke Pen.
"Have I asked you any questions about yourself, Mr. Huxter?" Pen snapped.
"Oh, beg pardon!" Huxter looked genuinely surprised. "I didn't mean offense. Hang it, you're acting quite savage."
"Do you remember what happened the other night?" Pen demanded, his anger rising.
"You forget? Probably. You were tipsy and rude," Huxter replied smoothly.
"Hang it, sir, I apologized!" Huxter blushed slightly.
"Yes, and I accepted it gladly. But recall my request: omit me from your acquaintance list forever. If we meet in public, do not bother recognizing me. Please remember this henceforth."

Huxter grabbed his hat, bowed to the stunned Pen, and exited. His friends paused in wonder before erupting into a roar of laughter, loud enough to summon the room's president, who shouted, "Silence, gentlemen! Do have silence for the 'Body Snatcher'!"—the popular song currently playing as Pen fled the room.

Pen convinced himself he had mastered his temper. He actually wished Huxter had challenged him to a fight; violence would have been preferable to this humiliation. He went home, but nothing soothed him. Work, dinner, the play, the whiskey, and the quarrel left him unrested. He slept poorly.

A few days later, Sam Huxter wrote a letter to his brother-in-law, Squire Hobnell, detailing Arthur's "confounded insolence" in London. Sam painted a damning portrait: an "abandoned criminal," a "regular Don Juan," and a danger to honest households. He claimed to have seen Pen dancing with an innocent girl from the lower ranks and, thanks to an Irish ex-army officer in his club, knew exactly who she was. He threatened to warn her father. The letter ended with polite thanks for the recent basket of game and a hint that more favors were welcome.

Coincidentally, a christening at the Warren occurred the very next day. The baby girl was named Myra Lucretia, after her godmothers. Since Hobnell had shown Sam's letter to his wife, Mrs. Hobnell immediately shared the scandalous tale with her gossip network. By afternoon, the entire town of Clavering knew the story.

Myra was too shocked to speak, but Mrs. Pybus had no such reservations. She discussed the matter with everyone from Mrs. Portman to Madame Fribsby, ordering the children out of the room whenever the topic arose. Madame Fribsby, peering at her portrait of a dragoon, sighed that "men will be men" and quoted *Marmion*. Mrs. Pybus, however, reserved her strongest condemnation for Pen, blaming his "early indulgence," "aristocratic airs," and attendance at "corrupt modern Babylonian parties." Mrs. Portman worried that literary success had turned Pen's head, causing him to forget the morals taught by Dr. Portman. Even Captain Glanders, the Dragoon, whistled and joked about it until Mrs. Glanders silenced him by sending the girls away.

At the local chapel, Pen became the subject of a second sermon. The congregation, eager to throw stones, assumed his guilt without hearing his side. They preached against the dangers of London and the sins of novel-writing, ignoring the fact that they were condemning an innocent man based on hearsay.

The following day, Mrs. Pendennis, trembling with fear and exhaustion, rushed to Dr. Portman's house. She held an anonymous letter—a vicious attack signed with biblical threats, accusing Pen of sin and detailing his alleged crimes. Terrified, she had dropped the letter, allowing her daughter Laura to read it. Laura blushed and trembled with rage.
"The cowards!" Laura cried. "It isn't true!"
"It is true, and you've done it, Laura!" Helen screamed hysterically. "Why did you refuse him? Why did you break my heart? You led him into this! You threw him into the arms of this woman! Don't speak to me! Never forgive you!"

She stormed out, refusing help, leaving Laura and the servants behind. Dr. Portman glanced at the letter, recognized the handwriting, and knew the accusation.
