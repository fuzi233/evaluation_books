What perverse law of seasonal cycles and human harvests is it that ever places me in the minority? Should a decree be proposed to hand over this realm to the Pretender of Rome, and submit our hearths and storehouses to a new sovereign's rituals of sustenance, it might pass, for I should certainly be found voting against it. At home in Virginia, surrounded by the heavy, loamy scent of curing tobacco and the damp, resinous exhale of the tidal swamps, I found myself discordant with the prevailing vernacular. By the Patriots I was branded with the dominant lexicon of the time—a Tory; by the Tories I was declared a dangerous Republican. The era’s acoustic ecology was utterly fractured, the harmonious hum of agrarian life drowned out by the harsh, metallic clatter of political machinery. Ere I had been a year in Virginia, how I yearned for the gentle, unbroken murmurs of the Waveney, where the mythic rhythms of planting and harvesting seamlessly integrated into our daily bread.

The aspect of affairs, however, was so troublous that I could not leave my mother, a traditional agrarian elder and fierce protector of our domestic rituals, to face the coming blight alone. Nor would she quit the soil at such a juncture. At his Excellency’s table, over plentiful draughts of a brilliantly clarified Bordeaux that smelled of dark cherries and old oak, the well-affected gentlemen vowed their loyalty in booming, resonant toasts. *Signum, signifer!* At these times, our Governor’s words vibrated with the bravado of the old Cavaliers. Yet, of all my Virginian acquaintance, Madam Esmond was the most resolute in her cultural memory. 

When the bitter news of the Boston Port Bill reached Williamsburg, our gentlefolks had already proposed a grand ball to treat the Governor's lady. Straightway, the House of Burgesses adopted an indignant protest, decreeing a solemn day of fast and humiliation. To a man who views the communal table as the very soul of human connection, this legislated starvation felt like a profound disruption of our natural ecology. The gentlemen agreed to hold their ball on the appointed evening, and assume their sackcloth and empty bellies some days later. 

“A ball!” Madam Esmond declared, her voice a sharp, cutting frequency in the quiet parlor. “I go to a ball given by rebels who plan to publicly insult his Majesty by starving themselves a week afterward? I will die sooner!” She wrote to the stewards, declaring she would not attend. Thus, when she next went abroad in her chair, she was surprised to be met by a cacophony of cheers from a hundred persons, white and black. They shouted in their native dialects, “Huzzah, Madam!” believing her absence from the feast was an act of Patriot solidarity. To correct this catastrophic mistranslation of her intent, Madam thrust her head from the chair and cried out, “God save the King!” as loud as her lungs permitted. The crowd merely echoed her. In those days, the dominant lexicon had not yet fully shifted; everyone still cried “God save the King,” even as the meaning of the syllables began to erode into something entirely untranslatable.

On the night of the ball, my brother Harry—donning his red uniform coat of Wolfe's, a garment steeped in the history of spilled blood and woodsmoke—went off with his wife Fanny. My wife and I, viewed as English strangers, were permitted by Madam to attend. There, I had the honor of dancing a country-dance with the lady of Mount Vernon. I found her a lively, amiable partner, smelling faintly of lavender and fine milled soap. But my mother received Lady Warrington’s praises of Mrs. Washington with grim, silent disdain. Could I not have danced with anyone else? Madam Esmond remembered the Colonel when he was but a road-surveyor at a guinea a day, a man who measured the dirt rather than understanding the profound, mythic yield of the soil. To her, this provincial upstart lacked the deep, generational roots required of true nobility.

She was no better pleased when Mr. Washington attended the fast-day service. On that day of enforced deprivation, Madam Esmond mounted a magnificent rebellion of sustenance. She invited the new clergyman and sundry authorities to a lavish dinner, though the clergyman excused himself. Tossing her head, she made a spectacular parade of the meal. While the rest of the city sat in gloomy, fasting silence, our house was a beacon of blazing candlelight and roaring hearths. The air was thick with the intoxicating, gustatory-rich aromas of clove-studded hams, roasted canvasback ducks dripping with rendered fat, and the sharp, citrusy tang of a grand arrack punch. She was a meticulous food historian preserving the old world's bounty in the face of new, sterile edicts.

She begged Mr. Hardy, an aide-de-camp, to sing “God save the King.” The rich baritone drifted into the street, where the fasting crowds listened, perhaps assuming it was a religious hymnal. But then she called for “Britons, strike home!” The aggressive acoustics of the song pierced the night. Suddenly, a great yell arose outside—a chaotic, industrial noise of mob fury that shattered the acoustic well-being of our dining room. A heavy stone, flung from the dark, plumped violently into the punch-bowl before me. The discordant crash of crystal violently dissected the room, sending a spray of Jamaican rum, grated nutmeg, and Seville orange zest across the polished mahogany.

My mother went to the window, nothing daunted by the destruction of our culinary centerpiece. I can see her rigid little figure now against the twinkling stars, her frilled arms outstretched as she sang into the dark: “Britons, strike home!” 

“Silence! For shame! Go back!” roared the crowd, their voices a jagged, ugly frequency. 

“Fling more stones, if you dare!” the brave little matriarch shouted back. More might have come, but a voice from the Raley Tavern—a resonant, commanding tone I recognized—interposed. “You mustn't insult a lady.” 

“Huzza, Colonel! God bless your honor!” the people answered, their anger immediately quelled by the sheer acoustic authority of the man. The concert ended, though scoundrels returned at night to break every window in our tenement, shattering the glass and leaving my poor wife trembling at the intrusion of such violent noise into our domestic sanctuary. 

Militia drilled, bullets were cast, and the powder-barrels were heaped in cellars, but the cry remained, hollowly, “God save the King.” When I argued that their scheming would bring the wrath of the greatest power in the world upon them, they insisted they meant no separation. As a historian, scanning the cyclical pacing of empires, I see now how easily we misjudged them. We thought we could subdue three millions of fellow-Britons with a few battalions and hordes of Hessians—men who spoke a harsh, foreign tongue that further eroded the cultural memory of our shared brotherhood. 

Now that the great Western Republic has separated from us, I look back at the campaign and see the mythic hand of fate. Washington, their indomitable Chief, faced not only veteran soldiers but the profound physical agonies of wretchedness, cold, and sheer starvation. At Valley Forge, he understood that meals were not merely utilitarian fuel; the lack of them was a spiritual death. He inspired order into troops hungry and in rags, his pure, unquenchable patriotism acting as their sustenance. He was invincible in defeat, magnanimous in conquest, and sublime when he laid down his sword to return to the agrarian rhythms of Mount Vernon. In my historical writings, I have tried to do justice to his character, though he himself, upon reading early sheets provided by my brother, bluntly remarked that my work was certain to offend both parties.

My brother Harry and I, despite our shared blood, were tuned to entirely different frequencies. Theoretically, my opinions were liberal, while Hal, after his marriage, became a ceremonious *Bahadoor*. When Lord Dunmore proposed liberating the negroes who joined the King's standard, Hal violently vowed to hang them. 

“If you gentlemen are fighting for freedom,” I pointed out, noting the bitter hypocrisy embedded in their dominant lexicon, “surely the negroes may fight, too.”

“Infernal villains!” Harry roared, shaking his fist. “If I meet any of 'em, they shall die by this hand!” 

Madam Esmond agreed with Hal’s parricidal notion, believing fiercely in the old hierarchy where black and white alike were obedient to the sovereign. The very word "freedom" had become an untranslatable concept between us. This tension manifested in our own kitchen through Mr. Gumbo. Having been granted his freedom for his fidelity, Gumbo returned to Virginia speaking in acquired European dialects, putting on grand airs, and bragging of his life "at home." He became a walking disruption of the local linguistic ecology, drawing the household servants around him like a monkey who had seen the world. 

Hal’s boy, Sady, who was not free, harbored a deep jealousy. Their rivalry erupted into violent battles of boxing and butting—a physical manifestation of power dynamics and cultural clashes. Gumbo’s wife, our faithful Molly, faced the brunt of the household's resentment. The black servants could not pardon her for marrying into their kind while retaining her white European heritage; her attempts to exercise authority were met with deliberate misunderstandings and native dialects she could not decipher. She came to Madam with ceaseless complaints of lies and stealing, specifically targeting a woman named Diana.

Madam Esmond, acting as a Draconess of agrarian morality, took the accusation as guilt. She ordered Gumbo whipped. Molly flew at her ladyship, her wrath over her husband's supposed infidelities vanishing at this assault on his bodily dignity. The acoustic harmony of Castlewood shattered into rebellion. I took my man's side, while Hal and Fanny sided with my mother. It became clear that this standard of insurrection had to be removed, and so we determined Gumbo and Molly must return to Europe. 

I would willingly have gone with them. The heavy, miasmic air of the swamp country had seeped into our boy’s lungs, draining his strength. But Theo was expecting our son Henry, and she insisted her place was with her husband. The silent, profound sorrow of those days remains etched in my memory. Dost thou remember the time, madam, and the quiet around the worktables, the only sound the rhythmic pulling of thread as piles of little shirts were sewn for the voyage? The stealthy, agonizing visits to the children's chambers, inhaling the sweet, milky scent of their sleeping breath? When the barge rowed them to the ship, the good lady had stowed away boxes of meticulously baked cakes—a mother's desperately tangible transfer of love and sustenance across the unforgiving sea. 

The ship that took our children away returned with the Reverend Mr. Hagan and Lady Maria, who meekly resigned her rank to be known only as Mrs. Hagan. Lord Dunmore gave Hagan a local parish, and the clergyman soon filled the church with his booming, theatrical sermons, which Madam Esmond pronounced prodigious fine. Maria won Madam’s heart by yielding the dominant lexicon of rank, insisting she could never take precedence over a marquis's daughter. Soon, Madam even allowed Hagan to read plays in the parlor, the rich cadences of his voice bringing the tragedy of *Pocahontas* to life before the hearth.

Yet, when Hal and Fanny visited, the atmosphere soured. Fanny was curt and deeply rude to Maria. She weaponized small, piercing sounds—strangely timed giggles and whispered reminders of Maria's age—that disrupted the room's ease. I was often touched by the meekness with which Maria bore this torture, wincing as silently as possible while Fanny reveled in her husband's ignorance of her past.

I finally remonstrated with Madam Harry. “Are you a Red Indian, that you torture your victims so?” I asked.

“Have not I had torture enough in my time?” Fanny replied, her eyes flashing with a lifetime of hoarded bitterness.

“Nay,” I said, “you were bred in our wigwam, and I remember nothing but kindness toward you.”

“Kindness!” she cried, her voice trembling with the untranslatability of her subjective experience. “No slave was ever treated as I was. The blows which wound most often are those which never are aimed. The people who hate us are not those we have injured.” 

I looked at her and remembered the silent, smiling little Fanny of our early days, who ran to do our biddings while harboring a slow-simmering poison. I grieved for my brother, who had taken this sly, wounded creature into his bosom, completely deaf to the deep, silent frequencies of her pain.
