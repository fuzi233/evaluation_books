*"Let no flower of the spring pass by us; let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they be withered."* — *Book of Wisdom.*

To Gwendolen Harleth, the idea of Offendene as a home for her childhood, or a place steeped in the tender, rooting memories of kinship, was a fantasy that offered little comfort. A human life, she felt, required a soil where love for the earth grew deep through the labor of daily life, where the sounds and accents of a native tongue haunted the soul, and where kindness toward neighbors—even the local dogs and donkeys—became a natural habit of the blood. Yet, at five years old, mortals are rarely prepared to transcend the parochial and embrace the abstract citizenship of the world. Our earliest prejudices, such as the blind preference for milk, are the necessary nourishment for the young body and soul until they learn to look outward.

In Gwendolen's case, however, that blessed persistence where affection takes root had been tragically absent. Only a year prior to her return from Leubronn, Offendene had been selected merely for its proximity to Pennicote Rectory. On a bleak late October afternoon, with rooks crowing overhead and yellow elm leaves swirling in the wind, Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen, and her four half-sisters had been driven down the avenue for the first time, trailed by the governess and the maid in another carriage.

The season seemed oddly suited to the grim aspect of the old oblong red-brick house, which was anxiously adorned with stone at every line, barring nothing from the double rows of narrow windows nor the large square portico. Stone encouraged a greenish lichen, and the brick wore a powdery gray, lending the building a rigid, rectangular severity that softened slightly only by the surrounding plantation. One might have wished for the house to stand on a knoll, commanding a view of distant thatched roofs, church towers, and the sweeping greens of the park; but instead, screened by flat pastures, it possessed only a fleeting glimpse of the wider world in the lofty curves of the chalk downs.

Though modest in size and difficult to let due to its sombre furniture and faded upholstery, the house carried a mysterious prestige. To reside within walls once sufficient for dowager countesses imparted a tangible tinge of elevation to Mrs. Davilow's satisfaction. For Gwendolen, this possibility materialized abruptly upon the death of her stepfather, Captain Davilow, who had visited only briefly and fitfully for nine years. While Gwendolen cared little for the mechanics of the inheritance, she relished the result: her prospects had instantly improved. She had grown tired of their previous nomadic existence, constantly moving from foreign watering-places to Parisian apartments, forever rejecting new suites of furniture and meeting strangers who treated her with casual indifference. The two years spent at a showy school, where she was invariably placed foremost, had only hardened her belief that a person of her exceptional nature deserved nothing less than advantageous circumstances.

Any lingering fear of mediocrity vanished now that her mother had an establishment. Gwendolen was secure regarding her birth; knowing her maternal grandfather was a West Indian, she assumed the mystery of his fortune was solved, while her father's family remained so high that they ignored her mother, despite her proud display of a miniature of "Lady Molly." She knew little of her father, save for a painful incident at age twelve. When Mrs. Davilow, on rare occasions, produced memorials of her first husband, she recounted with fervor that dear papa had died when Gwendolen was still in long clothes. Gwendolen, recalling the unlovable stepfather she had known throughout her youth, had bluntly asked, "Why did you marry again, mamma? It would have been nicer if you had not." Mrs. Davilow, flushing deeply, had violently shut away the memorials and accused her daughter of lacking feeling. Hurt and ashamed, Gwendolen had never dared ask again.

This guilt was not isolated. It was customary for Gwendolen to sleep in her mother's room, a privilege reflecting Mrs. Davilow's tenderness for her eldest. Once, during a pain attack, the sick woman had forgotten her medicine beside the bed and begged Gwendolen to fetch it. The healthy young lady, cozy in her warm couch, had stubbornly refused to step into the cold, leaving her mother to suffer without relief. Though Mrs. Davilow never reproached her, Gwendolen felt the sting of her mother's disappointment and attempted to make amends with excessive caresses. As the spoiled pet of the household, waited upon by mother, sisters, and maids, Gwendolen found it difficult to prioritize anyone else's needs over her own. When thwarted, she resented it with a passionate, childish fury. Though she delighted in rescuing drowning insects, she retained a shameful memory of strangling her sister's canary in a fit of exasperation at its noise. Later, she had bought a white mouse for her sister in retribution, but the guilt of the bird's death lingered. By twenty, Gwendolen's raw nature had tempered into a calculated self-control, guarding her from penitential humiliation, though a sharper will burned beneath the surface.

Upon arriving at Offendene, the place chosen by her brother-in-law, Mr. Gascoigne, the group stood under the porch, the stone hall and staircase visible through the open door, warmed by a bright fire. Silence reigned as the eyes of the mother, sisters, and governess fixed on Gwendolen, awaiting her verdict. The girls ranged from sixteen-year-old Alice to ten-year-old Isabel, dressed in increasingly shabby black gowns. Miss Merry, the governess, was elderly and neutral. Mrs. Davilow's beauty, though worn, seemed pathetic in her appeal to Gwendolen, who surveyed the landscape and entrance hall with rapid, judging glances. Like a young racehorse amidst untrimmed ponies, Gwendolen stood apart.

"Well, dear, what do you think of the place?" Mrs. Davilow asked gently.

"I think it is charming," Gwendolen replied quickly. "A romantic place; anything delightful may happen in it. No one need be ashamed of living here."

"There is certainly nothing common about it."

"Oh, it would do for fallen royalty or grand poverty. We ought properly to have been living in splendor." Gwendolen's tone shifted to sharp surprise. "But I thought my uncle and aunt Gascoigne would be here to meet us, and my cousin Anna!"

"We are early," Mrs. Davilow said, entering the hall and greeting the housekeeper. "You expect Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne?"

"Yes, madam," the housekeeper replied, noting the state of the fires and the dusting of the brasses. "They'll be here at five."

Satisfied, Gwendolen tripped up the matted stone staircase to inspect the rooms: a dining chamber of dark oak and worn red satin damask adorned with a painting of snarling dogs; a library smelling of old leather; and finally, a drawing-room crowded with venerable knick-knacks.

"Mamma, pray come here!" Gwendolen cried. "Here is an organ. I will be Saint Cecilia!" She threw off her hat and gloves, seating herself before the instrument in an admirable pose, hair loose. Miss Merry, obediently dubbed Jocosa, combed out Gwendolen's hair, letting it fall in a smooth, light-brown stream.

"A charming picture, my dear!" Mrs. Davilow smiled, pleased by the display.

"What a queer, quaint, picturesque room!" Gwendolen exclaimed, admiring the embroidered chairs and garlands. "That one with the ribs... I think it is Spanish."

"Oh, Gwendolen!" Isabel, a plain child with an alarming memory, gasped, holding open a hinged panel of the wainscot.

Everyone gathered to look. Inside lay the image of an upturned dead face, from which an obscure figure fled with outstretched arms. "How horrible!" Mrs. Davilow cried with disgust. Gwendolen shuddered silently. Isabel whispered, "You will never stay in this room by yourself, Gwendolen."

"How dare you open things which were meant to be shut up?" Gwendolen snapped, snatching the panel shut. "There is a lock—where is the key? Let the key be found, or let one be made, and let nobody open it again; or rather, let the key be brought to me."

Turning away, flushed with reaction, Gwendolen declared, "Let us go up to our own room, mamma." The housekeeper searched and found the key in a nearby cabinet drawer, handing it to Bugle, the lady's-maid, with significant instructions to deliver it to "Her Royal Highness."

Inside the black-and-yellow bedroom, where a white couch awaited beside the massive "catafalque" bed, Gwendolen's first move was to gaze at herself in the tall mirror.

"That is a becoming glass, Gwendolen," Mrs. Davilow observed. "Or is it the black and gold color that sets you off?"

"I should make a tolerable St. Cecilia with some white roses on my head," Gwendolen mused. "Only how about my nose, mamma? I think saint's noses never in the least turn up.
