When his peace offerings were refused, the young American nobleman—once so bold and confident—was consumed by anger, not only toward Colonel Lambert but against the entire family. “He has humbled me before the girls!” he fumed in private. “And Mr. Wolfe, always preaching virtue and acting like a guardian angel, has joined hands with him to brand me as a reckless wastrel once more. They are so pious they won’t even shake my hand, and when I try to show them a little gratitude, they toss my gifts back at me!”

“Why, sir,” murmured Parson Sampson, eyeing the two morocco boxes with covetous interest, “these must be worth a fortune! Look at those golden trinkets resting on their satin cushions!”

“They cost a fair sum,” replied the young man, “but I wouldn’t begrudge ten times that amount to those who’ve shown kindness.”

“No, indeed, sir,” Sampson chimed in quickly, never missing an opportunity to flatter his patron, “not if I know your honour!”

“The repeater watch,” the young man added, “they said was a bargain—worth a hundred pounds in Paris. And little Miss Hetty once told me she dreamed of owning a repeating timepiece.”

“Oh, what a treasure!” exclaimed the chaplain, eyes gleaming. “With its pearl-encrusted back and diamond-set crown—why, it could win any woman’s heart!”

At that, Mr. Warrington’s voice rose sharply. “I’ve a mind to throw it out to that apple-woman passing by!” he declared, his temper flaring.

Later, when Harry went into the city on business—through the Strand and past the Temple—his companion did not follow far. He turned aside, confessing a deep fear of Chancery Lane and its strange inhabitants. Left alone, Harry made his way to his broker, and together they walked to the Bank, where some minor transactions took place. After signing a few papers, Harry left with a bundle of crisp banknotes tucked securely in his pocket.

The broker then escorted him to one of London’s famed dining establishments, where they dined in silence. Afterwards, he showed Harry the Virginian walk on 'Change—the very path where Harry passed with shame, imagining how his father’s estate had slipped through his fingers. “What would my mother say,” he thought, “if she knew I was carrying off such a portion of our family’s inheritance in this endless gambler’s pocket? There they all are—Virginia merchants—talking about me: ‘That’s young Esmond of Castlewood, son of Madam Esmond. He’s been losing money at cards, selling stock after stock.’”

His spirits lifted only after he crossed under the traitors’ heads at Temple Bar and stepped clear of the City. From there, he walked home, pausing briefly to glance into St. James’s Street, though no one yet stirred—everyone still bound for dinner or later engagements.

Once inside, Harry unfolded his notes. He selected three, wrapped them in paper, sealed them carefully, and wrote within: “Much good may they do you. H. E. W.” He addressed the packet to Reverend Mr. Sampson and placed it on the mantelpiece, instructing his servants to deliver it upon the parson’s arrival.

Then, with his phaeton waiting at the door, he climbed in, intending to drive through the park. But rain began to fall, or the east wind grew sharp, and he changed course, turning down St. James’s Street instead. By three o’clock, he was back at White’s Club, where few had yet arrived—dinner hour still holding sway. Yet there, lounging over the *Public Advertiser*, was his cousin, Lord Castlewood, fresh from Court duties.

Lord Castlewood yawned over the newspaper. “Shall we play a game of piquet?” he asked.

Harry saw no reason to refuse. “Just for an hour,” said Castlewood. “I dine at Arlington Street at four.”

“Just for an hour,” agreed Harry. They called for cards.

“Or shall we move upstairs?” suggested Castlewood. “Away from the noise?”

“Certainly,” Harry replied.

By five o’clock, half a dozen gentlemen had come in after dinner—some playing cards, others sipping coffee, others talking. The ordinary diners still lingered at their tables. At White’s, men often sat until midnight.

One gentleman leaned toward the window. “Whose phaeton is that?” he asked.

“The Fortunate Youth’s,” came the reply.

“Not so fortunate lately,” said the first. “Luck’s been terrible these past nights. Lost thirteen hundred last night alone. Has Mr. Warrington been here today, John?”

“He’s here now, sir,” answered John. “In the little tea-room with Lord Castlewood since three. Playing piquet.”

“What fun for Castlewood,” muttered the first man, shrugging.

The second growled. “Curse the fellow! He doesn’t belong in this club. He never pays when he loses. Gentlemen shouldn’t play with him. Sir Miles Warrington told me the other day Castlewood owes him money on a bet—three years now.”

“Castlewood doesn’t lose when he plays alone,” said the first, grinning. “A big crowd unnerves him—that’s why he avoids the table.”

“Let’s go up and stop them,” growled the second.

“Why?” countered the first. “Better just watch from the window. Look—there’s the lamplighter climbing the ladder. Good sport. And that old fellow in the chair—have you ever seen such a face?”

“Who just left the house? It’s Fortunatus! He’s forgotten his phaeton’s been waiting. I’ll wager he’s lost to Castlewood.”

“Do you take me for a fool?” the first snapped. “Look at those horses—fine beasts. How fast he’s driving them!” They watched as Harry galloped away, scattering coachmen and chairmen alike. Soon, Lord Castlewood entered a chair and departed.

Harry drove home—just a short distance—but those poor horses had pounded the wet pavement for hours. At the door, Mr. Gumbo stood chatting with a country girl, who curtsied and hurried off. Gumbo was always surrounded by admirers.

“Gumbo,” Harry called from the carriage, “has Mr. Sampson been here?”

“No, sar,” Gumbo replied. “Mr. Sampson have not been here.”

“Go upstairs and fetch a letter addressed to Mr. Sampson,” Harry ordered.

“Addressed to Mr. Sampson? Oh yes, sir,” Gumbo said, unable to read.

“A sealed letter,” Harry corrected sharply. “On the mantelpiece, in the glass!”

Gumbo obeyed slowly. As soon as Harry had it, he turned his horses around and headed back toward St. James’s Street. From their window, the two men at White’s watched the Fortunate Youth return in seconds.

As Harry left the tea-room—having played piquet with Castlewood—he noticed several men had entered the gaming room. A bank was already set up. Some players wore their gaming jackets, kept at the club for regular sessions.

Harry went to the clerk’s desk, paid his account from the previous night, and sat down at the table, calling for fresh counters. This week had been particularly unlucky—and tonight offered no relief. He called for more counters, then more again. Pale and quiet, though polite when spoken to, he could not win.

Eventually, he rose. “Hang it, stay and mend your luck!” urged Lord March, sitting beside him with a mountain of green and white counters. “Take a hundred of mine and keep going!”

“I’ve had enough for tonight, my lord,” Harry said, rising and leaving. He ate a broiled bone in the coffee-room and returned to his lodgings around midnight.

After great misfortune, sleep often comes easily. But waking is another matter. Last night you proposed to Miss Brown. You quarreled with Captain Jones and pulled his nose. You played cards with Colonel Robinson and left behind a trail of IOUs. These memories, along with a pounding headache, return in the morning light. What a chasm lies between yesterday and today! It feels like years have passed. Can you leap back? Is yesterday merely a dream?

You lie in bed. No dawn yet. Pull the nightcap over your eyes, the blankets over your nose. Sleep away yesterday. Psha! It was only a dream! No—no. Sleep refuses to come. The watchman cries out the hour—what hour? Beneath your pillow, the repeating watch sings six times in the dark, followed by a soft chime marking the half-hour.

Poor little Hester—so bright, so innocent. He had meant for her to have that watch. What will Maria say? (Oh, that old Maria—how tiresome she’s becoming.) What will Madam Esmond think when she learns he’s lost every shilling of his ready cash—of his entire inheritance? All his winnings, plus five thousand pounds, gone in three nights. Could Castlewood have cheated? No. My lord knows piquet better than Harry does, but he wouldn’t betray blood. No—Harry was glad his kinsman had gotten the money. Not another shilling would he risk. It was only when he tallied his losses and realized they’d wiped out everything he owned that he stepped away from the table.

But oh, cursed company! Extravagance! Folly! Humiliation! Remorse! “Will my mother forgive me?” he wondered. “Oh, that I were home—never having left it!”

Dawn finally seeped through the shutters. The housemaid lit the fire and opened the windows to admit the gray morning. Gumbo followed, warming the dressing gown and setting out shaving tools. Then came the hairdresser to powder and curl his master’s hair while Harry read his letters. Finally, Parson Sampson arrived—eager, smiling, eager to serve his patron. He had intended to return the day before, but friends detained him at St. Alban’s, and they made a night of it.

“Oh, Parson!” groaned Harry. “It was the worst night you’ve ever known! Look here!”

The chaplain glanced at the broken envelope. “'Much good may it do you,'” he read aloud.

“Look outside!” Harry snapped. “It was addressed to you.”

Sampson’s face drained of colour. “Has someone opened it?” he asked, alarmed.

“Someone has,” Harry said. “I opened it, Sampson. If you’d come yesterday afternoon, you’d have found it full of banknotes. As it is, I dropped every last one at the infernal macco-table last night.”

“All?” Sampson whispered.

“Yes, all—every penny I brought from the city, every shilling I possessed. In the afternoon, I played piquet with my cousin—well, a gentleman at White’s—and he relieved me of all I had. Remembering there might still be money here—if you hadn’t taken it—I came home, fetched it, and left it at the table with every shilling I own. And—great heavens, Sampson—what’s wrong?”

“It’s my luck,” the chaplain cried, breaking into tears. “My usual luck!”

“You’re not sobbing like a child over the loss of a couple hundred pounds?” Harry roared, furious. “Leave the room, Gumbo! Confound you—why do you always stick your woolly head in that door?”

“Someone below wants to see master with a bill,” Gumbo said.

“Tell him to go to Jericho!” Harry bellowed. “I’m not at home! Let me see no one!”

A murmur, a scuffle—then silence. Harry’s anger only intensified. He turned on Sampson, who sat with his head buried in his chest.

“Perhaps you should take a bumper of brandy to steady yourself?” he said, voice laced with sarcasm. “Hang it, don’t snivel like a woman!”

“Oh, it’s not me!” Sampson insisted, lifting his head. “I’m used to it.”

“Not you? Then who?” Harry demanded.

“Myself—because someone else is hurt, and through my fault!” the chaplain said, regaining strength. “I’ve lived in London for years with a bootmaker—a decent man. A few days ago, trusting a friend who promised a loan, I borrowed sixty pounds from him—money he needed to pay his own bills. I can’t repay it. His goods will be seized. His wife and children—poor souls—will be cast out. This honest family will be ruined because of me. But as you say, Mr. Warrington, I shouldn’t snivel. I’ll remember your kindness—and bid you farewell.”

Taking his wide-brimmed hat, the chaplain walked out.

Harry burst into a curse and a bitter laugh. He was furious—furious at himself, at fate, at everyone. He didn’t know what he said. Sampson heard the laughter, then Harry’s voice from the stairs: “Sampson! Sampson! Hang you—come back! It’s a mistake! I beg your pardon!”

But the chaplain was too wounded to return. Harry heard the street door slam. It echoed in his own heart. He entered his room and collapsed into a luxurious chair. He was the Prodigal Son, among the swine—his guilt tripping him, wallowing over him. Gambling, extravagance, debauchery, reckless companions, dangerous women—they surged forward, trampling the fallen youth.

Yet the Prodigal was not entirely defeated. With a sudden surge of defiance, he shoved the demons aside, kicked away his shameful memories, and grabbed a large glass of brandy—just as he had recommended to the humiliated parson. Throwing off his fine damask robe, he rang for Gumbo.

“Not that!” he roared as Gumbo presented a green coat with gold buttons and cord. “A plain suit—the plainer the better! Bring the black clothes!”

Gumbo returned with the mourning coat Harry had worn months before.

Then Harry gathered:
1. His new gold watch;
2. The repeater—bought for Hetty—into his other fob;
3. The necklace meant for Theo;
4. Half a dozen rings—except his grandfather’s seal ring, which he kissed and placed on the pincushion;
5. Three gold snuffboxes;
6. His purse—knitted by his mother—containing three shillings, sixpence, and a silver coin from Virginia.

He put on his hat and stepped out.

At the landing, Mr. Ruff, his landlord, bowed low and handed him a long strip of paper. “Much obliged if Mr. Warrington will settle. Mrs. Ruff has a large account to settle today.” Mrs. Ruff was a milliner. Mr. Ruff was a senior waiter at White’s, serving under Mr. Mackreth.

The sight of the landlord did nothing to improve Harry’s mood.

“Perhaps your honour will settle the account?” Ruff asked.

“Of course I will,” Harry said, glowering down from the stair.

“Now, perhaps?”

“No, sir! I will not settle it now!” Harry snapped, pushing forward.

“I’m very much in need of money, sir,” Ruff pleaded. “Mrs. Ruff is—”

“Hang you! Get out of my way!” Harry roared, shoving Ruff backward, nearly sending him tumbling down the hall. He stomped down the stairs and stepped into Bond Street.

The Guards were drilling at the King’s Mews near Charing Cross. Their drums and fifes echoed as Harry passed. He looked in and saw them in formation. “I can shoulder a musket,” he thought grimly, “at least.”

Crossing St. Martin’s Lane—where he handled some business—he reached Long Acre and the bootmaker’s house where Sampson lodged. The landlady said the chaplain wasn’t home but would return by one. Since she knew Harry, she let him into the apartment. He sat down and, lacking anything better to do, picked up an unfinished sermon—on the Prodigal Son. He didn’t read it closely.

Soon, he heard the landlady’s shrill voice chasing someone up the stairs. Sampson burst in, followed by the weeping woman.

At the sight of Harry, Sampson froze. The landlady stopped mid-scream. “There’s only thirteen pounds in the house, and he’ll be here at one!” she shrieked.

“Hush, hush!” the chaplain gasped, pointing to Harry. “Don’t you see Mr. Warrington? I have urgent business with him. It will be settled—trust me!”

He coaxed the woman out, the children clinging to her coat.

“Sampson,” Harry said, rising, “I’ve come to apologize. What I said earlier was cruel, unjust—unlike a gentleman.”

“Not a word more, sir,” Sampson replied, coldly bowing, barely touching the hand Harry offered.

“I see you’re still angry,” Harry continued.

“An apology is an apology,” Sampson said. “A man of my station asks for no more from one of yours. You likely didn’t mean to wound me. And if you did—well, you’re not the only one in the family who has.” He gazed around the room, filled with sorrow. “I wish I’d never known the name Esmond or Castlewood—or that place pictured over my fireplace, where I’ve buried myself these many years. Your cousin took a fancy to me, promised to make my fortune, kept me as his dependant—until fortune passed me by. Now he refuses me my due.”

“What do you mean, your due?” Harry asked.

“Three years’ salary owed me as chaplain of Castlewood,” Sampson said. “When I couldn’t get help from you, I went to his lordship this morning. I fell on my knees and begged. He gave me civil words—though none of the money. Only five guineas, which I accepted. But five guineas among so many needs! Oh, those poor children!”

“Lord Castlewood said he had no money?” Harry exclaimed.

“He won eleven hundred pounds from me yesterday at piquet,” Harry said. “I paid him from this very pocketbook.”

“I dare say, sir,” Sampson replied, “one can’t trust a word his lordship says. But I’m thinking of execution here—ruin tomorrow!”

“That needn’t happen,” Harry said. “Here are eighty guineas, Sampson. That’s all I have. I wish I could give more—as I promised. But you didn’t come at the right time. I’m penniless until my remittances arrive from Virginia.”

Sampson stared, stunned, then turned pale. He dropped to his knees, clutching Harry’s hand.

“Great powers, sir!” he cried. “Are you an angel sent by Heaven? You scorned my tears this morning. I cannot help them now. They pour forth from a grateful heart. Even a stone would weep before such kindness! May Heaven bless you forever! May my prayers rise for you, my best benefactor! May—”

“Nay, nay!” Harry said, embarrassed. “Get up, friend—get up, Sampson!”

“I am glad to have helped you,” Harry said, feeling oddly pleased. “There—don’t kneel to me!”

“To Heaven who sent you!” the chaplain cried. “Mrs. Weston! Mrs. Weston!”

“What is it, sir?” the landlady asked instantly—she’d been listening at the door.

“We are saved!” Sampson shouted. “We are saved! Kneel, woman—kneel and thank our benefactor! Children, raise your voices and bless him!”

A chorus of sobs erupted. The chaplain led the prayer. Harry stood in the middle, awkward but delighted. They would worship him, no matter what. One child, confused, remained standing. Her mother slapped her cheek. “Drat it, Jane! Kneel down and bless the gentleman, I tell you!”

We leave them in their heartfelt gratitude. Harry walked away from Long Acre, almost forgetting the sorrows of the past days, tingling with the warmth of having done something truly good.

The young woman Gumbo had been speaking with that evening—the one he met when Harry returned from White’s—was Mrs. Molly, maid to the ladies at Oakhurst. Wherever Gumbo went, he left admirers in his wake. We mentioned it was a Wednesday evening when he and Mrs. Molly took a walk. They were exchanging tender farewells when Harry arrived, ending their twilight whispers.
