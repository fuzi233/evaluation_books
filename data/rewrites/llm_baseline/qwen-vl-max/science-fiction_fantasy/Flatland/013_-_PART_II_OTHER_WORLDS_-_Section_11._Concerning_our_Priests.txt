It is time I turned from these scattered observations about life in Flatland to the true heart of this tale—the moment when I stepped beyond the limits of my world and into the vast, incomprehensible realm of Space. That is what this book is truly about; everything that has come before serves only as preparation. For this reason, I must leave untouched many aspects of our existence that might otherwise hold interest—how we move without feet, how we build structures without hands or foundations, how rain falls across our land without being blocked by northern zones, how our hills rise, our mines are dug, our trees grow, our seasons turn, and our harvests come. I must also pass over our alphabet, our writing system adapted to our linear tablets, and countless other details of daily life. These are not forgotten, but omitted out of respect for your time.

Yet before I reach the core of my story, I must address one final matter that readers may expect: the highest class in Flatland society—the Circles, or as we call them, the Priests. When I use the word “Priests,” do not think only of religious figures. In our world, they are far more than that. They govern every aspect of life: science, art, trade, war, architecture, engineering, education, law, morality, theology—everything of value. Though they do little themselves, they are the unseen force behind all great works, directing others to achieve what no individual could alone.

Now, though everyone calls a Circle a Circle, those with deeper knowledge understand that no true Circle exists. All are, in fact, Polygons with an enormous number of very small sides. As the number of sides increases, the shape grows closer to a perfect circle. With three hundred or four hundred sides, even the most sensitive touch cannot detect angles—though, as I’ve already explained, such tactile recognition is forbidden among the elite. To touch a Circle would be an unbearable insult. This social taboo allows the Circle to maintain an aura of mystery around their form from childhood onward.

The average perimeter of a Circle is three feet. So, in a Polygon of three hundred sides, each side measures just a hundredth of a foot—less than a tenth of an inch. In a six- or seven-hundred-sided figure, the sides are barely larger than the head of a pin. By custom, we assume the Supreme Circle has ten thousand sides—though no one can truly verify it.

Unlike lower classes, whose offspring gain one side per generation, the advancement of the Circular line is governed by two opposing laws of nature. First, the rate of development accelerates as the lineage rises. Second, fertility declines proportionally. Thus, a Polygon with four or five hundred sides rarely has even one child—never more than one. Yet their offspring may possess significantly more sides. A son of a five-hundred-sided Polygon might have five hundred fifty, or even six hundred.

But evolution is not left entirely to nature. Medicine plays a role. Our physicians have found that the delicate sides of newborn high-class Polygons can be broken and restructured with remarkable precision. Done correctly, this process can cause a Polygon of two or three hundred sides to leap forward by hundreds of generations—doubling his ancestral rank in a single act. But it is dangerous. Only one in ten survives. Still, the ambition of noble families is so fierce that nearly every aspiring parent places their firstborn in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium within a month of birth.

One year later, the child is usually gone—another grave in the Gymnasium’s cemetery. But on rare occasions, a triumphant procession returns with a child transformed: no longer a Polygon, but a Circle—at least in name. One such miracle is enough to inspire thousands of parents to risk the same fate, even knowing the odds are against them. And so, the dream of becoming a Circle lives on, not through birth alone, but through sacrifice, science, and hope.
