Descending the steps toward the front door, I encountered Sergeant Cuff standing there, his presence as unwelcome as it was unavoidable. After all that had passed between us, I felt a deep reluctance to show any interest in his investigation—yet, despite myself, curiosity overpowered my pride. My dignity crumbled beneath the weight of the moment, and the words slipped out before I could stop them: “What news from Frizinghall?”

“I’ve seen the Indians,” he replied, without a flicker of expression. “And I’ve learned what Rosanna purchased privately in town last Thursday. The Indians will be released next Wednesday. There’s no doubt in my mind—or in Mr. Murthwaite’s—that they came here with the intent to steal the Moonstone. But their plans were disrupted by the events of Wednesday night. They had nothing to do with the actual theft—no more than you did. Still, let me tell you this, Mr. Betteredge—if we don’t recover the jewel, *they* will. You haven’t heard the last of those three jugglers.”

As he spoke, Mr. Franklin returned from his walk. He kept his composure far better than I had mine; he passed us silently and stepped into the house without so much as a glance.

I, having already surrendered my dignity, resolved to make full use of it. “So much for the Indians,” I said. “Now, what about Rosanna?”

Sergeant Cuff shook his head slowly. “The mystery surrounding her grows thicker,” he said. “I’ve traced her to a shop in Frizinghall run by a linen draper named Maltby. She made no purchases elsewhere—not at tailors’, milliners’, or other drapers’ shops. At Maltby’s, she bought only one item: a piece of long cloth. She was particular about the quality, and the quantity was enough to make a nightgown.”

“Whose nightgown?” I asked sharply.

“Hers,” he answered. “Between twelve and three on Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to Miss Verinder’s room while everyone else slept—likely to conceal the Moonstone. On her way back to her own chamber, her nightgown brushed against the wet paint on the door. The stain couldn’t be washed out, and she couldn’t destroy the garment without replacing it first, to maintain the appearance of her linen inventory.”

“But how do you know it was *her* nightgown?” I objected.

“The material she bought,” he replied. “If it had been Miss Verinder’s, she would have needed lace, frills, and other embellishments. She wouldn’t have had time to sew such a thing in one night. Plain cloth means a plain servant’s nightgown. No, Mr. Betteredge—this is clear. The real puzzle is why, after securing a replacement, she chose to hide the stained garment instead of destroying it. If she refuses to speak, there’s only one way to resolve it: search the hiding-place at the Shivering Sand. That’s where the truth lies.”

“And how will you find it?” I pressed.

“I’m sorry to disappoint you,” he said, with a quiet smile, “but that’s a secret I intend to keep.” (For your sake, not his—I’ll mention now that he had returned from Frizinghall armed with a search warrant. His experience told him Rosanna likely carried a written record of the hiding place, to guide her should she return later. With that document, he believed he’d have everything he needed.)

“Now, Mr. Betteredge,” he continued, “let’s put speculation aside and get to work. I instructed Joyce to watch Rosanna. Where is he?”

Joyce was the Frizinghall constable, left under Superintendent Seegrave’s orders to assist Sergeant Cuff. As he asked the question, the clock struck two. True to time, the carriage arrived to take Miss Rachel to her aunt’s.

“One thing at a time,” the Sergeant said, stopping me as I turned to send someone after Joyce. “Miss Verinder comes first.”

Though the rain threatened, the close carriage had been arranged for her journey. Sergeant Cuff beckoned Samuel, the footman, down from the rumble behind. “You’ll see a friend of mine waiting among the trees, just beyond the lodge gate,” he said. “Without stopping the carriage, he’ll climb into the rumble with you. Your only duty is to remain silent and keep your eyes shut. Otherwise, you’ll bring trouble upon yourself.”

With that, he sent Samuel back to his post. What Samuel thought, I cannot say—but I saw plainly that Miss Rachel would be watched closely from the moment she left our home. A spy hidden in the rumble of her mother’s carriage! A surveillance placed upon my young lady! I felt ashamed—ashamed that I had ever spoken to the Sergeant at all.

The first person to emerge from the house was my lady. She stood motionless on the top step, arms folded tightly across her chest, wrapped in a light garden cloak. Her lips were sealed, her face unreadable, like a statue watching the unfolding scene.

A moment later, Miss Rachel appeared—dressed in soft yellow fabric that contrasted beautifully with her dark complexion, her figure neatly shaped by a tailored jacket. A smart straw hat sat atop her head, adorned with a white veil. Primrose-colored gloves clung to her hands like a second skin. Her black hair lay smooth and satin-like beneath the hat. Her ears, delicate as rosy shells, each held a pearl drop. She moved swiftly toward us—straight as a lily, supple as a young cat.

Her face seemed unchanged—except for her eyes and lips. Her eyes burned with an intensity I found unsettling. Her lips had lost their color, their usual smile erased entirely. She kissed her mother quickly, then whispered, “Try to forgive me, mamma,” before pulling her veil down so violently that it tore.

In an instant, she raced down the steps and leapt into the carriage as if seeking refuge.

Sergeant Cuff was equally swift. He pushed Samuel aside and stood before Miss Rachel, holding the open door as she settled into her seat.

“What do you want?” she called from behind the veil.

“I wish to speak to you briefly, miss,” he replied. “Before you go. I can’t prevent you from visiting your aunt. But I must warn you—your departure now hampers my efforts to recover your Diamond. Please understand that. Now decide: stay or go.”

She didn’t answer. Instead, she shouted, “Drive on, James!”

Without another word, the Sergeant closed the door. Just as he did, Mr. Franklin rushed down the steps.

“Good-bye, Rachel,” he said, offering his hand.

“Drive on!” she called again, louder this time—ignoring him completely.

Mr. Franklin stepped back, stunned. The coachman hesitated, uncertain what to do. He glanced toward my lady, who still stood frozen on the top step. Her face bore a mixture of anger, sorrow, and shame. She gave a signal for the horses to start—and then turned abruptly into the house.

Mr. Franklin regained his voice and called after her as the carriage rolled away: “Aunt! You were right. Thank you for your kindness—and let me go.”

My lady turned as if to reply, then paused—perhaps fearing herself—and waved gently. “Let me see you before you leave, Franklin,” she said in a broken voice—and retreated to her room.

“Do me one final favor, Betteredge,” Mr. Franklin said, turning to me, tears glistening in his eyes. “Get me to the train as soon as possible.”

He too disappeared into the house. For a moment, Miss Rachel had utterly undone him. From that, judge how deeply he loved her.

Sergeant Cuff and I stood alone at the bottom of the steps. He faced a gap in the trees, scanning the winding drive. His hands were in his pockets, and he hummed softly—“The Last Rose of Summer.”

“There’s a time for everything,” I muttered, irritated. “This isn’t the time for whistling.”

At that moment, the carriage emerged from the distance, visible through the trees, heading toward the lodge gate. Another man—besides Samuel—was clearly visible in the rumble behind.

“All right,” the Sergeant murmured to himself.

He turned to me. “No time for whistling, Mr. Betteredge, as you say. It’s time to act—without mercy. We begin with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?”

We both called for him—no answer. I sent a stable-boy to find him.

“You heard what I said to Miss Verinder?” the Sergeant remarked, as we waited. “And you saw how she reacted? I warned her directly that leaving would hinder the recovery of her Diamond—and she left anyway. Your young lady has a traveling companion in her mother’s carriage, Mr. Betteredge. And the name of that companion is—the Moonstone.”

I said nothing. I clung stubbornly to my belief in Miss Rachel.

The stable-boy returned, followed by Joyce—reluctantly, it seemed.

“Where is Rosanna Spearman?” the Sergeant asked.

“I can’t explain, sir,” Joyce began, “and I’m truly sorry. But somehow—”

“Before I left for Frizinghall,” the Sergeant interrupted, “I told you to watch Rosanna without letting her know. Do you mean to say you’ve lost her?”

“I fear, sir,” Joyce stammered, trembling, “that I may have been *too* careful not to be noticed. This house has so many passages below—”

“How long ago did you lose sight of her?”

“Nearly an hour, sir.”

“You may return to your duties in Frizinghall,” the Sergeant said calmly, in his usual quiet tone. “I don’t think your talents suit our line of work. Your current role is beyond you. Good morning.”

The man slunk away.

I found it nearly impossible to describe the turmoil within me when I realized Rosanna was missing. I felt fifty conflicting thoughts at once. I stood staring at the Sergeant, speechless.

“No, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, as if reading my mind, “Rosanna won’t escape me so easily. As long as I know where Miss Verinder is, I can trace her accomplice. I prevented them from meeting last night. Very well—they’ll meet at Frizinghall instead. Our inquiry must shift—sooner than expected—from here to her aunt’s house. In the meantime, I must ask you to gather the servants once more.”

I led him to the servants’ hall. It’s shameful to admit—but true—I caught another wave of detective fever when he said that. I forgot my hatred of him. I seized his arm confidentially. “For heaven’s sake,” I urged, “what are you going to do now?”

The great Cuff froze, then turned slowly toward the empty air, speaking with a kind of melancholy reverence. “If this man,” he said, apparently referring to me, “only understood the growing of roses—he would be the most perfectly formed character on earth!”

Then he sighed, placed his arm through mine, and resumed his business tone. “Here’s the situation. Rosanna has done one of two things: either she went straight to Frizinghall—before I arrive—or she first visited her hiding place at the Shivering Sand. The first task is to determine which servant last saw her.”

After questioning the staff, it became clear: the last person to see Rosanna was Nancy, the kitchenmaid. She had watched her slip out with a letter in hand, stop the butcher’s man at the back door, and ask him to post it upon returning to Frizinghall.

The man had looked at the address—Cobb’s Hole—and remarked it was a roundabout route, especially on a Saturday, meaning delivery wouldn’t occur until Monday. Rosanna dismissed the delay, insisting only that the man follow her instructions exactly. He agreed and drove off.

Nancy returned to her duties. No one else saw Rosanna again.

“Well?” I asked, once we were alone.

“Well,” said the Sergeant. “I must go to Frizinghall.”

“About the letter?”

“Yes. The hiding-place memorandum is inside. I must check the address at the post office. If it’s the one I suspect, I’ll visit Mrs. Yolland again on Monday.”

I accompanied him to arrange the pony-chaise. In the stable-yard, we uncovered a new clue—one that shed unexpected light on the missing girl.
