It was understandable for Paulina to withhold further letters from Graham until her father gave his consent. But Dr. Bretton could not live within a mile of the Hôtel Crécy and remain untouched by the pull of her presence. At first, both were determined to keep their distance—no grand gestures, no open declarations. Yet in silence, their hearts drew closer than either had intended.

Whatever was noble in Graham stirred at the sight of Paulina. His mind, once stirred only by fleeting admiration for Miss Fanshawe, now awakened fully under her influence. His intellect—sharp, eager, hungry for meaning—found its truest nourishment in her company. He spoke casually of a book he’d been reading; she responded with a quiet resonance that struck a chord deep within him. The harmony between them surprised even himself. Each word she spoke seemed to unlock something hidden inside him—a new depth of thought, a warmth of feeling he hadn’t known before. Her voice, her manner, her very way of thinking—everything pleased him. And she, in turn, found delight in his wit, his charm, his effortless grace. Their minds met like polished stones, fitting together perfectly. Where he had always carried a natural joy, she, though usually quiet and reflective, now sparkled with a lightness as bright as dawn. In his presence, she bloomed—her beauty deepened, her spirit lifted. The reserve that once shielded her, that delicate armor of restraint, began to melt. Graham brought with him a warmth that thawed her carefully guarded heart.

They talked of the past—the old days at the Bretton home. At first, tentatively, almost shyly, then more freely, with growing honesty. Graham had built a life of his own, earned his independence without relying on the help Lucy had refused him. Now, he spoke of “little Polly” with a tenderness that made each memory feel fresh and alive. His words carried the weight of truth, spoken from the heart—not filtered through someone else’s lens. How much better this was than if I had tried to recall those moments for him.

More than once, when we were alone, Paulina would confide in me how astonishing it was to see how vividly he remembered things. As he looked at her, memories surged forward—images so clear they felt like dreams come true. He told her she used to gather his head in her arms, stroke his thick hair, and whisper, “Graham, I *do* like you!” She would bring a footstool beside him and climb up to sit on his knee. Even now, he said, he could feel the soft touch of her small hands smoothing his cheek, or burying themselves in his hair. He recalled the gentle press of her tiny finger, placed half trembling, half curious, into the cleft of his chin. She’d call it “a pretty dimple,” then look into his eyes and ask why they pierced so deeply, saying he had a “nice, strange face—far nicer, far stranger, than either his mamma or Lucy Snowe.”

“Child as I was,” she mused, “I wonder how I dared be so bold. Now he seems sacred to me—his hair is beyond reach, and Lucy, I feel a kind of awe when I look at his firm jaw, his sharp Greek features. Women are called beautiful—but he is not like a woman. So what is he? Do others see him as I do? Do *you* admire him?”

“I’ll tell you what I do, Paulina,” I replied, once, to her many questions. “_I never see him_. I glanced at him twice or three times last year, before he recognized me—and then I closed my eyes. If he passed before me twelve times between sunset and sunrise, I wouldn’t know what shape had gone by, except from memory.”

“Lucy, what do you mean?” she whispered.

“I mean I value my vision—and fear losing it completely.”

It was best to speak plainly, to shut down the tender confessions that came from her lips like honey, yet sometimes fell upon my ears like molten lead. From then on, she spoke no more of his beauty to me.

Yet she couldn’t stop speaking of him altogether. Sometimes softly, in quiet phrases; other times with a sweetness in her voice that made music itself seem tame. But often, that music grated on me—like a knife against glass. I gave her stern looks, harsh words. But she, wrapped in cloudless joy, mistook my pain for caprice.

“Spartan girl! Proud Lucy!” she would say, smiling. “Graham says you’re the most peculiar, wildest little woman he knows—but still, you’re excellent. We both think so.”

“You both think you know nothing,” I said. “Please leave me out of your thoughts and conversations. I have my own life—one apart from yours.”

“But ours, Lucy, is a beautiful life—or will be. You shall share it.”

“I shall share no one’s life in this world as you understand sharing. I may have one friend—but I’m not sure. Until I am, I live alone.”

“But solitude is sadness.”

“Yes—it is. But life has worse than that. Deeper than sorrow lies heartbreak.”

“Lucy, I wonder if anyone will ever truly understand you.”

There is a certain selfishness in love—an eagerness to have a witness to one’s happiness, no matter the cost. Paulina had forbidden letters, yet Graham wrote. She had vowed no correspondence, yet she answered—sometimes just to scold. She showed me the letters, demanding I read them, with the imperiousness of a spoiled heiress and the stubbornness of a child. As I read Graham’s words, I understood her pride. They were strong and tender, modest and brave. To him, her letters must have seemed radiant. Though she didn’t write to show off her talent—or express her love—she had set herself the task of hiding her feelings, tempering his passion. But how could such letters serve that purpose? He was already dearer than life to her. Everything he said, wrote, thought, or looked—carried an invisible power. Her letters glowed with unspoken confession, burning from greeting to farewell.

“I wish papa knew,” she murmured, again and again. “I *do* wish papa knew.” But she also feared. She couldn’t keep Graham from telling him. She longed to speak openly, to settle the matter—yet dreaded the moment. She knew he would be angry at first. He might even dislike her. It would be a shock, a surprise. She couldn’t predict how it would affect him.

And indeed, her father—long calm, long blind—was beginning to stir. A light, once ignored, now forced its way into his awareness.

To her, he said nothing. But when she wasn’t looking, I saw him watching her—quietly, deeply.

One evening, Paulina was in her dressing room, writing—likely to Graham. She had left me in the library, reading. M. de Bassompierre entered. I rose to go, but he asked me to stay—gently, but firmly. He sat near the window, some distance from me. He opened a desk and took out what looked like a journal. For several minutes, he studied one entry closely.

“Miss Snowe,” he said, closing the book, “do you know how old my daughter is?”

“About eighteen, sir?”

“It seems so. This old notebook tells me she was born May 5th, eighteen years ago. Strange—I’d lost count. I thought of her as twelve, or fourteen. A child.”

“She is about eighteen,” I repeated. “She is grown. She won’t grow taller.”

“My little jewel,” he said, in a tone that cut through me like one of her own voices.

He sat silent, lost in thought.

“Sir,” I said, knowing his grief, though it was never spoken, “don’t grieve.”

“She is the only pearl I have,” he said. “And now others will see her worth. They will want her.”

I said nothing. Graham Bretton had dined with us that day—radiant, confident, full of a quiet fire. Under the glow of hope, something in him had unfolded—something that demanded attention. I believe he meant to reveal the source of his ambition, the reason behind his efforts. M. de Bassompierre, slow to notice, was quick to reason. Once he grasped the thread, he followed it through a maze of meaning.

“Where is she?” he asked.

“Upstairs.”

“What is she doing?”

“Writing.”

“She writes? Does she receive letters?”

“Only those she can show me. And—sir—she… _they_ have long wanted to talk to you.”

“Pshaw! They don’t think of me—an old father! I’m in the way.”

“No, sir—that can’t be. But Paulina must speak for herself. And Dr. Bretton must speak for himself.”

“It is a little late. Things are moving.”

“Sir, nothing is done until you approve. Only—they love each other.”

“Only!” he echoed.

I, caught in fate’s design, had become confidante and mediator. I pressed on: “Dr. Bretton has been on the verge of speaking to you—many times. But despite his courage, he fears you terribly.”

“He may well fear me. He has touched the best thing I have. Had he left her alone, she would have stayed a child for years. So. Are they engaged?”

“They cannot be engaged without your permission.”

“It is good of you, Miss Snowe, to speak with such propriety. But this troubles me. My little girl was all I had. No more daughters. No son. Bretton might have looked elsewhere. There are many rich, pretty women who would not, I daresay, refuse him. He has looks, manners, connections. Why must it be my Polly?”

“If he had never seen your ‘Polly,’ others might have pleased him—your niece, Miss Fanshawe, for instance.”

“Ah! I would have given him Ginevra with all my heart. But Polly—I cannot let him have her. No. I cannot. He is not her equal,” he said gruffly. “In what way is he her match? They talk of fortune! I am not greedy—but the world thinks of these things. And Polly will be rich.”

“Yes, sir. All Villette knows her as an heiress.”

“Do they talk of my little girl in that way?”

“They do, sir.”

He fell into silence. I ventured: “Would you consider any man Paulina’s match? Would you prefer another to Dr. Bretton? Would higher rank or greater wealth change your feelings?”

“You touch me there,” he said.

“Look at the aristocracy of Villette—would you accept any of them?”

“I should not—never a duke, baron, or viscount among them.”

“I hear many of them think of her, sir. Other suitors will come if Dr. Bretton is refused. Wherever you go, aspirants will follow. Independent of her wealth, Paulina charms most who meet her.”

“Does she? How? My little girl is not considered beautiful.”

“Sir, Miss de Bassompierre is very beautiful.”

“Nonsense—though I beg your pardon, Miss Snowe. I think you’re too fond of her. I like Polly—I like everything about her—but I am her father. Even I never thought of beauty. She is amusing, fairy-like, interesting to me. You must be mistaken.”

“She attracts, sir. She would attract even without your wealth or position.”

“My wealth and position! Are these bait for Graham? If I thought so…”

“Dr. Bretton knows these things well, sir. He values them—as any gentleman would—as *you* would, under the same circumstances. But they are not his motives. He loves your daughter deeply. He sees her finest qualities—and they move him nobly.”

“What! Has my little pet ‘fine qualities’?”

“Ah, sir! Did you see her that evening when so many distinguished men dined here?”

“I was struck—surprised—by her manner. Its maturity made me smile.”

“And did you see the French gentlemen gather around her in the drawing room?”

“I did. But I thought it mere amusement—like playing with a pretty child.”

“Sir, she carried herself with distinction. I heard the Frenchmen say she was ‘pétrie d’esprit et de graces.’ Dr. Bretton thought the same.”

“She is a good, dear child. And I believe she has character. When I was ill, Polly nursed me. They thought I would die. She grew stronger, kinder, as I weakened. And when I recovered, she was a sunbeam in my room—light, quiet, cheerful. And now—she is sought in marriage! I don’t want to lose her,” he groaned.

“You’ve known Dr. and Mrs. Bretton so long,” I suggested. “Giving her to him would feel less like separation than giving her to anyone else.”

He pondered, darkly.

“True. I’ve known Louisa Bretton for years. She and I are old friends. A sweet, kind girl—when young. You speak of beauty, Miss Snowe! _She_ was handsome—tall, straight, blooming—not like my elfin Polly. At eighteen, Louisa had the bearing of a princess. She is comely and good now. The lad resembles her. I’ve always thought so. I favored him. Now he repays me—with this theft! My treasure loved her old father dearly. It’s over now, I suppose—I am an encumbrance.”

The door opened. His “little treasure” entered. She wore evening dress—her face flushed, her eyes bright with the warmth of twilight. A hint of summer crimson lit her cheeks. Her curls tumbled down her neck like silk. Her white gown suited the June heat. Thinking me alone, she held a letter—folded, unsealed. I was to read it. When she saw her father, her step faltered. A flush spread across her face.

“Polly,” he said, softly, with a grave smile, “do you blush at seeing papa? That’s new.”

“I don’t blush—I never *do* blush,” she insisted, though another wave of color rose. “But I thought you were in the dining room. I wanted Lucy.”

“You thought I was with John Graham Bretton, I suppose? But he’s been called away. He’ll return soon. He can post your letter—save Matthieu a ‘course.’”

“I don’t post letters,” she said, slightly annoyed.

“What do you do with them? Come here and tell me.”

Her hand hesitated—whether to come or not—but she stepped forward.

“How long have you been writing letters, Polly? It seems like yesterday you were struggling with pot-hooks, writing with both hands.”

“Papa, these aren’t letters to send. Just notes—I give them to someone directly, just to satisfy.”

“The person? That means Miss Snowe, I suppose?”

“No, papa—not Lucy.”

“Who then? Mrs. Bretton?”

“No, papa—not Mrs. Bretton.”

“Who, then, my little daughter? Tell papa the truth.”

“Oh, papa!” she exclaimed, earnestly. “I will—I *will* tell you—everything. I’m glad to—glad, though I tremble.”

She *did* tremble—excitement, emotion, courage—all shook her.

“I hate hiding things from you, papa. I fear and love you above all but God. Read the letter. Look at the address.”

She laid it on his knee. He picked it up, read it—his hand trembling, his eyes glistening.

He folded it back and looked at her with a strange, tender, mournful wonder.

“Can *she* write so—the little girl who stood at my knee yesterday? Can she feel so?”

“Papa, is it wrong? Does it hurt you?”

“There’s nothing wrong, my innocent little Mary. But it pains me.”

“But papa, listen! You mustn’t be hurt by me. I’d give up everything—almost,” she corrected herself, “I’d rather die than make you unhappy. That would be too wicked!”

She shuddered.

“Does the letter please you? Must it be torn? For your sake, I’ll destroy it, if you command.”

“I command nothing.”

“Command something, papa. Speak your wish. Only—don’t hurt Graham. I can’t bear it. I love you, papa—but I love Graham too—because—because—it’s impossible not to.”

“This splendid Graham is a young rogue, Polly—that’s my current opinion. I don’t love him one bit. Ah! Years ago, I saw something in that boy’s eye I never quite understood—something his mother lacks—a depth that warned a man not to wade too deep. Now, suddenly, I find myself dragged over my head.”

“Papa, you haven’t fallen in—you’re safe on the bank. You can do as you please. You’re the master. You could lock me in a convent, break Graham’s heart tomorrow—if you chose to be cruel. Now, autocrat, now czar—will you do it?”

“Send him to Siberia, red whiskers and all. I don’t like him, Polly. I wonder you do.”

“Papa,” she said, “you’re being very naughty. I’ve never seen you so disagreeable, so unjust, so—almost vindictive.”

“Off with him!” Mr. Home snapped, clearly annoyed—bitter, even. “But if he goes, Polly will pack a bundle and follow. Her heart is won—won, and turned from her old father.”

“Papa, you’re wrong. It’s naughty, it’s wrong, to speak like that. I am *not* turned from you. No human being, no force on earth, can turn me.”

“Be married, Polly! Marry the red whiskers. Stop being a daughter—be a wife!”

“Red whiskers! What do you mean? You should guard against prejudice. You’ve told me before that all Scots, your countrymen, are victims of bias. Now it’s proved—when no distinction is made between red and deep nut-brown.”

“Leave the prejudiced old Scot. Go.”

She stood, staring at him. She wanted to show strength, superiority. She had expected this scene—knew his ways. But dignity failed her. Suddenly, her soul broke. She threw herself into his arms.

“I won’t leave you, papa. I’ll never leave you. I won’t hurt you—I’ll never hurt you!” she cried.

“My lamb! My treasure!” he murmured, rough but loving. He said nothing more—those two words were choked.

The room darkened. I heard a movement outside. Thinking it a servant with candles, I opened the door gently. No servant stood there. A tall man was removing his hat, slowly taking off his gloves—waiting, lingering.

He said nothing, but his eyes said: “Lucy, come here.” And I did.

A smile played over his face as he looked down at me. Only his own nature could express such agitation with a smile.

“M. de Bassompierre is there—is he not?” he asked, pointing to the library.

“Yes.”

“He noticed me at dinner? He understood me?”

“Yes, Graham.”

“So I am brought up for judgment—and she too?”

“Mr. Home is talking to his daughter.”

“Ha! These are sharp moments, Lucy!”

He was agitated—his hand trembled. A vital suspense gripped him—held his breath, quickened his pulse. Yet his smile never faded.

“Is he *very* angry, Lucy?”

“_She_ is very faithful, Graham.”

“What will be done to me?”

“Graham, your star must be fortunate.”

“Must it? Kind prophet! So cheered, I should be a coward indeed to quail. I think I find all women faithful, Lucy. I ought to love them—and I do. My mother is good; _she_ is divine; and _you_—you are true as steel. Are you not?”

“Yes, Graham.”

“Then give me thy hand, my little god-sister. It is a friendly little hand—and always has been. Now for the great venture. God be with the right. Lucy, say Amen!”

He turned. I waited—then said “Amen!” to please him. The old charm returned. I wished him success—and I knew he would win. He was born a victor, as some are born defeated.

“Follow me!” he said. And I followed him into Mr. Home’s presence.

“Sir,” he asked, “what is my sentence?”

The father looked at him. The daughter kept her face hidden.

“Well, Bretton,” said Mr. Home, “you have given me the usual reward of hospitality. I entertained you—you have taken my best. I was always glad to see you—you were glad to see the one precious thing I had. You spoke fair—and yet, I will not say you *robbed* me—but I am bereaved. What I have lost, _you_, it seems, have won.”

“Sir, I cannot repent.”

“Repent! Not you! You triumph, no doubt. John Graham, you descend partly from a Highlander and a chief—and there is a trace of the Celt in all you look, speak, and think. You have his cunning, his charm. The red—(Well then, Polly, the *fair*) hair, the tongue of guile, the brain of wile—all come by inheritance.”
