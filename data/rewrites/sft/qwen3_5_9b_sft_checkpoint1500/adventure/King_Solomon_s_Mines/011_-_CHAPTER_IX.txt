The journey to Loo required no detailed chronicle. Two days traversed Solomon's Great Road, a path cutting steadily into the heart of Kukuanaland. With every mile, the land grew opulent; the kraals multiplied, encircled by broad belts of cultivation and guarded by formidable garrisons. Here, as among the Germans and the wild tribes of Africa, every able-bodied soul was a warrior, the entire nation mobilized for war. Thousands of soldiers hurried toward Loo, drawn by the promise of the great annual review, forming battalions of splendor unseen anywhere else.

At sunset on the second day, we paused atop a ridge overlooking the destination. Before us lay Loo, a native metropolis of immense scale, perhaps five miles in circumference, radiating outlying camps like spokes from a wheel. A peculiar horseshoe-shaped hill stood to the north, awaiting closer inspection. A river bisected the town, bridged at intervals, mirroring the crossings we had glimpsed from the slopes of Sheba's Breasts sixty miles distant. Beyond the plain, three massive, snow-capped peaks jutted upward, forming a triangle against the sky. Unlike the rounded gentleness of Sheba's Breasts, these mountains were sheer and precipitous.

Infadoos drew attention to them. "The road ends there," he noted, gesturing toward the Kukuanas' "Three Witches."
"Why?" I asked.
"Who knows?" he shrugged. "Caves fill the hills, and a great pit lies between them. Ancient sages traveled there seeking treasures, and now our kings rest in the Place of Death within."
"What treasures?" I pressed.
"Nay. Only those who have fallen from the Stars know," he replied, his eyes flashing with hidden knowledge. "But you, my lord, speak wisely. You may question Gagool the old at the king's place; she knows as much as you." He vanished quickly.
Turning to the others, I identified the peaks. "Solomon's diamond mines," I declared.
Umbopa, lost in one of his frequent trances, snapped awake. "Yes, Macumazahn!" he cried in Zulu. "The diamonds lie there. You white men love toys and money; you shall have them."
"How do you know this, Umbopa?" I demanded sharply, wary of his mystic air.
"I dreamed it," he laughed, turning away.
Sir Henry broke the tension. "What does our black friend mean? He hides secrets. Has he heard of my brother?"
"None have seen a white man here before," Quatermain reported. "Unless he arrived by miracle without the map, he could not have reached this place."
Henry sighed, a mixture of gloom and stubborn hope. "Yet I shall find him."

Night descended abruptly, devoid of twilight, plunging the world into shadow before the moon burst forth, casting silver arrows across the plain. The stars faded before this chastened majesty, leaving us breathless in the presence of beauty. Infadoos returned, inviting us onward. Within an hour, we stood at the outskirts of Loo, a city mapped by thousands of campfires that stretched endlessly. We crossed a drawbridge over a moat, greeted by clashing steel and hoarse challenges, passing through the central streets of this grass city.

Infadoos led us to a secluded compound of huts surrounding a courtyard of powdered limestone, designating it as our quarters. Inside, accommodations exceeded anything we had seen: beds of tanned skins on aromatic grass mattresses awaited us. Young women of striking beauty served roasted meats and maize cobs on wooden platters, bowing deeply. After washing in earthenware jars, we retired to a single hut, exhausted by our travels.

Awakening to a high noon, we found the female attendants waiting to assist us with "making ready." Good grumbled about the lack of trousers, which had been seized by the locals and taken to the king. Undeterred, we groomed ourselves the best we could. Good shaved the right side of his face, leaving a crop of whiskers on the left. Sir Henry's golden hair brushed his shoulders, giving him the likeness of an ancient Norse chieftain, while my own scrubbed beard reached an inch in length.

Before breakfast, Infadoos announced that King Twala awaited us. We feigned fatigue, delaying our departure for an hour—a prudent strategy with uncivilized nations, who often confuse haste with disrespect. During this interlude, we prepared gifts: a Winchester rifle and ammunition for the King, and beads for his court.

Guided by Infadoos, Umbopa carrying the gifts, we marched toward the palace. An enclosure, fifty times larger than our quarters, surrounded the king's domain. Rows of huts housed the king's wives, while the center was occupied by companies of warriors, totaling seven or eight thousand. They stood motionless as statues, a sea of waving plumes, glinting spears, and ox-hide shields.

Inside the throne area, we waited on stools amidst a profound silence, aware of eight thousand pairs of eyes fixed upon us. Eventually, the door opened. A giant emerged, draped in a tiger-skin kaffir, followed by the boy Scragga and a withered, monkey-like figure cloaked in fur. The giant sat, Scragga stood guard, and the figure retreated into the shade.

The giant rose, revealing a terrifying countenance. His lips were thick, his nose flat, and he possessed only one gleaming black eye; the other was a hollow void. His expression was a fusion of cruelty and sensuality. A magnificent ostrich plume crowned his head, his body armored in shining chains, adorned with ox-tails, and a massive uncut diamond burned dimly on his forehead.

Silence reigned until the King raised his javelin. Instantly, eight thousand spears lifted, and eight thousand throats roared the royal salute, "Koom!" The sound echoed like thunder. A thin voice, seemingly emanating from the monkey-figure, commanded humility, and the crowd obeyed.

Suddenly, chaos erupted. A soldier on our left dropped his shield. The King turned his single eye. "Come hither," he commanded. A youth stepped forward, trembling.
"It was thy shield that fell, thou awkward dog. Wilt thou make me a reproach in the eyes of these strangers from the Stars?"
The youth stammered, claiming it was chance.
"Prepare for death," the King roared. "Scragga, show me how thou canst use thy spear. Kill this fool."
Scragga grinned, lifted his spear, and struck. The spear pierced the soldier's back. He dropped instantly. A murmur rippled through the crowd, dying quickly. Four men carried the body away, while a girl sprinkled powdered lime over the bloodstains, erasing the evidence.

Sir Henry boiled with rage, but I held him back, whispering that our lives depended on calm.
"White people," the King addressed us, "whence do ye come, and why?"
"We come from the Stars," I replied. "We seek to see this land."
"Do ye seek a little thing?" he asked, pointing to Umbopa. "Does he also come from the Stars?"
"Yes," I assured him. "Ask not of matters too high for thee."
"You speak loudly," Twala warned, his tone sharp. "How if I make you as him whom they bore away?"
I laughed lightly, masking my fear. "O king, walk warily over hot stones. Hold the spear by the handle. Touch but one hair of our heads, and destruction shall come upon thee. Have not Infadoos and Scragga told thee what manner of men we are?"
"They have told me, but I believe them not," the King scoffed. "Let me see you kill. Slay a man among those who stand yonder, and I will believe."
"No blood of men," I countered. "Bid thy servants drive in an ox. Before he runs twenty paces, I will strike him dead."
"Nay," laughed the King. "Kill me a man."
"Good, O king, so be it," I said coolly. "Walk across the open space, and before thy feet reach the gate, thou shalt be dead. Or send thy son, Scragga."
Scragga howled and bolted into the hut. Twala frowned, relented, and ordered an ox driven in.

"Now, Sir Henry," I urged, handing him the express rifle. "Show this ruffian I am not the sole magician."
Henry aimed carefully as the ox approached the gate, confused by the vast crowd. "Now's your time!" I whispered.
*Bang!* The semi-hollow bullet struck the ox in the ribs. It collapsed, kicking on its back. A sigh of astonishment swept the assembly.
"Have I lied, O king?" I asked.
"Nay, white man, it is the truth," he admitted, awestruck.

"Listen, Twala," I continued. "We come in peace. Behold," I displayed the Winchester. "Here is a hollow staff that enables thee to kill, yet I lay a charm upon it: if thou lift it against a man, it shall kill *thee*."
I demonstrated by firing at a spear planted in the ground, shattering its blade effortlessly.
