"How can I not follow?" Amyas declared, his voice echoing with a stubborn resolve that belied the chaos surrounding him. "For how long have you loved and trusted us, truly? Yet, I would willingly take the yellow fever myself if it spared me from this madness, but there is no time for sickness while men lie dying."

His reasoning was sound, though born of necessity rather than philosophy. A man who remains idle while his comrades fall succumbs quickly to the disease; the spirit that thrives on labor inevitably collapses when the work ceases. By the following morning, however, the panic had seized the crew, bordering on mutiny. Amyas called a council—not merely a sanitary commission, but a desperate assembly of souls at their wits' end. He argued that killing him offered no advantage, nor did his potential demise, yet the men were paralyzed by fear.

Below deck, the doctors and seamen offered a cacophony of useless advice. The physician spoke of humors and complexions in terms that sounded more like nonsense than medicine. Jack Brimblecombe, a man of pulpit, attributed the plague to divine visitation. Cary, plagued by despair, masked his terror with a nervous smile. Yeo, the old veteran, adopted a stoic fatalism, quoting scripture to justify whatever horrors befell them. Master Drew remained silent, retreating behind the technicality that his business was to sail the vessel, not to cure fevers.

Seeing the futility of their counsel, Amyas pulled at his hair, breaking his usual composure. "Doctor!" he cried, his voice rising. "A fig for your humors! Can you alter a man's complexion? Can an Ethiopian change his skin, or a leopard its spots? Do not shift your ignorance onto God, sir. Tell me the cause of this sickness, and you know none. Jack Brimblecombe, stop speaking of God's visitation; this feels far more like the Devil's work. We serve God, Sir John, and He would not hinder us. Down with the devil, I say."

Turning to Cary, he added, "Laughter kills the cat, but it cures no Christian." To Yeo, he challenged, "When an angel tells me it is God's will for us to die like dogs in a ditch, I will accept it—but not today." To Drew, he pressed, "You say your duty is sailing; then sail her out of this infernal poison-trap this very morning."

Silence fell over the group. Then Amyas struck his final blow, revealing the truth he had observed. "Tell me why the boat's crew fell first. Because the mist, when we crossed it, barely rose above the stream, enveloping us, while you aboard remained dry. Those who succumbed today slept on the main deck, exposed to the air, and lived in constant fear. Therefore, two rules apply: keep as high as you can, and fear nothing but God."

"But the fog reached the round-tops at sunrise," Cary protested weakly.

"I know it," Amyas replied firmly. "Those on the half-deck were not trapped in it as long as those below. Furthermore, the heat of the evening draws the poison from the air, releasing it as the temperature drops."

Common sense, though lacking scientific proof, guided Yeo to speak. "Many times the Lord sends us a young Daniel for judgment," he recalled, citing the Spanish lore that such fevers lay only in low grounds, rarely rising hundreds of feet above the sea.

"Then let us ascend those few hundred feet," Amyas urged. "Gentlemen, look the devil straight in the face if you wish to strike him. We cannot launch the ship as she sits, nor return home empty-handed, nor remain here to die. We must leave the ship and move inland."

"Inland?" the voices echoed, confused.

"To the mountains!" Amyas shouted, pointing toward the obscured horizon. "Stockade a camp, gather the sick and supplies, and when we recover, march over the peaks to surprise St. Jago de Leon."

Cary swore a mighty oath. "Amyas, you are a daring fellow!"

"Not a bit," Amyas corrected. "It is the plain path of prudence." Old Yeo nodded, "And I follow you," while Jack Brimblecombe squeaked his agreement. Even Master Drew pledged his life to the venture, noting that Sir Francis would have advised the same.

Orders were given to depart within the hour. The Pelicans and the freed galley slaves rallied immediately, but the rest of the crew erupted in a storm of objections. Their central question haunted them: Where were the hills? Lost in a dense mangrove thicket, visibility did not extend beyond fifty yards.

"The hills are not three miles southwest of you," Amyas insisted, his tone sharp. "I marked every ridge as we entered."

"And you mean to lead us there?" a sailor demanded, suspicion igniting in his eyes.

Angry rumors spread like wildfire, but Amyas countered them with a stern warning. "Fools! If I lacked the wit to look ahead, where would you be? Are you mad and reckless to rise against your captain? Go with me, or by my life, I will blow up this ship and every soul upon it, sparing you the slow agony of rotting inch by inch."

The men knew Amyas never threatened lightly. The threat sufficed, and they agreed to follow, reassured by the hearty cooperation of the Pelicans' veterans.

Five or six weary hours slipped away under a broiling sun. They stumbled over mangrove roots, hacked through thorny thickets, and dragged the sick and provisions up steep mountain paths amidst curses, snakes, mosquitoes, and false alarms of Spanish ambushes. By sunset, they reached a level plateau a thousand feet above the sea, backed by an inaccessible cliff—the upper shoulder of a massive mountain. Defended by steep, wooded slopes, it required only the felling of a few trees to become impregnable.

Amyas settled the sick beneath the arched roots of an enormous cottonwood tree, then returned to the ship for hammocks and blankets. Yeo's wisdom proved invaluable; as a pioneer, he had located a small brook and convinced them to scale the cliffs, knowing water meant survival. He supported Amyas when the exhausted crew begged to turn back, climbing repeatedly to cheer them onward. Cary, bringing up the rear, bullied the stragglers into motion, carrying burdens and singing songs to keep morale high.

As Amyas walked beside Cary, filled with joy at finding these two men worthy of his trust, he whispered a secret plan. "Cortez burned his ships when he landed. Why should not we?"

Yeo jumped upright, then sat down again, whispering back, "Do you say that, Captain? It comes from above, surely; it has been haunting me all day."

"There is no hurry," Amyas said calmly. "First, we must clear the ship." Cary sat silent, musing, sensing schemes deeper than anyone dared imagine.

That evening, the men rested, but before dawn, Amyas had them fortifying their position. The site was naturally strong, commanded by high cliffs on three sides, shielded from behind by a dense wall of trees. An attack could only come from below. Amyas ordered the smaller trees felled and laid crosswise with boughs outward, creating an *abatis*—a deadly barrier that would stop horses, dogs, and men alike. Trunks were sawn into logs and stabilized with stakes, finishing a stockade capable of defying anything but heavy artillery.

Once the work was done, Amyas climbed into the branches of the colossal ceiba tree, sitting like a sentinel atop a tower. From this vantage, he surveyed the forest-covered plains and the blue sea beyond. Staying long was impossible; avenging La Guayra was futile; searching for Frank seemed equally hopeless, especially if he had fallen into the hands of the Inquisition. To sacrifice eighty men to his private grief would be a sin. Tears of rage and earnest prayer washed over him before he resolved to move.

Whither? For hours, he thought alone in his aerial nest before descending to draw Cary and Yeo aside. Refitting the ship was suicide, and fighting the Spaniards at St. Jago, who had likely fortified themselves, was a losing gamble.

"Let us try St. Jago, then," Amyas proposed, "sack it, descend on La Guayra, seize a ship, and return home."

"Nay, Will," Cary sighed, looking up at the vast walls of wood and rock. "If they strengthened themselves at La Guayra, surely they have done so at St. Jago. Besides, how can we cross these mountains without a guide?"

"What if I had a still more daring plan?" Amyas asked, a glint in his eye. "Did you ever hear of the Golden City of Manoa?"

Yeo laughed a grim, joyful laugh. "I have, sir, and so have the old hands from the *Pelican* and the *Jesus of Lübeck*."

Amyas began to recount what he had learned from a Spaniard, weaving tales of a civilization superior to Europe, a city standing on a lake, and mountains teeming with gold. Cary sat half-aghast as the phantasmagoria unfolded, and finally, the realization dawned.

"So that is why you wanted to burn the ship!" Cary exclaimed. "Well, after all, nobody needs me at home, and one less at table won't be missed. You want to play Cortez, eh?"
