The effect was immediate and astonishing, so sudden that even the marksman stood stunned for a moment. Then, recovering himself, he called to his companions, urging them to join in the pursuit, and began to climb the hill. But before he had reached halfway, a figure emerged from the trees—Martin Wittenhaagen, bow drawn and arrow nocked. Eric Wouverman, seeing him, darted behind a nearby tree, pressing himself flat against the bark, trying to vanish into the shadow. Martin’s skill with the longbow was legendary, and the dead dog lying at their feet was proof enough of his deadly precision.

Wouverman peered cautiously around the trunk. The tip of the arrow still trembled, aimed directly at him. He could see its gleam in the sunlight. He dared not stir. Minutes passed as he waited, breath held, heart pounding. At last, his comrades arrived in force, shouting and panting from the climb.

Then, with a mocking laugh, Martin disappeared into the woods. Moments later, the sound of hooves echoed—the mule carrying him away at a brisk pace.

All the hunters surged forward, scrambling up the slope. From the high ground, they saw the narrow glade stretching ahead, seemingly endless. Far off, tiny figures raced along—Gerard and Margaret, fleeing at breakneck speed. They looked like insects on the horizon. Behind them, Martin galloped full tilt, riding low over the saddle, every muscle taut with effort.

The hunters were both outwitted and outrun.

To understand Martin’s actions, one must look beyond mere chance. Coincidences often deceive, just as we might once have seen a hare stumble over a thornbush the instant a gun fired, only to watch it leap away unharmed. So too, the burgomaster’s mule stepped into a rabbit hole at nearly the same moment the crossbow bolt whistled harmlessly overhead. She faltered, then collapsed, throwing both riders to the ground.

Gerard caught Margaret in his arms, but her weight and momentum pulled him down with her. And there, scattered across the earth, lay the players in this unfolding drama.

The mule rose quickly, trembling but unharmed. Martin was the first to stand. Glancing about, he saw only one pursuer closing in. Instantly, he seized the opportunity. He made the young lovers flee on foot while he confronted the enemy—just as described.

Now, he rode after his companions, catching up only after a long, grueling chase. Without hesitation, he lifted Gerard and Margaret onto the mule, then ran beside it until exhaustion claimed him. Then he handed the reins to one of them, took his turn to ride, and so they rotated—always keeping one fresh runner at the front.

In this way, their pace never slackened. Long before the hunters slowed, every trace of the fugitives had vanished into the forest. Not a sound, not a footprint remained.

Dierich and his men returned disheartened, left to tend to their fallen leader and the useless hound whose keen senses had failed them.
