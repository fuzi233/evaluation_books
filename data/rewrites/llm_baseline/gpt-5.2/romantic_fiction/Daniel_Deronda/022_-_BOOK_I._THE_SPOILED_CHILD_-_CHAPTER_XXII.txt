Gwendolen’s little note came to Herr Klesmer as he was stepping into his carriage at Quetcham—one of those messages that arrive with the neatness of a well-timed course at dinner, neither raw with haste nor over-kept with delay. To answer it meant discomfort and rearrangement; he must sleep at Wanchester, though every hour he lingered under the Arrowpoints’ roof had begun to scald him. The house was too splendid, too full—hot with other people’s expectations, as kitchens grow close when too many hands crowd the hearth.

Quetcham that season held, as it always did, an abundant company; and among them Mrs. Arrowpoint had already distinguished a fresh claimant for Catherine’s fortune, as neatly as she might have pinned a card to an entrée. Mr. Bult came of good family, spoke as if a peerage were waiting in the sideboard drawer, and carried about him the wholesome pink solidity of a man who believed the world’s business to be done, like carving, with a steady wrist and no need of finer senses. He had strong opinions on distant rivers and remoter islands, and the air of one who could talk through a whole dinner without once hearing the room. To Mrs. Arrowpoint he had the flavor of suitability; to Catherine he was merely tiresome—dull as bread baked without salt.

Catherine had long disturbed her parents by the simple trouble of being clear-headed. She did not accept the creed—held by those who had money and meant to keep their rank—that an heiress must marry where her fortune ought to be lodged, as if she herself were but the silver dish beneath it. The Arrowpoints had been uneasy at her repeated refusals; but they were not uneasy about Klesmer. That blindness, so common in households as in politics, was ready at hand: people are amazed when the consequence arrives that their own arrangements have been preparing all along.

For a year Klesmer had been in and out of their lives—town and country—no more restricted than the seasons. It began, as such things often do, with an engagement that looked innocent under the light of money: Catherine’s musical talent asked for the best instruction; wealth could buy a great musician’s presence as it bought a conservatory or a tapestry. Mr. Arrowpoint’s large cheque seemed to make Klesmer safe, as if he were a splendid instrument locked in its case between lessons. Where marriage is “inconceivable,” people think a girl’s feelings are insured against risk.

But life does not take its pattern from what is conceivable to parents.

Klesmer was a man of honor; and yet love seldom begins with a formal proposal any more than a sauce begins with the last stir. It begins earlier—quietly—when heat and time do their work. Catherine’s mind and ear were so finely true, her judgment so quick and unpretending, that intimacy with her had the charm of finding, day by day, a new herb in familiar ground—a fragrance not shouted from a distance, but rising when you bruise the leaf. She was not an acknowledged beauty; and yet the strongest beauty is often that which appears only after sympathy has taught the eye what to look for: the play of meaning that gathers in a smile that never comes wrongly; the dignity that settles in a face where perception is alive. Klesmer, with all his susceptibility to obvious loveliness, was won by that deeper charm—by the sense that no intention would be wasted on her, that her understanding met his like a hand that knows the right pressure in kneading, neither slack nor brutal.

Between them there had been, at first, a safety of full cups: each feeling enough to be silent, and silent enough to pretend nothing overflowed. Klesmer could not imagine that an heiress would think of him as a lover; Catherine was not used to thinking herself capable of rousing more than friendship, or of receiving any passion that was not tied to her fortune like a label. Yet he knew—he knew as surely as a man knows the difference between hunger and appetite—that if she had been poor he would have spoken out long ago, instead of flinging storms through the piano or folding his arms and firing off a tirade on some impersonal pole of the earth. And she knew—quietly, with a kind of steady wonder—that if it had been possible for him to wish for her hand, she would have found reasons strong enough to take it.

Of late, the hidden fire had altered its color. Klesmer’s brusquerie grew more frequent, and Catherine, though naturally kind, was firm enough to resent it: she saw in it an unnecessary stamping of superiority, as if he must keep proving himself master even where he was already obeyed. And in this visit to Quetcham he had begun to taste departure—had begun to tell himself he would not come again.

Then Mr. Bult arrived, like a joint set down at table with the certainty of being eaten. He was all amiable confidence. He could not feel counterpoint; he could not be made to hear it; and he did not conceive that such insensibility could count against him. He regarded Klesmer as a clever foreign appendage—hardly a serious human creature with claims beyond entertainment—and Miss Arrowpoint’s devotion to music seemed to him a harmless feminine expense, like antique lace.

At dinner one evening, something in the talk—English politics, markets, distant races treated as ledgers—set Klesmer alight. His words burst out like fireworks lit by accident: he spoke of the lack of idealism, of the meanness of treating mutuality between peoples as mere buying and selling, of how even the crusades had at least carried a banner of sentiment that could rally generous feeling—scoundrels too, yes, but the generous had something to gather round, unlike the modern van shouting “Buy cheap, sell dear.” He flamed for a few moments; then, as suddenly, he sank into a silence as immovable as stone.

Mr. Bult was not surprised that a musician should be flighty; what amazed him was Klesmer’s command of English idiom—his power to point a sentence so it might have told at a constituents’ dinner. He explained it to himself as he explained most foreign phenomena: fermenting refugeeism.

Later in the drawing-room, Klesmer at the piano and Catherine near, Mr. Bult approached with a large good-nature, as if condescending to taste a dish he suspected was not nourishing.

“I had no idea before that you were a political man,” he said.

Klesmer folded his arms, thrust out his lower lip, and stared at him.

“You must have been used to public speaking. You speak uncommonly well, though I don’t agree with you. From what you said about sentiment, I fancy you are a Panslavist.”

“No; my name is Elijah. I am the Wandering Jew,” Klesmer answered, and flashed a smile at Catherine—then ran his hands in a swift, wind-like rush over the keys, a sound like something passing through reeds at night.

Mr. Bult found the buffoonery offensive, and, to his mind, suitably Polish; but Catherine’s presence kept him rooted.

“Herr Klesmer has cosmopolitan ideas,” Catherine said, gently, trying to lay a cloth over the awkwardness. “He looks forward to a fusion of races.”

“With all my heart,” said Mr. Bult, ready to be gracious. “I was sure he had too much talent to be a mere musician.”

Klesmer’s temper sprang as if struck on flint.

“Ah, sir, you are under some mistake there. No man has too much talent to be a musician. Most men have too little. A creative artist is no more a mere musician than a great statesman is a mere politician. We are not ingenious puppets, sir, who live in a box and look out on the world only when it gapes for amusement. We help to rule the nations and make the age as much as any other public men. We count ourselves on level benches with legislators. And a man who speaks effectively through music is compelled to something more difficult than parliamentary eloquence.”

With that he turned from the piano and walked away.

Catherine colored. Mr. Bult, with his heavy stolidity, observed, “Your pianist does not think small beer of himself.”

“Herr Klesmer is something more than a pianist,” Catherine said, with apology in her tone, as if smoothing a wrinkled napkin. “He is a great musician in the fullest sense. He will rank with Schubert and Mendelssohn.”

“Ah, you ladies understand these things,” Mr. Bult replied, none the less confirmed that such things were frivolous—especially when they came with vanity.

The next day Catherine found Klesmer alone in the music-room, scores spread before him like a cook’s clean mise en place: ordered, precise, ready to be transformed by heat and touch. Outside, Quetcham’s life went on with its muffled comforts—the far wheels on gravel, the faint tap of a door, the soft rustle of gowns. Within, the room held that particular hush that belongs to places where sound is made deliberately. Catherine, who always felt the air of a room as much as she heard it, had been thinking how violently his outburst had struck the household’s acoustics: the dinner-table noise had swallowed his passion as a busy dining-room swallows the fine crackle of a small flame.

“Why were you so heated last night with Mr. Bult?” she asked. “He meant no harm.”

“You wish me to be complaisant to him?” Klesmer said, fiercely.

“I think it is hardly worth your while to be other than civil.”

“You find no difficulty in tolerating him, then?—you have a respect for a political platitudinarian as insensible as an ox to everything he can’t turn into political capital. You think his monumental obtuseness suited to the dignity of the English gentleman.”

“I did not say that.”

“You mean that I acted without dignity, and you are offended with me.”

“Now you are slightly nearer the truth,” Catherine said, smiling—a small smile that tried to keep the room gentle.

“Then I had better put my burial-clothes in my portmanteau and set off at once.”

“I don’t see that. If I have to bear your criticism of my operetta, you should not mind my criticism of your impatience.”

“But I do mind it. You would have wished me to take his ignorant impertinence about a ‘mere musician’ without letting him know his place. I am to hear my gods blasphemed as well as myself insulted. But I beg pardon. It is impossible you should see the matter as I do. Even you can’t understand the wrath of the artist: he is of another caste for you.”

“That is true,” Catherine said, and the words came out with a tremor she could not keep from them. “He is of a caste to which I look up—a caste above mine.”

Klesmer, who had been seated, started up as if something in him had been struck awake. He walked away a little, then spoke from that distance—like a man who must place his heart across the room before he dares touch it.

“That is finely felt—I am grateful. But I had better go, all the same. I have made up my mind to go, for good and all. You can get on exceedingly well without me: your operetta is on wheels—it will go of itself. And your Mr. Bull’s company fits me wie die Faust ins Auge. I am neglecting my engagements. I must go off to St. Petersburg.”

Catherine made no answer.

“You agree with me that I had better go?” he said, irritation sharpening him, because silence is sometimes louder than speech.

“Certainly; if that is what your business and feeling prompt,” she replied at last, coolness returning as a defense. “I have only to wonder that you have consented to give us so much of your time in the last year. There must be treble the interest to you anywhere else. I have never thought of you consenting to come here as anything else than a sacrifice.”

“Why should I make the sacrifice?” Klesmer said. He sat down at the piano and, with the delicacy of something heard as if from a far room, touched out a melody he had set to Heine—Ich hab’ dich geliebet und liebe dich noch—each note like a taste remembered when the dish is gone.

“That is the mystery,” Catherine said. She would not pretend; but agitation made her hands seek refuge in a childish action. She began tearing a piece of paper into the smallest morsels, as if some cruel fairy had set her the task of making infinity from a single sheet.

“You can conceive no motive?” Klesmer asked, folding his arms again.

“None that seems in the least probable.”

“Then I shall tell you. It is because you are to me the chief woman in the world—the throned lady whose colors I carry between my heart and my armor.”

Her fingers trembled so that the paper would no longer tear. Her lips could not form a word. The room seemed full of sound though nothing moved but his breath.

“This would be the last impertinence in me,” he went on, “if I meant to found anything upon it. That is out of the question. I meant no such thing. But you once said it was your doom to suspect every man who courted you of being an adventurer, and what made you angriest was men’s imputing to you the folly of believing that they courted you for your own sake. Did you not say so?”

“Very likely,” Catherine murmured.

“It was a bitter word. Well, at least one man—who has seen women as plenty as flowers in May—has lingered about you for your own sake. And since he is one whom you can never marry, you will believe him. There is an argument in favor of some other man. But don’t give yourself for a meal to a minotaur like Bult. I shall go now and pack. I shall make my excuses to Mrs. Arrowpoint.”

He rose and moved quickly toward the door, as if flight were the only honorable way to keep from taking what he desired.

“You must take this heap of manuscript,” Catherine said suddenly—desperate, practical, snatching at the only thing that could detain him. She had risen and brought the folio sheets from the other table. When he came back, the thick papers lay between them like a barrier and a bridge at once.

“Why should I not marry the man who loves me, if I love him?” Catherine said. The words were a leap—like a woman springing from a deck into the lifeboat, not knowing whether the water will strike like stone.

“It would be too hard—impossible—you could not carry it through,” Klesmer answered at once. “I am not worth what you would have to encounter. I will not accept the sacrifice. It would be thought a mésalliance for you, and I should be liable to the worst accusations.”

“Is it the accusations you are afraid of?” Catherine said. Her voice steadied on the truth she had been circling. “I am afraid of nothing but that we should miss the passing of our lives together.”

After that decisive word, there was no longer doubt about what each willed. Only the road remained; and Catherine, who in music preferred the plain right progression to any affected flourish, chose the straightest course. She went to the library where her father and mother were, and told them she had promised to marry Klesmer.

Mrs. Arrowpoint’s mind, under the shock, was a spectacle of ruined self-consistency. She who had written with lofty indignation of the arts and their corruptions—she who had safely admired tragic lovers on paper—found the admired defiance stepping, alive and unmanageable, on to her own carpet. It is one thing to serve sentiment as a garnish in literature, another to swallow it as a daily food. She wanted the best of everything—fine feeling and fine position; it pleased her to be above her circle in eloquence, but not to be below it in “consideration.” While Klesmer was a patronized musician his oddities were picturesque, like spices used sparingly; to see him suddenly as a son-in-law was to smell the whole jar overturned, and with it the imagined cries of society.

Anger, pure and immediate, seized her like a fit.

“If Klesmer has presumed to offer himself to you, your father shall horsewhip him off the premises. Pray, speak, Mr. Arrowpoint.”

Mr. Arrowpoint took his cigar from his mouth and stood up, roused with difficulty to a proper paternal emphasis.

“This will never do, Cath.”

“Do!” Mrs. Arrowpoint cried. “Who in their senses ever thought it would do? You might as well say poisoning and strangling will not do. It is a comedy you have got up, Catherine. Else you are mad.”

“I am quite sane and serious, mamma, and Herr Klesmer is not to blame,” Catherine said, as steadily as she could. “He never thought of my marrying him. I found out that he loved me, and loving him, I told him I would marry him.”

“Leave that unsaid, Catherine,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, bitterly. “Every one else will say that for you. You will be a public fable. Every one will say that you must have made an offer to a man who has been paid to come to the house—who is nobody knows what—a gypsy, a Jew, a mere bubble of the earth.”

“Never mind, mamma,” Catherine said, indignation warming her. “We all know he is a genius—as Tasso was.”

“Those times were not these, nor is Klesmer Tasso,” Mrs. Arrowpoint retorted, the heat rising. “There is no sting in that sarcasm, except the sting of undutifulness.”

“I am sorry to hurt you, mamma. But I will not give up the happiness of my life to ideas that I don’t believe in and customs I have no respect for.”

“You have lost all sense of duty, then? You have forgotten that you are our only child—that it lies with you to place a great property in the right hands?”

“What are the right hands?” Catherine said. “My grandfather gained the property in trade.”

“Mr. Arrowpoint, will you sit by and hear this without speaking?”

Mr. Arrowpoint, who disliked scenes as he disliked sour wine, made his effort.

“I am a gentleman, Cath. We expect you to marry a gentleman.”

“And a man connected with the institutions of this country,” Mrs. Arrowpoint added quickly, seizing the larger phrase as if it were a stronger weapon. “A woman in your position has serious duties. Where duty and inclination clash, she must follow duty.”

“I don’t deny that,” Catherine said, growing colder as her mother grew hotter, as if she must cool herself to keep from breaking. “But one may say very true things and apply them falsely. People can easily take the sacred word duty as a name for what they desire any one else to do.”

“Your parent’s desire makes no duty for you, then?”

“Yes, within reason. But before I give up the happiness of my life—”

“Catherine, Catherine, it will not be your happiness,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, in a tone like a croak of prophecy.

“Well, what seems to me my happiness—before I give it up, I must see some better reason than the wish that I should marry a nobleman, or a man who votes with a party that he may be turned into a nobleman. I feel at liberty to marry the man I love and think worthy, unless some higher duty forbids.”

“And so it does, Catherine, though you are blinded and cannot see it. It is a woman’s duty not to lower herself. You are lowering yourself. Mr. Arrowpoint, will you tell your daughter what is her duty?”

“You must see, Catherine, that Klesmer is not the man for you,” Mr. Arrowpoint said. “He won’t do at the head of estates. He has a deuced foreign look—is an unpractical man.”

“I really can’t see what that has to do with it, papa,” Catherine answered. “The land of England has often passed into the hands of foreigners—Dutch soldiers, sons of foreign women of bad character:—if our land were sold to-morrow it would very likely pass into the hands of some foreign merchant on ’Change. It is in everybody’s mouth that successful swindlers may buy up half the land in the country. How can I stem that tide?”
