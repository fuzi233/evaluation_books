CHAPTER LV. “Return to your science,
That which is more perfect feels the good the more,
And thus the sorrow.” —DANTE.

When Daniel Deronda met Gwendolen and Grandcourt on the hotel staircase, his thoughts were deeply entwined with emotion and uncertainty. He had just returned from a second meeting with his mother—a conversation that had left him shaken, not by words spoken, but by what they revealed: the Princess Halm-Eberstein had already departed Genoa. In theory, he could have set off immediately for Mainz—his mission clear: deliver Joseph Kalonymos’s letter and claim the family chest that held secrets of his lineage. Yet something within him refused to move.

Two hours after parting from his mother, he remained rooted in place, caught between past and future, memory and duty. The weight of what he had learned pressed upon him like a storm at sea—unseen, yet powerful. He relived every moment of their encounter, not as history, but as living sensation, each detail sharpened by the pain of recognition. He mourned for her life—not only because it was near to his own, but because it was so utterly apart. He saw the world anew, transformed by the knowledge of bonds long hidden, ties that shifted the balance of hope and fear within him. It felt as though, in darkness, he had wandered into a foreign camp, only to awaken at dawn and find the true tents of his kin standing far away, faintly visible across the plain.

He imagined his grandfather—the man whose name was now linked to his own—not merely as an ancestor, but as a kindred spirit, stirred awake within him. The old man had lived with passion and purpose; perhaps those same fires were now stirring in Daniel’s soul. And through all this inner turmoil, Mordecai and Mirah stood beside him, silent companions in thought, their presence a quiet strength, a reminder that he was not alone in seeking truth.

Deronda was made of such sensitive fiber—so finely tuned to feeling—that even beneath his calm exterior, his heart beat with deep sensitivity. His early years had taught him reserve, but they had also forged a soul capable of profound empathy. When the bell rang again, marking the hour for departure, he reached for *Bradshaw*, the railway guide, intending to plan his journey. But his hand fell still. He did not pack. He did not summon the porter.

His mind drifted instead—to Mainz, where treasures awaited; to London, where friends and responsibilities called. But another longing held him fast: the desire to stay one night longer, to attend the evening service at the synagogue, where the ancient prayers might echo the voice of his ancestors. And then there was Gwendolen—her image rose unbidden, haunting him with a mixture of regret and concern. He had parted from her abruptly, sharply, and now, in this quiet space of time, he found himself wondering if it would be cruel to leave without offering some sign of care, some gesture of enduring kindness—despite Grandcourt’s likely disapproval.

So he stayed. He ate dinner, but tasted nothing. He rose quickly, drawn toward the synagogue, and paused only to ask the porter if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt were still at the hotel. The porter gave him their room number—but added, with a glance over his shoulder, that they had gone boating.

The news unsettled him. Not because he feared danger, but because it stirred a premonition—an unease that whispered of finality. As he stepped into the synagogue, the air thick with the scent of candle wax and old wood, he heard the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy rise in solemn waves. The worshippers, dark-faced and sparse in number, moved through prayer with a strange ease, as if sacred words were familiar as breath. Their reverence felt both alien and intimate—like a thread connecting generations across continents. Daniel thought of his grandfather, whom the Princess had described as a man of rare depth and conviction. Was he, too, an exception among Jews—as Mordecai was? Or were all great souls exceptions—men who dreamed beyond their times, whose visions shaped the world while others merely endured?

A melancholy impatience grew within him, mingling with his worry for Gwendolen. He left the synagogue earlier than intended, choosing instead to walk along the quay—a place he often visited, but now imbued with new meaning. Perhaps, he thought, he might catch sight of the Grandcourts returning. If so, he would greet them boldly, regardless of any coldness Grandcourt might show.

The sun had slipped behind clouds, casting a soft yellow glow over the restless sea. A breeze stirred the water, sending ripples across the surface like whispers. Deronda walked slowly, watching the shore. Then he saw it—a small sailing boat approaching rapidly, rowed by two men. A crowd gathered, voices rising in confusion. Some pointed with telescopes; others shouted. One insisted the boat carried a drowned man—the *milord*. Another swore it was the lady, lying prostrate. A Frenchman joked that perhaps the husband had taken his wife out to drown her, "according to national practice"—a remark met with laughter and swift dismissal by an English sailor, who declared plainly: “It’s a woman.”

Daniel’s heart pounded. Every stroke of the oars seemed to split his mind into visions—some plausible, others terrible. What had happened? Had Grandcourt fallen? Had she followed him into the water? Was this the end of everything?

Then the boat neared the shore. And there, beneath layers of tarpaulin and heavy coats, Daniel saw her—Gwendolen. She raised herself slightly on her hands, pale as death, her hair drenched and clinging to her face. Her eyes were wide, filled with shock, as if she had awakened in a world turned upside down, where judgment loomed and strangers closed in around her. The sailors helped her ashore, their rough hands gentle in their urgency. Her clothes clung to her limbs, soaked and heavy, making each step a struggle.

Then—she saw him. Her gaze locked onto his, and instantly, as if she had been waiting for him all along, she reached out her arms—only to be held back. In a voice barely above a whisper, broken and trembling, she said: “It is come, it is come! He is dead!”

“Hush,” Daniel said, stepping forward, his tone firm, calming. “Quiet yourself.”

To the men around her, he spoke clearly: “I am connected to this lady’s husband. Please bring her aboard the *Italia* at once—I will handle everything else.”

He waited until the last sailor confirmed the truth: Grandcourt had gone down—gone forever. His boat floated empty, abandoned. The two men had heard a cry, rushed to the scene, and seen Gwendolen leap into the water after him. They had pulled her out just in time.

With that, Daniel hurried back to the hotel. He ensured medical help would arrive swiftly. Then he sent telegrams—first to Sir Hugo, urging him to come without delay, then to Mr. Gascoigne at the rectory, knowing he was the closest link to Gwendolen’s mother.

As he wrote, certain words from her past echoed in his mind—words spoken in moments of vulnerability, when she had confessed her fears, her loneliness. She had once said her mother might help—if only she could reach her.

Now, Daniel understood. The moment had come. And he would not let her face it alone.
