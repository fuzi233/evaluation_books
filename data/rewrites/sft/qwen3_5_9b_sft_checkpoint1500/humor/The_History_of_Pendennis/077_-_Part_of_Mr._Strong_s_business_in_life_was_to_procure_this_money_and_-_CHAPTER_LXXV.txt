A Chapter of Match-Making

Upon the platform at Tunbridge, Pen swelled with indignation and impatience until the evening express thundered into London, a full half-hour later—or six hours, if one counted by the pounding of his heart. Yet even this vast interval vanished like mist before the sun; the train roared forward, the golden lights of London pierced the gloom, and a gentleman who had forgotten his carpet-bag leaped into a cab, shouting, "Drive as hard as you can go to Jermyn Street!" The cabman, though a Hansom-man, bowed gratefully to the tip and clattered away. Upstairs, Pen burst into Lady Rockminster's apartment.

Laura sat alone in the drawing-room, reading by a lamp with a pallor that seemed to glow with its own internal light. When the door swung open, she raised her eyes. *May we follow?* The great moments of life are often decided in seconds. A glance, a touch of the hand, a whisper of the lips—it all hangs in the balance.

Lady Rockminster, having enjoyed her post-prandial siesta, rose and glided into the sitting-room. As she crossed the threshold, the narrator invites you to follow suit. "Upon my word, young people!" was her first exclamation, and her attendant, peeking over her shoulder, goggled in wonder. Indeed, the young pair were in an attitude fit for a painting; Pen stood in a posture that every maiden of the age had either heard described, witnessed, dreamed of, or felt entitled to behold.

Directly inside, Pen marched straight to Laura, whose pale face held no time for the question, *"What, back so soon?"* Seizing her outstretched, trembling hand as she rose from her chair, he dropped to his knees. "I have seen her!" he cried breathlessly. "She has engaged herself to Harry Foker—and—and Now, Laura?"

The hand pressed gently—the eyes flashed a silent answer—the lips quivered without sound. Pen sank his head into the girl's lap, sobbing, "Come and bless us, dear mother," while arms as tender as Helen's once more enveloped him.

Just then, Lady Rockminster entered, declaring, "Upon my word, young people! Beck! leave the room. What do you want poking your nose in here?"

Pen sprang upright, still clutching Laura's hand, his face alight with triumph. "She is consoling me for my misfortune, ma'am," he declared.

"What do you mean by kissing her hand? I don't know what you will be next doing."

Pen kissed her Ladyship's fingers. "I have been to Tunbridge," he explained, adopting a tragic air, "and seen Miss Amory. I found, upon my return, that a villain has transplanted me in her affections."

"Is that all? Is that what you were whimpering on your knees about?" the old lady demanded, her temper flaring. "You might have kept the news till tomorrow."

"Yes—another has superseded me," Pen continued. "But why call him a villain? He is brave, constant, young, wealthy, and beautiful."

"What nonsense are you spouting, sir?" cried the old lady. "What has happened?"

"Miss Amory has jilted me and accepted Henry Foker, Esquire," Pen recited. "I found her warbling ditties to him as he lay at her feet. Presents had been accepted, vows exchanged, within these ten days. Harry was merely old Mrs. Planter's rheumatism, a condition that kept dearest Laura out of the house. He is the most constant and generous of men. He promised the living of Logwood to Lady Ann's husband and gifted her a splendid present upon her marriage; and the moment he realized he was free, he rushed to fling himself at Blanche's feet."

"And so, since you cannot have Blanche, you settle for Laura, is that it, sir?" the old lady queried sharply.

"He acted nobly," Laura interjected softly.

"I acted as she bade me," Pen replied. "Never mind how, Lady Rockminster; to the best of my knowledge and power. And if you imply that I am unworthy of Laura, I know it, and I pray Heaven to better me. But if the love and company of the best and purest creature in the world can mend me, at least I shall have those gifts to aid me."

"Hm, hm," murmured the old lady, regarding the pair with a somewhat appeased expression. "It is all very well; but I should have preferred Bluebeard."

Seeking to divert the conversation from themes that grew painful, Pen recalled his interview with Huxter in the morning and Fanny Bolton's affairs, which he had momentarily neglected amidst his own emotional storm. He recounted how Huxter had elevated Fanny to the rank of wife and the terrors concerning the arrival of his father. He described the scene with considerable humor, dwelling especially on Fanny's coquetry and her irrepressible desire to captivate mankind. His underlying message was clear: *"See, Laura, I was not so guilty in that little affair; it was the girl who made love to me, and I who resisted. Since I am no longer present, the little siren practices her arts upon others. Let that transaction be forgotten, if you please; or visit me with a very gentle punishment for my error."*

Laura grasped his meaning beneath the fervor of his explanation. "If you did any wrong, you repented, dear Pen," she said. "And you know," she added, with meaningful eyes and blushing cheeks, "that I have no right to reproach you."

"Hm!" grunted the old lady. "I should have preferred Bluebeard."

"The past is broken away. The morrow is before us. I will do my best to make your morrow happy, dear Laura," Pen said. His heart humbled itself before the prospect of his happiness, standing awestruck in contemplation of her sweetness and purity. He loved his wife better now that she had admitted her fleeting feeling for Warrington and laid bare her generous heart. And she—perhaps thinking, *"How strange it is that I ever should have cared for another! I am vexed almost to think I care for him so little, am so little sorry that he is gone. Oh, in these past two months how I have learned to love Arthur! I care about nothing but Arthur; he is never absent from me. And to think that he is to be mine, mine! That I am to marry him, and not serve as I expected only this morning! For I would have knelt to Blanche to beg permission to live with him. And now—Oh, it is too much. Oh, mother! Mother, that you were here!"* Indeed, she felt as if Helen were there, invisibly beside her. A halo of happiness radiated from her.

She moved with a different step, blooming with a new beauty. Arthur observed the change, and the shrewd-eyed old Lady Rockminster noted it instantly.

"What a sly demure little wretch you have been," she whispered to Laura—"and how you have kept your secret!"

"How are we to help the young couple?" Laura asked. Generous lovers, naturally, feel an interest in all young couples.

"We must go and see them," said Pen.

"Of course we must," agreed Laura. "I intend to be very fond of Fanny. Let us go this instant. Lady Rockminster, may I have the carriage?"

"Go now! Why, you stupid creature, it is eleven o'clock at night. Mr. and Mrs. Huxter have their nightcaps on, I dare say. And it is time for you to go. Good night, Mr. Pendennis."

Arthur and Laura pleaded for ten more minutes.

"We will go to-morrow morning, then. I will fetch you with Martha."

"An earl's coronet," Pen remarked, clearly pleased with himself, "will have a great effect in Lamb Court and Smithfield. Stay—Lady Rockminster, will you join us in a little conspiracy?"

"How do you mean conspiracy, young man?"

"Will you please to feign illness to-morrow? And when old Mr. Huxter arrives, will you let me summon him in? If he is put into a good humour by the notion of attending a baronet in the country, what influence won't a countess have on him? When he is softened, when he is ripe, we will reveal the secret, bring in the young people, extort the paternal benediction, and finish the comedy."

"A parcel of stuff," said the old lady. "Take your hat, sir. Come away, miss. There—my head is turned another way. Good night, young people." And who knows but the old lady thought of her own early days as she departed on Laura's arm, nodding and humming to herself?

With the early morning came Laura and Martha, according to appointment, and the desired sensation, we trust, was effected in Lamb Court. From there, the three proceeded to wait upon Mr. and Mrs. Samuel Huxter at their residence in Charterhouse Lane.

The two ladies exchanged glances of great interest, not a little emotion stirring within Fanny. She had not seen her "guardian"—as she delighted to call Pen due to his bequest—since the event uniting her to Mr. Huxter.
