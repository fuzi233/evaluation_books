He was, in every sense, a Yorkshireman—unyielding, blunt, and proud of it. At fifty-five, his hair had turned silver-white, lending him an air of severity that his sharp features only reinforced. His face was not sculpted for elegance; it bore the rugged marks of northern England—broad, strong, and unapologetically plain. Some might call it coarse; others would say it was alive with character. His eyes, shrewd and piercing, missed little, and his mouth often curled with a skepticism that bordered on scorn. Tall and lean, he carried himself with a stubborn dignity, as if daring anyone to mistake him for anything less than what he was: a man who answered to no one.

To capture his mind, however, was a harder task. He was no benevolent patriarch, no gentle sage. He could speak sense when he chose, even kindness—but these were flashes, not the steady light of his nature.

Mr. Yorke lacked reverence—not for God, but for the trappings of power. Kings, priests, parliaments—all were, to him, little more than pompous obstacles. He saw no virtue in submission, no merit in tradition for tradition’s sake. This absence of awe left him indifferent to beauty unless it served some practical end. He believed in heaven, but his faith was hard-edged, stripped of mystery or tenderness.

Nor had he much patience for weakness. His bluntness could wound, his sarcasm cut deep, and he seldom cared who bled from it. He had no time for poets or dreamers; imagination struck him as a frivolous affliction. A man’s worth, in his eyes, was measured by his usefulness—by the strength of his arm or the sharpness of his wits. Yet for all his hardness, he was not without principle. He was fair to those beneath him—provided they knew their place—and generous to the poor, though his charity came with an expectation of gratitude.

His roots ran deep in Yorkshire soil; his family had been here for generations, and he wore that history like armor. Educated, well-traveled, he could converse fluently in French and Italian, could appreciate art when it pleased him, yet he clung to his rough dialect as a badge of pride. "A Yorkshire burr," he declared, "is worth ten of your mincing southern tongues."

He knew everyone and was known by all—yet few truly knew *him*. Originality fascinated him; mediocrity repelled him. He would sooner pass an hour with a sharp-tongued mill worker than endure five minutes with a polished gentleman. There was one exception: those simple, unreflecting souls who accepted him without question, who bent to his will without thought of resistance. With them, he was at ease, for they asked nothing of his depths.

His grudging tolerance of Robert Moore had its reasons. Moore’s foreign inflection, his un-English face, stirred old memories of travels in younger days. There was, too, the matter of business—Moore was shrewd, and Yorke respected shrewdness. But the strongest tie was one of necessity: Yorke, as a guardian of the estate where Moore’s mill stood, was a man Moore could not afford to slight.

Then there was Mr. Helstone. Between these two, the air was thick with old animosity—not just of opposing views, but of a rivalry long buried yet never forgotten. Years ago, both had loved the same woman: Mary Cave, a quiet, pale creature who had stirred Yorke’s restless heart despite her stillness. Helstone, cooler and more calculating, had won her—only to let her fade into neglect. When she died, whispers followed: *broken heart*, they said. Yorke, though now married to a woman of fire and spirit, had never forgiven Helstone.

The evening’s debate was fierce. Talk turned to the unrest in the mills, the starving workers, the broken frames. Helstone, ever the magistrate, spoke of order, of force if necessary. Yorke scoffed. "Starve a man, then call him unreasonable when he begs for bread?" His words were sharp as flint. The government, the church, the aristocracy—all were targets of his scorn.

Helstone, rising with icy dignity, warned of divine judgment. Yorke shot back with equal fervor, quoting Scripture with a grin. Moore, watching, finally interjected. "You’ve each proved the other a villain by your own accounts. I’d sooner face a mob than share a roof with either of you tonight."

It was a jest, but beneath it lay truth: these two men, bound by history and hatred, would never see eye to eye. And Moore, caught between them, could only laugh—and wonder which of them was the greater fool.
