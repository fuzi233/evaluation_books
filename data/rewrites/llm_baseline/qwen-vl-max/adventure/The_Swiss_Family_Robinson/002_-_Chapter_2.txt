We would have suffered greatly without the shelter of our tent, for the night turned as cold as the day had been sweltering. Yet we slept soundly, each of us worn out by the day’s labors.

The crowing of our watchful rooster—loud and clear as he greeted the rising moon—was the last thing I heard before sleep, and it was his call that woke me at dawn. I roused my wife quietly so that, while the children still slumbered, we might speak together in peace about our situation and what lay ahead. It was clear to both of us that first we must try to learn the fate of our former companions, and then explore the land on which we had been cast ashore.

So we decided: after breakfast, Fritz and I would set off on a journey to search for signs of them and to learn more about this strange island. My wife would stay near the boat with the three younger boys, keeping watch over the camp.

"Rouse up, rouse up, my boys!" I called cheerfully, shaking the children awake. "Come and help your mother get breakfast ready!"

"As if we could do much," she replied with a smile. "We can only boil more soup."

"Oh, but you’ve forgotten Jack’s fine lobster!" I reminded her. "Where is it, Jack?"

"It’s been safe in this hole in the rock all night, Father," he said. "I thought since the dogs like good things, they might take a fancy to the lobster just as they did the agouti."

"A sensible idea," I said. "Even my careless Jack is learning wisdom. And thank goodness the lobster is large—we’ll need part of it for our journey today."

At the mention of an expedition, the four children leapt with joy, dancing around me and clapping their hands.

"Hold on now, hold on!" I laughed. "Not everyone can go. Such a trip would be too dangerous and tiring for the younger ones. This spot seems safe enough. Fritz and I will go alone, with one dog. The other will stay behind to guard you. Fritz, prepare the guns, and tie up Flora so she doesn’t follow."

At the word “guns,” the boy flushed deeply. He fumbled awkwardly with his rifle, unable to straighten it properly. I left him alone for a moment, but soon saw that he was truly sorry. I let him choose another weapon, pleased to see how shame had stirred a sense of responsibility within him.

A little later, he tried to grab Flora to tie her up—but the dog, remembering the blows she’d recently received, growled and refused to come near. Turk reacted the same way. I had to call them gently, using my voice to coax them forward. Then, in tears, Fritz begged his mother for some biscuit, saying he’d rather skip the rest of his breakfast than remain unkind to the animals.

He carried the biscuits to them, stroking and soothing them, every movement pleading forgiveness. Dogs, more than most creatures—even humans—are quick to forgive kindness and slow to hold grudges. Flora instantly softened, licking the hands that fed her. But Turk, fiercer and more independent, stayed wary.

"Give him a claw of my lobster," Jack called out. "I meant to give it to you anyway for your journey."

With that treat, Turk seemed to accept Fritz. We loaded our gear: each of us took a gun and a game bag. Fritz tucked two pistols into his belt; I slipped a small hatchet into mine. After finishing breakfast, we packed the leftover lobster, some biscuits, and a flask of water—and were ready to go.

"Wait!" I said suddenly. "We’ve missed something vital."

"Surely not," Fritz said.

"Yes," I replied. "We haven’t said our morning prayer. In the rush of life, we’re too quick to forget the One who gives us everything." We paused to pray, asking for His guidance and protection. Then I bid farewell to my wife and children, urging them not to wander far from the boat or tent. We parted with quiet concern—neither of us knowing what dangers might lie ahead in this unknown world.

As we moved along the stream, we found the banks so rocky that we could reach the water only through one narrow path. There was no matching trail on the opposite side. I felt relieved—this meant my family was in a place nearly impossible to reach, protected by steep cliffs on one side and the sea on the other.

Fritz and I followed the stream upward until we came to a waterfall cascading down from a height, where large rocks half-submerged in water allowed us to cross safely. Now the sea lay to our left, and to our right stretched a long ridge of rocky hills dotted with clusters of trees.

We pushed through dense grass, dry and tangled from the sun, when suddenly we heard rustling behind us. Turning, we saw the grass swaying as though something moved through it. Fritz spun around, raised his gun, and stood ready to fire.

I was proud of his composure—his calm under pressure showed he could be trusted in real danger. But instead of a beast, it was Turk, our dog, whom we had accidentally left behind. Our thoughtful wife must have sent him after us.

I praised Fritz for not acting on fear, for waiting to identify the threat before firing. This small incident made us realize how vulnerable we were—how easily a hidden predator could ambush us. So we hurried toward the open shore.

There, the view was breathtaking. Hills rose in the background, green grass waved in the breeze, and trees reached almost to the water's edge. It was a picture of beauty and peace.

On the smooth sand, we searched carefully for any trace of our missing companions—but found nothing. Not a single footprint.

"Should I fire a shot or two?" Fritz asked. "It might bring them if they’re nearby."

"It might," I answered, "or it might attract savages. No—let’s search quietly and thoroughly."

"But why should we care about them at all, Father?" Fritz said. "They abandoned us. I don’t want to see them again."

"You're wrong, son," I replied. "We shouldn't repay evil with evil. They might help us build shelter. And remember—they took nothing from the ship. They may be starving."

"But Father," he argued, "while we waste time searching, why not go back and rescue the animals on board?"

"When many duties call," I said, "we must choose the one that brings the greatest good. Saving a human life is nobler than caring for animals we’ve already fed. Besides, the sea is calm—the ship won great harm before we return."

We walked on, talking, until we reached a lovely grove stretching down to the water. We rested beneath a large tree beside a murmuring brook that flowed into the ocean.

Birds flitted above us, bright and chattering. Fritz suddenly jumped up. "A monkey!" he exclaimed. "I’m sure I saw one!"

He darted around the tree and tripped over a round object, handing it to me. "It’s a bird’s nest," he said. "I’ve read about them."

"Perhaps," I laughed. "But not every fuzzy round thing is a nest. This is a coconut. Remember—coconuts have a hard shell covered by fibrous husk, and outside that, a thick green casing. Time has worn away the outer layer here, revealing the fibers. Let’s crack it open—you’ll see the nut inside."

We struggled to break it, but when we did, the kernel was dry and useless.

"Hullo!" Fritz said. "I always thought coconuts were full of sweet milk, like almond juice."

"They are," I said, "when young. As they age, the milk thickens and turns into solid kernel. When ripe, it dries. But if the nut falls on good soil, the seed inside swells, breaks the shell, takes root, and grows a new tree."

"But how does such a tiny seed push through such a hard shell?" Fritz asked.

"Nature provides," I said, pointing to three small holes near the stem. "The seed pushes out through these. Now let’s find a fresh one."

Since coconuts fall only when fully ripe, it took effort to find one with usable meat. The one we got was oily and slightly sour—but better than nothing. The fruit gave us strength, so we postponed our midday meal and saved our supplies.

Pushing through a thicket thick with vines, we cut a path with our hatchets and emerged once more onto the shore. Before us lay a wide forest receding into the distance, with scattered trees standing apart. Fritz noticed them immediately.

"Oh, Father! Look at those funny trees—what odd bumps on their trunks!"

We approached and recognized them as calabash trees, whose fruits grow directly on the stems. These gourds have tough shells that can be carved into bowls, spoons, or bottles.

"Savages use them cleverly," I said. "They cook food in them."

"But that’s impossible!" Fritz protested. "Fire would burn through the shell."

"Not if they don’t put it directly on the fire," I explained. "After splitting the gourd, they fill it with water and add fish or meat. Then they throw in hot stones until the water boils. The food cooks, and the shell stays intact."

"Ah! A brilliant idea—simple too. I might have thought of that," Fritz said.

"The friends of Columbus thought it easy to stand an egg on end after he showed them how. Now, let’s prepare some calabashes to take home."

Fritz grabbed a gourd and tried to slice it evenly with his knife—but failed. The blade slipped, leaving jagged edges.

"Ugh!" he muttered, tossing it aside. "Ruined. It looked so simple."

"Stay," I said. "Those pieces aren’t useless. Try shaping them into spoons while I make a dish."

I pulled a string from my pocket, tied it tightly around a gourd near one end, then tapped it with the back of my knife. The string pierced the shell. I tightened it further and pulled hard—splitting the gourd cleanly in two.

"Clever!" Fritz exclaimed. "How did you think of that?"

"Savages use this method," I said. "I learned it from travel books."

"Looks like a perfect soup tureen—and even a plate," he said, examining it. "But what if you wanted a bottle?"

"Easier," I said. "Cut a hole at one end, scoop out the insides, drop in some stones or shot. Shake them—any remaining pulp will detach. Then you have a clean bottle."

"That wouldn’t be very handy," Fritz said. "More like a barrel."

"True," I agreed. "For a finer shape, you must act early. Tie a band around a young gourd while it’s still on the tree. That part won’t swell, creating a neck."

I filled the gourds with sand and marked the spot so we could return later.

"Do people in Europe grow bottle-shaped gourds the same way?" Fritz asked.

"No," I said. "They’re a different kind—those shapes are natural."

We continued for three hours, scanning the land for any sign of our companions. At last, we reached a high promontory jutting into the sea. From its rocky peak, we expected a broad view of the island.

We climbed easily and scanned the landscape—yet saw no trace of humans.

Before us lay a wide bay, golden sands curving into the distance, framed by two shadowy headlands. Between them, the water shimmered, reflecting the sun. The land beyond was lush and green—beautiful, yet strangely silent.

Fritz and I both felt a quiet loneliness settle over us.

"Cheer up, son," I said. "We chose this life long ago—settlers, not travelers. We didn’t expect solitude, but what difference does a few people make? With God’s help, we’ll live contentedly. At least we’re not stranded on a barren rock. Come—the heat is unbearable. Let’s find shade before we’re cooked alive."

We descended and headed for a clump of palm trees in the distance. To reach them, we had to pass through a thick reed bed—a difficult task. I feared at every step I might step on a venomous snake.

I sent Turk ahead and cut a reed, thinking it might be safer than a gun against reptiles. As I walked, I noticed a thick liquid oozing from one end. I tasted it—and smiled. Sweet and refreshing. Sugar cane!

I urged Fritz to cut one for himself. He did, and as he beat the ground, the reed split, coating his hand with juice. He touched it to his tongue, then again—more boldly. Then he sprang to me, shouting: "Father! Sugar cane! Taste it! Oh, it’s wonderful! We must take some home!"

"Slow down," I said. "Moderation, remember. Cut some if you like—but not more than you can carry."

He ignored me, cutting a dozen thick canes, stripping the leaves, and carrying them under his arm. We pushed through the cane brake and reached the palms.

As we entered, a troop of monkeys, playing on the ground, scrambled up the trees, chattering and making faces.

Fritz, angry at their mockery, raised his gun. "Stay!" I cried. "Never kill an animal unless necessary. A live monkey is worth more than a dead one. Watch."

I gathered small stones and threw them upward. The monkeys, mimicking instinctively, grabbed nearby coconuts and hurled them down like rain.

Fritz laughed and rushed forward, picking up the best nuts. We drank the milk from them, piercing the shells. It wasn’t tasty, but it quenched thirst. What we enjoyed most was the creamy layer clinging to the shell, which we scraped off with our spoons.

After this feast, we scorned the lobster we’d brought. We tossed it to Turk, who devoured it eagerly—then gnawed the sugar cane ends and begged for more coconut. I slung two coconuts over my shoulder, fastening them by their stalks. Fritz resumed his burden, and we began the return journey.

Soon, Fritz groaned. "I didn’t expect these canes to be so heavy. I feel terrible for the poor slaves who carry loads like this. But I’ll be glad when my brothers taste them."

"Patience, son," I said. "Remember Aesop’s breadbasket—heavy at the start, light at the end. Take a fresh cane and bind the bundle across with your gun."

We did, and moved on. Fritz noticed I kept sucking the end of my cane.

"Ah!" he said. "That’s a good trick. I’ll try it."

He sucked hard—but nothing came out.

"How do you do it?" he asked.

"Think," I said. "You’re capable of figuring it out."

"Oh!" he said. "Like trying to suck marrow from a bone without a hole at the other end."

"Exactly," I said. "You create a vacuum. Air can’t enter the other end, so the liquid won’t flow."

He quickly mastered the technique, learning to cut fresh openings at each joint. He dreamed of teaching his brothers, especially Ernest, who loved coconut milk.

"My dear boy," I said, "you needn’t have brought that flask. It’ll turn to vinegar by the time we return. The heat will ferment it."

"Vinegar?" he gasped. "No! Let me check!" He pulled the cork—*pop!*—and foam erupted like champagne.

"There!" I laughed. "Now you’ll need moderation again, friend Fritz! It may be delicious, but it’ll go to your head."

"Father!" he said, tasting it. "This is amazing! Like fine wine! Vinegar? Never!"

We felt renewed, walking briskly. The journey seemed shorter than expected. We found our gourds dry and light.

Just as we passed the grove where we’d eaten, Turk bolted forward, attacking a troop of monkeys playing nearby. They were caught off guard. The dog, hungry, seized one and tore it apart before we could stop him.

The victim was a mother monkey. Her tiny baby, trapped beneath her when she fled, hid in the grass, watching in terror.

Seeing Turk’s cruelty, Fritz rushed to save the infant, dropping everything—even his hat. Too late for the mother. But as he approached, the little monkey leapt onto his shoulders, gripping his curly hair tightly.

Fritz screamed and thrashed, but the creature held fast, making grotesque faces.

I laughed so hard I could barely help him. "It seems the little one sees you as a father figure," I said.

"Or," Fritz retorted, "he knows I’m a soft-hearted fool who can’t hurt anything that trusts me. But father—he’s pulling my hair! Please help me get rid of him!"

And so, with laughter and a bit of struggle, we finally freed Fritz from his furry passenger—and continued home, lighter in spirit and heart.
