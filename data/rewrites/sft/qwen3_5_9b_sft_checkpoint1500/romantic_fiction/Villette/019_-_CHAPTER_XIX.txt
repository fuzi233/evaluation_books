My stay at La Terrasse extended a fortnight past the end of the holidays, a respite arranged by Mrs. Bretton's kindness. After her son declared that "Lucy was not yet strong enough to return to that den of a pensionnat," his mother drove immediately to Rue Fossette. She secured permission from the director, arguing that prolonged rest and a change of air were essential for my recovery. Consequently, I was treated to an attentiveness I could easily have done without: a polite visit from Madame Beck.

On a fine afternoon, that lady actually arrived in a fiacre as far as the château. Presumably, she had decided to inspect the nature of the establishment Dr. John inhabited. The pleasant grounds and neat interior evidently exceeded her expectations. She praised everything she saw, calling the blue salon "une pièce magnifique," lavishly congratulating me on acquiring friends "tellement dignes, aimables, et respectables." Turning a neat compliment in my favor, she rushed forward when Dr. John entered, her buoyancy infectious. She launched into a torrent of rapid language, sparkling with felicitations and protests regarding his "château"—"madame sa mère, la digne châtelaine:" as well as his appearance, which was flourishing and further enhanced by the amused smile with which he invariably listened to Madame's fluent, florid French. In short, Madame Beck shone in her very best phase that day, entering and exiting like a living Catherine wheel of compliments, delight, and affability. Following her somewhat purposefully, and half to inquire about school matters, I trailed her to her carriage. Peering in after she was seated and the door closed, I witnessed a transformation within a fraction of a second. An instant prior, all sparkles and jests; now, she sat sterner than a judge and graver than a sage. A strange little woman!

I returned to tease Dr. John about Madame's devotion to him. He laughed, what fun shining in his eyes as he recalled some of her splendid speeches, repeating them and imitating her voluble delivery! He possessed an acute sense of humor and was the finest company in the world—provided he could forget Miss Fanshawe.

They say that "sitting in sunshine calm and sweet" is excellent for the weak, imparting vital force. When little Georgette Beck was recovering from her illness, I used to carry her in my arms and walk with her in the garden for hours beneath a wall draped in grapes, which the Southern sun ripened; that sun cherished her pale frame as effectively as it mellowed and swelled the clustering fruit.

There exist human tempers, bland, glowing, and genial, whose influence benefits the poor in spirit as surely as the glow of noon benefits the feeble in frame. Among these choice natures were undoubtedly both Dr. Bretton's and his mother's. They delighted in communicating happiness, as others delight in occasioning misery; they did it instinctively, without fuss and seemingly without conscious effort, for the means to give pleasure rose spontaneously in their minds. Throughout my stay, some little plan was proposed daily that resulted in beneficial enjoyment. Though fully occupied, Dr. John still managed to join us on each brief excursion. I could hardly tell how he managed his engagements, which were numerous, yet by dint of system, he classified them in an order that left him a daily period of liberty. I often saw him hard-worked, yet seldom over-driven, never irritated, confused, or oppressed. Whatever he did was accomplished with the ease and grace of all-sufficing strength; with the bountiful cheerfulness of high and unbroken energies. Under his guidance, I saw more of Villette, its environs, and its inhabitants in that one happy fortnight than I had seen in the whole eight months of my previous residence. He took me to places of interest in the town of whose names I had not previously heard, and with willingness and spirit, he communicated much noteworthy information. He never seemed to think it a trouble to talk to me, and I am sure it was never a task for me to listen. It was not his way to treat subjects coldly and vaguely; he rarely generalized, never prosed. He seemed to like nice details almost as much as I did, and he observed character not superficially. These points gave the quality of interest to his discourse; and the fact that he spoke directly from his own resources, rather than borrowing or stealing facts and phrases from books, ensured a freshness as welcome as it was rare. Before my eyes, his disposition seemed to unfold another phase, passing to a fresh day, rising in a new and nobler dawn.

His mother possessed a good development of benevolence, but he owned a better and larger one. Accompanying him to the Basse-Ville, the poor and crowded quarter of the city, I found his errands there were as much those of the philanthropist as the physician. I understood presently that he was achieving, among a very wretched population, a world of active good, cheerfully, habitually, and in single-minded unconsciousness of any special merit distinguishing his deeds. The lower orders liked him well; his poor hospital patients welcomed him with a sort of enthusiasm.

But stop—I must not, from the faithful narrator, degenerate into a partial eulogist. Well, full well do I know that Dr. John was not perfect, any more than I am. Human fallibility leavened him throughout; there was no hour, and scarcely a moment, of the time I spent with him that in act, speech, or look, he did not betray something that was not of a god. A god could not have the cruel vanity of Dr. John, nor his occasional levity. No immortal could have resembled him in his temporary oblivion of all but the present, nor in his passing passion for that immediate moment. Shown not coarsely by devoting it to material indulgence, but selfishly by extracting from it whatever it could yield of nutriment to his masculine self-love: his delight was to feed that ravenous sentiment, without thought of the price of provender, or care for the cost of keeping it sleek and high-pampered.

The reader is requested to note a seeming contradiction in the two views presented of Graham Bretton—the public and private, the out-door and in-door view. In the first, the public, he appears oblivious of self, modest in the display of his energies, earnest in their exercise. In the second, the fireside picture, there is expressed consciousness of what he has and what he is; pleasure in homage, some recklessness in exciting, some vanity in receiving it. Both portraits are correct.

It was hardly possible to oblige Dr. John quietly and in secret. When you thought that the fabrication of some trifling dedication to his use had been achieved unnoticed, and that, like other men, he would use it when placed ready and never ask whence it came, he amazed you by a smilingly-uttered observation proving that his eye had been on the work from commencement to close. He noted the design, traced its progress, and marked its completion. It pleased him to be thus served, and he let his pleasure beam in his eye and play about his mouth.

This would have been all very well if he had not added to such kindly and unobtrusive evidence a certain wilfulness in discharging what he called debts. When his mother worked for him, he repaid her by showering about her his bright animal spirits with greater affluence than his gay, taunting, teasing, loving wont. If Lucy Snowe were discovered to have put her hand to such work, he planned, in recompense, some pleasant recreation.

I often marveled at his perfect knowledge of Villette—a knowledge not merely confined to its open streets, but penetrating to all its galleries, salles, and cabinets. Of every door which shut in an object worth seeing, of every museum, of every hall sacred to art or science, he seemed to possess the "Open! Sesame." I never had a head for science, but an ignorant, blind, fond instinct inclined me to art. I liked to visit the picture-galleries, and I dearly liked to be left there alone. In company, a wretched idiosyncrasy forbade me to see much or to feel anything. In unfamiliar company, where it was necessary to maintain a flow of talk on the subjects in presence, half an hour would knock me up with a combined pressure of physical lassitude and entire mental incapacity. I never yet saw the well-reared child, much less the educated adult, who could not put me to shame by the sustained intelligence of its demeanour under the ordeal of a conversable visitation of pictures, historical sights, or lions of public interest. Dr. Bretton was a cicerone after my own heart; he would take me betimes, ere the galleries were filled, leave me there for two or three hours, and call for me when his own engagements were discharged. Meantime, I was happy—not always in admiring, but in examining, questioning, and forming conclusions.

In the commencement of these visits, there was some misunderstanding and consequent struggle between Will and Power. The former faculty exacted approbation of that which it considered orthodox to admire; the latter groaned forth its utter inability to pay the tax. It was then self-sneered at, spurred up, goaded on to refine its taste, and whet its zest. However, the more it was chidden, the more it refused to praise.
