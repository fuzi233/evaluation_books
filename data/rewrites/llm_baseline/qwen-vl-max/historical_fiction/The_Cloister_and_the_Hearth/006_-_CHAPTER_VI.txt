“Look into your own heart and write!” cried Herr Cant, and the earth’s cuckoos echoed his words. Look into the Rhine where it is deepest, or the Thames where it is thickest, and paint what lies beneath. Lower a bucket into a well of self-deception—what rises must be truth, mustn’t it? Yet no son of Adam ever truly reads his own heart, except through the lens of years spent studying others’ souls. Even then, with borrowed wisdom and reflected light, one can only decipher, not fluently read.

Halfway to Sevenbergen, Gerard paused and turned inward, asking his heart why he was going. Without hesitation, it answered: “We go out of curiosity—to know why she cast us aside, to show her our hearts remain unbroken, that we are content with our honors and future benefice, and that we need neither her nor any woman of her fickle kind.”

He soon found Peter Brandt’s cottage. At the doorway sat a girl, stitching quietly, while a broad-shouldered man leaned on a longbow, speaking to her. Gerard felt an unexpected pang at the sight. But the man proved to be over fifty—an old soldier whom Gerard remembered from the archery butts, renowned for strength and precision. A moment later, the youth stood before them.

Margaret looked up, dropped her needle, gasped softly, and flushed pale and red in turn. Then, as if catching herself, she grew colder than before, more aloof than if she had never shown such emotion.

“What! Is it you, Master Gerard? What brings you here?”

“I passed by and saw you,” he said, “so I thought I’d greet you and inquire after your father.”

“He is well. He’ll be here shortly.”

“Then I may as well wait.”

“As you wish. Good Martin, go to the village and tell my father a friend has come.”

“And not yours?”

“My father’s friends are mine.”

“That may be doubted,” Gerard replied. “It wasn’t like a true friend to promise to wait—and then vanish the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret, you little know how I searched for you. Without you, nothing brought joy.”

“Idle talk,” she said sharply. “If you truly wanted my father—or me—you would have returned. That day, I had a bed ready for you at my cousin’s. He would have welcomed you warmly. And who knows—I might have done so too. I was in a generous mood. You’ll never find me in such a frame again—not you, nor any young man, I swear.”

“Margaret,” he insisted, “I came back the moment the Countess released me—but you were gone.”

“No,” she said, “or you would have seen Hans Cloterman at our table. We left him to bring you.”

“I saw no one—only a drunken man who had just fallen.”

“At our table? How was he dressed?”

“I paid little heed—he wore dull-colored clothes.”

At this, Margaret’s face softened slowly. Then, feigning skepticism, she questioned him closely—each inquiry sharp and probing. Gerard answered every one with honesty. Gradually, the clouds lifted. They understood how the misunderstanding had arisen.

And then—like a tide turning—came a surge of tenderness, all the deeper because they had wronged each other. Confessions followed, mutual and heartfelt. Neither had been happy since their parting. Neither could imagine happiness without this reunion.

Gerard noticed a manuscript Vulgate open on the table and reached for it eagerly—books of such rarity were his passion. But before he could touch it, two white hands pressed flat against the page, and a flushed face leaned over them.

“Take away your hands, Margaret,” he said gently, “so I may see where you’ve been reading. I’ll read the same passage at home—so our souls may meet in sacred words.”

“You will not?” she challenged.

“Then I must kiss them away.”

And he kissed those hands again and again, until shame forced them to withdraw. The book lay open now at: *“An apple of gold in a network of silver.”*

“There,” she said, smiling faintly, “I’d searched for this verse for ages—just found it moments ago—and now caught!” With a hint of contradiction, she pointed it out with her slender finger.

“Ay,” he said, “but today it’s hidden under that great cap.”

“It’s a becoming cap, some say.”

“Perhaps. But what it hides is beautiful.”

“It is not—it’s hideous.”

“But it was beautiful in Rotterdam.”

“Ay,” she whispered, “everything was beautiful that day.”

Then Peter arrived, greeted Gerard warmly, and invited him to stay for supper. Margaret vanished briefly, returning with her hair veiled in silver netting. She glanced at Gerard—half teasing, half shy—then moved gracefully about them, setting the meal with radiant cheerfulness.

As evening cooled, Gerard coaxed her outside. She hesitated, but came. He urged her toward the road to Tergou; she declined, yet followed. There, hand in hand, they walked back and forth, the world forgotten. When it was time for him to leave, they vowed never again to quarrel or misunderstand. Their promise was sealed with a long, loving kiss—and Gerard returned home as if borne on wings.

From that night onward, Gerard spent most evenings with Margaret. Their bond deepened steadily, until the hours they shared became the only hours they truly lived. The rest were mere endurance.

At first, all went smoothly. Obstacles existed, but seemed distant, small—lost in the glow of hope, youth, and love. The passions of others threatened by this attachment gave no warning signs, no smoke to signal the fire beneath. True love flowed calm and clear—until it drew these two hearts into its current forever.
