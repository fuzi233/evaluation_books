The Treasure of Abbot Thomas

Let it be noted for those who seek the geography of my tales: St. Bertrand de Comminges and Viborg stand as real anchors in the mist, yet the scholarly fragments scattered through these pages are pure fabrication. There never existed the tome I cite in this narrative, nor did the specific chronologies of my other works hold true in the way they appear here. "Canon Alberic's Scrap-book" was a product of 1894, "Lost Hearts" a fleeting ghost in the *Pall Mall Magazine*, and the cluster of stories read by candlelight at King's College were born of winter evenings. This particular tale, however, was forged in the heat of a summer in 1904, a season ripe for the kind of dread that clings to old stone and forgotten dust.

The truth of the matter is that history often serves merely as a scaffold for the supernatural. In this story, the past is not a record but a trap, waiting for the living to step inside. The antiquary who seeks to catalog the dead finds instead that the dead have been waiting to catalog him.

The town of St. Bertrand de Comminges clings to the jagged spurs of the Pyrenees, a decayed relic of a bishopric long silenced by the Revolution. By the spring of 1883, it was merely a tourist stop for the cathedral, a place of crumbling grandeur where the air smelled of damp stone and ancient dust. Into this gloom arrived an Englishman named Dennistoun, a scholar from Cambridge who had left his less serious companions in Toulouse to spend a solitary morning documenting the church that dominated the hill. His mission was thorough: notes, photographs, and a complete inventory of every corner of the sacred space. To achieve this, he needed the verger's undivided attention.

The innkeeper, a brisk woman running the Chapeau Rouge, summoned the sacristan. What Dennistoun found waiting for him was not a man of striking appearance, but one of profound unease. The old man was wizened and dry, yet he moved with the frantic energy of a hunted animal. His shoulders were perpetually hunched, his eyes darting constantly over his shoulder as if expecting a blow from behind. Dennistoun struggled to categorize the fear: was it madness, guilt, or perhaps the tyranny of a wife? The odds favored the latter, yet the intensity of the man's dread suggested a persecutor far more formidable than any domestic tyrant.

Dennistoun, however, was soon buried in his notebook and camera, the sacristan trailing at his heels like a shadow. The old man would crouch in the stalls or press himself against the walls, twitching violently whenever the strange, muffled noises of the empty church drifted through the air. Once, Dennistoun swore he heard a thin, metallic laughter echoing from the tower. When he turned, the sacristan was pale as death, whispering that the door was locked and no one was there.

Later, examining a dark painting behind the altar depicting St. Bertrand saving a man from strangulation, Dennistoun was startled to see the sacristan on his knees, weeping before the canvas. The old man's agony was palpable, a silent scream directed at a mere daub of paint. Dennistoun, puzzled by the depth of this obsession, continued his work until dusk. As the shadows lengthened and the curious noises of the building grew insistent, the sacristan finally urged him to leave for the Angelus bell.

On the doorstep, the sacristan's demeanor shifted. He spoke of old choir-books and then, with a sudden, desperate plunge, offered Dennistoun a glimpse into his private collection. "If monsieur is *amateur des vieux livres*," he said, "I have something at home that might interest you." Dennistoun, initially skeptical of finding anything of value so near Toulouse, agreed. The sacristan's relief at having a companion was evident, though it quickly curdled into gloom when Dennistoun mentioned his friends arriving the next morning.

They walked to the sacristan's house, a stone mansion bearing the shield of Canon Alberic de Mauléon, a descendant of the bishop who had once ruled the diocese. The house was boarded and decaying, mirroring the town itself. Inside, a younger woman—the sacristan's daughter—greeted them with a look of acute anxiety, her eyes fixed on a crucifix hanging high on the wall. When her father mentioned that someone had been laughing in the church, the girl's terror was absolute.

In a small, high chamber warmed by a flickering fire, the sacristan produced a large folio wrapped in white cloth embroidered with a red cross. As Dennistoun unwrapped it, his breath caught. It was a scrapbook, bound in leather with the arms of Canon Alberic, filled with illuminated leaves from manuscripts dating back to the seventh and thirteenth centuries. But the true treasure lay at the end. Tucked between the ancient pages were two sheets of paper and a sepia drawing.

The first sheet held a plan of the church's cloisters, marked with planetary symbols and Hebrew characters, alongside a Latin inscription dated December 12, 1694: *Shall I find it? Thou shalt. Shall I become rich? Thou wilt.*

Then came the drawing. It depicted King Solomon on his throne, radiating power, yet his face twisted in horror. Surrounding him were four soldiers, frozen in terror, staring at a crouching figure on the pavement. Beside them lay a dead guard, his neck broken. The crouching creature was a nightmare given form: a skeletal mass of coarse black hair, with hands like talons and eyes of burning yellow that fixed the King with beastly hate. It was a depiction of a demon, drawn with such visceral realism that Dennistoun felt the chill of its presence.

The sacristan's hands were pressed over his eyes, his daughter praying fervently. When Dennistoun asked for the price, the old man hesitated, then stated firmly, "Two hundred and fifty francs." Despite Dennistoun's protests that the book was worth far more, the price stood. The transaction was completed, and the sacristan seemed transformed, his nervousness vanishing as he laughed and offered to walk Dennistoun home. Dennistoun declined, accepting instead a silver crucifix and chain from the daughter, who insisted he take it as a gift.

Back in his room at the inn, Dennistoun unpacked his treasures. The atmosphere was heavy; the landlady's laughter sounded hollow, and he felt a prickle of unease, convinced someone was watching him. He laid the crucifix on the table to inspect it, his mind drifting to the strange drawing. Suddenly, his gaze snapped to an object resting on the red cloth beside his elbow.

It was a hand.

Pale, dusky skin stretched over bones and tendons of terrifying strength. Coarse black hairs covered it, and the nails were long, grey, and curved like talons. Dennistoun screamed, scrambling backward in his chair. Rising from behind the seat was the figure from the drawing. It wore tattered black drapery, its face devoid of a nose, its jaw shallow and beast-like. Its eyes, fiery yellow with black pupils, burned with intelligent, exulting hatred. The creature lunged, and Dennistoun, clutching the silver crucifix, was thrown into a swoon.

When he awoke, two serving men were tending to him. His friends from Toulouse arrived the next morning, and after seeing the drawing and speaking with the sacristan, they believed his tale. The old man, who had visited the inn at dawn, confirmed Dennistoun's story with chilling simplicity: "It is he. I have seen him myself. Twice I saw him; a thousand times I felt him." He spoke of no origins, only of his impending death, adding, "I shall soon sleep, and my rest will be sweet."

The canon died that summer, reportedly seized suddenly in his bed. The drawing was later photographed by Dennistoun before he burned it, leaving only the memory of the encounter. Dennistoun, a man of reason, found himself unable to dismiss the event, quoting scripture about spirits of vengeance and wondering if the ruins of Babylon had indeed bred such night monsters. He concluded with a strange sentiment, hoping that the vicar would sing dirges for Canon Alberic's rest, admitting with a touch of Northern melancholy, "I had no notion they came so dear."

September 1811. The post-chaise halted before the iron gates of Aswarby Hall, Lincolnshire, and a small boy tumbled onto the gravel, his eyes wide with a curiosity that cut through the gathering dusk. Before him rose a formidable structure of red brick, a relic of Queen Anne's reign, crowned by a classical porch of 1790 and flanked by wings housing the stables, their cupolas gleaming like eyes in the twilight. The setting sun turned the glass of the tall, narrow windows into a grid of fiery squares, while the distant church tower, half-swallowed by the oak-studded park, struck six. The sound rolled down the wind, heavy and mournful, settling a chill of early autumn melancholy upon the boy's shoulders.

He was Stephen Elliott, an orphan from Warwickshire, now cast into the orbit of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney. To the neighbors, Abney was a recluse, a man wrapped in the dust of ancient texts, a Professor of Greek whose library held the secrets of Mithras and the Orphic mysteries. It was baffling, even to the local gossips, that this austere scholar should have volunteered to take in a stray child. Yet, when the front door swung open, Abney did not emerge with the cold demeanor his reputation suggested. Instead, he darted from his study, his hands rubbing together in genuine delight.

"How are you, my boy?" he asked, his voice warm. "How old are you?"

"Twelve, sir," Stephen replied, surprised by the repetition of the question.

"Eleventh of September," Abney noted, scribbling furiously in a leather-bound book. "I like to get these things down. Take him to Mrs. Bunch."

Mrs. Bunch was the heartbeat of the house, a woman born in the neighborhood fifty-five years prior. She made Stephen feel at home within a quarter-hour, her knowledge of the Hall's history vast and her explanations eager. She spoke of the strange happenings that had preceded Stephen's arrival: a little girl found wandering the grounds, vanished after three weeks amidst the singing of gypsies; a foreign boy named Giovanni, who played a hurdy-gurdy and disappeared just as mysteriously, leaving his instrument behind. These stories, told with a mixture of pity and superstition, planted seeds of unease in Stephen's young mind.

That night, the house seemed to hold its breath. Stephen dreamed of a disused bathroom at the top of the stairs, its door glazed with muslin curtains long gone. Through the glass, he saw a figure lying in a lead-lined bath, pale and dusty, wrapped in a shroud. Its lips curved in a dreadful, faint smile, and its hands were pressed tightly over its heart. A low moan drifted from the corpse, and the arms stirred. Stephen woke with a gasp, the moonlight flooding the passage, and rushed to the bathroom door. It was empty. Yet, the memory burned.

The following morning, Mrs. Bunch replaced the curtain, her face grave. At breakfast, Abney listened intently to Stephen's dream, making notes in his book with a scholarly detachment that unsettled the boy. As the spring equinox approached, Abney warned Stephen of the critical nature of the season, citing Censorinus and urging him to keep his windows shut.

Then, the marks appeared. One Friday night in March 1812, Stephen woke to find his nightgown torn with long, parallel slits on the left side of his chest. He pointed them out to Mrs. Bunch, noting they matched the scratches on his bedroom door. "Too high for a cat," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Like a Chinaman's fingernails." She advised him to lock his door, a precaution that did little to soothe the dread coiling in his stomach.

The atmosphere grew heavier. Mr. Parkes, the butler, who usually kept to his pantry, arrived flustered, claiming the wine cellar was infested with rats—or worse, speaking rats. He urged Stephen to listen at the bins, a tale Stephen dismissed as nonsense, though the hairs on the back of his neck stood erect.

March 24, 1812. The wind howled through the grounds, carrying the sensation of an endless procession of unseen spirits, desperate and aimless. After lunch, Abney summoned Stephen to his study at eleven o'clock, promising a revelation concerning Stephen's future. The library was prepared: a brazier burning bright, a silver cup filled with red wine, and papers scattered on the table. As Stephen entered, Abney sprinkled incense, his movements ritualistic.

Outside, the night was still, save for the eerie cries drifting from the woods—sounds neither owl nor bird, but the lamentations of the lost. Then, Stephen saw them. Standing on the gravel terrace were two figures: a girl and a boy. The girl smiled, her hands clasped over her heart, mirroring the specter from his dream. The boy, thin and ragged, raised his arms in a gesture of hungry menace. The moonlight illuminated his transparent hands, revealing nails so long they seemed to glow. On his chest, a black, gaping rent opened, and a desolate cry tore through the silence. They vanished as quickly as they had appeared.

Terror seized Stephen. He grabbed his candle and raced to the study. The door was unlocked, but Abney was inside, speaking to someone unseen. Stephen tried to scream, but the cry died in his throat. He pushed the door open.

The scene was a tableau of horror. Papers lay scattered, detailing Abney's experiments in spiritual ascension through the absorption of human souls. The text spoke of Simon Magus and the absorption of three hearts to gain dominion over elemental forces. It listed the first victim, Phoebe Stanley, and the second, Giovanni Paoli. The final entry was stark: "The final 'victim'... must be my cousin, Stephen Elliott. His day must be this March 24, 1812." The instructions were chilling: remove the heart from the living, reduce it to ashes, and mix it with red wine.

Abney sat in his chair, head thrown back, his face a mask of rage and mortal agony. A terrible wound lacerated his left side, exposing his heart. No blood stained his hands; a clean knife lay on the table. The coroner would later rule it a wild animal attack, but Stephen, reading the papers, understood the truth. The boy who had arrived in September was now dead, his heart claimed by the man who had welcomed him with open arms.

Some time ago, I believe I had the pleasure of recounting an adventure that befell a friend of mine, Dennistoun, during his hunt for artifacts for the Cambridge museum. He kept his tale close to his chest upon returning to England, yet whispers inevitably spread among his circle, reaching the ears of a gentleman presiding over an art museum at another university. It was natural that such a story should strike fear into the mind of a man whose vocation mirrored Dennistoun's; he was desperate for any explanation that would absolve him of the terrifying prospect of encountering such an emergency in his own life. It offered a grim consolation to reflect that his institution was not tasked with acquiring ancient manuscripts—that was the domain of the Shelburnian Library, whose authorities might scour the darkest corners of the Continent for such relics. He was relieved to be confined to the mundane task of expanding his museum's already unparalleled collection of English topographical drawings and engravings. Yet, even a department so seemingly homely and familiar possesses its own dark corners, and into one of these, Mr. Williams was unexpectedly thrust.

Those with even a passing interest in the acquisition of topographical prints know that there exists a London dealer whose assistance is indispensable. Mr. J. W. Britnell publishes, at irregular intervals, exquisite catalogues of a vast and shifting stock of engravings, plans, and sketches of English and Welsh mansions, churches, and towns. These catalogues formed the alphabet of Mr. Williams's trade; however, since his museum already held an immense accumulation of topographical pictures, he was a regular rather than a voracious buyer. He looked to Mr. Britnell not for rarities, but to fill the gaps in the ranks of his collection.

It was hardly thrilling, and the price seemed steep. Yet, trusting Mr. Britnell's judgment of both his craft and his client, Mr. Williams sent a postcard requesting the item on approval, along with other sketches from the catalogue. Thus, he passed from the anticipation of the purchase to the routine labors of the day.

Parcels invariably arrive a day later than expected, and Mr. Britnell's proved no exception. Delivered by the Saturday afternoon post, after Mr. Williams had left his work, the package was brought to his college rooms by the attendant so he need not wait over the Sabbath. It was there he found it upon arriving for tea with a friend.

The sole item of interest was the large, black-framed mezzotint described in the catalogue. While I cannot hope to convey the image's appearance with the clarity of my own memory, I can describe it. Duplicates of this print may be found in countless old inn parlors or the passages of undisturbed country houses. It was a mediocre mezzotint, and an indifferent print is perhaps the worst form of engraving known. It depicted a full-face view of a modest manor-house from the previous century, featuring three rows of plain sashed windows set against rusticated masonry, a parapet adorned with balls or vases at the corners, and a small central portico. Trees flanked the sides, and a vast lawn stretched before the house. The legend *A. W. F. sculpsit* was engraved on the narrow margin; there was no other inscription. The entire composition gave the impression of an amateur's hand. Mr. Williams could not fathom why Mr. Britnell would attach a price of £2 2s. to such an object. He turned it over with contempt; on the back, a paper label remained, the left half torn away. Only the ends of two lines survived: the first bore the letters *ngley Hall*; the second, *ssex*.

Perhaps it would be worth identifying the location using a gazetteer, after which he could return the item to Mr. Britnell with a sharp critique of the dealer's judgment.

He lit the candles, as darkness had fallen, prepared tea, and joined his friend, with whom he had been playing golf—a pursuit the University authorities encourage for relaxation. Tea was consumed amidst a discussion of strokes and luck, topics familiar to golfers but tedious to the uninitiated.

The consensus was that certain shots could have been better and that neither player had received the fortune due to a human being. It was then that the friend, let us call him Professor Binks, picked up the framed engraving and asked, "What's this place, Williams?"

"Just what I am trying to determine," Williams replied, reaching for a gazetteer. "Look at the back. Somethingley Hall, either in Sussex or Essex. Half the name is gone. Do you know it?"

"It's from that man Britnell, surely," Binks said. "Is it for the museum?"

"Well, I'd buy it for five shillings," Williams admitted. "But for some unearthly reason, he demands two guineas. I can't conceive why. It's a wretched engraving, lacking even figures to give it life."

"It's not worth two guineas," Binks countered. "But I don't think it's poorly done. The moonlight seems rather good to me. I thought there were figures, or at least one, just on the edge in front."

"Let's look," Williams said. "True, the lighting is clever. Where's your figure? Ah, yes! Just the head, right at the very front."

Indeed, there it was—hardly more than a black blot on the extreme edge of the engraving. It was the head of a man or woman, heavily muffled, with their back turned to the viewer, gazing toward the house. Williams had not noticed it before.

"Still," he murmured, "though it's more skilled than I thought, I cannot spend two guineas of museum funds on a picture of a place I do not know."

Professor Binks had work to attend to and soon departed. For nearly an hour, Williams vainly attempted to identify the subject. "If the vowel before the *ng* had remained, it would have been simple," he thought. "But as it stands, the name could range from Guestingley to Langley, and there are far more such names than I realized; this rotten book lacks an index of terminations."

College hall convened at seven. He met colleagues there who had also played golf, and words of no consequence were bandied about the table. An hour or more passed in the common-room after dinner. Later, a few retired to Williams's rooms, where whist was played and tobacco smoked. During a lull, Williams picked up the mezzotint from the table without looking closely and handed it to a guest mildly interested in art, recounting its origin.

The gentleman took it carelessly, glanced at it, and said with genuine interest, "It's really a very good piece of work, Williams; it has quite a feeling of the romantic period. The light is admirably managed, and the figure, though rather grotesque, is somehow very impressive."

"Yes, isn't it?" Williams replied, busy serving whisky-and-soda to others, unable to move to inspect the view again.

By this time, it was late, and the visitors were departing. After they left, Williams wrote a few letters and cleared up odd bits of work. Finally, past midnight, he prepared for bed, extinguishing his lamp after lighting his bedroom candle. The picture lay face-up on the table where the last guest had placed it. As he lowered the lamp, it caught his eye. What he saw nearly made him drop the candle; he declared that had he been left in the dark at that moment, he would have suffered a fit. In the middle of the lawn before the unknown house stood a figure where none had existed at five o'clock that afternoon. It crawled on all fours toward the house, muffled in a strange black garment bearing a white cross on the back.

I do not know the ideal course of action in such a situation. I can only tell you what Mr. Williams did. He took the picture by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he possessed. There, he locked it in a drawer, barred the doors of both sets of rooms, and retired to bed. First, however, he wrote out and signed an account of the extraordinary transformation the picture had undergone since entering his possession.

Sleep came late, but it was consoling to reflect that the picture's behavior did not rely solely on his unsupported testimony. Evidently, the man who had viewed it the night before had seen something similar; otherwise, he might have suspected a grave malfunction of his eyes or mind. He would ask his neighbor, Nisbet, to breakfast with him and spend the morning over the gazetteer.

Nisbet was free and arrived around 9:30. His host was not fully dressed, even at that late hour. During breakfast, Williams mentioned only that he had a picture requiring Nisbet's opinion. Those familiar with University life can imagine the wide and delightful range of topics two Fellows of Canterbury College discuss during a Sunday morning meal, from golf to lawn-tennis. The country-houses of Eastern England possess a peculiar, damp charm that has always drawn me in. They are typically modest structures, often in the Italian style, nestled within parks of eighty to a hundred acres. One imagines the grey palings of split oak, the noble trees, the meres choked with reeds, and the distant lines of wood. There is the pillared portico, perhaps clumsily attached to a red-brick Queen Anne house, disguised with stucco to mimic the "Grecian" fashion of the late eighteenth century. Inside, the hall stretches to the roof, ideally accompanied by a gallery and a small organ. The library holds treasures ranging from thirteenth-century Psalters to Shakespeare quartos. Above all, I delight in imagining the life within such a house during its founding days, amidst the landlords' prosperity, and even now, when money may be scarce but taste is varied and life remains intriguing. I dream of owning one, possessing enough wealth to maintain it and host friends modestly.

This is a digression. I must recount a series of curious events that unfolded in a house fitting this description: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While the building has undergone significant changes since the era of my tale, its essential features remain—the Italian portico, the white square block, the older interior, the park fringed with woods, and the mere. However, one defining feature has vanished. To the observer standing in the park, a great old ash-tree once grew within a dozen yards of the wall, its branches almost touching the building. It likely stood there since Castringham ceased to be a fortified place, surviving the filling of the moat and the construction of the Elizabethan dwelling. By 1690, it had nearly reached its full, terrifying dimensions.

That year, the district became the scene of numerous witch trials. It will take centuries to form a just estimate of the solid reason, if any existed, behind the universal fear of witches in those times. Did the accused truly believe they possessed supernatural power? Did they harbor the will, if not the power, to harm their neighbors? Or were the countless confessions merely extorted by the cruelty of witch-finders? These questions remain unsolved, and the present narrative gives me pause. I cannot dismiss it as mere invention; the reader must judge for themselves.

Castringham contributed a victim to the *auto-da-fé*. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole. She differed from the typical village witch only in her relative wealth and influence. Reputable farmers of the parish attempted to save her, testifying to her character and showing considerable anxiety regarding the jury's verdict.

Yet, what proved fatal to the woman was the evidence of the then proprietor of Castringham Hall, Sir Matthew Fell. He deposed that on three separate occasions, under the full moon, he had watched her from his window gathering sprigs "from the ash-tree near my house." She had climbed into the branches, clad only in her shift, cutting small twigs with a peculiarly curved knife while appearing to converse with herself. Each time, Sir Matthew attempted to capture her, but she took alarm at accidental noises he made. When he descended to the garden, all he could see was a hare running across the path toward the village.

On the third night, he followed at his best speed, going straight to Mrs. Mothersole's house. He waited fifteen minutes, battering at her door, before she emerged cross and sleepy, as if just awakened, offering no explanation for his visit.

Based largely on this testimony, supplemented by lesser evidence from other parishioners, Mrs. Mothersole was found guilty and condemned to die. She was hanged a week after the trial, alongside five or six other unfortunate souls, at Bury St Edmunds.

Sir Matthew Fell, then Deputy-Sheriff, attended the execution. It was a damp, drizzly March morning as the cart ascended the rough grass hill outside Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other victims were apathetic or broken by misery; Mrs. Mothersole, however, remained distinctively herself. Her "poisonous Rage," as a contemporary reporter noted, worked so violently upon the bystanders—even the hangman—that she was universally regarded as presenting the living aspect of a mad devil. Yet, she offered no resistance to the officers of the law. Instead, she looked upon those who laid hands upon her with such direful and venomous intensity that one officer later assured Sir Matthew that the mere thought of her gaze preyed inwardly upon his mind for six months afterward.

All that she reportedly said were the seemingly meaningless words: "There will be guests at the Hall." She repeated them more than once in an undertone.

Sir Matthew Fell was not unimpressed by the woman's bearing. After the assizes, he discussed the matter with the Vicar of his parish, Mr. Crome, with whom he traveled home. His evidence at the trial had been given reluctantly; he was not particularly infected with the witch-finding mania, yet he declared he could offer no other account than the one he had given and could not possibly have been mistaken. The whole transaction repugned him, for he was a man who preferred pleasant terms with his neighbors, but he felt a duty to be done. The Vicar applauded this sentiment, as any reasonable man must.

A few weeks later, under the full moon of May, the Vicar and the Squire met again in the park and walked to the Hall. Lady Fell was with her dangerously ill mother, leaving Sir Matthew alone at home; thus, the Vicar was easily persuaded to take a late supper at the Hall.

Sir Matthew was not good company that evening. Conversation centered on family and parish matters. By chance, Sir Matthew made a written memorandum of certain wishes regarding his estates, a document that would later prove exceedingly useful.

When Mr. Crome considered departing around half-past nine, the two men took a preliminary walk on the gravelled path at the rear of the house. The only incident that struck the Vicar occurred as they approached the ash-tree near the windows. Sir Matthew stopped and asked, "What is that running up and down the stem of the ash? Is it never a squirrel? They should all be in their nests by now."

The Vicar looked and saw the moving creature, but the moonlight obscured its color. The sharp outline, visible for an instant, was imprinted on his brain. He swore, though it sounded foolish, that the creature, whether squirrel or not, possessed more than four legs.

Still, little could be made of this momentary vision, and the two men parted. They may have met since, but it was not for a score of years.

The next day, Sir Matthew Fell was not downstairs at six, nor seven, nor eight. The servants knocked at his chamber door, listening anxiously and battering the panels. Finally, the door opened from the outside. They found their master dead and black. There were no immediate marks of violence, but the window was open.

One servant fetched the parson, who then rode to notify the coroner. Mr. Crome hastened to the Hall and was shown the room where the dead man lay. His notes reveal the genuine respect and sorrow felt for Sir Matthew. He transcribed a crucial passage regarding the circumstances of the death:

"There was not the least trace of a forced entrance to the chamber; the casement stood open, as my poor friend would always have it in this season. He had his evening drink of small ale in a silver vessel of about a pint measure, and tonight he had not drunk it out. This drink was examined by the physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, who could not, upon oath before the coroner's quest, discover any venomous matter within it. Naturally, given the great swelling and blackness of the corpse, neighbors spoke of poison. The body was much disorder'd, twisted in an extreme manner, suggesting my worthy friend expired in great pain and agony. What remains unexplained, and to myself the argument of some horrid and artful design by the perpetrators of this barbarous murder, is this: the women entrusted with laying out and washing the corpse, both sad and respected in their mournful profession, came to me in great distress of mind and body. They confirmed, upon the first view, that no sooner did they touch the breast of the corpse with their naked hands than they felt a violent smart and aching in their palms, which spread to their forearms. The pain continued, swelling them immoderately, forcing them to lay aside their calling for many weeks, yet no mark was seen on the skin.

"Upon hearing this, I summoned the physician still in the house. With the aid of a small crystal magnifying lens, we carefully examined the skin but detected nothing of importance beyond a couple of small punctures or pricks. We concluded these were the spots by which the poison might have been introduced, recalling the ring of Pope Borgia and other specimens of the horrid art of Italian poisoners of the last age.

"As to what I must add, it is merely my own experiment, left to posterity to judge its value. On the table by the bedside lay a small Bible, in which my friend, punctual as in matters of less moment, used nightly and upon rising to read a set portion. Taking it up—with a tear duly paid to him, as from the study of this poorer adumbration we pass to the contemplation of its great original—it occurred to me, in moments of helplessness, to try that old and superstitious practice of drawing the *Sortes*. A principal instance, in the case of his late sacred majesty King Charles and my Lord Falkland, was much talked of. I must admit my trial afforded little assistance; yet, as the cause and origin of these dreadful events may hereafter be searched out, I set down the results, in case they point the true quarter of the mischief to a quicker intelligence than my own.

"I made three trials, opening the book and placing my finger upon certain words. The first yielded Luke xiii. 7: 'Cut it down.' The second, Isaiah xiii. 20: 'It shall never be inhabited.' And upon the third experiment, Job xxxix. 30: 'Her young ones also suck up blood.'"

This concludes the quotation from Mr. Crome's papers. Sir Matthew Fell was duly coffined and buried. His funeral sermon, preached by Mr. Crome the following Sunday, was printed under the title "The Unsearchable Way; or, England's Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist." It was the Vicar's view, shared by the neighborhood, that the Squire was a victim of a resurgence of the Popish Plot.

His son, Sir Matthew the Second, succeeded to the title and estates. It is unsurprising that the new Baronet did not occupy the room in which his father had died, nor did anyone else sleep there during his entire occupation. He died in 1735, leaving no particular mark on his reign save for a curiously constant mortality among his cattle and livestock, which tended to increase over time. Those interested in details will find a statistical account in a letter to the *Gentleman's Magazine* of 1772, drawn from the Baronet's own papers. He finally ended the affliction by a simple expedient: shutting up all his beasts in sheds at night and keeping no sheep in his park. He observed that nothing was ever attacked that spent the night indoors. Afterward, the disorder confined itself to wild birds and beasts of chase. Lacking a good account of the symptoms and finding all-night watching unproductive, I do not dwell on what the Suffolk farmers called the "Castringham sickness."

The second Sir Matthew died in 1735 and was succeeded by his son, Sir Richard. It was in his time that the great family pew was built on the north side of the parish church. So large were the Squire's ideas that several graves on that unhallowed side had to be disturbed to satisfy his requirements. Among them was that of Mrs. Mothersole, whose position was accurately known thanks to a note on a plan of the church and yard made by Mr. Crome.

Interest was excited in the village when it was known that the famous witch, still remembered by a few, was to be exhumed. Surprise and disquiet were strong when it was found that, though her coffin was sound and unbroken, there was no trace inside of body, bones, or dust. It is a curious phenomenon, for at the time of her burial, resurrection-men were not dreamed of, and it is difficult to conceive any rational motive for stealing a body other than for the dissecting-room.

The incident revived stories of witch trials and witch exploits, dormant for forty years. Sir Richard's orders that the coffin be burnt were thought foolhardy by many, though they were duly carried out.

Sir Richard was a pestilent innovator. Before his time, the Hall had been a fine block of mellow red brick. Having traveled in Italy and become infected with the Italian taste, and possessing more money than his predecessors, he determined to leave an English house for an Italian palace. Stucco and ashlar masked the brick; indifferent Roman marbles were planted in the entrance hall and gardens; a reproduction of the Sibyl's temple at Tivoli was erected on the opposite bank of the mere. Castringham took on an entirely new, and I must say, less engaging, aspect. Yet it was much admired and served as a model for neighboring gentry in after-years.

One morning in 1754, Sir Richard woke after a night of discomfort. The wind had blown persistently, causing his chimney to smoke, yet it was so cold he needed a fire. Something had rattled about the window, denying him peace. Furthermore, guests of position were expected that day, and the inroads of the distemper among his game had been serious, threatening his reputation as a game-preserver. What touched him most, however, was the matter of his sleepless night. He could certainly not sleep in that room again.

This was the chief subject of his meditations at breakfast. Afterward, he began a systematic examination of the rooms to find one suitable to his notions. Long before he found one: this had an eastern window, that a northern; this door servants would always pass, and he disliked the bedstead there. Viborg stands as a jewel among the towns of Jutland, a place where history clings to the cobblestones with a grip that feels almost supernatural. It is the seat of a bishopric, crowned by a cathedral that is handsome yet startlingly new, surrounded by a garden of deceptive charm and a lake that mirrors the sky with unsettling clarity. Storks wheel overhead, their cries echoing the ancient murders committed nearby; just across the water lies Hald, a picturesque haunt, and closer still, Finderup, the site where Marsk Stig slaughtered King Erik Glipping in 1286. When Erik's tomb was finally breached in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows of iron maces were found scored into his skull, a grim testament to violence that seems to linger in the very air. Yet, I am not here to write a guidebook for tourists seeking the macabre.

My cousin, Mr. Anderson, arrived in Viborg not as a tourist, but as a scholar driven by a peculiar obsession with the Church history of Denmark. He sought the Rigsarkiv, hoping to unearth papers preserved from the great fire of 1726 that detailed the final, agonizing days of Roman Catholicism in the land. For his lodging, he chose the Golden Lion, an inn that survived the inferno which consumed much of the town's older architecture. It is a formidable red-brick structure, its façade imposing under the setting sun, though the courtyard behind reveals a jarring mix of black-and-white wood and plaster that speaks of a decayed grandeur.

Anderson was delighted by the old-fashioned atmosphere, promising himself a stay that would be both satisfactory and amusing. His mission was academic, yet his mind was already drifting toward the uncanny. He requested a room large enough to serve as both bedroom and study. The landlord, a man named Herr Kristensen, suggested he inspect the available quarters. The top floor was dismissed due to the climb, and the second offered no suitable dimensions. On the first floor, however, lay a choice of rooms.

The landlord favored Number 17, but Anderson rejected it for its lack of light, facing only a blank wall. He preferred Number 12 or 14, which overlooked the street. Number 12 was selected. It was a long, high room with three windows on one side, devoid of a fireplace but warmed by a handsome, ancient cast-iron stove depicting Abraham sacrificing Isaac, inscribed with "1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22." The only other decoration was a faded colored print of the town from around 1820.

As supper approached, Anderson examined the hotel's guest list, a large blackboard divided into columns. It was a mundane roster: an advocate, some German travelers, and baggage men from Copenhagen. The only anomaly was the conspicuous absence of Number 13. Anderson, a man of method, noted this omission with a frown. He had encountered this superstition often in Danish hotels, where the thirteenth room was simply skipped. He wondered if the taboo was so potent that no landlord dared to rent it, or if it was merely a polite fiction.

That evening, after unpacking and arranging his papers, Anderson decided to read before bed. He retrieved a book from his great-coat hanging near the dining room. The journey back to his room was short, yet when he reached the corridor, he found his door locked. A frantic movement echoed from within. Confused, he checked the number on the door he had just opened: it was 13. His actual room, Number 12, was to the left. He had been in bed for minutes, reading and extinguishing his candle, before the realization struck him: the blackboard listed no Number 13, yet the room physically existed. He felt a strange pang of regret, wishing he had claimed it, perhaps to prove to the landlord that a well-born Englishman could dwell there without ill fortune. He dismissed the thought, assuming it was a servant's quarters, and drifted into sleep, unaware that the room's nature was far more sinister than mere superstition.

The following day, Anderson immersed himself in the archives. The documents were a treasure trove, particularly correspondence regarding Bishop Jörgen Friis, the last Catholic bishop of the diocese. The letters revealed a scandalous tenant in the Bishop's house, a man named Mag Nicolas Francken, accused of practicing dark arts and selling his soul to the enemy. The Protestant leaders, led by Rasmus Nielsen, condemned Francken as a viper, while the Bishop defended his court's authority. Anderson noted the Bishop's boldness in demanding proof of Francken's crimes, yet he found no record of Francken's fate. A subsequent letter from the Bishop mentioned that Francken had been "suddenly removed," implying death, yet the circumstances remained a mystery.

Leaving the archives, Anderson walked with Herr Scavenius, the archivist, who confessed that the location of the Bishop's house was a puzzle, as the maps from 1560 were incomplete. Later, returning to the Golden Lion, Anderson's curiosity about Number 13 resurfaced. He paused before the door marked 13, hearing footsteps and a sharp, hissing breath inside. He retreated to his room, only to find it seemed smaller than before. His portmanteau, left by the porter, was gone, vanished as if by magic. He dismissed the idea of theft, attributing it to the clumsiness of the *stuepige*, the local maids.

That night, looking out his window, Anderson observed the shadows of his neighbors. Across the street, a figure in Number 13 leaned on the sill, illuminated by a flickering red lamp. The figure was tall and thin, draped in dark fabric. Suddenly, a distant step caused the figure to vanish instantly, the red light extinguished. Anderson, smoking a cigarette, placed the butt on the sill and went to bed.

Morning brought a rude awakening. The maid laughed at his demand to know where his portmanteau was, leaving him bewildered when he found it back in its original spot. Then, another shock: his cigarette butt was found on the sill of the middle window, not the right-hand one where he had smoked. He rushed to check the numbers again. He passed Number 14, then Number 12. There was no Number 13. Three errors in twelve hours drove a methodical man to the brink of madness. He decided to observe further, convinced he was witnessing a trick of the mind or a hallucination born of fatigue.

At supper, Anderson confronted the landlord about the missing number. Kristensen laughed it off, explaining that travelers simply refused to sleep in Number 13, fearing bad luck. He insisted the room did not exist. Anderson, however, remained uneasy. That evening, he invited the landlord to his room to smoke, hoping to verify the layout. As they sat, the lawyer in Number 14 began to dance wildly in his room, his shadow flailing against the opposite wall. Soon, a high, thin, and terrifying voice began to sing from the room next to the lawyer's—Number 13. The song was disjointed, a despairing moan that sent shivers down their spines.

The lawyer, Herr Jensen, burst into the room, furious and confused, claiming the noise was coming from his neighbor. Anderson and the landlord realized the horror: the singing was emanating from the non-existent Number 13. They armed themselves with sticks and umbrellas and marched down the corridor. The door to Number 13 was locked. Jensen pushed, but it held firm. Desperate, they called for the strongest servants. When the door finally gave way, it did not open into a room. Instead, the plaster wall of the corridor stared back, scarred by the crowbar's blow. Number 13 had ceased to exist.

In the silence that followed, broken only by a rooster's crow, the group stood frozen before the blank wall. The landlord, terrified, offered them a different room. The next morning, they returned to investigate. Servants, acting as carpenters, tore up the floorboards beneath the vanished room. They found no skeleton, no body of Mag Nicolas Francken. Instead, they uncovered a small copper box containing a vellum document with twenty lines of writing.

Anderson and Jensen, a paleographer, struggled to decipher the script. It was neither Latin nor Old Danish, but something ancient and alien. Anderson, shaken by the experience, refused to draw conclusions, though he knew the implications were dire. He recounted the tale to me months later in a wood near Upsala, his face grim. He spoke of Daniel Salthenius, a student who sold his soul to Satan, and scoffed at the boy's ignorance of the company he kept. Anderson would not elaborate on the document found in the copper box, leaving the mystery of Number 13 and the fate of Mag Nicolas Francken to haunt the silence between us.

The documents from which I have woven this narrative arrived in my possession through circumstances that remain, for now, a sealed mystery. Before presenting the extracts themselves, it is imperative to establish the provenance of these papers. They constitute a hybrid archive: a portion comprises the typical travelogues of the mid-nineteenth century—chatty, illustrated volumes detailing hotel accommodations, carriage routes, and the rambling conversations of foreign peasants, reminiscent of Horace Marryat's *Journal of a Residence in Jutland*. However, as the author's journey progressed, these mundane observations curdled into a singular, harrowing record of personal terror, culminating on the eve of his flight.

The writer was Mr. Wraxall. From the scant evidence of his own trembling pen, I deduce a man past middle age, wealthy but profoundly isolated, drifting through hotels and boarding houses with the intention of settling down—a dream that never materialized. It is likely that the catastrophic fire at the Pantechnicon in the early seventies consumed much of his history, including property he once stored there. He had previously published an anonymous work on a holiday in Brittany, but his final obsession was a projected book on Scandinavia. Seeking to interweave travelogue with the history of great Swedish families, he secured introductions to the nobility and departed for Sweden in the summer of 1863.

His inquiries led him to the manor of Råbäck, a formidable red-brick structure dating to the early seventeenth century, built by a scion of the De la Gardie family. Though the family received him with polite hospitality, Wraxall preferred the independence of the village inn, accepting the daily trek to the manor. The estate was surrounded by ancient timber and overlooked a small lake, with a church perched atop a rocky knoll nearby. To the English eye, the church was peculiar: low, crowded, and dominated by a grotesque "Last Judgment" painting. But it was the addition to the church that haunted Wraxall—a mausoleum erected by the founder of the house. An eight-sided, domed structure of stark white walls and copper roof, it stood separate from the nave, accessible only by its own northern portal.

On his first night, Wraxall noted the mausoleum's keyhole, glimpsing marble effigies and copper sarcophagi within, sparking an insatiable curiosity. The family papers he examined revealed a dark history: the first Count Magnus de la Gardie was a tyrant who suppressed peasant uprisings with brutal efficiency, ordering executions and burning encroaching homesteads. The local innkeeper whispered of a "Black Pilgrimage" the Count had undertaken, returning with something—or someone—unnatural. When pressed, the landlord evaded the question, citing a summons to Skara, leaving Wraxall to piece together the truth from the archives.

Among the account books, Wraxall discovered a tract titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis* (Book of the Black Pilgrimage). The fragment read: "If any man desires to obtain a long life... it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince..." The text hinted at alchemy bordering on necromancy. This revelation transformed the Count in Wraxall's mind from a mere historical figure into a sorcerer of grim renown.

That evening, driven by a solitary compulsion, Wraxall found himself inexplicably walking straight to the churchyard gate, having missed his own dinner. He spoke aloud to the tomb, unaware that a cleaning woman's dropped metal object had startled him. The next day, he interviewed the parish deacon. When asked about Chorazin, the clergyman spoke of Antichrist being born there and recounted a chilling tale of two men who ventured into the Count's forbidden woods. They heard a scream that seemed to twist a soul from its body, followed by a laugh that was not human, and finally, the sound of a great door shutting. The men who investigated found one survivor, Hans Thorbjorn, frantically pushing at invisible forces, and the corpse of Anders Bjornsen, whose face had been sucked clean from his skull, his eyes staring upward even in death.

Armed with this lore, Wraxall returned to the mausoleum. Inside, the sarcophagi were magnificent, but the lid of the Count's tomb bore a disturbing engraving: a hunter pursued by a monstrous, tentacled form, and a cloaked figure watching from a hillock. As Wraxall copied the epitaphs, he noticed that two of the three massive steel padlocks securing the coffin were loose. He collected them, placing them on the window ledge, unable to unlock the third. He felt a strange, morbid fascination with the "ferocious and grim" nobleman, unaware that the silence of the building was about to be shattered.

On his final day, delayed by the De la Gardie family's insistence on dining with him, Wraxall lingered on the churchyard knoll. Determined to bid farewell to the Count, he approached the mausoleum. As he spoke his goodbyes, a blow struck his foot. A heavy object clattered onto the pavement—the third padlock. Before he could fully rise, the sound of creaking metal echoed, and the lid of the sarcophagus shifted upward. Terrified, Wraxall fled the building, discovering to his horror that he could not turn the key in the lock. He ran, leaving the sound of the hinges behind him, unsure if he had heard them close again.

Wraxall survived the journey to England, but he was broken. His journals became disjointed, filled with frantic attempts to describe passengers on canal boats who were never counted at meal times: a man in a long black cloak, a short figure in a hood. Upon landing at Harwich, he hired a closed fly to escape to Belchamp St Paul. There, under a moonlit sky, he encountered two shadowy figures at a crossroads—one tall with a hat, one short with a hood—who caused his horse to bolt in sheer panic. He had seen them before.

Trapped in a lodging house, Wraxall wrote of his despair, wondering what sin he had committed to invite such pursuit. He locked his doors and cried to God, unaware that his story had already ended. Years later, the house at Belchamp St Paul stood empty until it came into my possession as part of a legacy. I had it demolished, and in a forgotten cupboard beneath the window of the master bedroom, I found the papers of the antiquary, the final testament of a man who had stared into the abyss and found it staring back.

"I suppose you'll be packing up soon, Professor, now that the term is over," a voice murmured beside the Professor of Ontography. They sat in the hospitable hall of St James's College, the air thick with the scent of roast meat and impending departure.

The Professor was young, his movements precise, his speech clipped.

"Yes," he replied, adjusting his cuffs. "My friends have coerced me into golf this term. I intend to head to the East Coast—Burnstow, I believe—for a week or ten days to refine my swing. I hope to leave tomorrow."

"Oh, Parkins," interjected his neighbor, a man whose face was etched with the dust of old libraries, "if you're heading to Burnstow, pray inspect the site of the Templars' preceptory. Let me know if it warrants a dig this summer."

It was, naturally, a man of antiquarian obsession who spoke, though his name remained unspoken in this prologue.

"Certainly," said Parkins. "If you describe the location, I shall assess the terrain upon my return, or correspond with you if you prefer."

"Don't bother," the antiquarian dismissed. "I plan to bring my family that way in the Long. Since few English preceptories were ever properly planned, I might find an opportunity to contribute something useful on my off-days."

Parkins sniffed delicately at the notion that planning a ruin could be termed 'useful.' The antiquarian pressed on. "The site lies submerged, I suspect. The sea has devoured that stretch of coast. Maps suggest it's three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn, at the town's northern tip. Where will you lodge?"

"At the Globe Inn," Parkins admitted. "I secured a room there. Most lodgings are shuttered in winter, and the only space available is a double-bedded chamber. They claim they lack a corner to store the second bed. However, I require a spacious room for my books and work. While I dislike the prospect of an empty bed—or two—in what I shall call my temporary study, I suppose I can endure the inconvenience for a fortnight."

"Do you call sharing a room 'roughing it,' Parkins?" a bluff voice boomed from across the table. "I shall descend and occupy it. It will be excellent company."

Parkins stiffened, forcing a courteous smile. "By all means, Rogers. Though I fear it may prove dull. You do not play golf, do you?"

"No, thank Heaven!" Mr. Rogers declared, his tone rough.

"When I am not writing, I shall likely be on the links," Parkins explained, his politeness masking a rising anxiety. "That might be tedious for you."

"Oh, I don't know! There's surely someone I know in the place. But if you don't want me, speak the word, Parkins. I shall not be offended. Truth, as you always remind us, is never offensive."

Parkins was scrupulously polite and strictly truthful, yet a conflict raged within his breast, silencing him for a moment. When he finally spoke, his voice trembled slightly. "Well, if I must speak the exact truth, Rogers, I am uncertain if the room accommodates us both comfortably. Furthermore, you might hinder my work."

Rogers laughed heartily. "Excellent, Parkins! Don't worry. I promise not to interrupt. No, I shall not come if you forbid it; but I thought it might ward off the ghosts." He winked at his neighbor. Parkins flushed crimson. "I beg your pardon," Rogers continued, feigning innocence. "I forgot you abhor levity regarding such matters."

"Since you raised the topic," Parkins said, raising his voice to assert authority, "I must admit I do not tolerate careless talk concerning ghosts. A man in my position cannot afford to appear to sanction such beliefs. To concede even a semblance of their existence is tantamount to renouncing my most sacred convictions."

"Your *undivided* attention," Rogers corrected softly, "was what Dr. Blimber actually said. But I apologize, Parkins; I am stopping you."

"No, not at all," Parkins said smoothly. "I do not recall Dr. Blimber. But I trust you understand my meaning."

"Yes, yes," Rogers hastened, "we shall discuss this fully at Burnstow."

The following day, Parkins arrived in Burnstow. He was welcomed at the Globe Inn and installed in the large double-bedded room. Before retiring, he arranged his materials in perfect order upon a commodious table surrounded by windows facing the sea. The central window looked straight out to the water, while the flanking ones commanded the shore to the north and south. To the south lay the village; to the north, only the beach and low cliffs. Between the inn and the sea stretched a narrow strip of rough grass dotted with old anchors, followed by a broad path and the shingle beach. The distance had shrunk to mere sixty yards.

The inn's population was predominantly golfers. The most conspicuous figure was Colonel Wilson, an *ancien militaire*, secretary of a London club, possessing a voice of thunderous volume and staunch Protestant views. These opinions often erupted after his attendance at the Vicar's services, a man of picturesque ritual whom the Colonel despised.

Parkins, driven by pluck, spent the day improving his game alongside the Colonel. By afternoon, the Colonel's demeanor had turned lurid, his features flushed red, his moustache bristling. Parkins decided it wiser to avoid walking home with him.

"I might walk home tonight along the beach," Parkins reflected. "There will be sufficient light to inspect the ruins Disney mentioned. I expect I shall stumble upon them."

He accomplished this literally. Picking his way from the links to the shingle, his foot caught on a gorse root and a stone, sending him tumbling. He rose to find himself in broken ground covered with depressions and mounds. Upon examination, these proved to be masses of flints embedded in mortar, overgrown with turf. He was undoubtedly on the preceptory site. The foundations were likely shallow enough to reveal the general plan. Remembering the Templars' habit of building round churches, he noted a series of mounds arranged in a rough circle.

Driven by a mix of amateur curiosity and a desire to oblige Mr. Disney, Parkins paced the circular area, noting dimensions in his pocket-book. He then examined an oblong eminence east of the center, likely a platform or altar. At its northern end, a patch of turf had been removed. Deciding to probe for masonry, he took out his knife and scraped away the earth.

Suddenly, soil fell inward, disclosing a small cavity. He struck matches, but the wind extinguished them all. Tapping the sides with his knife, he confirmed it was an artificial hole in masonry, rectangular and smooth. It was empty—until he withdrew his knife and heard a metallic clink. Reaching in, he retrieved a cylindrical object. In the fading light, he saw it was a metal tube, about four inches long, of considerable age.

By the time he verified the contents, darkness had fallen. He resolved to spare more daylight for archaeology the next day. The object, he felt, held at least slight value.

Bleak and solemn was the view as he headed home. A faint yellow light marked the links, the squat Martello tower, and the pale ribbon of sands intersected by black wooden groynings. The wind was bitter from the north. As he rattled through the shingle, he glanced back. An indistinct figure seemed to be making desperate efforts to catch up, yet the distance remained unchanged. Parkins dismissed it as a trick of the light, though he recalled childhood tales of fiends meeting travelers on lonely shores.

At dinner, peace reigned in the Colonel's bosom. Parkins, a respectable player, spent the evening at bridge. Retiring around midnight, he felt satisfied. Passing the lobby, he met the boots, who informed him a piece of pipe had fallen from his pocket and was placed on his chest of drawers.

Curious, Parkins examined the object by candlelight. It was bronze, shaped like a modern dog-whistle. He cleared the caked sand from its interior and took it to the window to empty the debris. Outside, the night was clear. He noted a belated wanderer on the shore before shutting the window.

Taking the whistle to the light again, he saw markings. Rubbing them revealed an inscription: *FLA FUR BIS FLE* on the front, and *QUIS EST ISTE QUI VENIT* on the back. "Who is this who is coming?" he translated tentatively. "The best way to find out is to whistle for him."

He blew tentatively. The note was soft yet carried an infinite distance, a sound that seemed to conjure images of a dark expanse and a lonely figure. Suddenly, a gust of wind slammed against the casement, shattering the vision. The pressure was immense. The window banged shut and latched itself. Candles were extinguished by the gale.

Parkins struggled to secure the window, feeling as though he were battling a burglar. The wind moaned desolately. Unable to sleep, he lay awake, convinced his organs were failing, until a neighbor tossed restlessly nearby. Images formed in his closed eyes: a long stretch of shore under a gathering storm. A black figure ran frantically over the groynes, looking back in terror. Behind him, a pale figure in fluttering draperies pursued, moving with terrifying speed. The runner collapsed under a groyne. The pursuer hovered, then darted forward.

Parkins opened his eyes, lit a candle, and read a book until sleep claimed him. He forgot to blow out the candle, leaving a mess of guttered grease on the table.

The next morning, a maid entered. "Would you like extra blankets, sir?"

"Yes, please. It seems likely to turn colder."

She returned with a blanket. "Which bed, sir?"

"The one I slept in last night."

"Oh! I beg your pardon, sir. You seemed to have tried both. We had to make them both up this morning."

"Did it seem slept in?"

"Oh yes, sir! Everything was crumpled and thrown about, as if someone had passed a very poor night."

Parkins apologized, attributing the disorder to his own unpacking, and mentioned a friend from Cambridge might occupy the other bed. The maid giggled and departed.

Parkins set out to improve his game. The Colonel, initially repining, became chatty. "Extraordinary wind last night," the Colonel boomed. "In my homeland, we'd say someone was whistling for it."

Parkins explained his skepticism of the supernatural. "I am a convinced disbeliever."

"Sadducee," the Colonel muttered.

Parkins offered a rational theory: eccentric individuals whistling on beaches were mistaken for wind-callers by superstitious villagers. "Last night, I was whistling. I blew a whistle twice, and the wind seemed to answer."

The Colonel interrupted. "Whistling, were you? What sort of whistle?"

Parkins described finding the bronze artifact the previous day. The Colonel grunted, advising caution with relics of Papists, before diverting to the Vicar's suspected Jesuit leanings. They played well until dusk, forgetting everything else.

Turning the corner of the house, the Colonel was nearly knocked down by a boy clinging to his legs, panting in terror. "I seen it wive at me out of the winder!" the boy wailed. "A figure in white... not a right person."

The Colonel insisted on investigating. The boy described the second-floor window of the Globe. Reconnoitering, Parkins realized it was his own room. He found the door locked, the key inside. Inside, the second bed was a tangled mess of clothes.

The maid confirmed she had made both beds that morning and hadn't been since. No one else had a key. Parkins suspected the boy was fabricating a story. The Colonel, however, grew silent and pensive. Before retiring, he warned Parkins, "You know where I am if you need me."

That night, Parkins rigged a screen of rugs and umbrellas to block the moonlight from his bed. He slept soundly for an hour until a sudden clatter shook him. His screen had failed. Moonlight flooded his face. He listened, certain of movement in the empty bed opposite.

Then, a figure sat up.

Parkins leaped from his bed, grabbing a stick, and dashed to the window. But the figure in the empty bed moved with a smooth, unnatural grace, slipping out and positioning itself between the two beds, arms outstretched, blocking the door. Parkins froze, paralyzed by the impossibility of passing it or being touched by it.

The figure moved in a stooping posture, groping blindly. It turned toward the bed Parkins had just vacated, bending over the pillows in a way that made Parkins shudder. Realizing the bed was empty, it moved into the light, facing the window.

Parkins saw it then: a horrible face of crumpled linen.

With formidable speed, the figure swept a corner of its drapery across Parkins's face. He cried out in disgust. Instantly, the figure leaped toward him. Parkins scrambled backward through the window, screaming, as the linen face thrust close to his own.

Deliverance came as the Colonel burst the door open, seeing the dreadful group at the window. Only one figure remained. Parkins collapsed into a faint. On the floor lay a tumbled heap of bedclothes.

The Colonel barred the room, tended to Parkins, and took the other bed. Early the next day, Rogers arrived. The three held a long consultation. For centuries, the canons of Steinfeld have whispered of a hidden hoard belonging to Abbot Thomas, a fortune in gold buried beneath the monastery grounds. Though they have searched in vain, the legend persists: Thomas, in the prime of his life, concealed a massive pile of wealth nearby. When pressed for its location, he would laugh and reply, "Job, John, and Zechariah shall reveal it to you or your descendants," adding that he bore no ill will toward those who might eventually claim it. Among his many works, the Abbot is remembered for filling the great eastern window of the south aisle with exquisite stained glass, a testament to his piety and artistry, and for restoring the Abbot's lodging with a marble-adorned well in the courtyard. He died suddenly in 1529, at the age of seventy-two.

"I suppose I must translate this," the antiquary murmured to himself, finishing his copy of the *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*. "Better to do it now than later." He rendered the Latin into English, noting the gossip of the treasure and the details of the Abbot's final years.

The antiquary's true quest, however, was to locate the lost stained glass of Steinfeld Abbey. Following the dissolution of German monasteries, vast quantities of such glass had found their way to England, adorning parish churches and private chapels. Steinfeld's contribution was significant, identifiable by inscriptions and narrative cycles. Yet, a specific passage in the *Sertum* had led him to a different conclusion. In a private chapel, he had encountered three large figures—Job, John, and Zechariah—each holding a scroll with a peculiar inscription. Job's scroll read, "There is a place for gold where it is hidden"; John's, "They have on their raiment a writing which no man knoweth"; and Zechariah's, "Upon one stone are seven eyes." These texts deviated strangely from standard scripture, hinting at a coded message. The antiquary, Mr. Somerton, deduced that these figures were part of Abbot Thomas's original window, and that the Abbot's cryptic answer regarding the treasure pointed directly to the glass itself.

Convinced that the secret lay within the painted figures, Somerton traveled to the chapel. His hypothesis was confirmed: the style matched the era, and another pane displayed the arms of Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen. The connection was undeniable. The antiquary spent weeks poring over the glass, tracing every mark and inscription, convinced that the Abbot had set a riddle for posterity. The first clue, Job's altered text, clearly referenced hidden wealth. The others remained enigmatic until Somerton returned to his Berkshire manor, determined to crack the code.

On a crisp autumn morning, Mr. Gregory, the Rector of Parsbury, stepped out to greet the postman. Among the mail was a letter bearing a foreign stamp, written in a clumsy but distinctly English hand. It was from William Brown, the confidential valet of Mr. Somerton. The missive was a frantic plea for help: Somerton had suffered a "naasty shock" and was confined to bed. He requested that Gregory travel immediately to Coblentz, then to Steinfeld, to assist him. The letter ended with a postscript correcting the destination: "The Village for Town I will not Turm. It is name Steenfeld."

The arrival of the letter plunged the quiet parsonage into chaos. By evening, Mr. Gregory was aboard a train to Antwerp and then to Coblentz, securing passage to Steinfeld. The town itself was a melancholy place, a small settlement surrounded by ruined abbey buildings, its ancient grandeur stripped away by time and revolution. The inn where Gregory and Brown were lodged was the only option, and Brown was a study in nervous energy, far removed from his usual composure.

Upon entering Somerton's room, they found the antiquary pale and trembling, sitting up in bed. He was relieved to see Gregory but immediately became agitated. "Don't ask me what it is," Somerton urged, gripping Gregory's hand. "I'm not fit to explain yet. It involves keeping something... putting it back. It is perfectly safe in the daytime. Brown will show you tomorrow." He instructed Brown to take Gregory to the spot where the object lay, warning them to keep the matter quiet and to lock the door, as "they" liked it that way. That night, Gregory slept fitfully, convinced he heard fumbling at his locked door, a sensation that haunted him until dawn.

The next morning, Gregory accompanied Brown to the designated location. The task was brief and uneventful, though Gregory was forbidden from asking what they had retrieved. Later that evening, as Somerton recovered his strength, he finally offered the explanation.

Somerton recounted his journey to Steinfeld, driven by the desire to solve the mystery of the painted glass. On his second visit to the chapel, he had meticulously examined the inscribed scrolls. The text on Job's robe, "There is a place for the gold where it is hidden," was the key. However, the other scrolls contained only jumbled letters. A stroke of luck occurred when Lord D——, the chapel's owner, sent a servant to clean the dusty window. As the broom scraped the black borders of the figures' robes, Somerton noticed yellow stains appearing where the pigment had been removed. Beneath the black layer, he discovered clear, yellow capital letters forming a cryptogram.

Each figure had a count of letters scratched on the border: thirty-eight for Job, thirty-nine for John, and forty for Zechariah. Realizing this was a numerical cipher, Somerton worked tirelessly. He noticed that Job held one finger up, John two, and Zechariah three. This was the key: skip one letter, then two, then three. Decoding the jumble revealed the first part of the message: "Decem millia auri reposita sunt in puteo in atrio..." (Ten thousand pieces of gold are laid up in a well in the court...). The remaining letters, when decoded using alternating skips, completed the phrase: "...domus abbatialis de Steinfeld a me, Thoma, qui posui custodem super ea. Gare à qui la touche." (By me, Thomas, who have set a guardian over them. Beware who touches it).

Armed with this knowledge, Somerton and Brown traveled to Steinfeld. They located the Abbot's house and the courtyard well, a structure adorned with Italian marble and biblical carvings. The well was deep, with a spiral staircase descending into the darkness. Somerton descended cautiously, counting the steps. At the thirty-eighth step, he found a patch of masonry that felt smoother than the rest. Striking it with a crowbar, he revealed a slab engraved with a cross composed of seven eyes—the mark of Zechariah.

Removing the slab, Somerton peered into the cavity. The air was foul, but a candle burned steadily. Inside, he felt a heavy, leather-like bundle. As he pulled it toward the entrance, the candle flickered out. Suddenly, Brown screamed and fled up the stairs, leaving Somerton alone in the dark.

In the silence, a terrible form emerged from the shadows. It was not a man, but a grotesque, humanoid entity with a sunken, grinning face and limbs that clung to Somerton's body. The creature smelled of rot and mold, pressing its cold face against Somerton's as it dragged him down into the well. Somerton screamed, clawing at the rope that bound him, until Brown, brave and quick, hauled him back to safety.

Brown later described seeing a "holdish man" with a fallen face laughing at them before vanishing, though no one else was there. The creature had been a guardian, a manifestation of the Abbot's curse, active only in the dark. Somerton spent a nightmare-filled night recovering, plagued by the smell of mold that seemed to emanate from outside his door, confirming that the guardians were creatures of the night.

With the dawn breaking, the horror receded. Gregory and Brown returned to the well, replacing the stone slab and sealing it with mud to restore the facade. Gregory noted a grotesque carving on the well-head labeled "Depositum custodi"—"Keep that which is committed to thee." The treasure remained, guarded by the unseen, waiting for a successor to the riddle of Abbot Thomas.
