As the seasons shifted, the rhythm of life on Oleron's stairs altered drastically. Soon, the only regular visitor was the postman, whose footsteps became so sparse—occurring merely once or twice a week—that they seemed to vanish into the silence of the house. Letters piled up unanswered; a missive from his publishers regarding his new manuscript gathered dust, followed by a second, and then nothing more.

Outside, spring arrived with deceptive cheerfulness. The privet bushes bloomed fiercely beside the jagged notice boards, and flower-sellers lined the curbs of the shopping streets. Oleron bought flowers every single day. His room screamed for them, demanding fresh blooms without cease, and he indulged this hunger lavishly. Yet, the simple necessity of venturing outdoors to procure them gnawed at him. Each return to the sanctuary of his home brought a profound, swelling relief.

He sensed a shift within himself—a recalibration of his senses. It was not a healing of old wounds, but an expansion of his perception, one that now encompassed a specific, terrifying dread. *Lux orco, tenebrae Jovi.* The name of this fear was agoraphobia. He began to hate the open air, the vast spaces where the unknown might strike at his unprotected back.

Soon, he arranged for his food and flowers to be delivered directly to his doorstep. When he realized this convenience, he rubbed his hands in satisfaction. *That* was better. Now, he could choose his confinement freely. Quickly, this choice hardened into a habit, then a pleasure. He delighted in being immured. Yet, beneath this self-imposed prison lay a strange, twisted unhappiness. He fretted, he wept from sheer weakness, yet he possessed a dim certainty that he would trade neither his sorrow nor his chains for the chaotic merriment of the world outside.

Noise, once ignored, now became an acute torture. It pressed against his eardrums with unbearable intensity. On the rare occasions he ventured forth, he clung to walls, seeking comfort in the cold iron of railings. Inside, he moved like a ghost in slippers, pausing for long moments to ensure a door closed with absolute silence, finding joy in the stillness itself.

Sunday mornings had become an ordeal. Under the influence of the fine weather, a group from the sect led by the long-nosed Barrett assembled in the square below his windows. They beat drums and clashed brass instruments, raising anguished cries as they struggled toward their God. Barrett himself, face upturned and brow furrowed, prayed aloud that his voice pierce the ears of all unbelievers—and inevitably, it pierced Oleron's.

One afternoon, mid-rhapsody, Oleron lunged to pull down his blind. Through the fabric, he heard his own name becoming the subject of a fresh torrent of prayer. Sometimes, unable to resist, he would stand frozen and whisper softly. Occasionally, he called "Romilly!" and waited, though most often his whispers formed no names at all.

There was one specific vantage point in his apartment he frequented with growing obsession: the threshold of his bedroom door. By opening every internal door except the main entrance—which he treated with reluctant hesitation—he could arrange himself to view the entirety of his living quarters from a single spot. He could survey his sitting room completely, see most of his bedroom, and catch fleeting glimpses of the kitchen, bathroom, and study. Often, he lingered there, breathless, finger pressed to his lips.

On one such occasion, staring into the void between rooms, he wondered if the deceased Madley had ever grasped the strategic value of that doorway. Light, too, had become an enemy. The direct sunlight flooding his rooms as the day progressed burned like a flame in his brain; even diffuse daylight was a dull, numbing ache. Consequently, he began the ritualistic lowering of his crimson blinds. He made brief, daring trips to adjust them, always ensuring his retreat remained accessible. Soon, this became a daily discipline. His rooms transformed into a photographer's darkroom, bathed in the blood-red gloom of twilight.

One evening, as he lowered the blind in his study and retreated, a soft laugh escaped him. "*That* bilks Mr. Barrett!" he whispered, finding amusement in having thwarted the priest's prayers for an entire hour. But the mood shifted swiftly. Another day, a terror seized him. Standing in the embrasure of the window above the seat where he had found the harp-bag, he had drawn the cord to darken the glass. In that moment, he thought he saw the tail of a black-and-white checkered skirt round the corner of the house. Was it Elsie? He wasn't certain, but the possibility sent him into an agony of suspense. He strained to hear her tread on the stairs... but silence answered him. After several minutes, he exhaled sharply.

"By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly!" he muttered, pacing. "Horribly compromising... no woman could endure that... not any kind of woman... oh, the extremity of it!"

Yet, despite these fears, he was not truly happy. Fits of quiet weeping visited him unpredictably, drifting through his mind like clouds across the square. Perhaps, he reasoned, if he was not happy, he was simply not unhappy either. Nothing had been taken from him, for nothing had ever been given. He waited for that granting, in his flower-laden, frighteningly enticing apartment, where pith-white walls were suffused by the crimson gloom of his blinds.

He ignored the dwindling supply of money and the cessation of his professional output. Had he stopped working? No. They were fools who believed Oleron had ceased to labor. He was merely beginning. He was crafting a masterpiece, birthing a Mistress in the gestation of his Art. Men would witness her soon enough, provided he survived this period of probation and agonizing waiting. How could mortal men comprehend the Fair One of Oleron until Oleron himself knew her? Radiant creations are not mass-produced; they require the weeping, the vain hope, and the relentless pursuit of the artist chasing a capricious, mocking spirit that ensures the chase never ends. If he hunted this Huntress a little longer, she would eventually be panting in his arms.

Those who claimed Oleron had stopped working were wrong. If everything else was slipping away, he welcomed it. Just as we all do when our Fair Ones beckon. At first, we pretend to resist, promising to delay our devotion, to turn her wiles against her, to ignore her wheedling advances. Perhaps a heartless sprite dwells within us all, never fooled, but ultimately, everything falls away. She beckons, and all goes.

So, Oleron maintained his strategic post at the bedroom door, watching, waiting, smiling with a finger on his lips. It was his duty, his worship, his vow. Even when he found himself hating the dead Madley and wishing for his non-existence, he recognized that hatred as an acceptable offering.

But as he prepared himself for this marriage, chafing at the Bride's silence, he made a discovery he should have reached weeks prior. Thinking of Madley, he recalled the fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*. Since the night he foolishly believed that calculated neglect would break the lovely Beckoner's resolve, he had shoved those manuscripts into the window seat, forgetting their existence entirely. But his own jealousy of Madley triggered a memory of Elsie's jealousy toward her flesh-and-blood rival. He remembered the pages.

"Fool that I was!" he cried silently. Had he expected his Desire to manifest while the evidence of his divided loyalty still existed? What of her passion, so fierce and centered that it had twice attempted to destroy her rival? Fool that he was!

If that was the price—the pledge and sacrifice—she demanded, she should have it. Quickly!

He retrieved the manuscript from the window seat and carried it to the fire, which he kept perpetually alight. The heat released the final scent of the flowers that banked his room. Time lost meaning; his clock had long since stopped ticking, seeming a foolish measure against the stupendous events unfolding around him. He knew it was late.

Kneeling before the flames, he held the *Romilly* manuscript. He had barely undone the binding holding the sheets together when he jumped, turning his head sharply to listen. The sound had been faint—merely two or three taps—but it filled him with alarm. His face darkened as the tapping resumed.

A voice drifted from the landing outside. "Paul!... Paul!..."

It was Elsie. "Paul!... I know you're in... I want to see you...."

He cursed her inwardly but remained statue-still. He had no intention of admitting her.

"Paul!... You're in trouble.... I believe you're in danger... at least come to the door!..."

Oleron stifled a low laugh. It amused him that she, supposedly in danger herself, spoke of *his* peril. Well, if she was, she deserved it; she knew, or claimed to know, all about it.

"Paul!... Paul!..." He mimicked her tone under his breath. "Oh, Paul, it's _horrible_!..."

_Horrible_, was it? thought Oleron. Then let her flee.

"I only want to help you, Paul.... I didn't promise not to come if you needed me...."
