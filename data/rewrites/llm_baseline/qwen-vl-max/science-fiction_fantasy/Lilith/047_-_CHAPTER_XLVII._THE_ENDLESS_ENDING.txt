I have not yet found Lona. But Mara walks beside me—closer than breath, deeper than thought. She has shown me truths I once thought beyond the reach of mind or soul, and still she teaches. Is it possible that the waking I believed to be real was itself only a dream? That I remain in the chamber of death, asleep, dreaming—not yet ready to awaken fully? Or perhaps I never truly surrendered to sleep, and so stirred awake too soon, before the time was right?

If that awakening was but a dream, then it must have been a dream of a greater awakening yet to come—a vision pointing toward a truth more radiant than any I have known. Such a dream cannot be false; it must contain within its very fabric a deeper reality, a lovelier truth pulsing beneath the surface of illusion.

When doubt creeps in, I cry aloud: *“Could God Himself fashion such beauty as I dreamed?”*
And Hope answers: *“Whence then came thy dream?”*
*“From my own darkness,”* I reply, *“into the light of consciousness.”*
But Hope persists: *“Then where did that darkness first receive the seed?”*
*“My brain gave birth to it,”* I say, *“and the fever in my blood fathered it.”*
*“Say rather,”* Hope suggests gently, *“that your brain was the violin, and the fever the bow that drew the music from its strings.”*
But then I pause—*“Who crafted the violin? Who guided the bow? Who placed each bird upon its bough in the tree of life, and startled it into song in perfect order?”*
*“Whence came the fantasia?”* I ask. *“And whence the life that danced to its rhythm?”*
Did I, deep in the silence of my unconscious self, whisper: *“Let beauty be; let truth seem”*—and at once, beauty bloomed, while truth wore only the semblance of being?

Man dreams and desires.
God broods, wills, and brings forth life.
When a man dreams his own dream, he is captive to its shape. But when another gives him the dream—the One who knows its end—he can trust it to unfold as it should.

I no longer seek the mirror. The hand that sent me back shall not find me returning through that door. I will not go out again by the way I came.
*“All the days of my appointed time,”* I say, *“I will wait until my change comes.”*

Sometimes, when I look upon my books, they waver—as if a hidden wind stirs their solid forms, and another world presses close behind. At times, when I walk outside, the sky and earth tremble, the trees and grass blur for an instant, as though all things were about to dissolve. Then, just as quickly, they settle—returning to their familiar shapes, as if nothing had changed.

Yet I hear whispers now—soft voices, like lovers speaking of me in hushed tones. When I turn, they vanish. No words remain. Only silence, thick and profound.
Are these echoes born within me, or do they drift in from some other realm? I do not chase them. They come unbidden, and I let them pass.

Strange memories flicker—shadows of moments I cannot name—appearing through misty windows of the past, even in broad daylight. But I no longer dream. Not in the old way.

Still—perhaps the moment I feel most awake, I am dreaming the deepest of all.

But when I finally awaken into that life which cradles this one as a mother holds her child, I will know. I will know with certainty. And then there will be no doubt.

I wait.
Whether asleep or awake, I wait.

Novalis once said: *“Our life is no dream—but it should and will perhaps become one.”*
