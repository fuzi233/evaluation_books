Mrs. Lambert stood by the roadside, her lily-white handkerchief pressed to her lips, watching the travelers pace down the path. Her daughters lingered beside her, their eyes fixed on the retreating figures until a cluster of lime trees obscured them from view. For this good matron, those green trunks served as a veil between the domestic sphere and the wider world. Through them, she had long since bid farewell to husbands marching to war, sons bound for school, and brothers venturing into unknown lands, trusting that Heaven's will would guide their return with joy and love. The quiet rhythm of home life—filled with the empty spaces where beloved ones had slept, the lingering scent of a boy's carpet bag, and the ghostly echo of his voice at evening prayer—had taught her patience and deepened her affection. Now, separated by the rustling leaves, she fell silent, a thoughtful figure amidst the garden. Beside her, Miss Theo, gifted with a poetic spirit, composed verses for the trees, which her delighted mother later sent to a periodical, while the Colonel, embarking on his journey, offered a final salute.

"I know my wife watches from her window until we round this bend," Colonel Lambert declared, tipping his hat to the departing gentlemen. "I trust, Mr. Warrington, you will find pleasure in visiting us again, especially with the boys returned."

"And I," replied Mr. Warrington, "shall count it a supreme happiness to leave friends so dear."

"The host for tonight has a son who is likewise an old friend of ours," the Colonel continued, his tone lightening. "My wife, a tireless matchmaker, would have paired him with one of my daughters, but fate intervened. Colonel James Wolfe chose another."

"Ah!" sighed Mr. Warrington.

"There were valiant souls before Agamemnon," the Colonel remarked with a twinkle in his eye. "Though I suspect you meant to ask if his name was Aga..."

"No, sir, James Wolfe," corrected the Colonel, laughing gently. "He is scarcely thirty, yet holds the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel, surpassing nearly all the nobility who hold commission before us. I have witnessed him leading captains twice his senior, men of greater age and experience, yet lacking his brilliance and fortune. Though enviable, his superiority is unresented; he is beloved by every soul in our old regiment and a scholar as profound as he is a soldier."

Harry Warrington bowed his head, a mixture of shame and humility coloring his features. "Sir, I confess I have squandered my youth. I return to England an ignoramus. If my brother George had lived, he would have upheld our name far better than I. He was a scholar, a musician, conversant with the wisest minds of our realm. In comparison, I feel but a hollow shell."

"If you recognize your ignorance, you have gained wisdom already," the Colonel smiled. "Do not despair. Here, the gentry may know little beyond hounds and horses, yet I assure you, a gentleman need not be a scholar to be worthy. Look around: His Royal Highness commands, Martin Lambert leads as Colonel, and Jack Hunt, who follows behind, was once a private soldier and now stands as a loyal groom. Does it matter if one wears epaulets or a currycomb? I once dreamed of glory and honor, but now I cherish duty, the quiet companionship of family, and the simple joys of home. Come, let us hasten; night falls quickly."

Their journey led them to Westerham, where they were greeted by a stately household comprising a veteran soldier, his wife, and their son—the aforementioned Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe. Though physically unattractive, with a gaunt frame, pale complexion, and sharp features, Wolfe possessed a courtesy so radiant and a conversational warmth so engaging that listeners forgot his appearance and marveled at his spirit.

Supper ensued, marked by lively jests. The host's wife, overhearing whispers between her son-in-law and the young Colonel, teased them about an impending alliance with a lady of the illustrious Lowther family.

"If she were the daughter of Charlemagne," Lambert quipped, "she would still be beneath James Wolfe."

"Nay, Mother," Wolfe protested, "if she knew her stature, she would hesitate."

"She is a priceless gem," the mother insisted, "while you are... well, you are just James."

The old Colonel chuckled. "We tend our children through fever and plague, sending them to distant wars with heavy hearts, only to welcome them back for fleeting weeks. Yet, in the end, a stranger arrives with bright eyes, and our boys, who cared not for us, depart forever."

"How did you meet James's father?" the younger Colonel asked curiously.

"His father was gout-ridden and required care," the lady explained simply, "more than I cared for him personally."

As dawn broke, Colonel Lambert released Harry Warrington into the custody of Mr. Wolfe, turning his steed toward home. Wolfe, eager for Tunbridge Wells, spurred forward, while Harry, his heart heavy with longing, trailed slightly behind. Despite his delay, Harry spoke highly of the Lamberts, describing them as paragons of virtue. Wolfe, however, seemed preoccupied with a vision of domestic tranquility.

"Glorious deeds and honors mean little now," Wolfe mused. "My dreams have shifted. Having served since adolescence, traversed garrison towns, and fulfilled nearly every duty of arms, I now seek peace, books, a loving wife, and children to dandle upon my knee. True love outweighs glory; a quiet fireside with the woman of one's heart is the highest gift the gods can offer."

Harry nodded, though his countenance betrayed a lack of genuine enthusiasm.

"You agree so readily," Wolfe observed, noting Harry's somber expression, "yet a fireside and a sweetheart seem to hold little appeal for a young man like yourself."

"Why, Colonel," Harry replied, "one cannot deny the allure of adventure. Having known but a fraction of life, I dream of campaigns, distinction, and fame. Only after witnessing the world's breadth might I consider retirement."

Wolfe, his mind elsewhere, launched into a passionate eulogy of marital bliss, recounting the virtues of his mistress with evident fervor—a subject that stirred him deeply, though it failed to resonate fully with Harry, whose outlook on marriage remained tinged with melancholy.

Their ride concluded uneventfully near Tunbridge Wells, interrupted only by a misunderstanding involving two mounted men who appeared to be highwaymen. Wolfe, amused, reassured Harry that the riders were merely agents for local inns. Gumbo, Harry's mount, bolted in a panic, requiring considerable effort to rein in before the pair reached their destination and separated to seek out their respective acquaintances.

In Tunbridge, Harry discovered his Aunt installed in grand lodgings, attended by a retinue of lacquers. Upon his arrival, she received him with boundless kindness. However, the atmosphere shifted dramatically with the entrance of a fashionable beau and two dowagers adorned in voluminous hoops and heavy rouge. Introducing Harry as her nephew, the "Young Croesus of Virginia," the Baroness launched into a torrent of exaggerations. She described his estate as larger than Kent itself, teeming with gold mines, spices, and diamond fields, and claimed his mother ruled a vast dominion akin to a kingdom. She spoke of his immense wealth and noble bearing, delighting the visitors with tales of his generosity and virtue.

"You could not have arrived at a finer moment, my dear," the Baroness confided to Harry once the guests departed. "These three have the sharpest tongues in the town. They will trumpet your virtues across every circle. I have introduced you to hundreds and spun a web of lies about Virginia's geography to elevate your status. I fear I have overstated the size of your estate, filling it with wonders that may not exist, and portrayed your mother as a sovereign princess. Yet, the world believes, and belief is power. A London newspaper has already heralded your arrival, and tomorrow, the entire congregation will turn from their prayers to gaze upon you as a golden idol."

"But how does this reputation benefit me?" Harry asked, bewildered.

"To enter the world, the key must be gold, real or imagined," she advised wisely. "To be thought rich is nearly as potent as being rich. Mothers will smile, daughters will curtsy, and doors will swing wide. Do not let vanity cloud your judgment. Remember, I saved you from the clutches of Lord Castlewood, a gambler who ruined himself and nearly destroyed my niece. Gaming is the madness of our family; I, too, am not entirely cured. Beware the card tables, my dear nephew. The world is cruel, and it thrives on appearances."

Harry listened, his cheeks flushing as he reflected on the stark contrast between the virtuous simplicity of Oakhurst and the gilded, deceptive opulence of Tunbridge. The old lady's words about gambling and her own "cures" struck a discordant chord, reminding him of the moral decay he had witnessed among his relatives. Yet, as his aunt patted his cheek, whispering of her protective instincts, Harry felt a pang of guilt. He could not bear to see the kind villagers of Oakhurst spoken of lightly by these worldly figures, nor could he reconcile the gap between the truth of his humble beginnings and the fabricated grandeur his aunt sold to the town. With a sense of unease, he realized that the world outside awaited him with a mask of falsehood, a mask he would soon be forced to wear.
