In recent times, a deluge of curates has descended upon the north of England, blanketing the hills in such density that every parish boasts one or more. They are young, vigorous, and theoretically destined to perform great deeds of charity. Yet, we shall not speak of this modern era. We turn our gaze backward to the beginning of the nineteenth century. The present age is dusty, sunburnt, and arid; we shall evade its harsh noon, retreat into a siesta, and dream only of the dawn.

Do not let this prelude mislead you into expecting a romance. Do not anticipate sentiment, poetry, or reverie. Do not look for passion, stimulation, or melodrama. Calm your expectations; lower them to a humble standard. Before you lies something real, cool, and solid—something as unromantic as a Monday morning when the working class wakes with the grim realization that they must rise and labor. It is not denied that you might later taste excitement, perhaps near the meal's conclusion, but the first dish served shall be austere enough for a Catholic, even an Anglo-Catholic, to consume on Good Friday during Passion Week: cold lentils and vinegar without oil, unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roast lamb.

In those distant years, the abundant shower of curates had not yet fallen. In 1811–1812, curates were scarce. There was no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to assist worn-out rectors in hiring vigorous young colleagues from Oxford or Cambridge. The successors of the apostles, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of the Propaganda, were then merely being hatched under cradle-blankets or undergoing regeneration via nursery-baptism in wash-hand basins. One could not guess, gazing at any of them, that the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps concealed the brows of preordained saints, nor foresee in the folds of their long night-gowns the white surplices they would later wield to exercise the souls of parishioners and confound old-fashioned vicars.

Yet, even in those days of scarcity, the precious plant existed, though rare. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a twenty-mile radius. You shall see them. Step into this neat garden-house on the skirts of Whinbury, walk into the little parlour. There they are at dinner. Allow me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's lodgings, belonging to John Gale, a small clothier. Mr. Donne has kindly invited his brethren to dine with him. You and I shall join the party to observe and listen. For now, however, they are merely eating; while they eat, we shall converse aside.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing the boundless energy of that age—an energy their moping old vicars wish to channel into pastoral duties, urging them to supervise schools and visit the sick. But the youthful Levites find this dull. They prefer lavishing their energies on a routine that, to others, appears heavy with ennui and cursed with monotony, yet yields them an unfailing supply of enjoyment and occupation.

I refer to their frantic back-and-forth movement between their respective lodgings—not a circle, but a triangle of visits maintained year-round, regardless of season or weather. With unintelligible zeal, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What attracts them is difficult to define. It is not friendship, for they quarrel whenever they meet. It is not religion; the topic is never named, though theology is occasionally discussed, piety is never mentioned. Nor is it the love of food and drink; each could enjoy equally good joints, puddings, and tea at his own lodging. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the good ladies mean themselves, for indeed they are kept in a perpetual "fry" by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are at dinner; Mrs. Gale waits on them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eye. She considers the privilege of hosting friends occasionally, without extra charge—a perk included in her letting terms—to have been exercised sufficiently recently. This week is only Thursday. On Monday, Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed for dinner. On Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely came to tea, stayed for supper, occupied the spare bed, and visited her again at breakfast on Wednesday. Now, on Thursday, they are both here for dinner, and she is certain they will stay all night. "C'en est trop," she would say if she could speak French.

Mr. Sweeting minces his slice of roast beef, complaining of its toughness; Mr. Donne remarks that the beer is flat. Indeed, that is the worst of it: if they were merely civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much. If they seemed satisfied with what they received, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and scornful, setting everyone beneath their 'fit.'" They treat her with less than civility precisely because she does not keep a servant but performs the household work herself, as her mother did before her. Then they constantly speak against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk; by that very token, Mrs. Gale believes none of them to be a true gentleman or of gentle kin. "The old parsons are worth the whole lump of college lads; they know what belongs to good manners and are kind to high and low."

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone that, though prolonged for only two syllables, proclaims him a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale hates Mr. Malone more than either of the others, yet she fears him. He is a tall, strongly built figure with genuine Irish legs and arms, and a face that is authentically national—not the Milesian type, nor Daniel O'Connell's style, but the high-featured, North-American-Indian visage belonging to a certain class of Irish gentry. It has a petrified, proud look, better suited to the owner of a slave estate than to the landlord of a free peasantry. Mr. Malone's father called himself a gentleman; he was poor, in debt, and besottedly arrogant, and his son was like him.

Mrs. Gale offered the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," said her guest; and the "woman" cut it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolted absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates had good appetites. Though the beef was "tough," they ate a great deal of it. They swallowed a tolerable allowance of the "flat beer," while a Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables disappeared like leaves before locusts. The cheese received distinguished attention, and a "spice-cake," served as dessert, vanished like a vision. Its elegy was chanted in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. He had counted on the reversion of the cake, and when his mother brought down the empty platter, he lifted his voice and wept sorely.

Meanwhile, the curates sat sipping their wine, a liquor of unpretending vintage, moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone, indeed, would have preferred whisky, but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, did not keep the beverage. While sipping, they argued—not on politics, philosophy, or literature, topics now entirely without interest for them—not even on practical or doctrinal theology, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline, frivolities that seemed empty as bubbles to all save themselves. Mr. Malone, contriving to secure two glasses of wine while his brethren contented themselves with one, gradually became hilarious in his fashion: insolent, speaking rudely in a hectoring tone, and laughing clamorously at his own brilliance.

Each companion became his butt in turn. Malone had a stock of jokes he served regularly on such occasions, seldom varying his wit, for he never considered himself monotonous and cared little for others' opinions. He favored Mr. Donne with hints about his extreme meagreness, allusions to his turned-up nose, cutting sarcasms regarding a threadbare chocolate surtout worn whenever it rained, and criticisms of a choice set of Cockney phrases and pronunciations, Mr. Donne's own property and certainly deserving remark for the elegance they communicated to his style.

Mr. Sweeting was bantered regarding his stature—he was a little man, a mere boy in height and breadth compared to the athletic Malone; rallied on his musical accomplishments—he played the flute and sang hymns like a seraph, some young ladies of his parish thought; sneered at as "the ladies' pet"; and teased about his mother and sisters, for whom poor Mr. Sweeting retained a lingering regard, and of whom he was foolish enough to speak in the presence of the priestly Paddy, from whose anatomy the bowels of natural affection had somehow been omitted.

The victims met these attacks in their own ways: Mr. Donne with stilted self-complacency and half-sullen phlegm, the sole props of his otherwise somewhat rickety dignity; Mr. Sweeting with the indifference of a light, easy disposition that never professed to have any dignity to maintain.

When Malone's raillery became too offensive, which it soon did, they joined forces to turn the tables, asking him how many boys had shouted "Irish Peter!" after him that day as he walked the road (Malone's name was Peter—the Rev. Peter Augustus Malone); requesting to know if it was the Irish custom for clergymen to carry loaded pistols in their pockets and a shillelah in their hands during pastoral visits; inquiring the meaning of words like *vele*, *firrum*, *hellum*, *storrum* (which Mr. Malone invariably pronounced *veil*, *firm*, *helm*, *storm*); and employing other retaliatory methods suggested by their innate refinement.

This, of course, would not do. Malone, neither good-natured nor phlegmatic, was presently in a towering passion. He vociferated and gesticulated; Donne and Sweeting laughed. He reviled them as Saxons and snobs at the very top pitch of his high Celtic voice; they taunted him with being a native of a conquered land. He threatened rebellion in the name of his "counthry," venting bitter hatred against English rule; they spoke of rags, beggary, and pestilence. The little parlour was in uproar; one might have thought a duel must follow such virulent abuse. It seemed a wonder that Mr. and Mrs. Gale did not take alarm at the noise and summon a constable to keep the peace. But they were accustomed to such demonstrations. They well knew that the curates never dined or took tea together without a little exercise of the sort, and were quite easy regarding consequences, knowing that these clerical quarrels were as harmless as they were noisy, resulting in nothing, and that, on whatever terms the curates parted that night, they would surely meet as the best of friends the next morning.

As the worthy pair sat by their kitchen fire, listening to the repeated, sonorous contact of Malone's fist with the mahogany plane of the parlour table, the consequent start and jingle of decanters and glasses following each assault, the mocking laughter of the allied English disputants, and the stuttering declamation of the isolated Hibernian, a foot was heard on the outer doorstep, and the knocker quivered to a sharp appeal.

Mr. Gale went to open the door.

"Whom have you upstairs in the parlour?" asked a voice—remarkable in tone, nasal and abrupt.

"O Mr. Helstone, is it you, sir? I could hardly see you for the darkness; it is so soon dark now. Will you walk in, sir?"

"I want to know first whether it is worth my while walking in. Whom have you upstairs?"

"The curates, sir."

"What! All of them?"

"Yes, sir."

"Been dining here?"

"Yes, sir."

"That will do."

With these words, a person entered—a middle-aged man dressed in black. He walked straight across the kitchen to an inner door, opened it, inclined his head forward, and stood listening. There was something to listen to, for the noise above was just then louder than ever.

"Hey!" he ejaculated to himself, then turning to Mr. Gale—"Have you often this sort of work?"

Mr. Gale had been a churchwarden and was indulgent to the clergy.

"They're young, you know, sir—they're young," he said deprecatingly.

"Young! They want caning. Bad boys—bad boys! And if you were a Dissenter, John Gale, instead of being a good Churchman, they'd do the like—they'd expose themselves; but I'll----"

To finish this sentence, he passed through the inner door, drew it after him, and mounted the stairs. After listening for a few minutes upon arrival at the upper room, he made entrance without warning and stood before the curates.

They were silent; they were transfixed; and so was the invader. He—a personage short of stature but straight of port, bearing on broad shoulders a hawk's head, beak, and eye, the whole surmounted by a Rehoboam or shovel hat, which he did not seem to think necessary to lift or remove before the presence in which he stood—folded his arms on his chest and surveyed his young friends, if friends they were, much at his leisure.
