"Eli," Catherine commanded, her voice cutting through the domestic haze with the precision of a surgeon's blade, "answer me one question like a man, and I shall ask no more today. What is wormwood?"

Eli faltered, momentarily paralyzed by the sudden demand upon his faculties, as if struck by a physical blow. Yet, he rallied quickly, offering a definition rooted in mere sensation rather than spirit. "It is something that tastes main bitter."

"That is a fair answer, my man, but not the one I seek," she replied, her eyes hardening.

"Then answer it yourself," he challenged, retreating behind the shield of his ignorance.

"And shall," Eli grunted, the weight of her scrutiny pressing down on him. "Wormwood is—to have two in the house a-doing nought, but waiting for thy shoes and mine."

The shaft struck home with devastating accuracy. Catherine's expression hardened further. "Methinks waiting for their best friend's coffin, that and nothing to do, are enow to make them worse than Nature meant. Why not set them up somewhere, to give 'em a chance?"

Eli hesitated, admitting his willingness but voicing a fear born of experience: "But I am afraid they will drink and gamble their very shelves away."

"Nay," Catherine countered, her tone leaving no room for doubt. "Dost thou take me for a simpleton? Of course I mean to watch them at the starting, and drive them with a loose rein, as the saying is." Her gaze sharpened, piercing the veil of his hesitation. "Where didst thou think of doing this? Not here; to divide our own custom."

"Not likely," Eli muttered. "I say Rotterdam against the world. Then I could start them."

Oh, self-deception! The true motive driving this entire maneuver was not merely commerce, but the desperate, aching proximity to little Gerard. Following many heated discussions and eager promises of amendment on these specific terms, Catherine departed for Rotterdam, accompanied by Kate, to hunt for a shop. Seeking a change of air and purpose, they soon located a premises in a respectable street, though it stood sadly out of order. Undeterred, they seized the opportunity; the dilapidation allowed them to secure it at a fraction of the cost. Instantly, they set to work, scrubbing the floors until they gleamed, fitting proper shelves for the burgeoning business, and rendering the dwelling-house habitable once more.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson found himself constantly approaching Margaret, inquiring what he might do to assist her. Her response invariably fell in a tone laden with sorrowful resignation: "Nothing, Luke, nothing."

"What, you that are so clever, can you think of nothing for me to do for you?" he pleaded, his hope flickering like a candle in a draft.

"Nothing, Luke, nothing," she repeated, closing herself off.

But eventually, she varied her reply, opening a narrow crack in her defenses: "If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate about."

The slave of love, consumed by devotion, consented joyfully. Soon, Luke had fashioned a small cart for Kate, cushioning it with care, and even yoked himself into it. At eventide, he drew her out of the town and along the pleasant boulevard, with Margaret and Catherine walking beside them. On the surface, it appeared a happier party than reality warranted. Kate, however, enjoyed the moment keenly; little Gerard was placed in her lap, and she doted on him with a tenderness that transformed the scene into something ethereal—a cherub carried by a little angel, or a rosebud resting in the cup of a lily.

Yet, beneath this fragile peace, the vulgar jeered. They mocked Luke, asking how a thistle tasted and questioning whether his mistress could not afford a horse with four legs instead of a man. Luke did not mind these taunts; he bore them with stoic silence. But Kate minded them for him.

"Thou hast made the cart for me, good Luke," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "'Twas much. I did ill to let thee draw me too; we can afford to pay some poor soul for that. I love my rides, and to carry little Gerard; but I'd liever ride no more than thou be mocked for't."

"Much I care for their tongues," Luke declared, his jaw set in defiance. "If I did care, I'd knock their heads together. I shall draw you till my mistress says give over."

"Luke, if you obey Kate, you will oblige me," Margaret interjected softly.

"Then I will obey Kate," Luke affirmed.

An honorable exception to the prevailing mockery was Jorian Ketel's wife, Joan. She spoke with a prophetic gravity that silenced the crowd. "That is strength well laid out, to draw the weak. And her prayers will be your guerdon; she is not long for this world; she smileth in pain."

These were the words of Joan, speaking of death and suffering. Single-minded Luke, however, dismissed such spiritual currency. "I do not want the poor lass's prayers," he stated firmly. "I do it to please my mistress, Margaret."

After that, Luke often pressed Margaret to give him something substantial to do, without success. But one day, seemingly tired of his persistent importuning, she turned on him. With a look and an accent that conveyed a depth of emotion I should in vain try to convey, she delivered her final command: "Find me my boy's father."
