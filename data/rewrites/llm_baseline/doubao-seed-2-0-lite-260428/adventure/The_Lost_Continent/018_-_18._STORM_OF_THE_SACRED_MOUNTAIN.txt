It was Nais herself who bade me lay aside my care for her and turn to the sterner duties calling from outside. The thunder of Phorenice’s attack rumbled through the thick stone walls of the chamber where I knelt tending her, and she lifted her head from the cushions, pale and still weak, to ask what the din meant. I kept the tale short — she had no strength for long, tedious accounts — and laid out the full story of her nine-year enchanted sleep, and the war that had torn Atlantis apart in that time.

A faint flush of color rose to her cheeks, and her fingers brushed my wrist. “My lord is the only man in all the world fit to wear Atlantis’ crown,” she whispered.

“I swore I would not touch a single step of this campaign until I had brought you back, safe and whole, to this Sacred Mountain,” I told her.

She laughed softly, a warm, throaty sound I had not heard in nine long years. “How we poor women are treated as mere chattels for your men’s great schemes! But I cannot say I mind being your bargaining chip, not a bit. You should not have let me stand in the way of Atlantis’ good, Deucalion. Still… it is such sweet joy to know you were weak, just once, for my sake.” Her gaze drifted to the sunken marble bath in the corner of the room, and she blinked, as if chasing a half-remembered thought. “My wits are still foggy. Did I imagine waking old and wrinkled, all those years trapped asleep?”

“You are just as you were the first night I saw you,” I said, “the most beautiful woman that ever walked the shores of Atlantis. Your strength will seep back fast now that you are awake — no hurrying, though. You must rest.”

“Come closer, one moment,” she murmured, tugging at my sleeve. “No mirrors here. Am I really not all lined and gray?” I reassured her again, and she took the bowl of warm broth I offered her, sipping slowly. “My hair has grown long, I see,” she said, twisting a lock between her fingers. “I once thought you loved ruddy gold hair best, not plain brown. I was half tempted to dye it for you, once.” Then her eyes drifted shut, and she settled back into the soft pile of cushions, smiling, and fell into deep, healthy sleep. I pulled a wool coverlet over her shoulders, kissed her light enough not to wake her — holding my ragged, overgrown beard back so it would not scratch her cheek — and stepped out of the chamber.

That beard had felt like a heavy, uncomfortable burden all those hours, and I found a small empty antechamber lined with old priest’s implements, shaved it clean down to bare chin, and changed my tattered, travel-stained rags for a fine, undyed linen royal robe I found hanging in a wardrobe there. If a man is to rule as king, he owes his people the dignity of looking the part.

But judging by the rising roar of fighting outside, my kingdom was shrinking by the minute. The battle sounded closer, fiercer, every heartbeat. The great cliff gates that all men had sworn were impregnable were falling before Phorenice’s fury. No wonder: spurred mad by the destruction I had wrought on her royal city the night before, the empress was no ordinary foe. She was throwing every resource she had at the Sacred Mountain, and the mountain’s defenses, for all their cunning old stonework, could not fight on their own. Stone walls need men to man them — lots of men. In a storm like this, desperate fighters will spit at death and charge hand to hand, slingers will slip fatal shots over the ramparts, fire darts will find their marks, and even the strongest walls bleed for every hundred attackers they kill.

The slaughter was already horrendous. The last remaining band of priests holding the lowest gate rolled boulders down on the waves of stormers until the narrow stair pass was paved with writhing, screaming dead. But Phorenice stood on a rocky spur below, screaming encouragement, sending company after company marching up into that death trap with no chance of coming back. Their scaling ladders were useless against the massive gates, their blows as futile as a child pounding a stone wall with an orange — but still Phorenice poured troops up, filling the pass with corpses until she would have a level bridge of bodies to march her army over. She never counted the cost. The end justified every dead soldier, every broken family left below in the plains.

She had already seized two of the lower gates, butchering every last priest that tried to hold them. That was when I marched down to join the fight. No trumpets blared, no heralds called my title, but the priests manning the ramparts took up the name *Deucalion* as their battle cry, shouting that with a king leading them, they would never cede another foot of ground.

The name carried down to the attacking troops. A company of doomed soldiers mid-charge froze, the word *Deucalion* passing back through their ranks in whispers. I smiled grimly. After nine years, the people still remembered me. Then a glittering officer panted up the path behind them, lashing out left and right with his sword, screaming that Deucalion was long dead, that if the men did not advance they would be tortured to death by the empress’s inquisitors. I recognized him instantly. It was Tatho, my old comrade in arms.

“So you lie, Tatho,” I shouted down from the ramparts.

He started, craning his neck up to see me. “Deucalion? You are actually alive, up there? Old friend, I am sorry, but there is no truce to be made now. You stand with these mangy priests, and the empress has decreed they will all be rooted out. I am her most loyal servant.”

“You were always a fine swordsman,” I called down. “Call off this senseless killing. Challenge me one on one, in a fair fight. The winner rules Atlantis, no more butchery.”

“It would be a grand fight, you and I,” Tatho shouted back, shaking his head. “But I cannot do that. Phorenice has strictly forbidden me to risk myself in single combat. Besides, you are no one’s chief. You are not even one of the Three High Priests.”

“I am King,” I said.

He laughed. “Only you call yourself that, old man.”

“Not just me. The High Council named me yesterday. I was in Atlantis last night, right under your noses, when we tore your royal pyramid down to rubble.”

He gaped. “You were inside the city?”

“I came for Nais. She is here on the mountain, safe, and she will be my queen tomorrow. Tatho, I beg you, think of the powers stored in the Ark of the Mysteries here on the Sacred Mountain. You have heard the old prophecies. If you press this attack, you will not just lose — you will unleash a catastrophe even the High Gods will weep to see.”

“Your magic tricks do not scare us,” Tatho said stubbornly. “Phorenice has found her own magic, equal to anything hidden in that old Ark.”

“Where was that magic last night, when her palace was sliding into the dust? You saw her standing there, helpless, just as I did. Zaemon foretold that, and it came true.”

“Admitted, you caught her by surprise. But she has the Great Secret now — the secret of immortality. I will be the first to share it with her. Her twin sons, my sons, will live forever too. We will be gods on earth, and death will never touch us.”

“Tatho,” I said, “listen to me. There is no immortality for her. You know I never lie. I was named king by the High Gods’ own mouthpieces. If I do not take that throne, there will be no Atlantis left to rule. Save yourself before it is too late. You can have your old viceroyalty of Yucatan back.”

He burst out laughing, hot with anger. “Yucatan? There is no Yucatan! A horde of savage, hairy near-beasts swept down out of the northern jungles and ate our entire kingdom off the face of the land, like a column of driver ants that eats every living thing in its path, even burning to death to put out the fires you light to stop them. Yucatan is gone. Seven Egypts would not tempt me to turn traitor on Phorenice, not now. You would not throw Nais to the wolves to save yourself, would you? No — and I will not betray the woman I love, the mother of my children.” He saluted me with his sword, spun around, and the slaughter began again.

The boulders rolled down again, the pass filled with new screams. But every now and then, a sling stone or fire-tube dart slipped over the ramparts, killing one of our already few defenders. Phorenice could replace her dead thousands over. We could not replace a single lost priest. I did the math fast. At this rate, she would work her way up every gate, one after another, until the last priest on the mountain was dead. There was only one way to stop her.

The old cliff pass, that thousand-year-old carved road that men of ancient days had built through the bare rock cleft to make the mountain easy to climb — we could destroy it. I walked the path three whole times, memorizing every curve, every pier, every stone, until I found the perfect spot to bring the whole roadway crashing down into the abyss. I called every spare hand we had, young, old, half-crippled, set them to work with crowbars, iron rams, and vats of corrosive acid, tearing down the beautiful ancient carved balustrades, the sacred statues, the inscribed temple stones, hewing at the living cliff rock beneath the road. It tore at my heart to destroy that incredible handiwork of the old masters — but better it crumble into the gulf than Phorenice’s impious sandals ever tread it.

We finished the work just in time. The last twelve priests manning the lowest defending gate were about to be overrun. I sent them word to hurl one last avalanche of boulders down on the attackers, then run for their lives back up the path, across the narrow single ledge we had left intentionally, just long enough to escape. As soon as all but one wounded priest — who lost his balance and toppled screaming into the abyss — were across, we lit the fires under our iron rams. The metal heated, expanded, roared, and tore the last remaining ledge right out of the cliff face. The entire road was gone. A thousand foot sheer gulf separated our tiny band of surviving priests on the Sacred Mountain’s peak, from Phorenice’s massive army camped at the foot of the pass.

I set guards on the uppermost ramparts, sent the rest of my exhausted men to rest, then went in search of Zaemon — but he was locked inside the Ark of the Mysteries with one of the Three High Priests, performing some secret rite, and I did not have the rank to disturb him. So I turned my steps straight back to Nais’s chamber. She woke the moment I stepped over the threshold, her face lighting up like a sunrise.

She teased me about my newly shaved chin, my fine new king’s robes, pretended to be awestruck at my new royal title — then grew soft, a little sad, admitting she was jealous that Phorenice had once called me her husband. I laughed and told her that marriage had been a sham, a meaningless empty ceremony Phorenice had made up herself, that I had never been anything more than her prisoner in name. All that mattered was that I was hers, completely, forever.

We spoke of small, sweet things, the private words that only lovers share, for a little while — but the peace did not last long. From the ramparts, the sentries raised the alarm. Phorenice had no intention of retreating. The Empress herself had scouted the gulf, measured every inch of it, and her skilled craftsmen down in the plains were already forging, cutting, and fitting together massive beams and metal planks for some enormous, impossible bridge machine that she intended to haul up the broken pass, to carry her army straight across the chasm, right to our peak. The storm of the Sacred Mountain was not over. It had only just begun.
