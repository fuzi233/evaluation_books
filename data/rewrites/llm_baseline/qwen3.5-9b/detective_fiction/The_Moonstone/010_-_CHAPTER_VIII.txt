At this juncture, I feel compelled to pause my narrative. By cross-referencing my own memory with Penelope's detailed journal, I have determined that the period following Mr. Franklin Blake's arrival until Miss Rachel's birthday offers little of consequence. Days drifted by, bringing no events worthy of record. Therefore, with your permission and Penelope's assistance, I shall skip forward, noting only specific dates. I reserve the day-by-day recounting for the eventual climax, when the Moonstone becomes the sole obsession of everyone in the household.

Resuming our tale, we pick up immediately after the incident involving the bottle of sweet-smelling ink discovered on the gravel walk.

The following morning—the twenty-sixth—I presented this piece of "jugglery" to Mr. Franklin and recounted the events. His assessment was twofold: first, that the Indians were indeed stalking the house in search of the Diamond; and second, that they were naively deluded by their own superstitions. He explained that they believed in the efficacy of marking a boy's forehead and pouring ink into his palm, expecting him to perceive things invisible to ordinary humans. Mr. Franklin noted that similar practices exist in Europe, though without the ink, known in France as *clairvoyance* or "brightness of sight."

"The Indians assumed," Mr. Franklin reasoned, "that we would keep the Diamond here. They brought their seer to guide them to it, provided they managed to breach the house last night."

"Do you anticipate further attempts, sir?" I inquired.

"It hinges entirely on the boy's abilities," Mr. Franklin replied. "If he can truly locate the Diamond within the iron safety deposit box at Frizinghall Bank, we shall be spared further intrusions for the foreseeable future. Conversely, if he fails, we shall likely catch them in the shrubbery again before long."

I awaited this second encounter with confidence, yet strangely, it never materialized. Whether the conjurers learned of Mr. Franklin's visit to the bank and adjusted their tactics, or if the boy genuinely perceived the Diamond in its current location—a possibility I vehemently deny—or perhaps it was merely a stroke of fortune, remains unclear. The indisputable fact is that not a single Indian approached the house during the weeks preceding Miss Rachel's birthday. While the jugglers continued their trade in the town, Mr. Franklin and I maintained our vigil, carefully concealing our suspicions to avoid alerting them. Thus concludes the matter of the Indians for the present.

By the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised a new pastime to alleviate the tedium of their confinement. This activity, seemingly trivial, holds significant relevance to future events.

Most genteel individuals face a peculiar hazard: the tyranny of idleness. Lives spent searching for purpose often lead blindly into destructive pursuits. Frequently, those claiming an intellectual bent resort to tormenting nature—capturing newts, beetles, and spiders only to dissect them with cold indifference, convinced they are cultivating their minds while merely creating a mess. One sees young masters and mistresses examining spider guts through magnifying glasses or watching headless frogs descend the stairs, rationalizing this cruelty as a passion for natural history. Similarly, flowers are mutilated with sharp tools out of a morbid curiosity regarding their structure, despite the destruction of their beauty. These activities stem from an emptiness of mind and a lack of constructive labor. Compare the hardship of honest toil to the degradation of splitting petals and poking into insect bellies; gratitude should be felt that one's mind has something essential to occupy it.

Fortunately, Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel chose a path of pure chaos rather than torture. Their only victim was the wood paneling of a door.

Mr. Franklin, a man of boundless, albeit indiscriminate, genius, turned his attention to "decorative painting." He claimed to have invented a novel liquid medium, a "vehicle," to thin the paint. Its composition remains unknown to me, but its odor was potent enough to induce sneezing in passing dogs. Eager to participate, Miss Rachel donned an apron and bib. Under Mr. Franklin's guidance, she decorated her boudoir, starting with the interior of the door. He scoured the existing varnish with pumice-stone to create a raw surface, upon which Miss Rachel applied intricate patterns of griffins, birds, flowers, and cupids, copied from designs by a renowned Italian artist (whose name escapes me). The work was tedious and filthy, yet the pair displayed infinite stamina. Whenever they were not riding, receiving guests, dining, or singing, they were huddled together, busier than bees, defacing the door. As the old adage suggests, "Satan finds some mischief still for idle hands to do"; had I occupied my position in the family observing this scene, I could have penned no truer verse.

The next notable date was Sunday, June 4th.

That evening, the servants' hall buzzed with speculation regarding a domestic matter that, like the door decoration, foreshadowed future developments. Observing the evident affection between Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, combined with their striking physical compatibility, we naturally wondered if their collaboration extended beyond art. Opinions varied: some predicted a wedding before summer's end, while others, including myself, acknowledged the likelihood of marriage but harbored doubts that Mr. Franklin would be the groom.

Mr. Franklin's affection was undeniably sincere; the challenge lay in deciphering Miss Rachel's feelings. Allow me to introduce her.

Her eighteenth birthday arrived on June 21st. If you appreciate dark-complexioned women of substantial presence, Miss Rachel stands as one of the most captivating figures imaginable. She possessed a slender, perfectly proportioned frame; her movements were graceful, proving that her charm was intrinsic to her flesh, not merely her attire. Her hair was jet-black, matching her eyes, while her nose was slightly too broad for perfection. Yet, her mouth and chin were divine, and her complexion radiated a warmth akin to sunlight, yet without the sun's harsh glare. She carried her head with the erect dignity of a spear, exuding a spirited, thoroughbred energy. Her voice was clear and resonant, and her smile began in her eyes before reaching her lips.

Regarding her character, she possessed as many flaws as any woman, neither more nor less. Most notably, she was fiercely independent. Unlike most girls of her age, she held firm opinions and dared to defy societal conventions if they conflicted with her views. While admirable in trifles, this autonomy occasionally bordered on recklessness in serious matters. She rarely sought counsel, seldom announced her intentions, and shared few secrets, even with her mother. She navigated life's joys and sorrows on her own terms. As my mistress often remarked, "Rachel's best friend and her worst enemy are, one and the same: Rachel herself."

However, amidst this stubbornness, there existed not a shred of deceit. I recall no instance where she broke a promise or lied. On numerous occasions in her youth, she had absorbed the blame for a friend's transgression, enduring punishment silently. When confronted with evidence, she would shake her head defiantly and declare, "I won't tell you!" Even when punished for her silence, she never confessed to the original fault. Stubborn, yes—even devilishly so at times—but fundamentally, the finest creature to tread this earth.

This brings us to her matrimonial prospects. On June 12th, my mistress issued an invitation to a London gentleman to assist in celebrating Miss Rachel's birthday. This was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, a cousin of hers and the suitor upon whom I believed her heart was secretly fixed.

Context requires a brief explanation of the Ablewhite family. My mistress's younger sister, the Honorable Caroline, had previously suffered a romantic disappointment. Adhering to the "neck or nothing" philosophy, she married Mr. Ablewhite, a banker from Frizinghall. Although initially considered a misalliance due to his humble origins, time and social progress validated the union. The Ablewhites were wealthy, respectable, and highly regarded in the neighborhood. Mr. Godfrey, the second son, was the focus of our attention.

Despite Mr. Franklin's efforts, his chances against Mr. Godfrey appeared slim. Mr. Godfrey was physically superior, standing over six feet tall with a handsome ruddy complexion, a smooth-shaven face, and magnificent flaxen hair. Professionally a barrister and temperamentally a ladies' man, he was also a celebrated philanthropist. He served as vice-president and manager for various charitable organizations dedicated to aiding women, earning him immense popularity. He was a public figure capable of moving crowds to tears and generosity at Exeter Hall. Compared to such a polished, beloved giant, Mr. Franklin—an average man of good reputation but lesser stature—seemed ill-equipped to compete.

On June 14th, Mr. Godfrey responded. He accepted the invitation from Wednesday through Friday evening, citing prior commitments to his charitable duties. Enclosed was a poem elegantly titled "On My Cousin's Natal Day." At dinner, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin mocked the verses. Penelope, staunchly supporting Mr. Franklin, triumphantly asked for my opinion. I countered that Miss Rachel had misled me onto a false trail, predicting that Mr. Ablewhite's poetry would soon be followed by his person.

Penelope suggested Mr. Franklin might act sooner. Indeed, he left no avenue unexplored to win her favor. He quit smoking cigars solely because she expressed distaste for the stale smoke clinging to his clothes.
