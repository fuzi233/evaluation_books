The modest feast prepared by the poor widow, aided by her faithful servant Martha—who chuckled at the German customs and managed the household with a cheerful efficiency—was attended only by Major Pendennis and his two younger companions. Helen had declined the honor, sending word that she was too unwell to dine, leaving Laura to bear her company in silence. During dinner, the Major chattered loudly, oblivious to—or perhaps deliberately ignoring—the heavy, suffocating gloom that clung to the two silent sisters.

It was not until evening that Helen and Laura emerged from their confinement into the sitting-room. Helen entered leaning heavily on Laura, her back turned to the dying light, shielding her pallid, woe-stricken countenance from Arthur's view. Yet, as she approached Pen, whom she had not seen throughout the day, she collapsed into him. She wrapped her fond, trembling arms around his shoulders and pressed a kiss to his cheek. Immediately, Laura withdrew, retreating to a distant corner of the room. Pen observed the scene with a mixture of shock and irritation; his mother's voice and entire frame vibrated with a terrible tremor. Her hand, clammy and cold as ice, clutched his forehead, and she embraced him with a piteous intensity. The sight of her misery, rather than softening him, seemed to inflame the young man's inherent wrath and testiness. He barely returned her kiss, meeting her desperate appeal with a hard, cruel expression. *"She persecutes me,"* he thought bitterly, *"and she comes to me with the air of a martyr!"*

"You look very ill, my child," she whispered, her voice failing. "I cannot bear to see you looking so." She tottered onto a sofa, still gripping one of his passive hands with her thin, freezing fingers.

"I have had much to annoy me, mother," Pen said, his chest heaving. As the words left his lips, Helen's heart hammered so violently against her ribs that she sat almost dead with terror.

Warrington, Laura, and the Major stood frozen, sensing the approaching storm.

"I have received letters from London," Arthur continued, his voice rising, "and one in particular has caused me more agony than anything in my life. It informs me that previous letters of mine have been intercepted and stolen—that a young woman who has shown me nothing but love and care has been cruelly betrayed by—you, Mother!"

"For God's sake, stop!" cried Warrington. "She is ill! Don't you see?"

"Let him go on," the widow murmured faintly.

"Let him go on and kill her!" Laura shouted, rushing to her mother's side. "Speak on, sir, and watch her die!"

"It is *you* who are cruel!" Pen roared, his heart revolting indignantly at the injustice laid at his door. "It is you who attribute all this pain to me! It is you who wield your wicked reproaches, your wicked suspicions, and your wicked persecutions against those who love me—against those who brave everything for me, whom you despise and trample upon solely because they are of lower birth! Shall I tell you what I will do? What I am resolved to do now that I know your conduct? I will return to that poor girl whom you cast out of my doors and ask her to share my home with me! I will defy the pride that torments her and the pitiless suspicion that insults us both!"

"Do you mean, Pen, that you—" Helen began, her eyes wide and hands outstretched, but Laura silenced her instantly. "Silence, hush, dear mother," she cried, and the widow obeyed. Though Pen spoke savagely, she hung on his every word, nearly swooning with eagerness.

"By Gad, I say he shan't go on, or I won't hear him, by Gad!" the Major thundered, trembling with rage. "If you choose, sir, after all we've done for you, after all I've done for you personally, to insult your mother and disgrace your name by allying yourself with a low-born kitchen-maid, go and do it, by Gad—but let us, ma'am, have no further connection with him. I wash my hands of you, sir! I wash my hands of you! I am an old fellow, and I haven't long for this world. I come of an ancient and honorable family, by Gad, and I hoped, before I went off the hooks, by Jove, that the fellow I liked, raised, and nursed through life, by Jove, would do something to prove that our name—the name of Pendennis, by Gad—was left undishonoured behind us. But if he won't, damny, I say, amen. By G—, both my father and my brother Jack were the proudest men in England, and I never thought there would come this disgrace to my name—never—and I'm ashamed that it's Arthur Pendennis."

The old fellow's voice broke into a sob, the second time Arthur had drawn tears from those wrinkled lids. The sound of his broken voice instantly stilled Pen's fury. He ceased pacing the room. Laura stood by Helen's sofa, and Warrington, who had remained a silent observer, watched the family storm with intense interest. Darkness had fallen as the parties spoke. Following the passionate outburst of the Major, a deep, trembling voice cut through the twilight.

"Will you let me tell you something about myself, my kind friends?" the voice asked. "You have been so good to me, ma'am, so kind to me, Laura—I hope I may call you so sometimes—my dear Pen and I have been such friends that I have longed to tell you my story as it is, though I would have sooner if it were not so sad and contains another's secret. However, it may do good for Arthur to know it—it is right that everyone here should. It will divert you from thinking about a subject which, born of a fatal misconception, has caused so much pain to all. May I please tell you, Mrs. Pendennis?"

"Pray speak," Helen whispered, her mind already racing with a terrifying hope that Pen's words had ignited.

George filled a bumper of wine, drained it, and began. "You all know me as a man with no desire to advance in the world: careless of reputation, living in a garret from hand to mouth, though I have friends and a name, and surely the capabilities for greatness if I had the mind. But mind I have none. I shall likely die in that garret, alone. I nailed myself to that doom in early life. Shall I tell you what fascinated me about Arthur years ago, and inclined me toward him when I first saw him? The men from Oxford and Cambridge told me tales of that early affair with the Chatteris actress—a topic Pen has discussed with me since—which, but for the Major's generalship, might have made you his daughter-in-law, ma'am. I cannot see Pen in the dark, but he blushes, I'm sure; and I dare say Miss Bell does too; and my friend Major Pendennis, I dare say, laughs as he ought, for he won. What would Arthur's lot have been had he been tied at nineteen to an illiterate woman older than himself, with no qualities in common to make companions? No equality, no confidence, no love quickly? He would have been most miserable. And when he spoke just now of threatening a similar union, be assured it was but a threat occasioned by anger, which you must allow was very natural on his part. After a generous, manly, self-denying conduct—which is rare—he has met with unkind suspicion from friends and complained of unfair treatment toward an innocent person, toward whom he and you all stand deeply indebted."

The widow rose, but Warrington, seeing her attempt, asked, "Do I tire you, ma'am?"

"Oh no—go on, go on," Helen said, delighted, and he continued.

"I liked him because of that early history, which reached my ears in college gossip, and because I like a man, if you'll pardon me, Miss Laura, who shows he can have a great, unreasonable attachment for a woman. That is why we became friends—and remain friends here, aren't we?" he added, leaning closer to her in a lower tone. "And Pen has been a great comfort and companion to a lonely, unfortunate man."

"I am not complaining of my lot," George continued softly. "No man's lot is what he wishes it to be. Up in my garret, where you left the flowers, with my old books and pipe for a wife, I am content, occasionally envying men with brighter careers or the ability to solace ill fortune with the affection of a woman or a child." A sigh escaped from near Warrington, and a hand extended toward him, only to be instantly withdrawn by prudish modesty. Checking the motion, kindly friendship shrank back, ashamed, and Warrington resumed his tale.

"My fate is what I made, and not lucky for me or others involved. Before college, as a boy of eighteen, I was at a private tutor's. Like Arthur, I became attached, or fancied I was, to a woman of much lower degree and greater age. You shrink from me..."

"No, I don't," Laura said. Resolutely, she placed her hand in his. She had divined his story from earlier hints.
