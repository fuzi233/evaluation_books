To comprehend the architecture of my current captivity, one must first understand the absolute tyranny of the Outer Gardens, a microscopic empire of nobles who had conspired to strip the soul from Xodar. I arrived amidst a cacophony of reviling and cursing, a scene of organized brutality where men slapped the fallen Dator's face and women spat venom upon his robes. When I stepped forward, the mob shifted its gaze, their collective malice momentarily suspended by curiosity.

"So this is the creature who overcame the great Xodar bare-handed," one voice cried, dripping with theatrical cruelty. "Let us see how it was done."

Another, a woman of striking beauty whose laughter cut through the tension, proposed a spectacle of humiliation. "Let him bind Thurid," she suggested. "Thurid is a noble Dator. Let Thurid show the dog what it means to face a real man."

"Dozens of voices erupted in agreement, chanting 'Thurid! Thurid!' until the air vibrated with their demand. A figure emerged from the throng—a massive black warrior weighed down with resplendent ornaments, advancing with a bearing that betrayed neither shame nor fear. Yet, as he approached, his demeanor curdled into open hostility.

'What now?' he bellowed, his eyes narrowing to predatory slits. 'What would you of Thurid?'

The crowd's explanation was swift and vicious. Thurid turned his gaze upon Xodar, and the mask of camaraderie shattered. 'Calot!' he hissed, the name tasting like bile. 'Ever did I think you carried the heart of a sorak in your putrid breast. Often have you bested me in the secret councils of Issus, but now in the field of war, where men are truly gauged, your scabby heart hath revealed its sores to all the world.'

With a gesture of supreme disdain, he kicked out. 'Calot, I spurn you with my foot,' he declared, aiming a blow designed to break the spirit of a defeated man.

My blood boiled instantly. Minutes prior, I had watched the cowardly treatment accorded to this once-powerful comrade simply because he had fallen from favor. While I held no affection for Xodar, I possess an intolerance for injustice that transcends personal loyalty; I cannot stand the sight of persecution without seeing red through a haze of bloody mist, compelled to act on impulse rather than deliberation.

I stood close beside Xodar as Thurid swung his foot. The degraded Dator remained erect and motionless, a carved image prepared to absorb insults in stoic silence. But as Thurid's limb rose, mine moved in tandem. I delivered a painful blow to the shin bone, shattering the trajectory of the kick and saving Xodar from added ignominy.

Silence stretched taut, broken only by the ragged breathing of the crowd. Then, with a roar of rage, Thurid sprang for my throat—the same move Xodar had attempted on the cruiser's deck. The result was identical. I ducked beneath his outstretched arms, and as he lunged past, I planted a terrific right hook to the side of his jaw.

The giant spun like a top, his knees buckling beneath him until he crumpled to the ruby-dust pathway at my feet. The blacks gazed in astonishment, first at the prostrate form of the proud Dator, then at me with disbelief.

'You asked me to bind Thurid,' I cried, my voice ringing clear. 'Behold!'

I stooped beside the fallen warrior, tearing the ornate harness from his shoulders and binding his arms and legs securely. 'As you have done to Xodar, now do you likewise to Thurid. Take him before Issus, bound in his own harness, that she may see with her own eyes that there be one among you now who is greater than the First Born.'

'Who are you?' whispered the woman who had instigated the duel.

'I am a citizen of two worlds; Captain John Carter of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.' I addressed the crowd directly. 'Take this man to your goddess, as I have said, and tell her, too, that as I have done to Xodar and Thurid, so also can I do to the mightiest of her Dators. With naked hands, with long-sword or with short-sword, I challenge the flower of her fighting-men to combat.'

'Come,' said the officer guarding me, returning us to Shador. 'My orders are imperative; there is to be no delay. Xodar, come you also.'

There was little disrespect in his tone regarding Xodar, yet a palpable shift occurred. Having witnessed the ease with which I disposed of the powerful Thurid, the guard's contempt for the fallen Dator evaporated, replaced by a wary respect. More telling was his behavior toward me; during the return journey, he walked or stood always behind me, a drawn short-sword in his hand, treating me not as a slave, but as a force to be reckoned with.

The return to the Sea of Omean was uneventful. We descended the awful shaft in the same car that had brought us to the surface, entering the submarine for the long dive to the tunnel far beneath the upper world. Through the passage and up again to the pool, we were transported on a small cruiser to the distant Isle of Shador.

Here, we found a small stone prison guarded by half a dozen blacks. There was no ceremony wasted in completing our incarceration. One of the guards opened the door with a huge key; we walked in, the door slammed shut, and the lock grated with a finality that sent a terrible feeling of hopelessness sweeping over me. This sensation mirrored the dread I had felt in the Chamber of Mystery beneath the gardens of the Holy Therns.

Tars Tarkas had been with me before, but now I was utterly alone in terms of friendly companionship. I fell to wondering about the fate of the great Thark and his beautiful companion, Thuvia. Even if they had escaped to a friendly nation, what hope did I have of receiving their aid when none on all Barsoom dreamed of such a place as this? Nor would knowledge of my location help, for who could penetrate to this buried sea against the mighty navy of the First Born? My case seemed hopeless.

Yet, I resolved to make the best of it. Rising, I swept aside the brooding despair attempting to claim me. With the intention of exploring my confinement, I began to look around.

Xodar sat with his bowed head upon a low stone bench near the center of the room. He had not spoken since Issus had degraded him. The building was roofless, its walls rising thirty feet to the sky, punctuated halfway up by small, heavily barred windows. Partitions divided the space into several rooms; ours was empty save for two doors leading elsewhere. I entered one, finding it vacant, and continued through the chambers until, in the last one, I discovered a young red Martian boy sleeping upon the stone bench.

Evidently, he was the only other prisoner. Leaning over, I studied his face. There was something strangely familiar about him, yet I could not place him immediately. His features were remarkably regular, his limbs graceful and beautiful. He was very light in color for a red man, but otherwise a typical specimen of this handsome race.

I did not awaken him. Sleep in prison is a priceless boon, transforming men into raging brutes when stolen by a fellow captive. Returning to my own cell, I found Xodar still seated in the same posture.

'Man,' I cried, 'it will profit you nothing to mope thus. It were no disgrace to be bested by John Carter. You have seen that in the ease with which I accounted for Thurid. You knew it before when on the cruiser's deck you saw me slay three of your comrades.'

'I would that you had dispatched me at the same time,' he replied quietly.

'Come, come!' I insisted. 'There is hope yet. Neither of us is dead. We are great fighters. Why not win to freedom?'

He looked at me in amazement. 'You know not of what you speak,' he replied. 'Issus is omnipotent. Issus is omniscient. She hears now the words you speak. She knows the thoughts you think. It is sacrilege even to dream of breaking her commands.'

'Rot, Xodar,' I ejaculated impatiently.

He sprang to his feet in horror. 'The curse of Issus will fall upon you,' he cried. 'In another instant you will be smitten down, writhing to your death in horrible agony.'

'Do you believe that, Xodar?' I asked.

'Of course; who would dare doubt?'

'I doubt; yes, and further, I deny,' I said firmly. 'Why, Xodar, you tell me that she even knows my thoughts. The red men have all had that power for ages. And another wonderful power: they can shut their minds so that none may read their thoughts. I learned the first secret years ago; the other I never had to learn, since upon all Barsoom is none who can read what passes in the secret chambers of my brain.'
