The morning sun had barely warmed the dust of Shepherd's Inn when Colonel Altamont burst into Captain Strong's sitting room, a whirlwind of dyed whiskers and desperate energy. Inside, the Chevalier sat comfortably in his easy chair, a newspaper draped over one knee and a cigar glowing in the other hand. Strong was a man who made a home anywhere, and by the time Altamont arrived, he had already demolished a breakfast of fried eggs and broiled rashers prepared with military precision by Grady. He possessed a temperament as resilient as his digestion; whether facing battle or the Fleet, he slept soundly and woke with a hearty appetite, his rosy cheeks untouched by the cares of the night.

Altamont's first act was to bellow for a pint of pale ale. Grady poured it into a pewter flagon, which the Colonel then transferred directly to his lips. He drained the tankard, wiped his mouth with his dressing gown, and let out a long, satisfied breath.

"Nothing beats that beer when the coppers are hot," Altamont declared, his voice booming. "I've drunk a dozen pints of Bass at Calcutta, and—"

"And at Lucknow, I suppose?" Strong laughed, lighting his cigar. "I got the beer for you specifically; I knew you'd need it after last night."

The Colonel began to recount his adventures of the previous evening, beating his head with his large hand. "I'm a madman once the liquor hits me," he confessed. "I can't trust myself with a spirit bottle. I start drinking and can't stop until it's empty, and then Lord knows what I say. I dined quietly at home, Grady gave me just two tumblers, and I intended to stay sober at the Black and Red like a parson. But why did you leave that sample bottle of Hollands in the cupboard? Grady had to leave me the kettle boiling, and I couldn't resist it. I washed it all down, by Jove, and probably had more at that thieves' den afterward."

"You were there too?" Strong asked. "Before you reached Grosvenor Place? That was early for a clear-out."

"Early hours for a drunkard, eh? Yes, like a great fool, I went there. Found Blackland and young Moss dining with two or three other rogues. If we'd gone to Rouge et Noir, I'd have won. But we tried hazard on the dining table. I dropped all the money you gave me in the morning. That set me wild. I went off thinking to get more from Clavering, but then... I don't remember much until I woke up this morning to hear old Bows playing the piano at No. 4."

Strong mused as he lit his cigar. "I'd like to know how you always extract money from Clavering, Colonel."

Altamont burst into laughter. "Ha, ha! He owes it to me!"

"I don't see that as a reason for Frank to pay," Strong replied dryly. "He owes plenty to others."

"Well, he pays because he's fond of me," Altamont grinned with a sneer. "He loves me like a brother. You know he does, Captain. —No? Well, perhaps he doesn't. But if you ask no questions, I'll tell no lies. Put that in your pipe and smoke it, my boy."

"I'll give up that brandy bottle," Altamont continued after a pause. "I must, or it will ruin me."

"It makes you say queer things," Strong said, staring him hard in the face. "Remember what you said last night at Clavering's table."

"Say? What did I say?" Altamont asked hastily. "Did I split anything? Dammy, Strong, did I split anything?"

"Ask me no questions, and I'll tell you no lies," the Chevalier replied. Strong recalled Altamont's abrupt departure from the Baronet's dining room upon recognizing Major Pendennis, but he resolved to seek an explanation elsewhere. "No," he said gently, "you didn't 'split,' Colonel. It was merely a trap to see if you'd speak. But you were too far gone to say a word anyone could understand."

Relieved, Altamont heaved a great sigh. "Yes, I own to my faults," he continued. "There are three things I can't resist: a bottle of brandy, a box of dice, and a beautiful woman. No man of pluck or spirit has ever escaped them. From the age of fifteen, when I ran away to be a cabin-boy on an Indiaman, until now at fifty, women have been my ruin. One of them, with black eyes and jewels and ermine like a duchess, swept off the best part of a thousand pounds I had in Paris. I was cautious at first, living like a gentleman at Meurice's hotel, never playing except at public tables. Then I met a swell fellow named Bloundell-Bloundell, with white kid gloves and a tuft to his chin. He took me to Madame the Countess de Foljambe's soirées. Such a woman, Strong! Her eye, her hand at the piano! She'd sing until she warbled your soul out of your body. She invited me to her Tuesday parties, and I gave her dinners and opera boxes. But it wasn't the dinners that drained Clavering's money; it was the tables."

"One night at the Countess's," Altamont recounted, "we had supper with Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, the Honourable Deuceace, and the Marquis de la Tour de Force. Champagne flowed, then brandy. The Countess mixed punch for me, and we played cards until I didn't know what I was doing. I was taken to bed, woke with a roaring headache, and found the Honourable Deuceace waiting. 'How are you, Colonel?' he asked. 'How long did you stay after I left? The play got too high, and I lost enough to you for one night.'

"'To me?' I asked. 'How's that, my dear feller?' He told me he'd borrowed thirty louis at vingt-et-un and produced an I.O.U. I pulled out my card-case—the Countess had worked it for me—and there was the note. He paid me thirty louis in gold. I was a gentleman, so I asked if he wanted anything else. But Deuceace didn't drink in the morning and left.

"Shortly after, Bloundell-Bloundell and the Marquis arrived. 'Bong jour, Marky,' I said. 'Good morning—no headache?' They declared I showed no signs of intoxication, taking my liquor as grave as a judge.

"'So,' said the Marquis, 'Deuceace has been with you. Has he settled? Get it while you can; he's a slippery card.'

"'He has paid me,' I lied. 'But I knew no more than the dead he owed me.'

"Their smiles grew sharper. Bloundell said, 'Colonel, you're a queer feller. No man could suppose you'd tasted anything stronger than tea and yet forget things. Come, tell that to the marines.'

"'En effet,' the Marquis twiddled his mustachios. 'Affairs of honour are best settled at once. Perhaps it won't be inconvenient to arrange our little matters of last night.'

"'What little matters?' I asked. 'Do you owe me money, Marky?'

"'Bah!' he snapped. 'I have your note for three hundred and forty louis.' Bloundell added, 'And mine for two hundred and ten.'

"I sprang out of bed in a rage. 'Are you here to make a fool of me? I don't owe you two hundred or two thousand louis! I won't pay a farthing. I laugh at your notes and at you. I believe you're a couple of——'

"'A couple of what?' Bloundell asked. 'We are men of honour, Colonel Altamont, and not here to trifle. Pay us, or we expose you as a cheat and chastise you as one.'

"'Oui, parbleu,' the Marquis agreed. I could have thrown him out the window, but Bloundell was a large man, weighing three stones more than me and standing six inches taller.

"'Monsieur will pay, or give me the reason why,' Bloundell insisted. 'You're little better than a polisson.'

"I was in the thick of the row when a friend I'd met at Boulogne entered. He hadn't played and had warned me against Bloundell. He told me the story: the Countess had entreated me to stop, the gentlemen had offered to intervene, but I insisted on large stakes. He charged dead against me. 'I left the Countess crying,' he said. 'She hates these two men. She warned you repeatedly. Now she's in hysterics lest the Marquis puts a bullet through your head. I believe,' he added, 'that woman is distractedly in love with you.'

"'Do you think so?' I asked. My friend told me she had gone down on her knees to him, shouting, 'Save Colonel Altamont!'

"As soon as I was dressed, I called on the lovely woman. She shrieked and nearly fainted. She called me Ferdinand—I'm blest if she didn't. 'I thought your name was Jack,' Strong laughed later, causing Altamont to blush furiously behind his dyed whiskers.

'"A man may have more names than one,' Altamont retorted. 'When with a lady, I like a good one. She cried fit to break your heart. I told her I wouldn't touch a farthing of her jewellery.

'"Then they'll shoot you,' she said. 'They'll kill my Ferdinand.'

'"They'll kill my Jack wouldn't have sounded well in French,' Strong noted.

'"Never mind names,' Altamont said sulkily. 'A man of honour may take any name he chooses.'

"She said they would kill me. I told her, 'No, I won't let that scamp of a Marquis send me out of the world; if he lays a hand on me, I'll brain him, Marquis as he is.'

"At this, the Countess shrank back. 'Do I understand aright?' she asked. 'That a British officer refuses to meet any person who provokes him to the field of honour?'

'"Field of honour be hanged, Countess,' I said. 'You wouldn't have me be a target for that little scoundrel's pistol practice.'

'"Colonel Altamont,' she said, 'I thought you were a man of honour. Good-bye, sir.' She swept out, her voice choking in her handkerchief.

'"Countess!' I rushed after her, seizing her hand.

'"Leave me, Monsieur le Colonel,' she shook me off. 'My father was a General of the Grand Army. A soldier should know how to pay all his debts of honour.'

"What could I do? Everyone was against me. Caroline said I'd lost the money, though I remembered nothing. I had taken Deuceace's money too, but he'd offered it. Every one of these chaps was a man of fashion and honour; the Marky and the Countess were of the first families in France. Rather than offend her, I paid up five hundred and sixty gold Napoleons, plus three hundred I lost trying to get my revenge. I can't tell you now if I was done or not. Sometimes I think I was, but then Caroline was so fond of me. That woman would never have seen me done."

Their conversation was interrupted by a knock at the door. Grady admitted no less a person than Sir Francis Clavering.

"The Governor, by Jove!" Strong exclaimed, surprised.

"What's brought you here?" Altamont growled, looking sternly at the Baronet. "It's no good, I warrant."

Whenever Clavering visited Shepherd's Inn, it was for money. He was a man who never faced his debts squarely; as long as he could renew a bill, his mind was easy. He was made to be ruined, a victim of sharper wits, niggardly and reckless, a dupe because he was too mean to be a successful knave. He had told more lies and endured more baseness to stave off small debts than a braver rogue would need to make a fortune. He was abject and a shuffler even in prosperity. Strong knew him well; they were not friends, but employer and factotum.

Clavering's pale face expressed extreme anger. "So, sir," he addressed Altamont, "you've been at your old tricks."

"Which of 'em?" Altamont sneered.

"You were at the Rouge et Noir last night!" Clavering cried.

"How do you know? Were you there?" Altamont asked. "I was at the Club, but I didn't play on the colours—ask the Captain. I played with the bones. It was hazard, on my word and honour."

"What the deuce do I care how you lose your money, whether at hazard or roulette?" Clavering screamed, his voice rising. "What I will not have is that you should use my name or couple it with yours! Damn him, Strong, why don't you keep him in better order? He's drawn a bill upon me and lost the money on the table. I can't stand it! Flesh and blood won't bear it! Do you know how much I've paid for you?"

"This was only a very little one, Sir Francis—only fifteen pounds, Captain Strong. They wouldn't stand another. It oughtn't to anger you, Governor. Why, it's so trifling I didn't even mention it to Strong. All on account of that confounded liquor I took."

"Liquor or no liquor, sir, it is no business of mine. I don't care what you drink, only it shan't be in my house.
