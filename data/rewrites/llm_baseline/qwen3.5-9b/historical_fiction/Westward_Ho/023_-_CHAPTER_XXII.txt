My narrative turns now to a chapter of profound sorrow. I shall keep it brief, yet it was imperative to record these events so that future generations may understand the grim adversaries the English faced in those austere times.

Three weeks have elapsed, and the setting shifts to a dim, subterranean corridor within the city of Cartagena. Here lies a long row of stone cells. Before one such cell stands an open door, flanked by two cloaked figures. One is Eustace Leigh; the other, a confidant of the Holy Office, holds a lantern whose feeble glow illuminates a straw mattress and the slumbering form of a man. We recognize the high forehead, the pale, refined features—this is Frank.

Rescued barely alive from the brutal savagery of African captors, he has now been surrendered to the subtler cruelties of civilized Christians. He endured the rack earlier today, and now Eustace, his traitorous kinsman, has arrived. Is he here to persuade, or to trap? Even Eustace struggles to distinguish between the two. Yet, deep down, he would trade his own life to save his cousin. Has he truly ceased to value his life? Perhaps. He acts solely out of a twisted sense of duty. Now, he must fulfill that duty again: rouse the sleeper, reason with him, cajole, and threaten him into renouncing his faith while "his heart remains tender from the torture," as his masters instruct.

Yet, look at him sleep! How peacefully. Is it merely the play of the lamplight, or does a faint smile grace his lips? Eustace lifts the lamp closer, bending over the prone figure. As he leans in, he catches a whisper escaping Frank's dreaming lips—a call for his mother, and a name far holier than mortal tongue. Eustace finds no strength within him to wake the boy.

"Let him rest," Eustace whispers to his companion. "In truth, I fear my words will avail naught."

"I suspect so, sir," replies the agent. "Never have I witnessed such stubborn heresy. He dared mock our Holinesses openly."

"Ah!" exclaims Eustace. "Great indeed is the depravity of the human heart, and the power of Satan! Let us depart for the time being."

"Where is she?" asks the agent. "The elder sorceress, or the younger?"

"The younger—the—"

"Señora de Soto? Alas, poor soul! One might pity her, were she not a heretic." The man regards Eustace sharply, then adds softly, "She is currently with the notary, for the greater glory of her soul, I trust."

Eustace halts abruptly, seized by a shudder. He gathers his composure with difficulty, managing only a choked "Amen!"

"Inside there," the agent gestures casually toward a nearby door as they descend the corridor. "We may eavesdrop a moment, if you wish; but do not betray me, *señor*."

Eustace knows the man watches closely for any sign of remorse, ready to report the slightest flicker of sympathy to his superiors. Yet, a terrible curiosity overrides his terror. He lingers near the ominous threshold. His face burns with fever, his knees knock together, his ears ring, and his heart pounds against his ribs until he feels it might burst forth. Pressing himself against the wall, he hides his convulsed countenance from his companion.

From within, a man's voice is clearly audible—low, yet distinct. The notary repeats the age-old accusation of witchcraft, a charge the Inquisitors frequently levy against their victims, either to soothe their own consciences or to paint their atrocities in a lighter hue before the mob. Then, Eustace's heart sinks as he hears a woman's voice answer, sharpened by indignation and agony.

"Witchcraft against Don Guzmán? What necessity exists for such a claim, Oh God! What need?"

"You deny it, then, *señora*? We regret your plight; however—"

A confused, choking murmur rises from the victim, mixed with words that hold meaning or none.

"She has confessed!" Eustace whispers, tears streaming down his face. "Saints preserve us! She—"

A wail erupts, piercing through Eustace's ears, his mind, and his very soul! He would tear at the door to break free, but his companion drags him backward. Wail after wail follows, as the wretched man hurries away, pressing his hands to his ears in futile attempts to silence the shrieks that pursue him like avenging angels through the dreadful vaults.

He escapes into the fragrant open air, bathed in the golden moonlight of the tropics, beneath a garden that might serve as a model for Eden itself. Yet, man's hell has invaded God's heaven, and those agonizing cries seem to echo endlessly in his mind.

"Oh, misery, misery, misery!" he mutters through gritted teeth. "And I have brought her to this! I was forced to lead her there! What other choice existed? Who dares condemn me? And yet, what diabolical sin have I committed that demands such punishment? Was there no one else available? None? And yet, it may save her soul. It may drive her to repentance!"

"It may, indeed; for she is delicate and cannot withstand much. You should know as well as I, *señor*, the merciful nature of the Holy Office."

"I know it, I know it," interrupts poor Eustace, trembling violently now for his own sake. "All in love—all in love. A paternal chastisement..."

"And the evidence of heresy is manifest, alongside the strong suspicion of enchantment and the notorious reputation of the elder sorceress. Recall, *señor*, that you yourself testified she was a famous witch in England before the *señora* brought her here as her attendant."

"Naturally she was; naturally. Yes; there was no other path. And though my flesh may be weak, sir, none can prove better to the Holy Office how willing my spirit has been!"

Thus, Eustace departs. Before another sun sets, he seeks out the principal of the Jesuits. Pouring out his entire heart—or as much of it as a broken man dares confess—he begs permission to complete his novitiate and join the order, on the condition that he be immediately dispatched back to Europe or elsewhere. "Otherwise," he states frankly, "I shall go mad, even if I am not already mad."

The Jesuit, a kind-hearted man, approaches the Holy Office and arranges everything with the Inquisitors. To clear Eustace of all suspicion, he recounts the young man's past valiant service to the Church. His testimony becomes unnecessary. That very night, Eustace leaves Cartagena for Nombre, sailing the following week to some unknown destination.

I say, I know not whither. Eustace Leigh disappears from these pages forever. He may have risen to become General of his Order. He may have spent his final years in some tropical forest, "conquering souls"—along with their bodies—that of the indigenous peoples. Or perhaps he returned to England, becoming the very Ballard who was hanged and quartered three years later for his role in the Babington conspiracy. I do not know.

This book chronicles the lives of men—their virtues and sins, their triumphs and failures. But Eustace is no longer a man. He has become an instrument, a tool of the Jesuits. He moves only where commanded, performing good or evil indiscriminately based on orders. Through an act of moral suicide, he has sacrificed his soul in a desperate attempt to save it; stripped of will, conscience, and personal responsibility—to God or man—but bound solely to "The Society."

In short, as Eustace himself admits, he is "dead." Twice dead, I fear. Let the dead bury their dead. We have no further concern with Eustace Leigh.
