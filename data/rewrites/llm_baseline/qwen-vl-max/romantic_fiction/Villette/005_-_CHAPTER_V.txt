With my mistress gone and no one left to guide me, I found myself once more alone in the world, adrift without a purpose or place. It was a time when my nerves, already strained by grief and loss, felt the weight of uncertainty pressing down upon me. I confess I did not look well—thin, pale, with eyes that seemed sunken from too many sleepless nights. I carried the weary air of someone who had watched through long vigils, or labored beyond their strength, or perhaps simply wandered too far from home. But though I looked worn, I was not destitute. Miss Marchmont had not lived long enough to fulfill her promise of kindness on that final night, yet after her passing, her second cousin—the heir—paid my wages promptly. He was a man of sharp features and narrow brow, his face etched with the lines of thrift, and later I learned he was indeed a miser, a stark contrast to the generous woman whose memory still warmed the hearts of those she had helped. So I held fifteen pounds in hand, a body still capable of work, and a spirit, though shaken, not broken. Compared to others, I might have been said to stand in a fortunate position. Yet fortune brought no comfort; instead, it only deepened the ache of uncertainty.

It was a day marked by silence and decision. The next week would see me leave this quiet house, but I had no new shelter waiting. In desperation, I turned to the only person I could trust—a former nurse of my childhood, now the housekeeper at a grand estate nearby. I spent hours with her, speaking softly, listening closely. She offered comfort, but no clear path forward. As twilight fell, I left her, walking two miles back under a sky bright with frost and stars. The night was cold and still, the fields silent, untouched by village lights or human voices. No moon rose to guide me—only the dim trail ahead, lit by scattered stars. And then, above the northern horizon, something stirred: the Aurora Borealis, a shimmering veil of light dancing across the heavens like a ghostly banner. It filled the sky with color and motion, strange and beautiful, as if nature herself were whispering secrets.

I did not fear it. Instead, it stirred something within me—something bold, unyielding. The crisp wind carried strength into my lungs, and a thought came, clear and strong: *Leave this place. Go out into the world.*

*Where?* I asked myself.

The answer came swiftly. From this quiet corner of England, I could see, in my mind’s eye, what I had never seen with my own: London. Not just a city, but a possibility—an escape, a future.

The next morning, I returned to the hall and spoke again to Mrs. Barrett. I told her of my plan. Though she was cautious, even skeptical, she did not call me mad. She was a woman of sense, and I had always carried myself with a calmness that masked my inner turmoil. That reserve had served me before—once allowing me to act boldly without drawing suspicion, to achieve things others might have dismissed as dreams.

As she slowly voiced her concerns, preparing orange peel for marmalade, a child burst into the room—laughing, joyful. It was Charles Leigh, son of a girl I had known in school. Once, she had been dull and plain, in a lower class than mine. Now, married and mother of two, she was transformed—kind, radiant, gentle in her bearing. I recognized her not at all. Nor did she recognize me. I did not try to remind her. What difference did it make?

But when she addressed her nurse in French—poorly spoken, with an accent so thick it echoed our school days—I noticed the woman was foreign. The boy chattered beside her in the same tongue. Later, Mrs. Barrett told me the nurse had come from abroad with her young mistress two years prior, after a trip to the Continent. She was treated almost like a governess, walked with the baby, taught the child French, and lived comfortably—just as many Englishwomen did in foreign homes. “And,” Mrs. Barrett added, “there are plenty of them.”

I filed that thought away, like a scrap of cloth saved for a future seam. Then, before I left, she gave me the address of a respectable old inn in the City—one her husband had once visited. My uncles had stayed there too, she said.

So I went to London. It was not such a daring leap as it may sound. Fifty miles separated us—no great distance, especially in those days. My funds would carry me there, sustain me for a few days, and bring me back if I found nothing worth staying for. I did not think of it as a gamble, nor as a flight into danger. More like a short holiday—rest for overworked thoughts, a breath of fresh air in a life that had grown too tight.

Fifty miles, yes—though now, looking back, I can hardly believe how much the world has changed. My hair, once dark and resilient against time, is now white, covered by a cap as pale as snow beneath snow.

I arrived in London late, on a wet February night. The rain fell hard, the streets were black and slick, and the air thick with unfamiliar sounds. I know my reader does not care for poetic descriptions of first impressions, and truly, I had none to give. There was no time for wonder, no mood for awe. I stepped off the coach into a city that felt like Babylon—vast, chaotic, indifferent. The voices around me—cabmen, porters, strangers—spoke English, yet chopped it up in ways I’d never heard. It sounded alien, clipped, harsh. Still, I managed to understand enough to get my trunk and myself to the inn.

How strange it all felt! First time in London. First time in an inn. Tired, chilled, lost. No experience, no advice, no map—but still, I had to act. I trusted common sense. But common sense, too, was frozen, uncertain. Only necessity drove it forward. It paid the porter—though I was badly cheated, and I did not scold it too harshly. It asked for a room. It called for the chambermaid. And when she appeared—proud, smart, her waist tightly bound, her cap neat, her speech laced with a sneering elegance—I did not flinch. I kept my composure. I remembered she was only a servant, though she acted like a queen.

She looked me up and down, as if I were a country fool. Her tone rebuked me. Her clothes mocked mine. But I said nothing. I waited. And slowly, she softened. Perhaps she thought I was a maid at first. Then she saw my manner—quiet, steady—and hesitated between condescension and respect.

I ate a little, warmed myself by the fire, and finally entered my room. Only then, as I sat on the bed, leaning my head and arms on the pillow, did the full weight of my situation crash over me.

What was I doing here? Alone in London? With no friends, no prospects, no certainty of tomorrow? Where had I come from? Where was I going? What would I do?

Tears welled up, hot and fast. I wept silently, drenching the pillow, my hands, my hair. A long, dark silence followed—full of sorrow and fear. Yet even in that storm, I did not regret my choice. A quiet conviction rose above the doubt: it was better to move forward than to stay behind. A way would open—narrow, perhaps, difficult—but it would open. That belief calmed me. It let me breathe. I whispered my prayers, blew out the candle, and lay down.

Then, just as I closed my eyes, a deep, resonant sound rolled through the night—low, powerful, echoing. Twelve times it rang. On the twelfth toll, I knew it: the bell of St. Paul’s Cathedral.

“I lie in the shadow of St. Paul’s,” I whispered.

And in that moment, I felt less alone.
