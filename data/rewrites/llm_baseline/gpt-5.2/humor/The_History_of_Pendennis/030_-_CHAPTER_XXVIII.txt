Civil war, as our ancestors modestly called it, was raging in the corner of the ballroom—high words, hotter faces, and a dense press of shoulders and satin sleeves jammed about an open window as if the night-air were the prize in a raffle. Outside, the street below contributed its own music to the entertainment: a chorus of nasal encouragement—“Pitch into him!” “Where’s the police?”—rising between the fiddlers’ last stifled squeak and the incessant stamping of impatient shoes.

On one side of the ring Madame Fribsby blazed—fan fluttering, eyes shining, bosom heaving with that peculiar compassion which never fails to find a public quarrel irresistibly touching. In the middle, conspicuous as a caper in a blanc-mange, stood Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant—Sir Francis Clavering’s little culinary celebrity—pale, curled, glaring, vibrating with wrath like a kettle beginning to sing. Opposite him, with gentlemen and ladies clinging about him as if he were a mast in a storm, stood Arthur Pendennis, looking as if he could swallow a man whole and complain that the bones were sharp.

Through the crush shouldered the Chevalier Strong, large, noisy, and cheerful, as if he had entered a pantry rather than a battlefield. Madame Fribsby, delighted to see him—delighted, indeed, to see anybody in a uniform of authority—caught at his arm and cried in frantic pathos, “Save him! save him!”

Him, it turned out, was the cook.

For shortly after Strong had quitted the room, and while Mr. Pen—still bleeding inwardly from his waltzing catastrophe, still smarting at Miss Amory’s coolness, and therefore doing what irritated men do when they wish to appear philosophical—had marched to the open window to take the sea-breeze and practice looking indifferent, he had been engaged in the stern domestic task of composing himself.

The sea, sparkling far off, murmured with that calm, old, tireless sound which has never yet been bribed by fashion, or drowned by a county ball; it was saying, in its ancient language, “Hush,” while inside, violins and vanity said, “Look at me.” Pen, for a moment, half owned to himself that he had been absurd—peevish—childish; which confession, like many a good resolution made near salt-water, lasted exactly until a human hand laid itself upon his shoulder.

He wheeled round—and there, to his utter surprise and horror, was Monsieur Mirobolant’s pale face with its fierce eyes, its ringlets, its lips compressed as if he were tasting vinegar. To be tapped on the shoulder by a French cook was, to the blood of Pendennis, like being served soup in a boot. Astonishment and indignation boiled together.

“You speak French?” Mirobolant said, in French, with the clipped certainty of a man presenting a dish he knows to be good.

“What is that to you, pray?” Pen answered, in English, as if the language itself were a rampart.

“At any rate, you understand it?” the other persisted, bowing—oh, with politeness!—as men bow before they strike.

“Yes, sir,” said Pen, stamping; “I understand it pretty well.”

“Vous me comprendrez alors, Monsieur Pendennis,” replied the little Gascon, rolling out his r as if it were a peppercorn to be cracked between the teeth, “quand je vous dis que vous êtes un lâche. Monsieur Pendennis—un lâche, entendez-vous?”

“What?” said Pen, turning in a flash.

“You understand the meaning of the word and its consequences among men of honour?” asked Mirobolant, hand to hip, eyes fastened on Pen with the rigid attention of a man checking whether a sauce has taken.

“The consequences are, that I will fling you out of window, you impudent scoundrel!” bawled Pen; and he darted.

He might very likely have performed the whole operation—window being close, Frenchman being small, and Pendennis being young and vexed—had not Captain Broadfoot and another heavy officer thrust themselves between, like two beefy joints interposed between knife and appetite; had not ladies screamed; had not fiddles stopped mid-scrape; had not the crowd surged up; had not Laura’s frightened face appeared over shoulders; and had not Strong, at that blessed second, come in from the refreshment-room to find Alcide grinding his teeth and spitting oaths in his “Galleon French,” and Pen wearing that wicked calm which, in certain men, is only anger with gloves on.

“What has happened?” Strong asked the chef, in Spanish—choosing a third language, as if to cool the quarrel by changing the air.

“I am Chevalier de Juillet,” cried Mirobolant, slapping his breast, “and he has insulted me.”

“What has he said to you?” Strong inquired.

“Il m’a appelé—Cuisinier,” hissed the little man, as if the word were a cinder.

Strong could hardly help laughing. “Come away with me, poor Chevalier,” he said. “We must not quarrel before ladies. Come away; I will carry your message to Mr. Pendennis.” Then, bending, he murmured to one or two, “The poor fellow is not right in his head.”

Meanwhile others clustered round Pen, and Laura—anxious, imploring—asked what had happened.

Pen himself scarcely knew, the manner in which rage makes a man stupid. “The man was going to give his arm to a young lady, on which I said that he was a cook, and he called me a coward and challenged me to fight. I own I was so surprised and indignant, that if you gentlemen had not stopped me, I should have thrown him out of window.”

“D— him,” said the gentlemen; “serve him right—the impudent foreign scoundrel.”

“I—I’m very sorry if I hurt his feelings, though,” Pen added; and Laura’s face brightened, grateful even for that grain of justice, while some of the young bucks muttered that foreigners wanted thrashing, like under-done meat.

“You will go and shake hands with him before you go to sleep—won’t you, Pen?” Laura said, close to him now. “Foreigners may be more susceptible than we are, and have different manners. If you hurt a poor man’s feelings, I am sure you would be the first to ask his pardon. Wouldn’t you, dear Pen?”

She looked all gentleness and pardon; and Pen—who could be melted by goodness as readily as he could be inflamed by a slight—took both her hands and looked into her kind face and said indeed he would.

“How fond that girl is of me!” he thought. “Shall I speak to her now? No—not now. This absurd business with the Frenchman must be ended first.”

She asked him to stop and dance with her. She was as eager to keep him in the room as he to escape it. “Won’t you stop and waltz with me, Pen? I’m not afraid to waltz with you.”

It was affectionate, and it was unlucky. In an instant Pen saw again his own legs betraying him, Miss Roundle and a dragoon forming a trap, Blanche shot against the wall as if launched by a culinary catapult, and the whole room laughing—Laura and Pynsent amongst them.

“I shall never dance again,” he replied, with a dark face of martyrdom. “Never. I’m surprised you should ask me.”

“Is it because you can’t get Blanche for a partner?” asked Laura, with that little wickedness that sometimes peeps out even in kind souls as a kitten peeps out from a basket.

“Because I don’t wish to make a fool of myself for other people to laugh at me,” Pen answered. “For you to laugh at me, Laura. I saw you and Pynsent. By Jove! no man shall laugh at me.”

“Pen, Pen, don’t be so wicked!” cried the poor girl, hurt at the savage vanity and morbid twist of his mood. He was glaring towards Mr. Pynsent as if he would gladly have had another duel, and another window.

“Who thinks the worse of you for stumbling in a waltz? If Laura does, we don’t. Why are you so sensitive, and ready to think evil?”

At that unlucky moment Mr. Pynsent came up, smooth as a well-buttered roll, and said, “I have it in command from Lady Rockminster to ask whether I may take you in to supper?”

“I—I was going in with my cousin,” Laura said.

“O—pray, no!” said Pen. “You are in such good hands, that I can’t do better than leave you; and I’m going home.”

“Good-night, Mr. Pendennis,” said Pynsent, drily—with that dryness which, translated into plain English, signifies, “Go to the deuce, you jealous boy; I could box your ears with pleasure.” Mr. Pendennis vouchsafed only a bow; and, in spite of Laura’s imploring looks, left the room.

Outside the atmosphere changed at once—music muffled, laughter dimmed, the sound of the sea coming in again like an old tune remembered. Within, Pynsent, offering his arm, remarked, “How beautifully calm and bright the night outside is! and what a murmur the sea is making! It would be pleasanter to be walking on the beach than in this hot room.”

“Very,” said Laura, whose heart was not on beaches or ballrooms.

“What a strange congregation of people,” Pynsent continued. “I have had to go up and perform the agreeable to most of them—the attorney’s daughters—the apothecary’s wife—I scarcely know whom. There was a man in the refreshment-room who insisted upon treating me to champagne—a seafaring-looking man, extraordinarily dressed, and seeming half tipsy. As a public man one is bound to conciliate all these people, but it is a hard task—especially when one would so very much like to be elsewhere”—and he blushed as he spoke, as if he could hear his own voice in the heated air and was ashamed of its vanity.

“I beg your pardon,” said Laura. “I—I was not listening. Indeed—I was frightened about that quarrel between my cousin and that—that—French person.”

“Your cousin has been rather unlucky to-night,” Pynsent said. “There are three or four persons whom he has not succeeded in pleasing—Captain Broadwood; what is his name—the officer—and the young lady in red with whom he danced—and Miss Blanche—and the poor chef—and I don’t think he seemed to be particularly pleased with me.”

“Didn’t he leave me in charge to you?” Laura asked, looking up at him and dropping her eyes instantly, like a guilty little coquette who has told a truth with a twist.

“Indeed, I can forgive him a good deal for that,” Pynsent cried eagerly; and she took his arm, and he led his little prize towards supper.

Supper was served, as the county paper afterwards observed, “in Rincer’s well-known style”—meaning that everything was abundant, varnished with sauces, and arranged to flatter eyes that were already half-tired of looking at each other. There were jellies that trembled at every stomp of a boot; chickens trussed as compactly as secrets; lobsters flaming red as if embarrassed by their own publicity; and wines popping and fizzing with an ambition to drown conversation. The room rang with that particular racket of plates, laughter, and officious waiters which makes a table feel less like a family and more like a factory of chewing.

Laura, however, had no appetite for triumphs of cream and truffle; she tasted little, heard less, and felt only the ache of Pen’s temper. Captious and quarrelsome; jealous and selfish; fickle and violent and unjust when anger led him astray—how could her mother, by a thousand hints, ask her to give her heart to such a man? And if she did—would it make him happy?

At the end of half an hour—a long half-hour, measured not by the clock but by the pounding of her own thoughts—a waiter brought her a small note in pencil. It was from Pen.

“I met Cooky below ready to fight me; and I asked his pardon. I’m glad I did it. I wanted to speak to you to-night, but will keep what I had to say till you come home. God bless you. Dance away all night with Pynsent, and be very happy.—PEN.”

She could almost have cried with gratitude over those scratchy lines. There was forgiveness in him still, and a sort of goodness, stubborn but real—like honest bread under burnt crust.

Pen, after leaving the ballroom, had gone downstairs with his heart reproaching him. Laura’s gentle looks followed him like a quiet melody that won’t be silenced by stamping. He longed to turn back and ask pardon—but he remembered he had left her with that confounded Pynsent. He could not apologize before him; he would compromise by making peace with the Frenchman.

The Chevalier Strong found him in the hall, where Mirobolant paced like a small general awaiting cannon. Strong’s own face was lit with mischief.

“I have got him in the coffee-room,” he said, “with a brace of pistols and a candle. Or would you like swords on the beach? Mirobolant is a dead hand with the foils, and killed four gardes-du-corps with his own point in the barricades of July.”

“Confound it,” Pen burst out, “I can’t fight a cook!”

“He is a Chevalier of July,” Strong returned gravely. “They present arms to him in his own country.”

“And do you ask me, Captain Strong, to go out with a servant?” Pen cried. “I’ll call a policeman on him—but—but—”

“You’ll invite me to hair triggers?” Strong laughed. “Thank you for nothing; I was but joking. I came to settle quarrels, not to fight them. I have been soothing down Mirobolant; I have told him you did not apply the word ‘Cook’ to him in an offensive sense: that it was contrary to all the customs of the country that a hired officer of a household—as I called it—should give his arm to the daughter of the house.” And then Strong, lowering his voice, told Pen the grand secret Madame Fribsby had confided: the violent passion under which the poor artist laboured.

When Arthur heard it, he broke into a hearty laugh, and Strong joined him; and Pen’s rage vanished as swiftly as steam when a lid is lifted. For he had himself been jealous all evening, and hungry for a pretext to insult Pynsent; he remembered old jealousies in older follies, and felt suddenly generous to any man possessed by so ridiculous and human a fever.

So he went into the coffee-room where Mirobolant waited with pistols and pride, and offered his hand outright. He made a speech in French—very solemn, very gentlemanlike—declaring himself “sincèrement fâché d’avoir usé une expression qui avoit pu blesser Monsieur Mirobolant, et qu’il donnoit sa parole comme un gentilhomme qu’il ne l’avoit jamais, jamais—intende,” said Pen, aiming at a French word for “intended,” and privately delighted with his own fluency, as if he had whipped up an omelette without spilling a drop.

“Bravo, bravo!” cried Strong, as pleased as he was amused.

And the Chevalier Mirobolant, of course, withdrew his pistols, withdrew his insult, withdrew his chin.

“Monsieur Pendennis has disproved my words himself,” Alcide said with profound politeness. “He has shown that he is a galant homme.”

Thus hands were shaken, dignity restored, and peace made; Arthur first despatching his pencilled note to Laura, before he and Strong committed themselves to the Butcher Boy.

As they drove along, wheels humming on the road with that steady rural sound no ballroom can imitate, Strong complimented Pen on his conduct and his French. “You’re a good fellow, Pendennis, and you speak French like Chateaubriand, by Jove.”

“I’ve been accustomed to it from my youth upwards,” said Pen; and Strong, with the grace of a man trying not to be rude, held out for five minutes—then exploded into such laughter as might have been heard, had one been listening, above even the far murmur of the sea.

It was daybreak when they got to the Brawl, where they parted. The ball at Baymouth was over too. Madame Fribsby and Mirobolant were on their way home in the Clavering fly; Laura, with an easier heart, slept at Lady Rockminster’s. The Claverings rested at the inn; and shortly after the disturbance Blanche had emerged from the refreshment-room, pale as a lemon-ice, and had told her maid—having no other confidante—that she had met the most romantic adventure: a singular man who had known the author of her being—her persecuted, unhappy, heroic, murdered father; and she began a sonnet to his manes before sleeping, as other people take a posset.

So Pen returned to Fairoaks without having uttered the message he had been so anxious to deliver at Baymouth. He could wait till Laura’s return, which would be the next day. He was not seriously jealous of Pynsent’s progress; and, being young and accustomed to the homage of his small world, he felt pretty certain that, in this as in other arrangements, he had but to ask and have; that Laura, like his mother, could refuse him nothing.

When Helen’s anxious looks questioned him—what had happened, and whether her darling project was fulfilled—Pen told of the waltzing calamity in a gay tone, made light of it, and laughed. “There will be plenty of time for sentiment, dear mother, when Laura comes back,” he said; and he looked in the glass with a killing air. Helen put his hair off his forehead and kissed him, and thought, of course, that no woman could resist him, and was exceedingly happy that day.

When he was not by her sofa, Mr. Pen occupied himself with packing books and portmanteaus, burning and arranging papers, cleaning his gun and putting it into its case—in short, making dispositions for departure. For though he was ready to marry, he was also eager to go to London; at three-and-twenty he considered it high time to begin the serious business of life, and make a fortune as quickly as possible.

His plan was already a banquet in his imagination, every dish served in due order. “I shall take chambers,” he said, “and enter myself at an Inn of Court. With a couple of hundred pounds I shall carry through the first year very well; after that my pen will support me, as it is doing with several Oxbridge men in town. I have a tragedy, a comedy, and a novel, all nearly finished, and for which I can’t fail to get a price. So I shall live pretty well without drawing on my poor mother, until I have made my way at the bar. Then, some day, I will come back and make her dear soul happy by marrying Laura. She is as good and as sweet-tempered a girl as ever lived—besides being really very good-looking—and the engagement will serve to steady me,—won’t it, Ponto?” Thus, smoking his pipe and talking to his dog as he sauntered through the gardens and orchards of Fairoaks, he built castles in the air with the ease of a man spreading butter on warm bread. “Yes, she’ll steady me, won’t she? And you’ll miss me when I’ve gone, won’t you, old boy?” Ponto quivered his tail and thrust his brown nose into his master’s fist; and licked hand and shoe, as they all did in that house, while Mr. Pen received their homage as other folks receive flattery and call it affection.

Laura came home rather late on the second day; and Mr. Pynsent, as ill luck would have it, drove her from Clavering. She could not refuse his offer; but his very appearance brought a dark cloud upon Arthur Pendennis’s brow. Laura saw it and was pained; but Helen—eager widow, matchmaking dove—noticed nothing, and, anxious that the delicate question should be asked at once, would have gone to bed very soon, rising from the sofa where she generally lay and where Laura would sit and work or read beside her. But when Helen rose, Laura—blushing and alarmed—said she too was tired and wanted to go to bed; so the widow’s scheme was thwarted for that night at least, and Mr. Pen was left another day in suspense.

His dignity was offended at being kept in the ante-chamber when he wished for an audience. A sultan like he could not be expected to wait in the corridor. Still, he went to bed, slept comfortably upon his disappointment, and did not wake until early morning, when he opened his eyes and found his mother standing in his room.

“Dear Pen, rouse up,” she said. “Do not be lazy. It is the most beautiful morning in the world. I have not been able to sleep since daybreak; and Laura has been out for an hour. She is in the garden. Everybody ought to be in the garden on such a morning as this.”

Pen laughed at the transparent artifice. He saw what thoughts were uppermost in that simple heart. His laughter cheered her.

“Oh you profound dissembler,” he said, kissing her. “Oh you artful creature! Can nobody escape from your wicked tricks? and will you make your only son your victim?” Helen laughed too, blushed, fluttered—happy as only a tender, plotting mother can be when her dearest project seems within reach.

After knowing looks and hasty words she left him; and Pen rose, decorated his handsome person, shaved his ambrosial chin, and in half an hour issued forth into the garden in quest of Laura.

Yet his reflections while dressing were dismal. “I am going to tie myself for life,” he thought, “to please my mother. Laura is the best of women, and—and she has given me her money. I wish to Heaven I had not received it; I wish I had not this duty to perform just yet. But as both the women have set their hearts on the match, I suppose I must satisfy them—and now for it. A man may do worse than make happy two of the best creatures in the world.” So, now that he was come to the point, he felt grave rather than elated, and thought it a great sacrifice he was about to make—like a man who praises the virtue of fasting while staring at a full table.

It was Miss Laura’s custom, on her garden excursions, to wear a sort of uniform—homely, but not unbecoming. A large straw hat, ribbon streamer flying for show more than use, shaded her face. Over her gown she wore a blouse or pinafore fastened at the waist by a smart belt; and her hands, guarded from rose-thorns by a pair of gauntlets, gave her a resolute, almost martial air—an innocent gardener armed against beauty’s prickles.

She had, somehow, the very same smile with which she had laughed at him the night before; and the recollection of his public tumble offended Pen afresh. Still, when she saw him coming down the walk—gloomy, solemn, full of care—she met him with provoking good humour and held out a gauntleted hand, as if offering him a knightly challenge.

He condescended to shake it; but his face did not lose its tragic expression.

“Excuse my glove,” said Laura, laughing, and pressing his hand kindly through the leather. “We are not angry again, are we, Pen?”

“Why do you laugh at me?” he said. “You did the other night, and made a fool of me to the people at Baymouth.”

“My dear Arthur, I meant you no wrong,” Laura answered. “You and Miss Roundle looked so droll as you—as you met with your little accident, that I could not make a tragedy of it. Dear Pen, it wasn’t a serious fall. And besides, it was Miss Roundle who was most unfortunate.”

“Confound Miss Roundle!” bellowed out Pen.

“I’m sure she looked so,” said Laura, archly. “You were up in an instant; but that poor lady sitting on the ground in her red crape dress, and looking about her with that piteous face—can I ever forget her?” And Laura began to mimic Miss Roundle’s expression—but checked herself, repentant. “Well, we must not laugh at her; but I am sure we ought to laugh at you, Pen, if you were angry about such a trifle.”

“You should not laugh at me, Laura,” he said bitterly. “Not you, of all people.”

“And why not? Are you such a great man?” asked Laura.

“Ah no, Laura—I’m such a poor one,” Pen answered. “Haven’t you baited me enough already?”

“My dear Pen—and how?” she cried. “Indeed, indeed, I didn’t think to vex you by such a trifle. I thought such a clever man as you could bear a harmless little joke from his sister.” And she held out her hand again. “Dear Arthur, if I have hurt you, I beg your pardon.”

“It is your kindness that humiliates me more even than your laughter,” Pen said. “You are always my superior.”

“What! superior to the great Arthur Pendennis? How can it be possible?” said Miss Laura, who had a little wickedness as well as a great deal of kindness in her. “You can’t mean that any woman is your equal?”

“Those who confer benefits should not sneer,” said Pen. “I don’t like my benefactor to laugh at me, Laura; it makes the obligation very hard to bear. You scorn me because I have taken your money, and I am worthy to be scorned; but the blow is hard coming from you.”
