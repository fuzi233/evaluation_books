Jorian Ketel strode directly to Margaret’s chamber, and there, to his utter astonishment, he found the very man he had been sent to seize—pale, motionless, his head cradled in Margaret’s lap as she knelt over him, her own face frozen in silent horror. Her eyes, wide and glazed, saw nothing, heard nothing, cared for nothing but the lifeless face before her. Jorian stood rooted, the candle trembling in his grip.

“Where was he hiding all this time?” he muttered.

Margaret gave no answer, lost in her grief. Jorian moved to the empty chest, examining it, and slowly the truth dawned on him. The girl’s mute despair struck him deeply.

“A grim night’s work,” he said gruffly. “All for a few scraps of parchment! Better he had fled with us than come to this.” She remained deaf to his words. He hesitated, then reached for a small hand-mirror hanging nearby. Holding it to Gerard’s lips, he watched. A faint mist formed upon the glass.

*"There’s life in him yet,"* Jorian murmured.

At those words, Margaret awoke as if from a trance. She flung herself upon Jorian, arms clasping his neck with desperate gratitude.

“Bless you for those words!” she cried, her voice breaking between sobs and wild relief.

Jorian, unused to such fervor, cleared his throat. “Enough of that. Let’s get him warm.” He lifted Gerard gently onto the bed, then drew a flask from his belt. Splashing its contents—sharp, stinging Schiedam—twice across Gerard’s face, he waited. A faint sigh escaped the young man’s lips.

Margaret swayed, hands pressed to her mouth, torn between rushing to him and fearing she might harm him further.

“Leave him be,” Jorian advised. “He’ll come round. Youth’s on his side.”

As Gerard’s breathing steadied, Jorian turned to leave. Before he could reach the door, Margaret caught him, sinking to her knees, clutching his legs.

“You won’t betray him?” she pleaded. “You’ve saved his life—don’t cast him back into danger!”

Jorian shifted uncomfortably. “I’ve done you favors before. I’ll not speak a word to Dierich Brower. But those parchments—there’s a hundred crowns for them. My Joan and the little ones could use that gold.”

“They shall have it,” Margaret vowed.

“You know where they are?”

“Not here, but I swear by all that’s holy—come to me tomorrow, alone, and the reward is yours.”

Jorian grunted in satisfaction. “Then my lips are sealed. But mind you keep your word.” With that, he slipped away, leaving Margaret to kneel beside Gerard, her prayers trembling on her lips.

---

When questioned by Dierich Brower, Jorian shrugged. “The girl took fright, that’s all. Half in a fit. Her father’s seeing to her.”

Dierich frowned. “We ought to check—”

“And send her to her grave with more terror? Leave it be.” His casual tone disarmed suspicion, and soon the men retreated to the warmth of the *Three Kings*, grumbling about wasted effort.

“Told you we’d be too late,” Dierich muttered as they left.

---

That night, Gerard had skirted prison and death—not by cunning or wit, but by the flicker of mercy in a rough man’s heart, and the lure of gold. Such are the threads of fate: tangled, unforeseen.

For those who have never known extremes of joy or anguish, the depth of what followed might be impossible to grasp. To watch life and color return to a beloved face—to see the eyes brighten, the lips part in speech—this was Margaret’s rapture. And for Gerard, to wake from darkness into the arms of his love, her tears warm on his skin, her voice whispering devotion—no bliss could rival it.

They clung to one another, murmuring vows, as if the horrors of the night had been a fleeting nightmare. Here, in this quiet space, they were husband and wife in spirit, betrothed before God, shielded by sacred promises.

But exhaustion soon stole over Margaret. Her limbs grew weak, her hold on him slackening.

“Stay,” she whispered as Gerard stirred anxiously. “Only let me feel you near. While I touch you, I know you’re safe.”

So he cradled her instead, stroking her hair as she trembled against him. There was sweetness in her fragility, a trust that humbled him.

Tomorrow would bring parting. But tonight? Tonight, they had cheated death. And in that victory, even farewells seemed bearable.

Yet innocence, in its ignorance, walks ever on the edge of peril. And for these two, the greatest danger lay not in prison or grave—but in their own untested hearts.
