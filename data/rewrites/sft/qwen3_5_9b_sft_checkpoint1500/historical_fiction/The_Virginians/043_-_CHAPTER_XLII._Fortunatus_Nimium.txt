Despite Harry Warrington's stubborn resolve to honor the dismal bargain his cousin had wrung from him, let no benevolent soul judge him harshly for lacking genuine pleasure in the arrangement. He would have gladly escaped it were it possible. Likely, the beating inflicted upon poor Will served this exact purpose. Harry probably reasoned thus: "A boxing match between us is bound to ignite a family quarrel. In that storm, Maria will undoubtedly side with her brother. I shall offer no retraction or apology. Then Will may challenge me to decide which of us is the stronger man. Amidst the feud, the agreement will surely dissolve, freeing me at last."

Thus, honest Harry laid his trap and lit the fuse. Yet, when the smoke cleared, no lasting damage emerged save a swollen nose and a bruised eye for William Esmond, who limped for a week. He issued no challenge to his cousin, Harry Warrington; consequently, neither slew the other. Will fell, rose again, and the score stood settled. One wonders how many sensible men would act similarly if they could settle their accounts privately, unseen by the gossips who record every tally.

Maria, however, did not align herself with her family. Upon hearing of the disturbance, she—and my lord—declared openly for her cousin. Lord Castlewood asserted that Will had struck the first blow, provoked by the chaplain's display. It was noted that Will was not uncommonly found quarreling while intoxicated. Mr. Warrington, conversely, displayed a becoming spirit in resenting the insult. It was for Will, not Harry, to beg forgiveness.

Harry refused any apology unless his horse were returned and his wager honored. Though the chronicler cannot unearth the final resolution of this betting dispute from the available papers, it is certain that shortly thereafter the cousins met in the homes of mutual acquaintances, avoiding further physical altercation.

Maria's elder brother had initially been content to see his sister, who had remained unmarried for years, wed any suitable suitor, particularly a gentleman from Virginia. In his eyes, she would simply retire to his wigwam in the forest, where her robe, stained by the brambles and mud of her journey through life, might be quietly disposed of. Naturally, Harry would outlive his elderly bride and console himself, or fail to, as he saw fit.

However, following an interview with Aunt Bernstein upon arriving in London, the lordship altered his stance. He went so far as to attempt dissuading Maria from the match, expressing pity for the young man condemned to a life of misery due to a foolish promise made at twenty-one.

"Mi-ser-y!" cried Maria, bewildered. "Pity?" My lord at Castlewood had deemed it no pity at all. Maria understood the meaning of pity. Her brother had confided in Aunt Bernstein, who had offered funds to break off the union. She grasped the implication behind my lord's words, yet Mr. Warrington was a man of honor, and she trusted him implicitly.

With this decision, my lord departed for White's or whatever haunt he favored. It is probable Maria guessed too accurately the nature of his conversation with Madame Bernstein.

"And so," he mused inwardly, "the end of my virtue is likely that the Mohock falls prey to others, rendering my sparing efforts futile. *Quem deus vult*—what was that schoolmaster's adage? If I do not have him, someone else certainly will. My brother has taken a slice; my dear sister intends to swallow him body and bone. Here I have been at home, respecting his youth and innocence, declining to play beyond the value of a sixpence, acting as his guardian and mentor. Why, I am but a fool fattening a goose for others to devour! Few good actions have I performed in this life, and here is this one benefiting whom?—others. Talk of remorse! By all the fires and furies, the remorse I feel is for things I haven't done and might have! Why did I spare Lucretia? She hated me ever after, and her husband met his predestined fate. Why have I let this lad go? Those of March and the rest, who do not want him, may pluck him! I hold a bad reputation; I am the wicked lord women warn their sons against! Pardi, I am not a penny worse, merely vastly unluckier than my neighbors, and it is only my cursed weakness that has been my greatest enemy!"

Manifestly, in recording a speech a gentleman only thought, the chronicler exaggerates for the patient reader, who holds no obligation to accept such a draft on credulity. Yet, have not Livy, Thucydides, and scores of historians fabricated speeches for their heroes which the latter never uttered? Knowing my Lord Castlewood's character so intimately, how may we not declare what passes within his mind and transcribe his thoughts? A whole pack of wolves hunts this lamb, destined to feast upon him soon, while one hungry old hunter stands by, denied a single cutlet? Who has not admired that noble speech of my Lord Clive, when reproached for his dealings with jaghires, lakhs, gold mohurs, diamonds, and pearls? "Upon my life," said the hero of Plassy, "when I reflect on my opportunities, I am surprised I took so little!"

To recount disagreeable tales of a gentleman until compelled is painful to a sensitive mind. Thus, though I knew from the first page of this history the sort of person my Lord Castlewood was and how he was esteemed, I have withheld much unpleasantness, permitting only the candid reader to perceive that he is a nobleman unlikely to earn our affection. It is true that my Lord March and other gentlemen with whom he associated would have considered betting with Mr. Warrington for his last shilling and keeping the winnings no more scrupulously than picking the bones of a chicken. They would exploit the game, their superior skill, or private knowledge of the horses; insofar, they followed the practices of all gentlemen. But when they played, they played fair; and when they lost, they paid.

Madame Bernstein was reluctant to reveal everything she knew concerning her nephew's family, moved by my lord's forbearance toward Harry upon his first arrival in Europe and pleased with his compliance with her wishes regarding the match. However, in the conversation she shared with her nephew, Lord Castlewood, speaking of Maria's designs on Harry, he voiced his mind with his customary cynicism. He declared himself a fool for sparing a lad whom no sparing could ultimately save from ruin, pointing out Harry's undeniable extravagances, his spendthrift associates, his nights at faro and hazard, and his rides to Newmarket. He asked why he alone should refrain from the young fellow?

In vain did Madame Bernstein plead that Harry was poor. "Bah!" exclaimed Castlewood. "He is heir to a principality which ought to have been his, ours, and might have restored our ruined family." Indeed, Madame Bernstein believed Mr. Warrington's Virginian property far exceeded reality. Were there not money-lenders in town who would advance sums on post-obits? Castlewood knew this to his cost; he had applied during his father's lifetime, and the cursed crew had devoured two-thirds of his miserable income. Speaking with desperate candor and ill humor, he alarmed Madame Bernstein, who resolved to caution him at the first opportunity.

That evening she began to pen a letter to Mr. Warrington. Yet, throughout her life, she had been slow with her pen and disliked its use. "I never knew any good come of writing more than *bonjour* or business," she often remarked. "What is the use of writing ill when so many clever people can do it well? And even then, it were best left alone." Consequently, she dispatched one of her men to Mr. Harry's lodgings, instructing him to visit her the following day for tea, when she intended to warn him.

But the next morning, illness confined her to chambers, preventing Mr. Harry from seeing her. She remained shut in for a couple of days. The following day, a great engagement arose, and the next, Mr. Harry embarked on some expedition of his own. In the whirlwind of London life, how often does a man see his neighbor, a brother his sister, or a schoolfellow his old friend? Many days passed before Mr. Warrington and his aunt finally had that confidential conversation she desired.

She began by mildly scolding him for his extravagance and madcap frolics (though, in truth, she was charmed by both). He replied that young men must be young men, and that it was in dutifully attending to his aunt that he had made the acquaintance with whom he currently resided. With some prelude, she then began to warn him regarding his cousin, Lord Castlewood. To this, he burst into a bitter laugh, stating that the good-natured world had already told him enough about Lord Castlewood. "To say of a man of his lordship's rank, or of any gentleman, 'Don't play with him,' is more than I like to do," continued the lady. "But..."

"Oh, you may say on, aunt!" said Harry, with something akin to an imprecation on his lips.

"And have you played with your cousin already?" asked the young man's worldly old monitress.

"And lost and won, madam!" answered Harry, gallantly. "It doesn't become me to say which. If we have a bout with a neighbor in Virginia, over a bottle, a pack of cards, or a quarrel, we don't go home and tell our mothers.
