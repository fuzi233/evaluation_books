A sudden commotion erupted on the terrace of the manor house, drawing the attention of those gathered within. The disturbance was not caused by any external threat, but rather by a singular, overwhelming force that had seized hold of the household itself. As the various narratives unfolded, one truth became undeniable: the object at the center of this storm—the precious diamond known as the Moonstone—was no longer merely a piece of jewelry, but a catalyst for chaos. The air grew heavy with tension as secrets were laid bare and alliances fractured under the weight of greed and suspicion. It became clear that the stone possessed a power that transcended its physical form, influencing the thoughts and actions of all who sought to possess it. In the midst of the confusion, a sense of foreboding settled over the group, hinting at darker consequences yet to come. These lines, penned far from home amidst the heat and dust of India, are addressed to my kin across the sea. My sole purpose is to lay bare the motive behind my refusal to extend the right hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. For months, a heavy silence has fallen between us, a reserve that has been mistaken for malice by those who hold my regard in high esteem. I implore you, dear relations, to withhold judgment until you have read the following account. Let me swear, upon my honor, that what follows is not mere speculation, but the strict and literal truth.

The rift between my cousin and me did not spring from petty grievances, but from a great public calamity in which we were both bound: the storming of Seringapatam, under General Baird, on the fourth of May, 1799.

To make sense of what transpired, I must briefly step back in time, to the eve of the assault, and recount the wild tales circulating within our encampment regarding the treasures hidden within the Palace of Seringapatam.

Among these fables, none was more extravagant than the story of the Yellow Diamond, a legendary gem revered in the ancient chronicles of India. Tradition holds that this stone was once embedded in the brow of the four-armed Hindu deity who personifies the Moon. Its hue, combined with a superstitious belief that it somehow shared the life cycle of its divine wearer—growing brighter with the waxing moon and dimming with its waning—earned it the name it bears to this day: *The Moonstone*. A similar, though lesser, mythos once gripped ancient Greece and Rome, where a semi-transparent stone was thought to tremble under lunar influence, earning the same moniker among collectors.

The saga of the Yellow Diamond begins in the eleventh century. At that time, the Muslim conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni swept through India, seizing the holy city of Somnath and stripping its magnificent temple of its riches. Yet, amidst the looting, the moon-god alone remained untouched. Saved by three Brahmin priests, the deity, bearing the Yellow Diamond upon his brow, was secretly spirited away from the desecrated shrine and carried to Benares, the second holiest city.

There, in a newly consecrated hall inlaid with precious stones and roofed with pillars of gold, the moon-god was reinstated. But on the very night the shrine was finished, Vishnu, the Preserver, visited the three guardians in a dream. He breathed his divine essence upon the Diamond, causing the priests to kneel and veil their faces in terror. Their lord commanded that the Moonstone be watched eternally, day and night, by three priests in rotation, passing down through endless generations. He prophesied that any mortal bold enough to seize the sacred gem would invite ruin upon himself, his lineage, and his name. This warning was etched in golden letters above the shrine's gates.

Centuries bled into one. Generation succeeded generation, keeping vigil over their priceless charge. Until the dawn of the eighteenth century, when Aurangzeb, Emperor of the Moghuls, unleashed chaos upon the temples of Brahma. The shrine was profaned, idols shattered, and the Moonstone snatched by a high-ranking officer of the imperial army.

Though powerless to reclaim it by force, the three guardian priests trailed the stolen jewel in disguise. Generations passed; the original thief perished in misery, and the cursed stone drifted from one outlaw's hand to another, always carrying the weight of Vishnu's wrath. Still, the descendants of the guardians kept their watch, waiting for the day destiny would return their gem.

Time marched on through the late eighteenth century until the Diamond fell into the grasp of Tipu Sultan, ruler of Seringapatam. He ordered it affixed to the hilt of a dagger, placing it among his most prized armaments. Yet, even within the Sultan's own palace, the three guardians, disguised as trusted officers of his household, continued their silent vigil. They were strangers to the court, having won favor by outwardly adopting the Muslim faith, but beneath their uniforms lay the old watch.

Thus ran the tale told around our campfires. To most of us, it sounded like nonsense, a fairy tale unworthy of serious thought. My cousin, however, possessed a mind that hungered for the marvelous. On the night before the assault, he grew absurdly incensed at me and others for dismissing the story as fiction. A foolish squabble ensued, fueled by Herncastle's volatile temper. In a boastful fit, he declared that if the English forces conquered Seringapatam, he would wear the Diamond upon his finger. The remark drew a roar of laughter, and so, we believed, the matter ended.

Herncastle and I were assigned to separate parties tasked with preventing the plunder and chaos that inevitably follow a conquest. The camp followers had already committed horrific excesses, but worse still, soldiers had slipped through an unguarded gate into the palace treasury, loading themselves with gold and jewels. I saw plainly that Herncastle's fiery spirit had been pushed past the point of sanity by the sheer horror of the slaughter we had witnessed. He was, in my view, utterly unfit for the solemn duty entrusted to him.

Inside the treasury, there was indeed riot and confusion, yet no overt violence that I observed. The men shamed themselves with a grim, good-humored debauchery, trading rough jests and catchphrases. Then, unexpectedly, the story of the Diamond resurfaced, twisted into a malicious game. "Who hath the Moonstone?" became the rallying cry, driving the looters from one heap of loot to another whenever they paused. It was John Herncastle, clutching a torch in one hand and a dagger dripping with blood in the other. The dying Indian collapsed to his knees, pointing a trembling finger at the weapon in Herncastle's grip. Speaking in his native tongue, he gasped, "The Moonstone shall have its vengeance yet upon you and yours!" With that, he fell dead upon the floor.

I scrutinized him closely. The frenzied rage of the previous day had settled, leaving a cold calm in its wake. I resolved to grant him another chance.

"That is all you have to say?" I asked.

His answer was curt. "That is all."

I turned my back upon him. And ever since, we have not spoken a word.

To establish the person prior to the object, as courtesy dictates, Rosanna Spearman was the sole newcomer to our employ. Fewer opportunities could have been afforded a young girl. In return, she proved herself diligent, modest, and uncomplaining, despite frailty and recurring fainting spells. Yet, she struggled to forge friendships among the other maids, save for Penelope, who treated her with kindness, albeit without intimacy.

The nature of her offense remains unclear. Lacking beauty to incite envy—with the added disadvantage of asymmetrical shoulders—she primarily alienated the staff through her taciturn nature and solitary habits. While others gossiped, Rosanna read or labored. Her outings were invariably solitary; nine times out of ten, she departed quietly, avoiding company. She neither quarreled nor took umbrage, maintaining a rigid, civil distance. Furthermore, beneath her plain exterior lay a subtle, unplaceable quality reminiscent of a lady, whether in her voice or countenance. The other servants seized upon this trait immediately, unjustly accusing Rosanna of "giving herself airs."

Transitioning from Rosanna's peculiarities to the geography of the sands: Our residence stands high upon the Yorkshire coast, surrounded by exquisite walks in every direction save one, a desolate route leading through a gloomy fir plantation to a bleak, isolated bay. Here, the sandhills descend to the sea, terminating in two rocky spits, the North and the South, whose opposing juts eventually vanish into the water. Between them lies a treacherous expanse of quicksand, notorious for its seasonal instability, earning the local moniker "Shivering Sand." The tides cause the seabed to tremble visibly, creating a mesmerizing, eerie motion. A massive breakwater shields the inner bay from the ocean's fury. Winter and summer alike, the sea retreats silently, rolling back smoothly to cover the sand. It is a forbidding retreat; no boat dares venture inside, no children from the nearby village of Cobb's Hole play here, and even the avian inhabitants seem to avoid it.

That a young woman, with countless alternatives and companionship available, should favor this forsaken spot for solitude and reading during her allotted outdoor time passes comprehension. Nevertheless, it was indeed Rosanna Spearman's preferred haunt, barring occasional visits to Cobb's Hole to see her sole neighborly acquaintance. Sitting on a sandy slope requires considerable effort for one of my vintage. By the time I settled, Rosanna had independently dried her tears using a cheap cambric handkerchief. Here, I must pause the narrative for a brief moment to gather the facts. By cross-referencing my own memory with Penelope's journal, it becomes evident that the period following Mr. Franklin Blake's arrival until Miss Rachel's birthday offers little of consequence save for the passage of time. For those days, the house drifted in a quiet hum, bringing forth no events worthy of chronicling. Therefore, with your permission and aided by Penelope, I shall skip forward, noting only specific dates. I reserve the detailed, day-by-day account for the later era, when the Moonstone itself became the sole obsession of everyone within our walls.

Resuming our thread, we return to the incident involving the bottle of sweet-smelling ink discovered on the gravel walk.

On the twenty-sixth, I presented this piece of "jugglery" to Mr. Franklin, recounting what I had previously shared. His assessment was immediate and sharp: he concluded not only that the Indians were stalking the house seeking the Diamond but also that they possessed a delusion of their own magic. He referred to the practices of marking a boy's forehead and placing ink in his palm, believing these acts endowed them with visions beyond mortal sight. "In both East and West," Mr. Franklin observed, "there exist individuals who practice this sort of hocus-pocus, though usually without the ink. They call it 'clairvoyance,' implying a brightness of vision." He reasoned that the Indians, convinced we held the Diamond at Frizinghall Bank, had summoned their seer to guide them if they managed to breach our defenses.

"Do you anticipate further attempts?" I asked.

"It hinges entirely on the boy's capabilities," Mr. Franklin replied. "If he possesses genuine sight capable of piercing the iron safe at the bank, we shall be spared further disturbances for some time. If not, however, we shall likely intercept them in the shrubbery before many more nights have passed."

I awaited the latter scenario with confidence, yet strangely, it never materialized. Whether the jugglers had learned of Mr. Franklin's sighting at the bank and altered their strategy, or if the boy truly glimpsed the Diamond in its hiding place—a possibility I vehemently dispute—or if it was merely a stroke of luck, remains uncertain. This much is plain fact: not a single Indian approached the house during the weeks preceding Miss Rachel's birthday. The tricksters continued their trade in town, while Mr. Franklin and I remained vigilant, determined not to tip our hand regarding our suspicions prematurely. Thus concludes my report on the Indians for the present.

By the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised a new pastime to fill the lingering silence of their days. It is crucial to examine this occupation, for it bears significant weight on future events.

It is a peculiar trait of gentlefolk, especially those of intellectual inclination, to drift blindly into pursuits that are ultimately destructive. Nine times out of ten, driven by a desire to improve their minds, they resort to torturing or ruining something, firmly convinced they are cultivating their intellect while, in truth, they are merely creating a mess. I have witnessed ladies and gentlemen alike embarking on expeditions with empty pillboxes, returning with captured newts, beetles, spiders, and frogs, only to dissect them with pin-pricked cruelty or slice them into fragments without a pang of remorse. One sees young masters peering into spider guts with magnifying glasses, or young mistresses encountering frog-headed corpses descending the stairs. When questioned on this cruel filth, the excuse is invariably a passion for natural history. Sometimes, they spend hours dismantling beautiful flowers with pointed instruments, driven by a foolish curiosity to understand their composition, unaware that knowing how a flower is built does not enhance its beauty or scent.

These activities stem from a singular root: a vacant mind and idle hands. Just as children once dabbled in muddy pies, adults indulge in "nasty science," dissecting insects and ruining blooms because they lack anything else substantial to occupy their thoughts. The result is stained canvases, fetid jars of tadpoles, and grit dropped into victuals. While laborers may curse the grind of earning a living, compare that honest toil to the idleness that splits flowers and probes spider stomachs, and one should be grateful for a purpose that fills the brain and directs the hands.

Fortunately, Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel avoided torture. Their only sin was making a mess, specifically of the door paneling.

Mr. Franklin, a man of universal genius, dabbled in what he termed "decorative painting." He claimed to have invented a novel liquid to thin paints, a "vehicle" of unknown composition. Its function, however, was simple and olfactory: it stank. Eager to try the process, Miss Rachel received the materials from London. Mr. Franklin mixed them, creating an aroma so potent it caused the dogs to sneeze upon entering the room. Donning an apron and bib over her gown, Miss Rachel began decorating her boudoir, guided by Mr. Franklin. They started with the interior of the door. He scraped away the original varnish with pumice-stone to prepare the surface, and she covered it with patterns and devices—griffins, birds, flowers, and cupids—copied from designs by a famous Italian painter (whose name escapes me, though he is known for his Virgin Marys and a baker's sweetheart). The work was tedious and dirty, yet neither seemed to tire. When not riding, visiting, dining, or singing, they were united in their bee-like industry, spoiling the door. If a poet ever asked, "Does Satan find some mischief still for idle hands to do?" he could have looked upon this scene and confirmed it with absolute truth.

The next notable date was Sunday, the fourth of June.

That evening, the servants' hall buzzed with speculation on a domestic matter that, like the door decoration, foreshadowed future developments. Observing the evident affection between Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, and recognizing their compatibility, we naturally wondered if their union extended beyond art. Some predicted a wedding before summer's end. Others, including myself, conceded Miss Rachel might marry but doubted whether Mr. Franklin Blake was the intended groom, for reasons that would soon become clear.

Mr. Franklin's affections were undeniably directed toward her. The challenge lay in deciphering Miss Rachel's sentiments. Allow me to introduce her fully, for understanding her is key to solving this puzzle.

Her eighteenth birthday, approaching on the twenty-first, marked a milestone. If you favor dark-complexioned women of slender, perfect proportions, you will find Miss Rachel a vision. Her hair was the deepest black I have ever seen, matching eyes of similar intensity. Her nose was slightly small, but her mouth and chin, as Mr. Franklin noted, were "morsels for the gods." Her complexion radiated a warmth akin to the sun itself, yet maintained a flawless order. She carried her head with the upright spirit of a dart, possessing a clear, resonant voice and a smile that first lit her eyes before reaching her lips.

Yet, despite her charm, she harbored flaws. Possessing a host of attractions, she had one distinct defect: she was stubbornly independent. Unlike most girls of her age, she formed her own ideas and defied fashion if it conflicted with her views. This independence served her well in trifles but, in matters of gravity, it led her astray. She judged for herself, rarely sought advice, and seldom revealed her intentions beforehand. She confided in no one, not even her mother, separating herself completely in joy and sorrow. As my lady often remarked, "Rachel's best friend and her worst enemy are, one and the other, Rachel herself."

However, beneath this self-will lay an absolute integrity. I cannot recall a single instance where she broke her word or said "no" while meaning "yes." In childhood, she frequently took the blame for a playmate's fault, suffering punishment silently rather than confessing. Even when charged, she would shake her head defiantly and declare, "I won't tell you!" Only later, when punished for her refusal, would she admit regret, yet she never revealed the truth. She was devilishly self-willed, perhaps contradictory, but certainly the finest creature I have ever encountered.

This brings us to the question of her matrimonial prospects.

On June the twelfth, my mistress invited a gentleman from London to assist in celebrating Miss Rachel's birthday. I believed this suitor held her heart privately. Like Mr. Franklin, he was a cousin. His name was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Context requires a brief digression into family affairs. My mistress's second sister, having suffered a romantic disappointment, subsequently entered a "misalliance"—a marriage deemed unequal due to the husband's lower station. She wed plain Mr. Ablewhite, a wealthy banker at Frizinghall. While initially controversial, time and social progress vindicated the union. The Ablewhites were respectable, prosperous, and highly regarded, residing near Frizinghall. Though we shall encounter them sparingly, their eldest son, Mr. Godfrey, warrants attention for Miss Rachel's sake.

Despite Mr. Franklin's brilliance, his chances of surpassing Mr. Godfrey appeared slim. Firstly, in terms of stature, Mr. Godfrey was superior, standing over six feet tall with a handsome countenance and flowing flaxen hair. Secondly, his reputation as a barrister and a "ladies' man" was formidable. A public character and accomplished philanthropist, he presided over countless charitable committees, managing funds and soothing the tempers of ladies' councils. He was a tear-jerker at public meetings, beloved by the destitute and the benevolent alike.

The twenty-first of June, the eve of the anniversary, broke under skies heavy with clouds, yet by midday the weather had cleared with a sudden, brave determination. In the servants' hall, we commenced our annual rites of devotion to Miss Rachel. As per tradition, I, acting as the principal officiant, prepared to deliver my yearly address—a ritual as formalized as the Queen's opening of Parliament. Like Her Majesty, I adhered to the strategy of delivering the same sentiments annually; indeed, the anticipation for my speech was as fervent as if it were a novel revelation, even though, upon delivery, it inevitably proved to be the familiar cadence everyone expected. The household, much like the parliamentary constituency, was content to grumble slightly at the lack of innovation, only to hope earnestly for fresh surprises next year. An easy people to manage, both within the statehouse and the kitchen.

Following breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I held a private consultation regarding the Moonstone. The moment had arrived to transfer the gem from the bank at Frizinghall into Miss Rachel's direct custody. Whether Mr. Franklin was attempting renewed courtship of his cousin and met with rejection, or whether his chronic sleeplessness was exacerbating the internal contradictions of his nature, remained unclear. What was certain, however, was that he appeared ill-suited for the occasion, oscillating in twenty different states of mind within minutes. While he wavered, I remained steadfast on the unvarnished truth: no incident had occurred to warrant alarming the lady concerning the jewel, nor could any circumstance negate the legal imperative binding Mr. Franklin to place it in his cousin's hands. This was my position, and despite his frantic twisting and turning, he was ultimately compelled to adopt it. We agreed that after lunch, he would ride to Frizinghall to retrieve the Diamond, bringing Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies to accompany him home.

With this arrangement finalized, the young gentleman returned to Miss Rachel. The remainder of the morning and a portion of the afternoon were consumed by the endless labor of adorning the front door. Penelope assisted in mixing the paints as directed, while Miss Rachel paced the room, handkerchief pressed to her nose due to the fumes rising from Mr. Franklin's vehicle, vainly attempting to disperse the artists. It was not until three o'clock that they removed their smocks, liberating Penelope—who emerged considerably intoxicated by the vehicular emissions—and cleansing themselves of the artistic debris. Yet, they had achieved their objective; the door was decorated, and they boasted immensely of their handiwork. The griffins, cupids, and attendant figures were undeniably beautiful, albeit so numerous, so entangled in floral motifs, and so contorted in their actions that they lingered unpleasantly in the mind long after viewing them. I must note that Penelope concluded her morning's toil by vomiting in the back kitchen, a sacrifice I offer without malice, for art demands such tributes—even if the artist happens to be my own daughter.

Mr. Franklin seized a scrap of food from the luncheon table and departed for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though privately known to fetch the Moonstone. Meanwhile, I assumed my post at the sideboard, commanding the dining service. Having attended to the wines and reviewed the staff, I withdrew to compose myself. A sniff of lavender water and a perusal of a certain volume helped settle my body and mind. I was awakened from a reverie—not a nap, I suspect—by the clatter of horsehooves. Stepping to the door, I received a cavalcade consisting of Mr. Franklin and his three cousins, accompanied by one of Old Mr. Ablewhite's grooms.

Strangely, Mr. Godfrey appeared lacking in his customary spirits. Though he greeted me with polite warmth, noting my improved condition, a distinct cloud hung over him. When I inquired about his father's health, he offered a curt reply: "Much as usual." Conversely, the two Misses Ablewhite were buoyant beyond measure. Nearly as tall as their brother, they were rosy, yellow-haired lasses overflowing with robust vitality. Their arrival caused the horses' legs to tremble, and when they leapt from their saddles without assistance, they bounced upon the earth as if crafted of India rubber. Every utterance began with a resonant "O"; every action executed with a thunderous "bang." They giggled and screamed at the slightest provocation. These were "bouncers" indeed.

Taking advantage of the din, I approached Mr. Franklin in the hall. "Have you secured the Diamond safely, sir?" I asked. He nodded, tapping his breast pocket. "And have you sighted the Indians?" "Not a glimpse," he replied, then requested Miss Rachel. Learning she resided in the small drawing-room, he proceeded directly thither. Moments later, the bell tolled, summoning Penelope to inform Miss Rachel that Mr. Franklin Blake wished to speak with her.

Half an hour later, crossing the hall, I was abruptly halted by a chorus of screams issuing from the drawing-room. Recognizing the distinctive vocal signature of the Misses Ablewhite, I was not alarmed, yet I entered on the pretext of seeking instructions regarding the dinner to ascertain if any calamity had transpired.

Inside, the scene was arresting. Miss Rachel stood at the table, transfixed, holding the Colonel's unluckily inherited Diamond. Kneeling on either side of her were the two Bouncers, devouring the gem with their eyes and shrieking with ecstatic delight whenever it caught the light anew. Opposite them stood Mr. Godfrey, clapping his hands like a delighted child and murmuring, "Exquisite! Exquisite!" Beside a bookcase, Mr. Franklin sat, pulling nervously at his beard, his gaze fixed anxiously toward the window. There, framed by the glass, stood the object of his scrutiny: Miss Rachel herself, clutching the extract from the Colonel's Will, her back turned to the assembled company.

When I entered to inquire of my duties, she faced me. I observed a furrow of familial concern etching her brow and a flicker of ill-temper tightening the corners of her mouth. "Retire to my room in half an hour," she commanded. "Then I shall have something to say to you." Departing, it became evident that she was plagued by the same dilemma that had troubled Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sands. Did the legacy prove her brother's treatment of her unjust cruelty, or did it reveal him to be far worse than her darkest imaginings? These were weighty questions for her to resolve, especially with her innocent daughter holding the Colonel's birthday gift.

Before I could depart, Miss Rachel, ever considerate of the ancient servant who witnessed her birth, called me back. "Look, Gabriel!" she exclaimed, flashing the jewel before me in a shaft of sunlight piercing through the window. By Jove, it *was* a diamond! Of a size nearly equal to a plover's egg, it emitted a radiance akin to the harvest moon. Gazing downward into the stone, one seemed to peer into a yellow abyss that swallowed all vision save itself. It appeared unfathomable; this jewel, held between finger and thumb, seemed infinite as the heavens. We positioned it in the sun, then extinguished the lights, whereupon it glowed with a terrible, self-generated luminescence, casting a moony sheen in the darkness. No wonder Miss Rachel was entranced; no wonder her cousins screamed. The Diamond's grip upon me was so absolute that I erupted with a roar of "O" rivaling the Bouncers'. The sole individual retaining his faculties was Mr. Godfrey. Encompassing each sister with an arm, he glanced compassionately between the stone and me, remarking, "Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!"

Presumably, his aim was instruction, though his effect was merely a reminder of the impending dinner. I shuffled downstairs to brief my army of waiters. As I exited, Mr. Godfrey addressed me warmly, "Dear old Betteredge, I harbor the truest regard for you!" He embraced his sisters and gazed admiringly at Miss Rachel while paying me this token of affection. It seemed a reservoir of love to draw from. Compared to him, Mr. Franklin appeared a veritable savage.

Forty-five minutes elapsed before I presented myself in Miss Rachel's chamber, as directed. Our conversation largely mirrored the previous discussion at the Shivering Sands, with one crucial distinction: I exercised caution to withhold information regarding the juggling acts, deeming there no justification to alarm the lady. Upon receiving my dismissal, I noted her grim interpretation of the Colonel's motives and her firm intention to remove the Moonstone from her daughter's possession at the earliest opportunity.

Returning to my quarters, I encountered Mr. Franklin, who inquired if I had seen his cousin Rachel. I had not. Could I locate Cousin Godfrey? I admitted ignorance, though I suspected Godfrey was not distant from Rachel. Mr. Franklin's suspicions aligned with mine; he pulled vigorously at his beard and retreated to the library, slamming the door with a resonance that spoke volumes.

My preparations for the birthday dinner proceeded uninterrupted until the hour for dressing. As I adjusted my white waistcoat, Penelope appeared at my toilet, feigning the need to brush my remaining locks and improve my cravat tie. Her spirits were high, and she possessed news. Bestowing a kiss upon the crown of my bald pate, she whispered, "News for you, father! Miss Rachel has refused him."

"Refused whom?" I asked.

"The Ladies' Committee-Man, father," she declared.

The remainder of the household trailed behind the Ablewhites until the entire retinue was assembled. Counting the servants, the number totaled twenty-four. It was indeed a magnificent tableau when they were finally seated around the dinner table, and the Rector of Frizinghall, delivering grace with a voice of exquisite elocution, rose to begin the benediction.

There is no necessity to catalogue the guests for you. You are unlikely to encounter most of them again within the scope of my narrative, save for two exceptions. These two individuals occupied the seats flanking Miss Rachel, who, reigning as the sovereign of the evening, naturally drew every gaze. Indeed, she was the focal point of the gathering, though this fact caused a quiet irritation to my mistress. Her annoyance stemmed from the fact that she wore her magnificent birthday present, the Moonstone, which overshadowed all other adornments. Although the diamond arrived unmounted, the ubiquitous Mr. Franklin, utilizing his nimble fingers and a scrap of silver wire, had ingeniously fashioned it into a brooch pinned to the bodice of her white gown. Everyone, of course, marveled at the prodigious size and beauty of the gem. However, the only two guests who ventured remarks beyond the ordinary were those seated at her immediate right and left.

To her left sat Mr. Candy, our local physician. He was a pleasant, affable little man, albeit with a distinct flaw: an excessive propensity for jesting, both in season and out, coupled with a tendency to plunge abruptly into conversation with strangers without first gauging the waters. In social settings, he frequently erred, inadvertently setting people at odds. Yet, in his medical practice, he proved far more prudent, seemingly guided by an instinct for discretion that saved him from the errors plaguing more cautious colleagues. Regarding the diamond, his comment, delivered as per his custom, was intended as a mystification. He gravely implored Miss Rachel, citing the interests of science, to permit him to remove the stone home and incinerate it. "First, we shall heat it, Miss Rachel," the doctor declared, "to a specific temperature; then, exposing it to an airflow, we shall gradually, puff! evaporate the diamond, thereby sparing you the torment of guarding such a valuable treasure!" My lady, observing him with a somewhat distressed countenance, appeared to hope that the doctor was speaking seriously and that Miss Rachel possessed sufficient zeal for science to sacrifice her gift.

On the young lady's right sat an eminent public figure: the renowned Indian traveler, Mr. Murthwaite. He had previously risked his life to penetrate regions where no European foot had tread. He was tall, lean, wiry, and brown, possessing a weary demeanor yet a steadiest, most attentive eye. Rumors suggested he grew weary of the mundane life surrounding us and yearned to return to the wild tramps of the East. Beyond his singular observation regarding her jewel, I doubt if he uttered six words or consumed a drop of wine throughout the entire meal. The Moonstone was the sole object of his interest; the legend of it had evidently reached him during his perilous wanderings. After gazing at it silently for so long that Miss Rachel grew confused, he addressed her in his cool, immovable tone: "If you ever journey to India, Miss Verinder, do not carry your uncle's birthday gift. A Hindoo diamond may serve a religious function. I know a certain city and a temple therein where, dressed as you are, your life would not suffice for five minutes' purchase." Safe in England, Miss Rachel was delighted to learn of her hypothetical danger. The footmen, known as the Bouncers, were equally thrilled, dropping their cutlery with a clatter and exclaiming, "O! how fascinating!" My lady shifted uncomfortably in her seat and deflected the topic.

As the dinner progressed, I became increasingly aware that this celebration was failing to flourish as previous festivities had. Looking back from the vantage point of subsequent events, I am half convinced that the cursed diamond cast a blight over the entire assembly. I circulated wines generously, acting as a privileged figure to ensure the unpopular dishes were tried, whispering confidentially, "Please reconsider trying this; I assure you it will benefit you." Nine times out of ten, they relented, ostensibly out of respect for their old steward, yet to no avail. Gaps of silence widened as the meal advanced, rendering me personally uneasy. When speech resumed, it was employed innocently but with disastrous intent. Mr. Candy, the doctor, for instance, uttered unluckier words than I had ever witnessed before. Consider this example of his misfortune: One of our ladies, the worthy Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor, habitually discussed her deceased spouse without acknowledging his death to strangers. She presumably believed every able-bodied adult in England should know this fact. During a lull in conversation, someone broached the dry and disagreeable subject of human anatomy. Whereupon, true to form, Mrs. Threadgall introduced her late husband without mentioning his demise, describing anatomy as the Professor's favorite pastime. By ill fortune, Mr. Candy, sitting opposite and ignorant of the gentleman's status, overheard her. Being the epitome of politeness, he seized the opportunity to further the Professor's anatomical pursuits. "They have acquired remarkably fine skeletons recently at the College of Surgeons," announced Mr. Candy, loudly and cheerfully across the table. "I strongly recommend the Professor, madam, to visit them whenever he finds an hour." You might have heard a pin drop. The company, respecting the memory of the dead, sat in stunned silence. Standing behind Mrs. Threadgall, pouring her hock, I watched as she lowered her head and murmured, "My beloved husband is no more." Unluckily, Mr. Candy, deaf to the truth and miles removed from suspicion, continued louder and more politely. "The Professor may not realize," he stated, "that membership in the College admits him, on any day but Sunday, between ten and four." Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her cap, repeating the solemn words in a voice barely audible, "My beloved husband is no more." I blinked furiously at Mr. Candy across the table; Miss Rachel touched his arm; my lady stared at him with unutterable expressions. It was futile! Onward he went with a cordiality impossible to stop. "I shall be delighted," he proclaimed, "to send the Professor my card, if you will kindly inform me of his present address." "His present address, sir," cried Mrs. Threadgall, suddenly losing her temper with an emphasis that rang the glasses, "is the grave! The Professor has been dead ten years." "Oh, good heavens!" exclaimed Mr. Candy. Save for the Bouncers, who laughed aloud, a profound blank descended upon the company, as if everyone were departing in the wake of the Professor, hailed from the direction of the tomb.

Such was Mr. Candy. The others were nearly as irritating in their respective ways. When they should have spoken, they remained silent; when they did speak, they were perpetually at cross-purposes. Mr. Godfrey, though eloquent in public, declined to exert himself privately. Whether sullen or bashful following his discomfiture in the rose-garden, I could not determine. He reserved his discourse exclusively for the lady seated beside him, a committee-woman of spiritual disposition, distinguished by a prominent collarbone and a refined taste for dry champagne. Being stationed directly behind them at the sideboard, I testify that the company lost a significant amount of edifying conversation, which I intercepted while drawing corks and carving meat. Their discussion transcended charity work; they debated serious subjects. Religion, according to Mr. Godfrey, equated to love, and love equated to religion. Earth was heaven, slightly worn, and heaven was earth, refurbished to appear anew. Earth harbored objectionable elements, yet to compensate, all women in heaven belonged to a prodigious committee free from quarrels, attended by men serving as ministering angels. Beautiful! Beautiful! Yet, why did Mr. Godfrey hoard these sentiments to himself and his companion?

And what of Mr. Franklin? Surely, you would assert, Mr. Franklin endeavored to stir the company into a pleasant evening? Nothing of the sort! Having fully recovered his composure, likely informed by Penelope of Mr. Godfrey's reception in the garden, he displayed wonderful force and spirits. However, nine times out of ten, he selected the wrong subject or addressed the wrong person, offending some and puzzling all. His foreign training—that French, German, and Italian aspect of his nature I have noted—manifested in a bewildering fashion at my lady's hospitable table. Consider this: he discussed the extent to which a married woman might admire a man who was not her husband, articulating it in his clear-headed, witty French to the maiden aunt of the Vicar of Frizinghall. Then, shifting to German, he told the lord of the manor, while the latter quoted his expertise in breeding bulls, that such experience counted for nothing and that the proper method was to introspect, evolving the ideal bull from one's mind. What say you when our county member, heated by cheese and salad, lamented the spread of democracy in England, asking, "If we lose our ancient safeguards, Mr. Blake, what remains?" To which Mr. Franklin replied, from the Italian perspective: "Three things remain, sir—Love, Music, and Salad"? He not only terrified the company with such eruptions but, when the English side of him resurfaced, lost his foreign smoothness. Addressing the medical profession, he uttered direct ridicule of doctors,

As the final carriage wheels rattled away, dissolving the evening's festivity into silence, I retreated into the inner hall where Samuel was already stationed, managing the decanter of brandy and soda water. From the drawing-room emerged Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel, trailed by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a glass, while Mr. Franklin remained untouched by the spirit. He sank into a chair, utterly exhausted; the relentless chatter of the birthday celebration had clearly drained his reserves.

Lady Verinder, turning to bid them farewell, fixed her gaze intensely upon the wicked Colonel's legacy gleaming in her daughter's attire.

"Rachel," she asked sharply, "where will you place your Diamond tonight?"

Miss Rachel, buoyed by a manic giddiness typical of youth after an exhilarating day, embarked on a whimsical tour of logic. First, she claimed ignorance of the location. Next, she suggested her dressing-table, citing the convenience of proximity to her other treasures. Then, recalling the diamond's terrifying luminescence in the dark, she abandoned the idea. Finally, she settled on the Indian cabinet in her sitting-room, proposing a romantic reunion of two beautiful native productions. Just as her stream of nonsense reached its crescendo, her mother interjected.

"My dear!" Lady Verinder exclaimed, "your Indian cabinet has no lock!"

"Oh, heavens, Mamma!" cried Miss Rachel, her tone shifting instantly to feigned terror. "Is this an inn? Are there thieves lurking in the house?"

Dismissing her theatricality, Lady Verinder bid the gentlemen good night. Turning to her daughter, she offered a tender kiss and a proposition: "Why not let *me* keep the Diamond for you tonight?" Miss Rachel received this offer with the same disdain a child might show toward a suggestion to discard a beloved doll. Reasoning was futile. "Come into my room, Rachel, the first thing tomorrow morning," Lady Verinder instructed, leaving slowly as if weighed down by a heavy, unshared burden.

Next, Miss Rachel said her farewells. She shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, who stood gazing abstractedly at a portrait, before turning to Mr. Franklin, who sat weary and silent in the corner.

Though I cannot report their exact words, standing near the oak frame holding the large mirror, I witnessed a telling exchange. Miss Rachel slipped the locket Mr. Franklin had gifted her from beneath her gown, displaying it to him with a smile that betrayed a depth of feeling far beyond simple friendship, before retiring to bed. This glimpse shattered my confidence in my own judgment; perhaps Penelope was correct regarding the state of her young mistress's affections.

Immediately after Miss Rachel departed, Mr. Franklin's attention shifted to me. His mood, as variable as the weather, had already turned toward the Indian performers.

"Betteredge," he murmured, "I am half convinced I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously during our conversation in the shrubbery. Pray, does he intend to weave any of his traveler's tales around us? And do you truly intend to unleash the dogs?"

"I shall remove their collars, sir," I replied, "and grant them liberty to wander if they scent a reason."

"All right," Mr. Franklin agreed. "We shall decide tomorrow. I am disinclined to alarm my Aunt without compelling cause. Good night."

His pallor and fatigue were so pronounced as he rose to fetch his candle that I ventured to suggest a nightcap of brandy-and-water. Mr. Godfrey, approaching from the far end of the hall, supported my counsel, urging Mr. Franklin gently to accept a drink before sleeping.

These trifling details mattered greatly to me, for amidst the day's turmoil, it brought me comfort to observe that the two gentlemen remained on cordial terms. Their verbal sparring, which Penelope had overheard in the drawing-room, and their competition for Miss Rachel's favor, had bred no genuine animosity. Both were men of the world, generally good-humored. Indeed, there is a certain civility among the genteel classes that prevents the petty squabbles often found among the lower orders.

Mr. Franklin declined the drink initially but, on the stairs, seemingly swayed by persuasion or a change of whim, requested a bottle be sent up. I dispatched Samuel with the liquor and then released the dogs. Astonished by their sudden freedom in the deep night, they leaped upon me like playful pups, until the falling rain quickly dampened their ardor. They lapped a single drop of water each and retreated to their kennels. As I entered the house, the skies offered omens of clearing weather, though for the moment, the rain poured heavily, turning the grounds into a swamp.

Samuel and I conducted the nightly rounds, securing the house. I personally inspected every corner, trusting no one else, and found everything secure and fastened when I finally retired between midnight and one. The day's anxieties had overtaken me, and I suffered a bout of insomnia akin to Mr. Franklin's malady. It was sunrise before I succumbed to slumber. Throughout the long vigil, the house lay as silent as a tomb, disturbed only by the rhythmic splash of rain and the sighing of the wind among the trees as dawn broke.

About half-past seven, I awoke to a fine, sunlit day. The clock had struck eight, and I was stepping out to chain the dogs when a frantic skittering of petticoats echoed on the stairs behind me.

Turning, I found Penelope descending like a whirlwind. "Father!" she shrieked, "Come upstairs immediately! The Diamond is gone!"

"Are you out of your mind?" I demanded.

"Gone!" Penelope repeated, breathless. "Gone, nobody knows how! Come up and see."

She dragged me into the young lady's sitting-room, which opened onto her bedroom. There, standing at the threshold, was Miss Rachel, her complexion as惨白 as her white dressing-gown. Beside her stood the double doors of the Indian cabinet, swung wide open. Inside, one drawer had been drawn out to its limit.

"Look!" Penelope pointed. "I saw Miss Rachel place the Diamond in that drawer last night."

I approached the cabinet. The drawer was hollow.

"Is this true, miss?" I asked.

With a gaze alien to her usual demeanor and a voice devoid of its normal cadence, Miss Rachel echoed Penelope's words: "The Diamond is gone!"

As the pair departed, a strange, paralyzing restlessness seized me. I wandered aimlessly within the house, performing a dozen futile tasks, unable to focus on anything other than the mounting mystery.

Before long, Duffy returned, breathless, carrying a scrap of paper torn from the Sergeant's pocket-book. It bore a frantic scrawl: *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman's boots, and be quick about it.*

Though I knew a messenger would have delivered the boot faster, I hesitated. A stubborn, protective instinct resurfaced at the eleventh hour; I needed to verify the situation personally before entrusting evidence to the authorities. Clutching the heavy leather shoe, I raced toward the shore, pushing my failing limbs to their limit.

The sky had turned a bruised purple, and rain lashed down in driving sheets, whipped into white foam by the gale. Thunder rolled across the bay, shaking the ground beneath my feet. Near the dunes, I spotted Duffy huddled for shelter, but beyond him lay the terrifying scene: the churning grey sea, the crashing rollers, and a solitary, dark figure standing defiantly on the wet expanse of the beach.

It was Sergeant Cuff.

He raised a hand toward the north, shouting over the roar of the storm, "Stay on that side! Come down here!"

I stumbled forward, gasping for air, my heart hammering a frantic rhythm against my ribs. Words failed me; a hundred questions swirled in my mind, yet none escaped my lips. The Sergeant's expression chilled me. There was a look of sheer horror in his eyes, a mask of dread that froze the blood in my veins.

He snatched the boot from my hand and pressed it firmly into a footprint etched into the sand. That print ran south, leading straight toward the jagged, submerged rocks of the South Spit. The mud was fresh, unblurred by the deluge, and the fit was perfect.

Cuff pointed silently to the evidence, then began to trace the path with his gaze, following the trail down where the sand gave way to stone. The South Spit was already being battered by the rising tide, the water heaving over the hidden treacherous slopes of the Quicksand. With a grim, obsessive patience, the Sergeant tested the boot against the prints, confirming they led unequivocally *to* the rocks, yet offered no sign of any steps leading *away*.

Exhausted, he finally dropped the matter. Turning to me, his eyes locked onto mine, then drifted out to the heaving waters. I saw the terrible conclusion forming in his mind. A cold, numb tremor seized me, crawling from my spine to my toes. I sank to my knees on the hard earth.

"She has returned to her hiding spot," Cuff murmured, almost to himself. "A fatal accident has occurred on those rocks."

Instantly, memories flooded back. Rosanna's altered demeanor, the hollow, deadened tone of her voice when I found her sweeping the corridors mere hours ago. These warnings clashed violently with the Sergeant's grim assessment. I tried to stammer out the truth—that her death was a suicide, a choice she had made willingly—but the words choked in my throat. The paralysis held me fast. I could not feel the biting rain, nor see the encroaching tide. In a trance-like vision, I saw her again as I had on that morning weeks prior, telling me that the Shivering Sand seemed to call to her, wondering if her grave awaited her there. The horror of it struck me with crushing force, echoing through the tragedy of my own daughter, who was just Rosanna's age. A mother's grief, raw and ancient, tore through me.

Gently, the Sergeant lifted me from the ground and turned me away from the gruesome sight.

Relieved, I began to catch my breath, seeing the world as it truly was. From behind the dunes, the male servants and a fisherman named Yolland rushed down, asking if the girl had been found. Cuff briefly explained the evidence of the footprints, confirming a tragic loss. Then, he singled out Yolland.

"In such weather," Cuff asked, gesturing to the churning surf, "could a boat have reached those rocks?"

"No boat ever built," Yolland shouted, pointing to the massive walls of foam smashing against the headlands, "could have gotten through that!"

Cuff studied the fading tracks, now beginning to blur under the relentless rain. "There," he declared, "proves she could not leave by land. And here," he nodded at the fisherman, "proves she could not escape by sea." He paused, considering the facts. "She was seen running here an hour ago. By now, the tide should be high on the southern side of the spit, covering the rocks entirely."

"The tide hasn't risen enough yet to drown a kitten on that southern side," Yolland corrected, glancing at the exposed flats. "An hour ago, the Shivering Sand itself would have been barely covered."

Cuff turned north, toward the dangerous quicksand. "Then the accident must have happened on this side."

My tongue suddenly loosened. "No accident!" I cried, my voice cracking. "She came here weary of life, intending to end it."

The Sergeant recoiled slightly. "How do you know?" he asked sharply. The others crowded around, listening intently. Cuff regained his composure immediately, shielding me from the group's scrutiny. "Let him alone for a moment," he commanded gently, then turned back to Yolland. "Is there any chance of recovering her body when the tide goes out?"

"Not a chance," Yolland replied grimly. "What the Sand takes, it keeps forever."

Stepping closer, the fisherman addressed me directly. "Mr. Betteredge, I must speak plainly. Four yards out, along the base of the Spit, lies a shelf of rock, half-fathom deep beneath the sand. If she had slipped accidentally, she would have fallen onto that footing, perhaps waist-deep, but alive. To disappear, she must have waded out into the Deep beyond, or deliberately jumped. It was no accident, sir. The Deep has taken her, and she sought it herself."

Silence fell over the group. The Sergeant, the servants, and I all stood still, absorbing the weight of the confession. Together, we turned back up the slope.

Waiting for us at the dunes was the under-groom, running from the house with a small note clutched in his hand. His face bore a solemn sorrow. "Penelope sent this, Mr. Betteredge," he said softly. "She found it in Rosanna's room."

It was her final farewell to the kind old man who had striven so faithfully to protect her.

"You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in days past. When you next view the Shivering Sand, pray to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where it waited for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness."

Those few words shattered me. Young men may cry easily when starting their journey, and old men when ending it, but I, a broken soul, could not hold back the tears. They poured out freely.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, his expression softening with genuine compassion. "Do not touch me," I whispered, shrinking back in fear. "It is the dread of you that drove her to this."

"You are mistaken, Mr. Betteredge," he answered calmly. "But there will be time to discuss this when we are safely indoors."

Supported by the groom's arm, I followed the others back through the lashing rain, returning to the house to face the storm of emotions and the terrible truths that awaited us within.

The rumors preceding us had already set the household ablaze. Upon arriving, we found the servants in a frenzy of terror. As we approached my mistress's door, it flew wide open from within. She emerged, flanked by Mr. Franklin who struggled vainly to steady her, utterly consumed by the horror of what had transpired.

"You are responsible for this!" she shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the Sergeant. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money and let me never look upon him again!"

Of all the men standing there, the Sergeant alone possessed the composure to meet her fury. He was the only one fully in command of his faculties.

"I hold no greater accountability for this calamity, my lady, than you do," he replied, his voice respectful yet unyielding. "If, within half an hour, you still demand my departure, I will accept your dismissal rather than your payment."

His words carried a weight that cut through her hysteria. Even as she calmed sufficiently to allow Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside, the Sergeant's gaze swept across the remaining maids. While they trembled with fear, he noted that Penelope wept openly. "Once your father has changed his wet garments," he instructed gently, "come to me in his chamber."

Within the allotted hour, I had dressed in dry clothes and procured a spare outfit for the Sergeant. When Penelope entered, the sense of duty I felt toward her was overwhelming. I seated her on my lap, clasped her close, and offered a prayer for her soul. She buried her face in my chest, her arms tightening around my neck, and together we waited in a heavy, shared silence. I believed the tragedy stemmed from the deceased maid, whose spirit haunted both Penelope and me. The Sergeant moved to the window, gazing out into the night. Grateful for his consideration of our grief, I expressed my thanks quietly.

Noble folk possess the luxury of indulging their emotions; those of lower station do not. Necessity spares the former but shows no mercy to the latter, forcing us to bottle our feelings and press onward with our duties. This truth did not burden me; rather, it sharpened my resolve. Penelope and I stood ready when the Sergeant was prepared.

When questioned by the Sergeant regarding the motive behind her fellow servant's suicide, Penelope confessed, with the inevitability of fate, that it was her love for Mr. Franklin Blake. Pressed further, asking if she had confided in anyone else, she answered, "I kept it secret for Rosanna's sake." I felt compelled to interject, "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear. If Rosanna died because of him, it was neither known nor caused by his fault. If he leaves the house today, let him depart without the torment of knowing the truth." The Sergeant nodded in agreement, falling silent as he compared Penelope's account with some private calculation of his own.

Just as the half-hour elapsed, my mistress's bell rang.

Heading to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin exiting his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was ready to receive the Sergeant in my presence, though he wished to speak briefly with the officer first. On our return to the corridor, he paused to examine the railway timetable hanging in the hall.

"Are you truly departing, sir?" I asked. "Surely Miss Rachel will recover if you grant her time?"

"She will recover," Mr. Franklin answered, "only when she realizes I am gone and can never see me again."

I initially mistook his tone for resentment over how Miss Rachel had treated him, but the truth lay deeper. Since the police arrived, the mere mention of his name had ignited Miss Rachel's temper. Having loved his cousin too deeply to admit it to himself until the reality forced itself upon him, he had made his decision the moment she fled to her aunt's—a resolution born of a man's spirit: to leave.

Speaking in my presence, Mr. Franklin described his aunt's willingness to acknowledge her hasty words. He then asked if the Sergeant would, under these circumstances, accept his fee and withdraw from the investigation of the Diamond. The response was immediate and firm.

"No, sir," the Sergeant declared. "My fee is earned only by the performance of my duty. I cannot accept it until my duty is complete."

"I fail to follow you," Mr. Franklin said, confused.

"I shall clarify, sir," the Sergeant continued. "Upon arrival, I undertook to shed light on the disappearance of the Diamond. Now, I am prepared to fulfill that promise. Once I have laid out the facts to Lady Verinder as they stand, and advised her on the necessary steps to reclaim the Moonstone, the burden of this inquiry will fall from my shoulders. Only then, after she decides whether to permit me to proceed, shall I consider my work done and claim my reward."

In those words, the Sergeant reminded us that even within the Detective Police, a man's reputation was a fragile currency.

The logic of his stance was irrefutable, silencing all objections. Rising to escort him to my lady's room, I asked if Mr. Franklin desired to attend.

"Not unless Lady Verinder insists," Mr. Franklin replied. Leaning down to whisper to me as I followed the Sergeant out, he revealed a vulnerability that chilled me. "I know exactly what that man intends to say concerning Rachel. I love her too much to listen to his accusations and expect to maintain my temper. Please, leave me alone."

I departed, leaving him leaning against the window sill, a broken figure with his face buried in his hands, while Penelope hovered near the doorway, longing to offer solace. In his position, I might have summoned her, for there is often great comfort in having another human being validate one's suffering. Yet, perhaps, when I turned my back, he did call her in? If so, justice demands I record that Penelope would have spared no effort to comfort Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, the Sergeant and I proceeded to my lady's chamber.

During our previous consultations, she had rarely lifted her gaze from the book resting on her desk. This time, however, a transformation had occurred. She met the Sergeant's stare with an intensity equal to his own. A fierce family pride illuminated every line of her face, and I knew that when a woman of her steel faced the worst the Sergeant could conjure, he would find a formidable adversary indeed.

"Sir," Lady Verinder began, her voice steady despite the weight of the afternoon, "perhaps I should apologize for my tone a short while ago. I regret any offense I may have caused you, and I ask no excuses."

Sergeant Cuff, a man of blunt features and sharper instincts, accepted her apology with a bow. He rose to offer his own defense, framing it as an act of loyalty to his employer. He explained that his investigation had reached a critical juncture; any alarm to Rosanna Spearman would have ruined the delicate work he had performed. "I trust, Sir," he added, turning his gaze to me, "that you can confirm I adhered strictly to that rule." I assailed him readily, confirming that he had indeed spared Rosanna from knowing the full scope of his suspicions until the final moment.

The conversation, which might have concluded there, took a darker turn. The Sergeant, sensing the opportunity to force a confrontation, pressed forward. "Madam," he said, "a motive for the young woman's suicide has been suggested to me—one entirely separate from this theft. Yet, my professional judgment points elsewhere. I believe an unbearable anxiety regarding the missing Diamond drove her to despair. While I cannot define the nature of that anxiety, I am prepared to identify the individual capable of verifying my theory."

"Is that person present?" Lady Verinder asked.

"He has departed, Madam," Cuff replied.

The implication hung heavy in the air, pointing directly to Miss Rachel. A profound silence fell over the room, broken only by the howling wind and driving rain outside. Finally, Lady Verinder spoke, her composure unwavering. "Speak plainly, Sir. Are you referring to my daughter?"

"I am, Madam."

Lady Verinder reached for her chequebook, presumably to settle the Sergeant's fees, but paused, returning it to the drawer. Seeing the tremor in her hand—the hand that had generously supported her aging servant—sent a pang through my heart. "I had hoped," she continued, her voice slow and quiet, "to compensate you for your services and bid you farewell without the name of Miss Verinder being uttered. However, since you have named her, you have crossed a threshold from which you cannot return. I must insist that you remain here and finish what you started."

Cuff checked his watch. "If time had permitted, Madam, I would have committed these findings to paper rather than spoken them aloud. But urgency dictates brevity. It is a painful subject for both of us."

Lady Verinder interjected once more, seeking to mitigate the blow. "Perhaps I can ease this burden. You suspect Miss Verinder concealed the Diamond for personal gain. Is that accurate?"

"It is, Madam."

"And yet," Lady Verinder countered, "as her mother, I declare that she is absolutely incapable of such treachery. You know her only from the past few days; I have known her since birth. State your suspicions as fiercely as you wish; you cannot wound me. I am convinced, even with your vast experience, that the circumstances have fatally misled you. I possess no private secrets of my daughter's—I am as ignorant of her thoughts as you are. My sole basis for this assertion is simple familiarity. I know my child."

She extended her hand to me, a silent gesture of solidarity, before turning back to the Sergeant. "Proceed."

Cuff bowed, his expression softening momentarily with a hint of pity, though his conviction remained ironclad. "Allow me, Madam, to view this situation through the lens of my experience. For twenty years, I have acted as a confidential agent in matters of family scandal. From that long tenure, I have learned a singular lesson: young ladies of high standing occasionally incur private debts they dare not confess to their kin. Sometimes, the debtors are milliners or jewellers; sometimes, the funds are required for purposes I need not detail here. Let us apply this general principle to the specific facts before us."

He paused, considering his words with a grim deliberation. "My initial intelligence regarding the Moonstone's loss came from Superintendent Seegrave. He was, in my view, woefully incompetent. His only useful observation was that Miss Verinder refused to answer his questions, treating him with rude and inexplicable contempt. At first, I attributed this to the Superintendent's clumsiness. However, when I took command, the pattern became clear. I discovered a smear on the door, corroborated by Mr. Franklin Blake's testimony linking it to the theft. Until that point, I suspected a servant. Then, the scene shifted. Miss Verinder emerged from her chamber, visibly agitated forty-eight hours after the theft. She treated me with the same disdain as the Superintendent, and she was mortally offended by Mr. Franklin Blake."

"The facts align," Cuff argued, his voice rising slightly. "A young lady of impetuous temperament, suddenly bereft of a valuable jewel, displays an irrational hostility toward those attempting to assist her. This suggests a guilty conscience. Applying my twenty years of experience, I conclude that Miss Verinder possesses private debts. Therefore, the loss of the Diamond likely signifies its secret pledge to satisfy those obligations."

"What counter-evidence exists?" Lady Verinder asked calmly.

"Circumstantial misdirection," I murmured internally, thinking of *Robinson Crusoe* stranded on a desert isle, utterly alone with his thoughts. If the Sergeant had found himself isolated in such a void, he would have remained trapped by his own logic.

"But the evidence continues," Cuff insisted. "To test this hypothesis, I proposed searching the wardrobes for the garment that created the smear. All parties agreed except Miss Verinder, who flatly refused. Furthermore, when I warned her that leaving the house would hinder the recovery of the stone, she drove away immediately. Later, on the steps of her mother's home, she publicly insulted Mr. Franklin Blake, who had done more than anyone else to uncover the clue. If Miss Verinder is innocent, what explanation accounts for her flight, her refusal to cooperate, and her public vitriol?"

Cuff glanced at me, expecting support, but I remained silent, steeling myself against the weight of his logical trap. "Let us examine the case not only against Miss Verinder but against her and the deceased Rosanna Spearman jointly."

He returned to the timeline. Upon seeing Rosanna bring the laundry book, his doubts vanished. He suspected her instantly of complicity. "In a standard theft, I might have afforded Rosanna the benefit of the doubt, assuming she was a repentant soul. But this was not a common theft; it was a calculated fraud orchestrated by the Diamond's owner. Consequently, I needed an accomplice to maintain the illusion of a lost gem while concealing the truth. Rosanna was the ideal candidate. She possessed the necessary cunning, and, crucially, she had a connection to a money-lender in London willing to advance a substantial sum on the security of the Moonstone."

Cuff detailed Rosanna's actions, painting a damning portrait of her involvement. Even Lady Verinder appeared daunted by the accumulation of evidence, falling silent as the Sergeant laid out his plan.

"To conclude," Cuff announced, "I see two paths forward. The first is a certainty: I will station a reliable spy within the household to monitor Miss Verinder's movements, correspondence, and visitors, effectively circling the stone until it is recovered. Secondly, I propose a bolder experiment. I intend to reveal to Miss Verinder, without warning, that Rosanna Spearman has died. I hope that the shock will trigger her remorse and compel her to confess."

"My lady," Cuff observed, noting her reaction, "seems inclined to approve."

"She does not," Lady Verinder snapped, her anger flaring for the first time. "Both proposals are declined. Go on with your alternative method."

"The alternative," Cuff smiled, unfazed, "is to appeal to Miss Verinder's better nature. I propose telling her of Rosanna's death immediately. Perhaps her conscience will drive her to a confession."

"I agree to that trial," Lady Verinder declared, astonishing everyone with her sudden resolve. "However, I claim the maternal right to administer the test myself. You shall remain here, Sergeant, and I shall proceed to Frizinghall."

For the first time, the formidable Sergeant Cuff stood speechless, caught off guard by her determination. Lady Verinder rang for her carriage, ordering her waterproof gear. Though the rain poured down heavily, she refused my offer to accompany her with an umbrella. The pony-chaise arrived, and she stepped inside, taking the reins herself.

"You may rely on two things," she told the Sergeant as she prepared to depart. "First, I shall apply your suggested experiment to Miss Verinder with the utmost boldness. Second, you will receive the results of this inquiry, either in person or via letter, before the last train departs for London tonight."

With that, she drove off, leaving the house and the Sergeant behind to await the storm.

With my mistress departed, I found leisure to reflect upon the Sergeant. He sat ensconced in a cozy corner of the hall, his memorandum book open, his lips curled in a distinctively vicious sneer.

"Taking notes on the case?" I asked.

"No," the Sergeant replied, "I'm merely checking my schedule for the next professional engagement."

"Oh!" I exclaimed. "You deem it all resolved here, then?"

"I believe," Sergeant Cuff answered, "that Lady Verinder ranks among the sharpest women in England. I also hold that a rose holds far greater value than a diamond. Pray, where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?"

It became impossible to extract a single word regarding the Moonstone from him. His curiosity in the inquiry had evaporated completely; he persisted solely in seeking the gardener. An hour later, I overheard them engaged in a heated squabble within the conservatory, the dog-rose once again serving as the butt of their dispute.

Meanwhile, it fell to me to ascertain if Mr. Franklin adhered to his resolve to depart via the afternoon train. Having learned of the conference in my lady's chamber and its conclusion, he promptly altered his plans, deciding to await news from Frizinghall. This entirely natural modification of his itinerary—which would have amounted to nothing for ordinary folk—produced one regrettable consequence in Mr. Franklin's specific instance. It left him unsettled, burdened with a surplus of idle hours, and, consequently, allowed every foreign facet of his character to spill forth, one atop another, like rats tumbling from a sack.

Now as an Italian-Englishman, now as a German-Englishman, and now as a French-Englishman, he drifted in and out of every sitting room in the house, conversing on naught save Miss Rachel's treatment of him, and addressing none save me. I found him, for instance, in the library beneath the map of Modern Italy, utterly unaware of any remedy for his tributes other than the method of vocalizing them. "I harbor several worthy aspirations, Betteredge; yet what am I to do with them now? I am brimming with dormant virtues, were Rachel but willing to assist in their awakening!" He was so eloquent in painting a portrait of his neglected merits, and so pathetically lamenting their existence once depicted, that I stood at my wits' end for consolation, until it struck me that here lay a perfect case for the wholesome application of *Robinson Crusoe*.

I shuffled to my own chamber and returned bearing that immortal volume. The library was deserted; the map of Modern Italy gazed at *me*, and *I* stared back at it.

I checked the drawing-room. His handkerchief lay on the floor, proof that he had drifted in. The emptiness of the room proved he had drifted out again.

I inspected the dining-room and found Samuel with a biscuit and a glass of sherry, silently surveying the void. Just a moment prior, Mr. Franklin had rung furiously for a slight refreshment. Upon its hurried production by Samuel, Mr. Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had ceased its ringing from the violent pull he had applied.

I sought the morning-room and located him at last. There he sat by the window, tracing hieroglyphics with his finger in the condensation on the glass.

"Your sherry awaits you, sir," I said. It might as well have been addressed to one of the four walls; he was sunk in the bottomless depths of his own meditations, past all retrieval. "How do *you* explain Rachel's conduct, Betteredge?" was the sole response I received. Lacking the requisite reply, I produced *Robinson Crusoe*, wherein I was firmly convinced some explanation might be found, were we but diligent enough in our search. Mr. Franklin shut the book and floundered back into his German-English gibberish on the spot. "Why not examine it?" he said, as if I had personally objected to scrutiny. "Why, devil take me, lose your patience, Betteredge, when patience alone suffices to arrive at the truth? Do not interrupt me. Rachel's conduct is perfectly intelligible, if you will grant her the common justice of adopting the Objective view first, the Subjective view next, and the Objective-Subjective view to conclude. What do we know? We know that the loss of the Moonstone, on Thursday morning last, cast her into a state of nervous excitement from which she has yet to recover. Do you intend to deny the Objective view? Very well, then—do not interrupt. Now, being in a state of nervous excitement, how are we to expect her to comport herself as she might otherwise towards anyone around her? Reasoning thus, from within-outwards, what do we reach? We reach the Subjective view. I defy you to controvert it. Very well, then—what follows? Good Heavens! The Objective-Subjective explanation follows, of course! Rachel, properly speaking, is *not* Rachel, but Some Other Person. Do I mind being cruelly treated by Some Other Person? You are unreasonable enough, Betteredge; yet you can hardly accuse me of that. Then how does it end? It ends, despite your confounded English narrowness and prejudice, in my being perfectly happy and content. Where is the sherry?"

By this time, my head was in such a condition that I was scarcely certain whether it belonged to myself or to Mr. Franklin. In this deplorable state, I contrived to perform, what I take to have been, three Objective acts. I procured Mr. Franklin his sherry; I retired to my own room; and I solaced myself with the most composing pipe of tobacco I had ever smoked in my life.

Do not suppose, however, that I remained free of Mr. Franklin on such easy terms. Drifting again, out of the morning-room into the hall, he found his way to the adjacent offices, smelled my pipe, and was instantly reminded that he had been foolish enough to relinquish smoking for Miss Rachel's sake. In the twinkling of an eye, he burst in upon me with his cigar-case and delivered a powerful lecture on the one everlasting subject, in his neat, witty, unbelieving, French fashion. "Light me, Betteredge. Is it conceivable that a man can smoke as long as I have without discovering a complete system for the treatment of women hidden at the bottom of his cigar-case? Follow me carefully, and I shall prove it in two words. You select a cigar, you test it, and it disappoints you. What do you do then? You discard it and try another. Now observe the application! You select a woman, you test her, and she breaks your heart. Fool! Take a lesson from your cigar-case. Discard her, and try another!"

I shook my head. Wonderfully clever, I dare say, but my own experience stood in direct opposition. "During the time of the late Mrs. Betteredge," I said, "I frequently felt inclined to adopt your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. Yet the law insists that you smoke your cigar, sir, once you have selected it." I punctuated this observation with a wink. Mr. Franklin burst into laughter, and we were as merry as crickets until the next new facet of his character surfaced in due course. Thus things continued between my young master and me; and so, while the Sergeant and the gardener wrangled over the roses, we two spent the interval awaiting news from Frizinghall.

The pony-chaise returned a good half-hour before I dared expect it. My lady had decided to remain, for the present, at her sister's residence. The groom delivered two letters from his mistress: one addressed to Mr. Franklin, the other to me.

I dispatched Mr. Franklin's letter to him in the library—into which refuge his wanderings had now taken him for the second time. I read my own letter in my chamber. A cheque, which fell out as I opened it, informed me (before I had fully grasped the contents) that Sergeant Cuff's dismissal from the inquiry into the Moonstone was now a settled affair.

I sent to the conservatory to announce that I wished to speak to the Sergeant directly. He appeared, his mind preoccupied with the gardener and the dog-rose, declaring that an equal to Mr. Begbie for obstinacy had never existed, nor would ever exist again. I requested him to dismiss such wretched trifles from our discourse and to devote his best attention to a truly serious matter. Upon this, he exerted himself sufficiently to notice the letter in my hand. "Ah!" he said, in a weary tone, "you have heard from her ladyship. Do I have anything to do with it, Mr. Betteredge?"

"You shall judge for yourself, Sergeant." I then read him the letter (with my best emphasis and discretion), in the following words:

"MY DEAR GABRIEL,—I request that you inform Sergeant Cuff that I have fulfilled the promise I made to him, with this result concerning Rosanna Spearman. Miss Verinder solemnly declares that she has never spoken a word in private to Rosanna since that unfortunate woman first entered my house. They never met, even accidentally, on the night when the Diamond was lost; and no communication of any sort whatsoever took place between them, from the Thursday morning when the alarm was first raised in the house, to this present Saturday afternoon, when Miss Verinder left us. After informing my daughter suddenly, and in so many words, of Rosanna Spearman's suicide—thus is what has come of it."

I stood by the hall door, the pony-chaise waiting, wheels silent but ready, anticipating the possibility that Mr. Franklin might choose to depart by rail that very night. Yet, the clatter of heavy trunks descending the stairs, followed closely by Mr. Franklin himself, confirmed that he had, for once in his life, resolved upon staying.

"So you have truly made your decision, sir?" I asked, stepping forward to greet him. "Might you not wait a few days longer? Surely, Miss Rachel deserves another chance?"

The veneer of composure that Mr. Franklin often wore seemed to have stripped away completely now that the moment of farewell approached. Instead of a verbal response, he extended a letter. It bore the seal of Lady Verinder, addressed to him. While the majority of the correspondence reiterated sentiments I had already encountered in previous missives, the final paragraph concerned Miss Rachel. This addition alone illuminated the ironclad nature of Mr. Franklin's resolve.

"You will likely wonder," Lady Verinder wrote, "at why I have allowed my own daughter to keep me in absolute ignorance regarding this catastrophe. A diamond of twenty thousand pounds has vanished, and I am forced to deduce that Miss Rachel knows precisely what has occurred. Some invisible power, some entity utterly unknown to me, has imposed a vow of silence upon her, driven by motives I cannot fathom. Could I possibly permit myself to be toyed with in such a fashion? It is entirely plausible, considering Miss Rachel's current state. She exists in a condition of nervous agitation so pitiable it breaks the heart. I dare not broach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has worked its healing balm. To aid this recovery, I have not hesitated to dismiss the police officer. The mystery that confounds us baffles him as well; he is a stranger to our grief, and his presence only adds to my sorrow, driving Rachel mad if so much as his name is mentioned.

"My future arrangements are as settled as circumstances permit. My intention is to take Rachel to London—to soothe her mind through a complete change of scenery and to seek the counsel of the finest medical minds. Will you join us in town? My dear Franklin, you must emulate my patience and await a more propitious season. Though your assistance in the initial inquiry was invaluable, it remains an unforgiven offense in light of Rachel's dreadful mental state. Acting blindly, you have inadvertently threatened her secret with exposure, thereby compounding the burden of anxiety she bears. It is impossible to excuse the stubbornness that holds you responsible for consequences unforeseen by either of us. She cannot be reasoned with; she can only be pitied. I am grieved to utter this, but for the present, separation between you and Rachel is advisable. My sole counsel to you is to grant her time."

I returned the letter, feeling a profound pang of sympathy for Mr. Franklin. I knew how deeply he cherished my young mistress, and I saw clearly how Lady Verinder's words had struck a fatal blow to his hopes. "You know the proverb, sir," I said gently. Mr. Franklin folded the letter, though his countenance offered little sign that my consolation had taken root.

For seven long days, I and my small retinue camped on the fringes of a vast desert, waiting in vain. Finally, he emerged from the dust, bringing both the money and the letters, standing right at the flap of my tent.

"I fear I bring ill tidings, sir," he stammered, pointing to a single envelope. Its borders were rimmed with black mourning, and the address was penned by Mr. Bruff.

Suspense is a torment worse than any grief. That somber letter was the first I tore open. It shattered my world, informing me that my father had died and naming me the sole heir to his immense fortune. Yet, with this wealth came a heavy mantle of responsibility. Mr. Bruff implored me not to waste a moment, urging an immediate return to England.

Before dawn broke on the following morning, I was already bound for home.

As my old friend Betteredge described me upon my departure, perhaps slightly exaggerated in his quaint, serious fashion, I was indeed wounded to the core by Rachel's treatment. He believed I possessed distinct French, German, and Italian facets to my soul—a notion likely born of my lively cousin's jests rather than reality—but he did capture the essence of my anguish. I had gone abroad, resolving that if change and distance could heal, they must erase her. I am convinced that separation does aid a man in sorrow, forcing his gaze away from his own pain. I never truly forgot her, yet the sharpest sting of memory dulled gradually, softened by the slow erosion of time, miles, and new experiences.

Conversely, as I turned homeward, the cure I had cultivated so diligently began to unravel. With every mile closer to the land she inhabited, to the hope of seeing her face again, her influence clawed back with irresistible force. Upon leaving England, she was the last name I would allow on my tongue; returning, she became the first inquiry on my lips the moment I reunited with Mr. Bruff.

Naturally, I was briefed on everything that had transpired in my absence—that history recounted in Betteredge's narrative—save for one crucial detail. Mr. Bruff, feeling constrained by propriety, withheld the private motives that had driven both Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite to recall their broken marriage vows. I spared him the embarrassment of asking why. After the jealous agony of learning she had once considered wedlock with another, knowing she had ultimately realized her mistake and freed herself from that engagement was a relief sufficient to calm my storm.

My thoughts, inevitably drawn back to her, shifted to the present. Who watched over her now? Where did she live?

She resided under the guardianship of the widow, Mrs. Merridew, sister to the late Sir John Verinder. Her mother's executors had entrusted her to this kind woman, who had graciously accepted. Reports reached me that they flourished together, currently settled for the season in Mrs. Merridew's townhouse on Portland Place.

Within half an hour of learning this, I was en route to Portland Place, lacking the courage to confess my haste to Mr. Bruff.

The man who opened the door hesitated, unsure if Miss Verinder was within. I sent my card upstairs, hoping to settle the matter swiftly. He descended with an impenetrable expression, declaring Miss Verinder was out.

I could have suspected others of deliberately shunning me, but it was impossible to doubt Rachel. I instructed him to tell her I would return at six.

At six, the denial repeated itself. "Not at home?" I asked, my voice tight. "Has any message been left?" "No." "Did she not receive my card?" The servant bowed, offering a polite apology, confirming that Miss Verinder *had* received it.

The conclusion was undeniable. Rachel refused to see me.

I could not stand this rejection without seeking cause. I sent my name to Mrs. Merridew, requesting an audience at her earliest convenience. She received me immediately, ushering me into a cozy little sitting room where I found a pleasant, elderly lady. She expressed profound regret and surprise regarding my visit, yet she confessed she was powerless to explain Rachel's silence or pressure her on matters of private feeling. Her patience was endless, yet it yielded nothing.

My final resort was writing. My servant delivered a note the next day, ordered to await a reply. The response arrived, a single, stinging sentence: *"Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake."*

Though fond of her, I felt a hot flush of indignation at this insult. Before I could compose myself, Mr. Bruff entered on business. I brushed aside his affairs and laid the entire situation before him. Like Mrs. Merridew, he offered no enlightenment. Did slander circulate? No. Had she questioned my whereabouts during my long absence? Not a word.

Then I produced the letter from Lady Verinder, written from Frizinghall just as I departed Yorkshire. Two lines burned like acid: *"The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel's mind... you have added to the burden of anxiety..."*

"Is it possible," I asked, my voice trembling, "that the bitterness described there remains unchanged?"

Mr. Bruff, looking deeply distressed, admitted he saw no other interpretation.

I rang the bell. "Pack my portmanteau," I commanded, "and fetch a railway guide."

"What are you going to do?" Mr. Bruff asked, astonished.

"To Yorkshire," I replied, my resolve iron. "By the next train."

"For what purpose?"

"Mr. Bruff, my innocent aid in finding the Diamond was an unpardonable sin in Rachel's eyes nearly a year ago, and it remains so today. I will not accept this cold shoulder! I am determined to uncover the secret behind her silence to her mother and her hatred toward me. If time, effort, and gold can reveal the truth, I will lay my hand on the thief who stole the Moonstone!"

The worthy old gentleman tried to dissuade me, to urge reason, to remind me of my duty. I heard none of it. Nothing on earth could shake the fire in my blood.

"I shall resume the inquiry exactly where I left off," I declared, stepping forward. "Step by step, until I reach the present. There are missing links in the evidence I gathered, and I go to Gabriel Betteredge to fill them!"

Towards sunset that evening, I stood once more on the terrace of the beloved country house, gazing at its peaceful, silent grounds. The gardener was the first sight in the desolate park. I knew he had left Betteredge an hour prior to sunbathe in the back yard's usual corner. I decided to find him myself.

Walking through the familiar paths, I peered into the open gate of the yard.

And there he was—the dear old friend of happier, vanished days. He sat on his old beehive chair in the familiar corner, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* on his lap, flanked by two dozing dogs. From where I stood, the last slanting rays of the sun cast my shadow directly before me. Perhaps the dogs sensed it, or their keen noses caught my scent; they sprang up, growling. Startled, the old man quieted them with a gentle word, shaded his failing eyes with his hand, and looked curiously at the figure at the gate.

Tears filled my eyes. I was forced to pause, gathering strength, before I could trust myself to speak.

"Betteredge!" I called out, gesturing toward the familiar volume resting on his knees. "Has *Robinson Crusoe* told you this evening that Franklin Blake might be expected?"

"The Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!" cried the old man, his voice thick with emotion. "That is precisely what *Robinson Crusoe* has revealed!"

"There is the passage, Mr. Franklin!" he exclaimed once his tongue had returned to its normal function. "As I live by bread, sir, this is the very line I was reading the instant before you arrived! I had anticipated, quite naturally, that he would overwhelm me with a barrage of inquiries. Yet, no such thing occurred. The hospitable impulse held absolute sway in the old servant's mind whenever a family member, however unexpected, appeared as a guest within the house.

It grieved me to disappoint him. However, this was now Rachel's house. Could I dine within its walls, or sleep beneath its roof, after the turmoil that unfolded in London? The simplest dictates of self-respect—forbade, properly speaking, to cross the threshold.

Taking Betteredge by the arm, I guided him out into the gardens. There was no alternative. I was compelled to speak the truth. "Miss Rachel possesses her flaws, I have never disputed it," he began. "And occasionally, she rides the high horse. Indeed, she has been attempting to ride over *you*, and you have endured it. I had listened to his tales of the late Mrs. Betteredge frequently, invariably presented as his irrefutable proof of the innate weakness and perversity inherent in the fairer sex. In this role, he produced her instance now.

His argument was utterly unassailable! I could only assure my faithful companion that even his formidable powers of persuasion were, in this specific instance, wasted upon me.

The mention of the place sparked an immediate recollection. The farmhouse sat nestled in a sheltered inland valley, beside the prettiest stream in that stretch of Yorkshire. The farmer maintained a spare bedroom and parlor, which he customarily rented to artists, anglers, and tourists alike. We retraced our steps to the yard where I had abandoned my traveling bag. Leading the way out, he wagged his head and growled ominously. "There exists but one further miracle capable of occurring," he muttered over his shoulder. "The next action you will undertake, Mr. Franklin, will be to repay me those seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a lad."

"The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what drives you now, sir?"

I retain only the haziest fragments of what transpired at Hotherstone's Farm. My memory is a blur of a fervent welcome, a supper so vast it seemed intended to sustain a whole eastern village, and a chamber scrubbed so immaculately that the sole blemish was that execrable artifact of ancestral madness: the feather-bed. That evening, arrangements had been made with Betteredge for me to summon him en route to Cobb's Hole, whenever I chose. My Dear Sir,

Do you retain any memory of a somewhat uncouth gentleman you encountered at dinner in London last autumn? Allow me to jog your recollection: his name was Murthwaite, and we engaged in a lengthy discourse afterward, centered upon an Indian diamond known as the Moonstone and a shadowy conspiracy to seize it.

Since that evening, I have wandered through the vast expanse of Central Asia. Fate, however, has brought me back to the northern and northwestern regions of India, where some of my earliest adventures unfolded. A fortnight ago, I found myself in a remote province scarcely known to Europeans, called Kattiawar.

Here, an adventure occurred—one, I suspect, in which you hold a personal stake.

The landscape of Kattiawar is savage; even the tillers of the soil cultivate their fields armed to the teeth. The populace remains fanatically devoted to ancient Hinduism, worshipping Brahma and Vishnu with unyielding zeal. The few Muslim families scattered among the villages live in terror, abstaining from all meat. A Muslim even suspected of harming the sacred cow is summarily executed without pity by the surrounding pious neighbors. To fortify this fervor, two of Hinduism's most renowned pilgrimage sites lie within Kattiawar's borders: Dwarka, the cradle of Krishna, and Somnauth. The latter, a holy city sacked and razed centuries ago by the conqueror Mahmud of Ghizni, stands today as a magnificent ruin.

Having returned to these romantic lands, I resolved not to depart Kattiawar without paying homage to the desolate grandeur of Somnauth. Some three days' journey on foot separated me from the sacred city when I first noticed others traveling in my direction, appearing in twos and threes.

To those who spoke to me, I introduced myself as a Hindu-Buddhist pilgrim from a distant province, clad in garments suited to the disguise. Knowing the local tongue fluently, and possessing a lean, sun-browned complexion that masked my European origins, I easily passed as a stranger from afar, though not quite one of the locals.

My Hindu guides, owing to their elevated status, were granted access to the shrine, a privilege they generously extended to me. There, beneath two majestic trees, a curtain concealed the sanctuary. Below, a flat projection of rock served as a natural dais. Standing there with my guides, I gazed backward down the slope.

Before me lay the most sublime spectacle of nature and humanity combined that I have ever beheld. The gentle slopes of the hill dissolved imperceptibly into a grassy plain where three rivers met. To one side, the waters wound gracefully through the forest, vanishing and reappearing like silver ribbons; to the other, the ocean lay glass-smooth under the night sky. Tens of thousands of human beings, robed in white, cascaded down the hillsides, spilling onto the plain and fringing the riverbanks. Wild red flames of torches and cressets flickered upward from the endless throng, casting a halo of fire against the unclouded glory of the Eastern moon.

Suddenly, a mournful melody, played on strings and flutes, drew my attention away from the vista. I turned to behold three figures standing upon the rocky platform. In the center stood the man I had spoken with in England when the "Indians" first appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder's residence. The other two, his companions from that night, walked beside him.

A spectator near me observed my start. Whispering urgently, he explained the significance of the figures. "They are Brahmins," he murmured, "who have forfeited their caste in the service of the god. The divine commandment requires their purification through pilgrimage. Tonight, the three shall part forever. Each must embark alone as a pilgrim to the shrines of India. They shall never again gaze upon one another's face, nor find respite in their wanderings, from this day of separation until the day of their death."

As these words faded, the mournful music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves before the curtain hiding the shrine. Rising, they looked upon one another, embraced in a final, tragic bond, and then descended separately into the crowd. The people parted for them in a hushed, reverent silence. As they moved apart, the massive white tide of pilgrims closed behind them, erasing the tracks of the condemned men. We saw them no more.

Then, a new strain of music, loud and jubilant, erupted from the hidden shrine, causing the crowd to shudder and press tighter together. The curtain was drawn aside, revealing the sanctuary.

High upon a throne, seated upon his symbolic antelope, with four arms outstretched toward the corners of the earth, loomed the God of the Moon, dark and awe-inspiring in the mystical light. And there, embedded in the deity's brow, gleamed the yellow diamond. Its splendor, last seen by me in England nestled within a woman's gown, burned with renewed radiance.
