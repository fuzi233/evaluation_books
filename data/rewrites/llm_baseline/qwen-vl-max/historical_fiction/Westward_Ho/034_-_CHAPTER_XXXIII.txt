“Would you hear a Spanish lady,
How she woo'd an Englishman?
Garments gay and rich as may be,
Deck'd with jewels had she on.”
—Elizabethan Ballad.

It was the first of October. The morning broke clear and still, the sky streaked gently from east to west with soft gray clouds, as though nature herself paused in quiet reflection after the tumult of summer storms and battle. The *Vengeance* slipped silently over the bar, her sails low, her flag flying at half-mast—a silent tribute to the man who lay within. Salvation Yeo was dead, and his body rested upon the ship.

A boat pulled away from the vessel, heading for the western edge of the shore. Cary and Brimblecombe helped Amyas Leigh from the skiff and guided him slowly up the hill toward Burrough, his home. A crowd gathered around him—some cheering, some weeping, many moved by pity. All of Appledore and Bideford knew what had happened: the great seaman had returned not in triumph, but in sorrow, blind and broken, carrying with him the weight of loss.

“Spare me, my good friends,” Amyas said, his voice steady despite the pain. “I have come ashore that I might return home quietly, without being paraded through the town like a spectacle. Do not speak of me. Think not of me. But follow me decently, as Christian men, and bear this body to its rest—the body of a better man than I.”

As he spoke, another boat approached, bearing the coffin draped in the English flag. The people obeyed his words. One man was sent ahead to Burrough to warn Mrs. Leigh that her son was coming.

The coffin was carried ashore, and Amyas took his place among the mourners, flanked by Cary and Brimblecombe. Behind them came the crew in solemn order, followed by the growing throng. By the time they reached halfway to Northam, the procession numbered nearly five hundred souls.

They had sent word the day before to the sexton, who had already dug the grave. When they arrived at the churchyard, the parson stood waiting at the gate. Mrs. Leigh remained at home—too overwhelmed to face the gathering. Yet her heart swelled with pride and sorrow. She sat in the bay window, Ayacanora beside her, and when the bell ceased tolling, she opened her Prayer-book and began to read the Burial-service.

“Ayacanora,” she murmured, “they are burying old Master Yeo—the man who loved you, who searched for you across the world, who saved you from the jaws of the crocodile. Are you not sorry for him, child? You look so bright today.”

Ayacanora flushed, her eyes dropping. She thought of nothing but Amyas.

The service ended. The blessing was given. The parson stepped back—but the crowd lingered. They stared at the grave, then at Amyas, the blind giant standing at its head, as if sensing something unspoken yet inevitable.

And then it came.

Amyas straightened himself to his full height, raised his hand, and swept it forward with regal grace. Every eye turned to him. He tried twice to speak—but the words choked in his throat. Then, at last:

“Good people all, and seamen—among whom I was born and bred—and to whom I return today, blind, to dwell until death—Here lies the flower and pattern of bold mariners; the truest of friends, the most terrible of foes; steadfast in purpose, cunning in counsel, swift in action; sober in victory, and in defeat—God knows I’ve known many such days—of endurance beyond mortal measure.

He was the first of Britons to humble the pride of Spain at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre; the first to sail those South Seas which, by God’s grace, shall one day be as free to English ships as this harbor outside our doors.

Having endured strange torments and sufferings, purged of youthful sins, he faced the Popish enemy, and learned from fire and exile to fear God alone. And having served faithfully as a scourge against Spain and a soldier of Christ, he is now lifted up—like Elijah—to his reward, in a chariot of fire unto heaven.

And I pray, I trust, that he has left behind the mantle of his courage and piety—that these shores may never lack brave and godly sailors, who will count their lives cheap in defense of Country, Bible, and Queen. Amen.”

He paused, then reached out for the hands of his companions. Together, they walked from the churchyard, across the village street, and up the lane to Burrough gates. The crowd parted in silence, as though afraid to disturb the sacred moment.

Amyas seemed to know when they reached the gate. He unlocked it himself and stepped forward boldly along the gravel path, while Cary and Brimblecombe followed, trembling—not from fear, but from emotion. They expected either a storm of grief or a sudden collapse. Their hearts fluttered like those of young girls.

Up to the door he went, as if he could see. He felt for the entrance, stepped inside, and called softly: “Mother!”

In an instant, she was in his arms.

Neither spoke for a long while. She wept silently, her eyes dry with sorrow; he stood firm, calm, his great arms wrapped around her.

“Mother,” he said at last, “I am home. You see, I must come. Will you take me in? Look after this useless body? I shan’t be too much trouble, shall I?” He smiled down at her, kissed her brow.

She said nothing, only passed her arm gently around his waist and led him inside.

“Take care of your head, dear child,” she whispered. “The doors are low.”

They entered together.

“Will! Jack!” Amyas called.

But the two men had already hurried off.

“I’m glad we’re gone,” said Cary. “I’d have been a fool in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. How her face trembled—and how she held it in!”

“Ah, well,” said Jack, “there goes a brave servant of the queen, cut down before his work was done. Heigho! I must go home to my father, and then—”

“And then home with me,” said Cary. “You and I have sailed together too long, Jack. You’ll not waste your prize-money on taverns. I’ll watch over you ashore, or we’ll have you treating half the town for a week.”

“Oh, Mr. Cary!” said Jack, scandalized.

“Come home with me,” said Cary. “We’ll poison the parson, and your father can give me the rectory.”

“Oh, Mr. Cary!” said Jack again.

And so the two set off for Clovelly that very day.

Amyas sat alone.

His mother had stepped out briefly to speak with the sailors who had brought his luggage, to feed and shelter them. He remained in the old bay-window—the same spot where, as a boy, he had read *King Arthur*, *Fox’s Martyrs*, and *The Cruelties of the Spaniards*. He reached out and found the books—still there, side by side, untouched for twenty years.

The window was open. A cool breeze carried the scent of four-season roses, rosemary, and autumn gilliflowers—just as it always had.

On the table, he sensed a dish of apples. He knew them by smell—the same apples he used to gather as a boy. He reached out, touched them, rolled them in his palms, played with them—just as he had long ago.

And in that moment, his whole life flashed before him—not in dreams, but in memory: every sea, every storm, every word spoken, every face seen. He saw the fairy island on the Meta, heard the roar of the cataract once more, saw the palm-tops glowing above the spray, stepped across the smooth trunks, leaped down to the gravel beach beside the pool.

Then, rising from the fern-covered rocks, came the vision of Ayacanora.

Where was she?

He had not thought of her until now. How he had wronged her! But punishment had come—justice had been served. Perhaps she no longer cared for him. Who would care for a blind ox, doomed to be fed and tended like a child for the rest of his days?

Tut! How long had his mother been gone?

He returned to playing with the apples, lost in memories of climbing with Frank in the orchard.

Then one slipped from his fingers—fell to the floor.

He bent to find it—but could not.

Frustrated, he turned sharply—his head struck the table.

Was it the pain? The small disappointment? Or was it the cruel absurdity of blindness, made real in such a trivial way—making it all the more humiliating?

Or was it the breaking point of nerves stretched too thin?

He didn’t know. But in that instant, he stamped his foot—pettishly, like a child. Then, catching himself, he burst into violent tears.

A rustle passed by. The apple was placed back in his hand.

And Ayacanora’s voice, trembling, sobbed: “There! There it is! Do not weep! Oh, do not weep! I cannot bear it! I will get you everything! Let me fetch and carry, tend you, feed you, lead you—like your slave, your dog! Say I may be your slave!”

She fell to her knees, seized both his hands, and covered them with kisses.

“Yes!” she cried. “I will be your slave! I must be! You cannot help it! You cannot escape me now! You cannot go to sea! You cannot turn your back on wretched me. I have you safe now! Safe!”

She clutched his hands, triumphant.

“Oh, and what a wretch I am,” she whispered, horrified. “To rejoice in this! To taunt him with his blindness! Oh, forgive me! I am only a wild girl—a savage Indian, you know—but—” And she broke into sobs.

A great shudder ran through Amyas Leigh. He sat silent for a moment. Then, solemnly: “And is this still possible? Then God have mercy upon me, a sinner.”

Ayacanora looked up, uncertain.

But before she could speak, he bent down, lifted her as a lion lifts a lamb, pressed her to his chest, and covered her face with kisses.

The door opened.

A rustle of silk. Ayacanora sprang back, breathless, defiant—half trembling, half claiming her place: “He is mine now. No one shall part us.”

“Who is it?” Amyas asked.

“Your mother.”

“You see,” he said, smiling, “that I am bringing forth fruits meet for repentance.”

He heard her approach.

A kiss passed between the women. A sob. Then he felt Ayacannota sink once more into his arms.

“Amyas, my son,” said Mrs. Leigh, her voice silver and dreamy, like distant chimes from the highest heavens, “fear not to take her to your heart again—for it is your mother who has laid her there.”

“It is true,” Amyas whispered to himself. “What God has joined together, man cannot put asunder.”

* * * * *

From that hour, Ayacanora’s voice returned. Day by day, year by year, her song rose from that happy home, soaring like a skylark into the highest skies—carrying with it the peace of the blind giant, drawing him back to the paradises of the West, in the wake of the heroes who, from that time forth, sailed forth to build a new and greater England, shouting with Heaven’s favor: **Westward Ho!**
