Black despair hung heavy over the chamber like a shroud of ash. "Ah," rasped Zat Arras, his voice dripping with feigned cordiality, "to what kindly circumstance am I indebted for the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?"

As he spoke, a guard stepped forward and severed the leather gag from my lips. I said nothing, offering no reply to the treacherous Jed of Zodanga. Instead, I stood there in a statue-like silence, my gaze leveled and unblinking upon him. I doubted not that the icy contempt burning within me etched a mask of fury onto my features, a look that surely betrayed the depth of my hatred for the man who would sell his prince for a crown.

The eyes of the chamber shifted from me to Zat Arras, and slowly, a flush of genuine anger crawled up the Jed's pale cheeks. "You may go," he commanded the guards who had brought me. When the room was cleared, leaving only his two silent companions, he turned back to me. His voice was that of glacial ice, delivered with agonizing slowness and deliberate pauses, as if he were choosing each word from a library of malice.

"John Carter," he intoned, "by the edicts of custom, by the laws of our religion, and by the verdict of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone hold the key to your end. I possess absolute dominion over your life; I may kill you instantly, or I may grant you freedom, and if I choose to slay you, no soul would dare question the silence of your grave."

His lips curled into a sneer. "Consider this: if you walk free within Helium for a year, bound by the conditions of a reprieve, the populace will likely forget the sentence entirely. However, there is a price. You may depart free within two minutes, provided you swear an oath that Tardos Mors shall never return to Helium. Nor shall Mors Kajak, nor Dejah Thoris. Helium must select a new Jeddak within twelve moons, and Zat Arras will be that Jeddak. Say that you will espouse my cause. This is the currency of your liberty. I am finished."

A cold knot formed in my stomach. I knew the scope of Zat Arras's cruelty; he would not hesitate to extinguish my flame. If I died, the path to the throne would clear for him effortlessly. But if I lived, I could hunt for Dejah Thoris. To refuse his offer, however, risked sealing her fate in the Arena of Issus, condemning her to the jaws of the white apes.

For a fleeting second, the weight of this dilemma paralyzed me. *Would the proud daughter of a thousand Jeddaks choose death over dishonor?* Yes. And could John Carter, the Son of Helium, do less?

I turned to face the usurper. "There can be no alliance," I declared, my voice ringing with defiance, "between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is truly dead."

Zat Arras merely shrugged, a gesture of bored dismissal. "It will not be long, John Carter," he mused, "until your opinions are of interest only to yourself. Make the best of them while you may. I will permit you time to reflect upon this magnanimous offer. Tonight, you descend into the silence and darkness of the Pits. Know this: if you fail to agree to this alternative within a reasonable time, you shall never emerge from the gloom and silence again. Nor shall you know the precise moment when a hand reaches out from the dark with a keen dagger, stealing from you forever the warmth, freedom, and joy of the outer world."

Clapping his hands, he signaled the guards. They returned, and with a wave, Zat Arras pointed to me. "To the pits!"

Four men escorted me through corridors that seemed to stretch into infinity, descending deeper and deeper beneath the sprawling city of Helium. A radium hand-light flickered ahead, casting long, dancing shadows. Finally, we halted in a cavernous chamber. Rings embedded in the rough rock walls held chains, many ending in the bleached ribs of human skeletons. One of the guards kicked aside a pile of bones and unlocked a massive padlock securing a chain around what had once been a human ankle. With a sharp snap of iron, the band circled my leg, binding me to the stone.

They departed, plunging me into an abyss of utter darkness. For moments, the clanking of armor echoed, fading into a profound, crushing silence. I was alone with my gruesome companions—the bones of the dead, whose fate was merely a prelude to my own. How long I stood listening, I could not say, but eventually, I sank to the hard floor, leaning my head against the stony wall, and succumbed to sleep.

Hours later, I awoke to find a young man standing before me. In one hand, he held a dim light; in the other, a vessel containing a gray, gruel-like mixture—the staple diet of Barsoomian prisoners.

"Zat Arras sends his greetings," the youth said, his tone flat. "Though he is fully aware of the conspiracy to make you Jeddak of Helium, he remains unmoved regarding his offer. To regain your freedom, you need only instruct me to tell Zat Arras that you accept his terms."

I shook my head, a silent rejection. The boy said no more, placed the food on the floor beside me, and retreated into the tunnel.

For weeks, this same youth returned twice daily, delivering the same fare and the same hollow salutations from Zat Arras. I attempted to engage him in conversation, to probe the nature of his mission, but he remained a wall of polite silence. Eventually, I desisted, knowing that curiosity would only bring danger.

Months bled into a haze of anxiety. I devised schemes to signal Carthoris, scraping relentlessly at a single link of my massive chain, dreaming of wearing it through so I could crawl back to the surface and warn my son. I feared for the expedition intended to rescue Dejah Thoris. Had Zat Arras' spies overheard our planning? Was Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, or Xodar already fallen to assassination? Or perhaps, like me, trapped in these subterranean tombs?

Determined to find a thread of truth, I altered my strategy when the youth returned. Observing that he was handsome, roughly the size and age of Carthoris, yet clad in shabby rags that clashed with his noble bearing, I decided to test him.

"You have been kind to me during this imprisonment," I began, keeping my voice low and soothing. "Knowing that I may have but a short time to live, I wish, ere it is too late, to express my profound appreciation for your courtesy. You have brought my food promptly, ensuring it was pure and sufficient. You have never abused my defenseless state. Your kindness is the sole reason I feel gratitude.

"In the guard-room of my palace, there are many fine trappings. Go there and select the harness that pleases you most—it shall be yours. I ask only that you wear it, so I may know my wish has been realized. Tell me you will do it."

The boy's eyes lit with pleasure, and I saw him glance from his tattered clothes to the imagined splendor of the palace. He paused, weighing his options, and my heart hammered against my ribs.

"And if I went to the Palace of the Prince of Helium with such a demand?" he murmured, his voice trembling slightly. "They would laugh at me, and likely cast me headlong into the avenues. No, it cannot be. Though I thank you, if Zat Arras even suspected..."

"There can be no harm in it, my boy," I urged gently. "Go by night. Carry a note from me to Carthoris, my son. Read it first to ensure it holds no poison for Zat Arras. My son will be discreet; only the three of us need know. It is a harmless act, condemnable by no one."

Silence stretched between us, thick with tension. He turned to leave, still deep in thought. I could not guess his decision, and hours passed in a fever of fretting.

*If he accepts,* I reasoned, *it means Carthoris lives.* *If he returns wearing the harness and sword,* I would know the message was delivered and Carthoris understood my plight. A Zodangan messenger would suffice to explain my captivity.

Excitement, barely contained, vibrated through me as I awaited his return. When he arrived, I offered no greeting beyond the customary nod. As he placed the food, he also set down writing materials. My heart soared. I feigned surprise, then allowed a look of dawning comprehension to soften my face. Picking up the quill, I penned a brief order to Carthoris to send Parthak a harness of his choice and the jeweled short-sword I described. It was simple, yet it meant everything.

I laid the note open. Parthak snatched it up and vanished without a word.

Three hundred days had passed since my incarceration. If Dejah Thoris was to be saved, it must be swiftly, for those chosen by Issus lived but a single year. Her end approached inevitably.
