It is not granted to all to walk the easy, sunlit path, and when our own way grows dark, what use is it to rage? His will shall be done, no matter if we bow to resignation or kick against the pricks. The very impulse of creation carries it forward; powers seen and unseen hold its fulfilment in their charge. That proof of a life to come must be written in fire, in blood if need be—we trace its mark across every wild corner of nature, across every sharp, unyielding stretch of our own experience. Sufferer, do not faint at the touch of that burning evidence. Tired wayfarer, gird up your loins, lift your eyes, and march onward. All of us who walk this wilderness together, equal in our weariness, steady in our tread, let us bear our cross as our banner. We have His promise for a staff, His providence for present hope, His open bosom for our final rest, and a glory beyond all imagining that waits for us at the end. So let us run that we may win, endure hardship as good soldiers, finish our course and keep the faith, and come off more than conquerors at the last: Art thou not from everlasting, my Holy One? We shall not die.

One soft July Thursday morning, we were all gathered in the first classe, waiting for our literature lesson. The hour was due, and we expected him any moment.

The pupils sat perfectly still, their neat, ribbon-tied compositions laid out on their desks, ready for Professor Emanuel’s quick, impatient rounds. The air was warm, the glass door to the garden propped ajar, and fresh breeze drifted in, making the jasmine plants at the lintel nod and sway, as if they were carrying secret, unspoken tidings.

M. Paul was not always perfectly punctual, so no one wondered much at the small delay—until the door opened, and instead of his swift, fiery step, cautious, unhurried Madame Beck stepped quietly into the room.

She walked straight to M. Emanuel’s desk, stood before it, pulled her light summer shawl tighter around her shoulders, and spoke in low, firm, unwavering tones. “There will be no literature lesson this morning.”

A full two minutes of dead silence followed before she spoke again.
“Lessons will be suspended for at least a week. I shall need that long to find a suitable replacement for M. Emanuel. In the meantime, we will fill the gap usefully.
“Your Professor, ladies,” she continued, “intends, if possible, to come and take formal leave of you all. He has no time for that ceremony right now. He is preparing for a long voyage: a very sudden, urgent duty summons calls him to a great distance. He has decided to leave Europe for an indefinite stretch of time. Perhaps he will tell you more of it himself. This morning, instead of your usual lesson with M. Emanuel, you will read English with Mademoiselle Lucy.”

She gave a small courteous nod, drew her shawl closer, and left the classroom.

A great, heavy silence fell, then a low, murmurous wave of sound rolled across the room. I saw more than one girl press her handkerchief to her face, her shoulders shaking.

The whispering and soft sobs only grew louder with time. Discipline frayed; it felt as if the school’s usual vigilance had vanished entirely. Habit and long practice let me rally fast, though. I stood, spoke in my usual firm, steady tone, and slowly, steadily restored order. I kept their English reading long and rigorous, occupying them for the whole rest of the morning. I was sharp, unsparing, even half-mocking of the girls who wept so loudly. Their emotion was only hysteric agitation, hollow as froth—and the truth was, I could not bear their gasping, noisy tears, not when every thread of my own self-control was stretched almost to snapping. When one weak, low-spirited pupil still sobbed after all the others had calmed, I spoke to her so sternly that she was forced to conquer the convulsion.

That girl might well have hated me—until the end of the school day, when I ordered her to stay back after the others left, and pressed her to my chest, kissing her cheek, a kindness I had never shown any student before. The second I did it, I sent her away, for the small burst of tenderness made her weep all the harder.

I stuffed every minute of that day full of work, and would have sat up all night by candlelight if I could, but the dark hours that followed were cruel, leaving me raw and unfit for the next day’s ordeal of endless, unthinking gossip. The news spread like wildfire. Reserve wore off fast; every mouth, from teachers to pupils to the lowest kitchen servant, chattered M. Emanuel’s name. The man who had been at the school’s side from its very opening, gone all at once! Everyone thought it strange.

They talked so much, so long, that bits of real information began to surface. On the third day, I heard he was sailing in a week, bound for the West Indies. I searched Madame Beck’s face, her eyes, her every small gesture, for confirmation or denial—but there was nothing there, only her usual unperturbed, commonplace calm. She declared his departure an immense loss to her, that he was her right hand, that she had fought the decision but he had convinced her it was his unavoidable duty. She said this loudly, publicly, in the classroom and at the dinner table, directly to Zélie St. Pierre, never once glancing at me, never once seeming to suspect I might have any care in the matter.

The week dragged on. No one spoke of M. Paul coming to say goodbye. No one asked if he would show up. No one betrayed the quiet, gnawing terror that he might slip away silent and unseen. They chattered endlessly, and never once touched that single vital point. Of course Madame could see him whenever she liked. Why would she care if he came back to the classroom?

At last the news was confirmed: his ship sailed for Basseterre, in Guadaloupe, and the business abroad was for a friend, not his own. I hardly slept those days. Whenever I didze off, I would jolt awake instantly, the words *Basseterre* and *Guadaloupe* ringing in my ears, scrawled in zigzag lines of red or violet light across the dark of my room.

There was no help for what I felt. How could there be? M. Paul had grown so kind to me lately, softer, warmer, hour by hour. It had been a month since we settled that sharp theological quarrel, and there had been no fight between us since. Our peace was no cold, distant standoff: he came to see me more often, talked to me far more freely, spent hours at my side with his temper smoothed, his eyes soft, his manner as home-like and gentle as I could have ever hoped. He asked after my plans to open my own small school, made me tell him the details again and again, teasing that it was nothing but a silly Alnaschar dream. The old jarring was gone. We understood each other. Feelings of union, of quiet hope, had settled deep in my heart: affection, deep esteem, a dawning trust that had woven itself into an unbreakable bond.

Our lessons in those last weeks were so quiet, so sweet. No more taunts about my “intellect”, no more threats to drag me up in front of the whole school to perform. In place of the jealous gibe and the half-passionate eulogy, there was only silent, indulgent help, fond guidance, a tender forbearance that forgave all my faults but never stooped to empty praise. Sometimes he would sit for minutes without speaking at all, and when dusk or duty pulled him away, he would murmur, *“It is soft, this rest. This quiet happiness is so precious.”*

Not ten days before that final announcement, he had joined me while I walked in my private alley after dark. He took my hand, and I looked up at his face, certain he was about to say something that would change everything.

*“Bonne petite amie!”* he said, soft as the twilight. *“Sweet little comforter!”* His touch, his words, stirred a strange new hope in me. Could it be that he cared for me as more than a friend, more than a brother? Did his look hold a kindness far beyond fraternity?

His eyes had more to say, his hand drew me closer, his lips were about to form the words—when the alley’s quiet was broken. Two ominous figures stepped out of the dusk: Madame Beck, and Père Silas.

I will never forget the priest’s face. At first it softened with that overdone sentimental sensibility, as if he was shocked to stumble on such a tender moment, then it hardened fast with the jaundiced bite of ecclesiastical jealousy. He spoke to me with cloying unction, and turned to M. Paul with sharp, unyielding sternness. Madame, of course, pretended to see nothing at all—even as M. Paul kept my hand clasped tight in his, not letting me pull it away.

After that, the news of his sudden departure had seemed impossible to believe at first. Only the constant repetition, the hundred and fifty voices all around me speaking of it as fact, forced me to accept it. That week of suspense, those blank, burning days with no word, no explanation from him, I remember, but I can find no words to describe how it felt to live through them.

The last day broke. That was the day he would either come and speak his farewell, or vanish silent, and we would never see him again.

It seemed no one else in the whole school shared that sharp, waiting dread. Everyone rose at the usual hour, ate their breakfast as usual, went about their usual duties with their usual phlegm, not a single one of them seeming to spare a thought for their old Professor. No one would band together to beg Madame Beck for one last interview with the man who had, at least in their own way, been loved by so many. What was the love of the crowd, after all, but a shallow, fleeting thing?

I knew where he lived. I knew exactly where to find him. The distance was barely a stone’s throw away. But I could not go. For all my quiet longing, I had no faculty to seek him out, to run after him, to remind him I was there. Even if he had walked within arm’s reach of me, silent and unnoticing, I would have stood still, and let him pass.

The morning wasted away. Afternoon came, and I thought it was all over. My heart trembled in my chest. My blood ran thick and troubled. I was so sick I could barely stay at my post, barely do my work, while every other person in that little world plodded on, indifferent, jocund, free of all care. The very girls who had wept hysterically a week prior had completely forgotten the news, what it meant, what they had felt.

A little before five, the hour of school dismissal, Madame Beck sent for me to her chamber, to translate an English letter she had received and write out a reply. Before we started, I noticed her softly close both doors to the room, then shut and fastened the casement even though the day was hot, and free air was usually non-negotiable for her. Why that precaution? A sharp, fierce suspicion flared in me. She was keeping sound out. What sound?

I listened harder than I ever had in my life, like the winter wolf scenting prey on the snow, hearing the distant tramp of a traveller miles off. I could write and listen at the same time. Halfway through the letter, I heard it: a tread in the vestibule. No bell had rung; Rosine had been sent ahead to open the door for him, no doubt on direct orders. Madame saw my pen halt. She coughed, bustled around, spoke louder. The tread moved on, towards the classrooms.

“Proceed,” she said. But my hand was fettered, my ear was caught, all my thoughts were held captive.

The classrooms were in a separate wing, the hall dividing them from the dwelling house, but through all the partitions and distance, I heard a sudden stir of dozens of people, a whole division rising to their feet at once.

“They are putting away their work,” Madame said.

It was the hour to put away work, yes—but why that sudden hush, that instant, quiet lull across the whole room?

“Wait, Madame. I will go see what it is.”

I set down my pen and left her. She could not detain me, so she rose and followed, close as my shadow. I turned on the last step of the stairs.

“Are you coming too?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said, her face clouded, but resolute.

We walked forward, not together, but she stepped exactly in my tracks.

He was there. When I stepped into the first classe, I saw him, that familiar face, that form I had longed for so badly. They had tried to keep him away, but he had come anyway.

The girls stood in a semicircle. He was making his way around them, saying his farewells, pressing each hand, touching each girl’s cheek in a parting kiss, the custom of the land for such a long, solemn goodbye.

It felt unbearable that Madame Beck was dogging me so close, watching my every move. My neck and shoulder burned under the warmth of her breath at my back, and a sharp, wild anger goaded me forward.

He was almost done making his way around the semicircle. He reached the last pupil, and turned. But Madame stepped out in front of me, suddenly, her whole frame seeming to swell, her shawl spreading wide to eclipse me, to hide me from view. She knew my weakness, that moral paralysis that could fall on me in a crisis, that I would never shout or push or draw attention to myself. She hurried straight to her kinsman, talking volubly, seizing all his attention, rushing him towards the glass garden door. I think he looked over his shoulder. If I could have caught his eye, courage would have rushed in to aid my feeling, I think I would have run to him, and snatched that meeting for myself—but the room was all confusion, the semicircle broken into chattering groups, and my plain, small figure was lost among thirty far more conspicuous girls. Madame had her will. She got him out the door, and he never saw me. He thought I was absent. Five o’clock struck. The dismissal bell blared. The school emptied.

For some minutes I stood alone in that empty room, swallowed up in total darkness and distraction, a grief so inexpressible it felt as if all my life’s hope had been torn up by the roots right out of my chest. What was I to do? When a small child, the tiniest pupil in the whole school, trotted in, all innocence, straight into the middle of my silent, raging grief.

“Mademoiselle,” she lisped in her treble voice, “I have to give you this. M. Paul said I was to look for you all over the house, from the attic to the cellar, and when I found you, give this to you.”

She handed me a note, that small dove bringing back the olive leaf. There was no address, no name, only these words:
> It was not my intention to say farewell to you when I greeted the rest, for I hoped to find you in the classroom. I was disappointed. Our interview is deferred. Be ready for me. Before I sail, I must see you at leisure, and speak with you at length. Be ready. My moments are numbered, and at present they are all monopolized. I have a private business I will share with no one, not even you. — PAUL.

Be ready? That meant tonight. His ship sailed tomorrow, I was certain: I had seen the departure date advertised. Oh, I would be ready. But could that longed-for meeting ever actually happen? The time was so short, the schemers watching me so active, so hostile. The path to him was narrow as a gully, deep as a chasm, and Apollyon himself straddled it, breathing flames. Could my Paul, my Greatheart, reach me through all that?

Who could tell? But some small courage, some small comfort crept into me. I could feel the pulse of his heart, still beating true, matching every throb of mine.

I waited. The whole evening I waited, clinging to the promise of that little note, even as a heavy, cold dread settled over me, the kind of presentiment that is never wrong. The first hours dragged on slow as honey. They passed like drift cloud, like wrack scudding fast before a storm.

The long, hot summer day burned away like a Yule log. The crimson of the sunset faded. I was left in the cool blue shades of the classroom, bent over the pale ashen glow of the night outside.

Prayers were over. It was bedtime. All the other boarders had retired to their rooms. I still remained in the dark, empty first classe, disregarding rules I had never once broken before.

I paced the length of the room for hours, mechanically shoving benches and desks aside to make a clear path for myself. There, when I was sure the whole household was asleep, out of earshot, I finally let myself weep. I had kept my tears sealed, my sobs chained, for too long. They heaved my heart, tore their way out. What grief in this house could ever be sacred?

Just after eleven o’clock—an unheard of late hour in the Rue Fossette—the door opened, quietly but not stealthily. A lamp’s flame cut through the moonlight. Madame Beck walked in, her air as composed as if she were visiting at an ordinary, reasonable hour. Instead of speaking to me at once, she went to her desk, pulled out her keys, and pretended to rummage for something, lingering far too long over the feigned search. She was too calm. My frayed nerves could not stand the pretence. I had long since left all my usual old respects and fears behind me. I would bear no yoke, no curb, not now.

“It is far past time for bed,” she said. “You have broken the house rules far too long.”

I said nothing, did not stop my pacing. When she stepped in my way, I moved her gently but firmly aside.

“Let me persuade you to calm, Meess. Let me lead you to your chamber,” she said, trying to speak soft and soothing.

“No,” I said. “Neither you nor anyone else will persuade or lead me.”
