This is true, the poet sings, that a sorrow's crown of sorrow / Is remembering happier things.

On a fine evening near the end of July, Deronda rowed alone upon the Thames. It had been nearly a year since his return to England, a return marked by the formal conclusion of his education and the pressing expectation that he assume his station within the rigid architecture of English society. Though he had yielded to Sir Hugo's wishes, fencing off his potential idleness with the study of law, this decision had achieved nothing but to entrench his indecision deeper. Yet, now that he was in town with the Mallingers, his old passion for boating had surged with renewed vigor. Nowhere else could he secure the profound, still seclusion the river afforded. He possessed a boat of his own at Putney, and whenever Sir Hugo did not require his presence, it became his primary sanctuary to row until the sun surrendered the sky to the stars.

He was not in a sentimental phase, nor was he lost in the romantic haze of youth. Rather, he inhabited a contemplative state increasingly common among the young men of his generation: a skeptical inquiry into the utility of engaging in the world's great battles. Specifically, he questioned the value of participating in a struggle sustained by the unproductive labor of questioning, fueled by a mere three or five percent return on capital amassed by others' toil. This hesitation puzzled Sir Hugo, who found it baffling that a figure presenting such a striking contrast to the sickly, whining elements of society should be hamstrung by ideas that, to an accomplished Whig, appeared as spectral illusions. Especially since Deronda rejected authorship—a profession widely believed to convert foolish thinking into financial assets.

Clad in a dark-blue shirt and skull-cap, his curls closely cropped and his mouth framed by abundant, soft waves of beard, Deronda carried only disguised traces of the seraphic boy once described as trailing clouds of glory. Yet, even someone who had not seen him since childhood might regard him with a slow, dawning recognition, perhaps due to the peculiar intensity of the gaze Gwendolen had once termed "dreadful," though it possessed a genuinely mild, scrutinizing quality. His voice, occasionally audible in subdued fragments of song, proved to be merely a high baritone; indeed, observing his lithe, powerful frame and the firm gravity of his countenance would suffice for an experienced observer to deduce that he lacked the rare, ravishing tenor that nature reluctantly gifts at some sacrifice. Look at his hands: they were neither small and dimpled with tapering fingers suggesting a deprecating touch, nor were they clumsy. They were long, flexible, and firmly grasping, reminiscent of the hands Titian painted when aiming to depict the synthesis of refinement and raw force. There was a kinship, too, between the faces that belonged to such hands—in both, the uniform pale-brown skin, the perpendicular brow, and the calmly penetrating eyes. No longer seraphic, he was thoroughly terrestrial and manly; yet, he retained a quality capable of inspiring belief in a human dignity that can afford to recognize its poorer relations.

Such types appear sporadically amidst average conditions; consider, for instance, a workman whistling over a measurement, lifting his eyes to answer a query about the road. Often, the grand meanings embedded in faces, as well as in written words, reside chiefly in the impressions that happen to hold immediate importance relative to Deronda. Rowing on the Thames in very ordinary attire for a young Englishman at leisure, he passed beneath Kew Bridge with no thought of an adventure where his appearance might play a role. Indeed, he vehemently objected to the notion, which others had failed to allow him to dismiss, that his visage was inherently attention-grabbing. Hints of this, intended as compliments, resonated angrily within him, stemming from mixed experiences to which a clue had previously been provided. For many years, his reflection in the glass had been inextricably linked in his mind to thoughts of someone he must resemble—a figure regarding whose character and fate he continually wondered, yet never dared to inquire.

Between six and seven o'clock in the vicinity of Kew Bridge, the river was no longer a solitude. Several persons sauntered along the towing-path, and boats plied here and there. Deronda had rowed swiftly to bypass this congestion, only to guide his vessel aside and rest his oar within a couple of yards of the riverbank as a great barge advanced toward him. Throughout this maneuver, he unconsciously continued the low-toned chant that had haunted his throat all the way upstream—the gondolier's song from *Otello*, where Rossini worthily set to music the immortal words of Dante:

"Nessun maggior dolore / Che ricordarsi del tempo felice / Nella miseria."

And as he rested on his oar, the pianissimo fall of the melodic wail "nella miseria" drifted distinctly onto the water's brink. Three or four bystanders had paused to watch the barge pass the bridge, and undoubtedly, their glances included the young gentleman in the boat; yet, presumably, the low vocal sounds registered with greater significance to only one ear, cutting through the sum of current noises like an insect-murmur. Deronda, waiting for the barge, turned his head toward the riverside and spotted a figure a few yards distant that might have been an impersonation of the misery he was unconsciously voicing: a girl hardly older than eighteen, of low, slim stature, with a most delicate face. Her dark curls were pushed behind her ears beneath a large black hat, and a long woolen cloak draped her shoulders. Her hands hung down, clasped before her, her eyes fixed on the river with an immovable, statue-like despair.

This arresting stillness caused him to cease singing. Apparently, his voice had entered her inner world without her registering its origin, for when it abruptly stopped, she shifted her stance slightly. Looking around with a frightened glance, she met Deronda's face. It lasted but a couple of moments, yet felt like an eternity for two souls locked in mutual scrutiny. Her expression resembled that of a fawn or other gentle creature before it turns to flee: no blush, no specific alarm, only a timidity that nonetheless could not prevent her from sustaining a prolonged gaze before averting her eyes. In truth, it seemed to Deronda she was only half-conscious of her surroundings; was she starving, or was there some other cause for her bewilderment? A surge of interest and compassion leaped within him; yet, the next instant, she turned and walked away to a nearby bench beneath a tree. He had no right to linger and watch her; poorly dressed, melancholy women were common sights. It was only the delicate beauty, the picturesque lines and coloring of the image that were exceptional, rendering it all the more impossible for him to intrude his interest upon her.

He began to row away, soon finding himself far up the river, yet no other thoughts were vigorous enough to expel that pale image of unhappy girlhood. He fell repeatedly into speculating on the probable romance lurking behind that loneliness and look of desolation, then smiled at his own complicity in the prejudice that interesting faces must harbor interesting adventures, and finally justified his feeling that sorrow was more tragic when it befell delicate, childlike beauty.

"I should not have forgotten the look of misery if she had been ugly and vulgar," he muttered to himself. Yet, there was no denying that the attractiveness of the image ensured its persistence. It was clear to him as an onyx cameo: the brown-black drapery, the white face with minute features and dark, long-lashed eyes. His mind swept over the girl-tragedies unfolding in the world, hidden and unheeded, as if they were merely dramas of the copse or hedgerow, where the helpless dragged wounded wings forsakenly and stained the shadowed moss with the red moment-hand of their own death. Lately, in his solitary excursions, Deronda had been preoccupied chiefly with uncertainties regarding his own course. But these uncertainties, having ample leisure, tended to forge wide-sweeping connections with all life and history, allowing the new image of helpless sorrow to blend seamlessly with what seemed to him the strong array of reasons why he should recoil from entering the routine of the world that forces men to apologize for all its wrongdoing, treating opinions as mere professional equipment—and why he should not draw strongly at any thread in the hopelessly entangled scheme of things.

He used his oars sparingly, content to drift with the tide and be returned by it. It was his habit to indulge in that solemn passivity that naturally arises with lengthening shadows and mellow light, when thinking and desiring melt together imperceptibly, transforming arguments into passionate visions. By the time the tide carried him back past Richmond Bridge, the sun was nearing its setting. The approach of his favored hour—with its deepening stillness and darkening masses of trees and buildings caught between the double glow of sky and river—disposed him to linger as if they were an unfinished strain of music. He sought a perfectly solitary spot where he could moor his boat against the bank, throw himself backward with his head propped on the cushions, and watch the fading light of sunset and the opening of that bead-roll which some Oriental poet describes as God's call to the little stars, each answering, "Here am I."

He selected a location in the bend of the river directly opposite Kew Gardens, where a vast expanse of water reflected the sky's glory while he remained in shadow. Lying with his hands behind his head, propped level with the boat's edge, he could see all around yet remain unseen by anyone a few yards distant. For a long while, he never turned his eyes from the view directly ahead.
