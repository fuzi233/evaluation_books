The crisp, leftover tang of Carnival confetti and spiced fried pastries still clung to Florence’s cobblestones on the morning of February 27th, when the hands of the communal clocks neared ten. Ordinarily at that hour, the city’s foot traffic would split itself between the clatter of the Mercato Vecchio, the lines outside the Duomo, and the stalls lining the Arno embankment. That day, every current of passengers swept decidedly toward San Marco. All Florence knew a second grand Bonfire of the Vanities was being stacked in the piazza before the Palazzo Vecchio that very morning—pagan love poems, pearl-edged veils, chess sets, perfumes, all the fripperies Fra Girolamo had denounced as snares of the devil—but no one was hurrying there first. The entire pulse of the populace drew toward San Marco.
The Piazza di San Marco was packed shoulder to shoulder, the only movement rippling outward from new arrivals shoving their way through every narrow alley mouth feeding into the square. The front ranks stood pressed tight against a rough semicircle of wooden barriers strung up before the church doors, their weight steady enough to resist the crush of the crowd surging from behind. Inside those barriers, the black-robed Dominican brethren of San Marco filed into place, arranging their psalters on the stands set aside for the choral office. But the temporary wooden pulpit jutted out over the church’s main entrance sat empty.
It would be occupied at any moment by the man the Pope had formally banished from every Florentine pulpit, the figure all other local clergy were forbidden to speak to, the preacher the city’s citizens were threatened with excommunication for even hearing. This man had openly declared that the wicked, simoniac Pope who had bought the chair of Saint Peter with gold was no true Vicar of Christ: that his curses were broken swords, hollows hilts with no blade fixed to them, that his commands that ran counter to godly living were not just permissible to disobey, but *forbidden* to obey. And still the crowds flocked to hear him in his own church, still lined up to take Communion from his excommunicated hands, even as Rome threatened to lay all Florence under interdict, strangle its wool trade, and turn every hostile Italian state against the republic if it did not hand the schismatic over to be “corrected” in Rome.
For the mass of Florentines, the central question hung heavy in the February air: what if this ragged friar held far more sway over heaven’s lightnings than the silk-robed pontiff in Vatican palaces? They had no use for the Frate’s own rigorous test of spiritual truth, no appetite for parsing whether his words aligned with the highest divine law. They wanted a sign. As long as belief in Savonarola had brought only hope of safety and reform, no miracle was needed: his preaching was a rousing march rhythm the people had been happy to step to. But now that clinging to his cause meant immediate ruin for their commerce, the shattering of their republic’s alliances, a city-wide interdict that would doom every soul who dwelt within its walls to the risk of damnation, the fatal, unspoken question swelled louder every day: “What miracle can you show us?”
That demand had hummed in Savonarola’s ears for months, growing faster and faster, drawing first his public promise that a clear sign would come at the appointed hour, then his own private, wavering faith that the great, world-altering work he had begun was far too precious to the Divine will for God to let it falter. His faith shifted, as all mortal faith does, but he did not waver in his speech: it is the fate of every man who speaks to satisfy a crowd to draw courage from yesterday’s conviction, hoping it will return to him on the morrow.
The makeshift pulpit over the church door had been erected expressly to answer the populace’s growing impatience for supernatural proof of the Frate’s mission. While the ordinary black-mantled friars settled into their places, the crowd’s gazes were not yet fixed on that empty platform. Most were scanning the ranks of monks, picking out familiar faces: the sons of old patrician Florentine houses, the young men whose wool-carder fathers, baker brothers, and artisan cousins stood right there in the crowd around them. It was only when the last brother had taken his place, when they had rustled the pages of their psalters and begun the low, droning chant, that the crowd whispered to one another: “Fra Girolamo is coming now.”
That expectation, more than the hypnotic pull of the psalmody, spread a quiet, pale solemn light over the upturned sea of faces, all at last turned toward the empty pulpit. The next instant, it was no longer empty. A figure swathed from crown to toe in black cowl and mantle had stepped over its threshold, kneeling with his head bowed, his face turned away from the crowd. The minutes dragged for the throng of eager onlookers as the black figure knelt and the chant rolled on, but no one dared break the stillness: the Frate’s private audiences with Heaven still crackled with enough electric awe that even the men in the crowd who had come ready to stone him felt their arms go heavy, their anger drained out of them.
Then a ripple ran through the crowd, every man brushing his neighbor like a quivering aspen leaf, the way crowds move when they have been staring at the sky waiting for some promised portent and finally see it unfurl. The Frate had risen, turned to face the people, and pushed his cowl half back. The monotonous hum of psalmody cut off entirely, and for everyone within reach of the pulpit, the lingering sound of chant melted entirely into the sharp, burning weight of Savonarola’s gaze as he swept it over the silent piazza.
Then he stretched out his hands: exquisitely fine, delicate hands, that looked less like ordinary flesh made for grasping, and more like vessels of feeling so acute they had no use for gross, physical touch. They rose like a gentle, pleading speech from the softest part of his soul, hidden behind the harsh, passionate face carved far deeper with lines around the brow and mouth than forty-four years of ordinary life could ever leave.
At the first stretch of those hands, the people in the front ranks dropped to their knees, and here and there a devout follower knelt further back in the throng. The majority stood firm, though: some fighting the impulse to bow before an excommunicated man, half-convinced God might strike him dead mid-blessing, others burning with scorn for what they saw as a cheap, staged performance by an ambitious deceiver—yet all of them feeling powerless to resist the spell that hung over the crowd, as they would be powerless to push back against the tide of a city-wide craze.
Then his voice came: clear, low at first, uttering the opening words of absolution: *“Misereatur vestri.”* More knees hit the cobblestones. It rose higher, clearer, until at the final blessing, *“Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus,”* it burst forth in a ringing, masculine cry, as if fighting its way out from under the grip of a demon that sought to stifle it. It boomed like a trumpet call all the way to the far edges of the piazza, and under that sound, every head bowed.
When the blessing was done, Savonarola himself sank to his knees, hiding his face in his mantle, drained to the bone. These great surges of raw emotion were the very air he breathed: he had told the people long ago that he could not live without preaching. But that life was eating him apart, shattering his strength one great effort at a time.
A few minutes later, a handful of people had gotten back to their feet, but far more remained kneeling, every face fixed intently on him as he lifted a clear crystal vessel holding the consecrated Host. He spoke, his voice cutting through the stillness to remind them of the vow he had made three days prior: that when he held the Sacrament aloft before all of them, he had bid them pray that if his work was not truly from God, the Lord would send fire to consume him on the spot, to vanish into eternal darkness rather than lead his people astray with falsehood. “I beseech you to pray that prayer again now,” he said, “pray it this very instant.”
No one dared not to. The whole piazza held its breath as they heard Savonarola’s quiet, distinct voice rise again, cutting through the wide stillness: “Lord, if I have not wrought in utter sincerity, if my word does not come from Thee, strike me down this instant with Thy thunder, let the fires of Thy wrath wrap around me.”
He went silent, standing perfectly motionless, the consecrated Mystery held high in his hand, his eyes lifted to the pale grey February sky, a faint, quivering excitement thrumming through every line of his frame. The crowd was equally still, equally silent, as thin streaks of sunlight began to poke through the overcast, gilding patches of the convent wall and making more than one awe-struck spectator flinch, half-convinced they would see lightning crack down that very second.
Then the clouds pulled apart wider, soft and sudden as a smile, and a flood of warm, golden sunlight poured down. It hit the crystal vessel first, turning it to a sphere of glowing light, before spilling over Savonarola’s face, wrapping it in a mild, unearthly glow.
An instantaneous shout burst from a thousand throats, echoing off the church walls: “Behold the answer!”
The warm radiance seeped through Savonarola’s bones, and so did that shout. It was his last moment of unclouded triumph. In that rush of rapturous confidence, he saw a vision of a grander stage still to come, a crowd drawn from every corner of Christendom gathered before him, where he would be openly anointed as the messenger of supreme righteousness, armed full with divine strength. The vision lasted no more than a heartbeat. While the shout was still ringing in his ears, he turned and stepped back into the dimness of the church, the strain too great for his fraying strength to bear a second longer.
The moment he vanished, the sunlight lost its unearthly glow, spreading evenly over cobblestones, beggars’ rags, and the polished mantles of patricians alike. The crowd began to mill, shifting restlessly, and a confusion of voices rose up: sharp arguments, snickers, cynical laughter, all making plain that the hostility and scorn that had been cowed into silence by the momentary spell were bubbling back to the surface.
“Seems the cheers are already giving way to bickering,” Tito Melema remarked, leaning against the stone balustrade of an upper loggia in the house directly opposite the church, where he had watched the whole scene unfold with sharp, attentive eyes. “Still, it was a striking spectacle, eh, Messer Pietro? Fra Girolamo is exactly the sort of man to make you understand why once upon a time, a monk’s frock was a symbol of power over men’s minds, not a fool’s excuse to rummage through the keys of a rich woman’s wardrobe.”
“Assuredly,” Pietro Cennini replied, his face lined with quiet thought. “Until I see proof that Fra Girolamo has far less fear of God’s judgments than the common run of men, rather than far more, I will never believe he would dare to brave Heaven in this way if his soul was laden with a deliberate, conscious lie.”
