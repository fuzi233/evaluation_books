Summer draped Villette in sweltering heat, and with it came fever—little Georgette, the youngest of Madame Beck’s brood, fell ill. Désirée, miraculously recovered from her own ailments, was whisked away to the countryside with Fifine, a precaution against contagion. The situation demanded a physician, but Madame, with deliberate disregard for the returned Dr. Pillule, summoned his English rival instead.

Soon, whispers of malaise spread among the pupils—headaches, lethargy—symptoms mild yet suggestive. *Surely now*, I thought, *Madame will relent. She would never risk the propriety of young Dr. John attending the girls.* But Madame Beck was not one to be dictated to by convention. To my astonishment, she ushered him into the sanctum of the school quarters, placing him at the bedside of the haughty Blanche de Melcy and her coquettish shadow, Angélique.

Dr. John accepted the charge with a quiet satisfaction, his demeanor so measured that one might almost believe the arrangement unremarkable. Almost. But this was Labassecour, where convents and confessionals bred suspicion, and the presence of a man—young, handsome—in a pensionnat de demoiselles was a scandal waiting to unfurl. It did. Gossip rippled through the school, the kitchen, the town itself. Letters of protest arrived; parents stormed in, bristling with indignation.

Yet Madame did not falter. She was a woman who could wield charm like a rapier, disarming outrage with a laugh, a shrug, those plump hands clasped in mock innocence. *"Ce pauvre Docteur Jean!"* she would sigh, eyes twinkling. *"The best of men! My own children adore him—what could I do?"* Blanche and Angélique, obliging as ever, sang his praises in dulcet tones, and soon the other pupils joined the chorus, declaring they’d have no other doctor. The parents, disarmed, departed laughing.

But why? Why gamble her reputation for him? The answer, according to the whispers, was obvious: *She means to marry him.* The notion took root, watered by Madame’s sudden attention to her appearance—the morning dishevelment abandoned, replaced by silk dresses and neat boots, her auburn braids arranged just so. She was not beautiful, but she was *pleasing*, a woman of warmth and wit, her laughter never stale. Did she dream of a shared future, her savings smoothing his path? And he—did he guess her hopes? I had seen him emerge from her presence once, lips curled in a half-smile, eyes alight with the gratification of a man who knows himself admired.

Yet for all his charm, Dr. John was not flawless. If he encouraged what he did not intend to honor, then he was crueler than I had thought. But perhaps he *did* intend it. He had no fortune, they said, only his profession. And Madame, though older, was the sort of woman who defied time.

The mystery deepened one morning when Georgette, feverish and fretful, wailed for relief. I waited for Dr. John, certain he would come—his visits were swift, his step light on the stairs. But minutes passed, and he did not appear. Descending, I found the corridor empty. Where had he gone?

Then I heard it—a laugh, bright and girlish, from the portress’s cabinet. The door stood ajar. Inside, Rosine, the pretty, frivolous grisette, trilled a French tune as she trimmed a cap, bathed in sunlight. No one else was there. Yet moments before, I had heard a man’s voice, low and urgent: *"For God’s sake!"* And then Dr. John had emerged, his face a portrait of turmoil—not anger, but something raw, wounded.

What had transpired? Rosine, with her mercenary whims, seemed an unlikely tormentor. Yet there she sat, humming, while he bore the look of a man unjustly stricken.

Upstairs, I found him beside Georgette’s crib, Madame fretting over his health. *"He looks ill,"* I ventured, watching him stiffen. For the first time, he seemed to *see* me—really see me—when I spoke in English. But he said nothing.

After he left, Madame sank into his vacant chair, her face hardening. Before the mirror, she plucked a single white hair from her brow and sighed—a sound of such quiet defeat that I pitied her. Disappointment had come knocking, and she, for all her strength, was not immune.

As for Rosine? I studied her all that day, seeking the secret of her power. Pretty, yes. Young, certainly. But enough to unravel a man like Dr. John? If so, the world was a stranger place than I knew.

Madame, at least, did not crumble. She had sense, purpose, pride. And though love—or the specter of it—had challenged her, she met it as she did all things: with a steel spine.

*Brava, Madame Beck.* You faced your Apollyon and prevailed.
