"Well, I suppose we must." This was the reluctant verdict reached by friends in council regarding the upcoming social obligation.

Mr. Thornton had engaged in a spirited campaign to elevate his mother to a sufficient level of civic engagement. Though she rarely ventured forth, her occasional outings were conducted with the gravitas of a queen on state business. For these solemn occasions, John had procured a carriage, yet Mrs. Thornton steadfastly refused to allow him to maintain horses for it. Instead, they were merely hired for the duration of the visit, ensuring that the cost remained a temporary burden rather than a permanent liability. Having utilized borrowed steeds for three days prior, she had successfully "killed off" every acquaintance within reach, leaving them with no choice but to reciprocate with equal expenditure and effort. However, the destination in question, Mr. Hale's residence, lay too distant for foot travel. Consequently, Mrs. Thornton had pressed her son repeatedly on whether his desire to call upon the Hales outweighed the necessity of hiring a cab. She expressed a profound relief if the trip proved unnecessary; indeed, she questioned the utility of cultivating intimacy with the teachers and masters of Milton, fearing that soon she would be summoned to visit the spouse of Fanny's dancing master.

"And so I would, Mother," John countered gently, "if Mr. Mason and his wife were friendless in a strange land, like the Hales."

"Oh! Do not speak so hastily!" she chided. "I am going tomorrow. I only wished to ensure you understood the arrangement precisely."

"If you are going tomorrow, I shall order the horses immediately."

"Nonsense, John. One would think you were made of pure money."

"Not entirely, yet. Nevertheless, I am resolved. Recall the last occasion you rode in a cab; you returned home suffering from a severe headache caused by the jolting motion."

"I never complained of it, I assure you."

"No! My mother is not inclined toward complaints," he declared, a note of pride coloring his voice. "Precisely because you do not complain, I feel compelled to look after you. Consider, however, the case of Fanny there; a touch of hardship would prove beneficial to her constitution."

"She is not forged from the same metal as you, John," she retorted softly. "She could not withstand it."

Mrs. Thornton fell silent thereafter, her final remark striking a chord that mortified her. She harbored an unconscious disdain for a fragile spirit, particularly when contrasted with the resilience inherent in both herself and her son. Fanny lacked strength in the very areas where her parents excelled. Mrs. Thornton was not a woman given to prolonged introspection or debate; her sharp judgment and iron will acted as a substitute for lengthy self-discussion. Instinctively, she perceived that nothing could fortify Fanny to endure trials patiently or confront obstacles with courage. Although this admission caused her a pang of regret, it transformed into a specific, pitying tenderness toward her daughter—a demeanor akin to how a mother treats a frail or sickly child. To an unobservant stranger, Mrs. Thornton's behavior toward her children might appear to indicate a far greater devotion to Fanny than to John. Such an interpretation, however, would have been profoundly mistaken. The boldness with which mother and son articulated unpalatable truths to one another revealed a deep, mutual reliance on the solid core of their respective souls. In contrast, the tender, uneasy affectation Mrs. Thornton displayed toward Fanny, coupled with her shameful desire to conceal her daughter's poverty behind the grand virtues she possessed and highly valued in others, exposed a lack of a secure foundation for her maternal affection. She never addressed her son by any name save "John"; terms of endearment like "love" and "dear" were exclusively reserved for Fanny. Yet, her heart offered thanks to God for her son day and night, and she strode proudly among the women of Milton solely for his sake.

"Fanny dear!" Mrs. Thornton announced suddenly, breaking the silence. "I shall have horses attached to the carriage today to visit these Hales. Pray, go and see Nurse? She lives in the same direction and delights in your company. You could proceed there while I attend upon Mrs. Hale."

"Oh, Mamma, it is such a long distance, and I am so weary," Fanny protested.

"With what?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her brow furrowing slightly.

"I do not know—the weather, I suppose. It feels so oppressive. Could you not bring Nurse here, Mamma? The carriage could collect her, and she could spend the remainder of the day here, which I am certain she would appreciate."

Mrs. Thornton offered no immediate reply. She laid her embroidery down upon the table and appeared to deliberate silently.

"It will be a considerable journey for her to walk back at night," she finally remarked.

"Oh, but I will dispatch her home in a cab. I had not considered her walking."

Just as the conversation turned practical, Mr. Thornton entered the room, moments before departing for the mill.

"Mother! I scarcely need remind you that if there exists any minor convenience that could serve Mrs. Hale, who is an invalid, you will certainly offer it."

"If I can discover what suits her needs, I shall. However, having never suffered illness myself, I am somewhat unfamiliar with the whims of invalids."

"Well! Here stands Fanny, who is perpetually afflicted by some ailment. She ought to be able to suggest something, perhaps—would you not, Fan?"

"I do not always suffer from ailments," Fanny sniffed pettishly. "And I am not accompanying Mamma. I have a headache today, and I shall not venture outdoors."

Mr. Thornton's expression darkened with annoyance. His mother's gaze was fixed intently on her needlework, where she stitched away with industrious fervor.

"Fanny! I require you to go," he stated authoritatively. "It will benefit you rather than harm you. Please consider it done, and do not expect me to repeat this request."

He departed abruptly, leaving the room. Had he lingered even a moment longer, Fanny likely would have burst into tears at the commanding tone of his voice, even when phrased as "You will oblige me." As it stood, she merely muttered under her breath.

"John always speaks as if I imagine I am ill, and I am positive I do not harbor such delusions. Who, pray, are these Hales that he creates such a commotion over?"

"Fanny, do not speak of your brother in that manner," her mother urged gently. "He possesses valid reasons, or he would not insist upon our visit. Hasten and prepare yourself."

However, the brief friction between her son and daughter did not soften Mrs. Thornton's disposition toward "these Hales." A jealous spark ignited within her heart, echoing Fanny's whispered query: "Who are they, that he is so anxious we should pay them all this attention?" It lingered like a burden in a song, long after Fanny had forgotten the grievance amidst the delightful excitement of admiring her new bonnet in the mirror.

Mrs. Thornton was inherently shy. Only in recent years had she found leisure to engage in society, and therein, she found little joy. While she derived satisfaction from hosting dinners and critiquing the culinary arts of others, the act of forming acquaintances with strangers was a distinctly different ordeal. She felt ill at ease, appearing sterner and more forbidding than usual as she crossed the threshold into the Hales' modest drawing-room.

Margaret Hale was occupied with embroidering a small piece of cambric intended for an article of dress for Edith's unborn child—"Flimsy, useless work," Mrs. Thornton murmured to herself. She preferred Mrs. Hale's double knitting, finding it more sensible and substantial. The room, in its entirety, seemed cluttered with trinkets that required extensive dusting; for people of limited means, time was synonymous with currency.

As Mrs. Thornton delivered her stately greetings and uttered the standard pleasantries that blind the senses, Margaret responded with increasing effort, captivated by the sheer antiquity of the lace Mrs. Thornton wore. "Lace," Margaret later noted to Dixon, "of that ancient English point which has not been fashioned for seventy years, nor purchased commercially. It must have been an heirloom, proving that she descended from ancestors of consequence." Thus, the possessor of such ancestral treasures became worthy of more than the languid exertion required to be agreeable, which would otherwise have constrained Mrs. Hale's conversational range. Soon, Margaret, straining her brain to converse with Fanny, overheard her mother and Mrs. Thornton descend into the interminable topic of domestic service.

"I suppose you are not musical," Fanny observed, noting the absence of a piano. "For I see no instrument."

"I have a fondness for listening to fine music; unfortunately, I cannot play well myself. Furthermore, neither Papa nor Mama cares greatly for it, so we sold our old piano upon arriving here."

"I wonder how you manage existence without one. It almost seems to me a fundamental necessity of life."

"Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved from the remainder!" Margaret thought to herself. "Yet she must have been very young. She likely forgot her own youthful experiences, though she must recall those days." A subtle chill permeated Margaret's manner when she next spoke.

"You have excellent concerts here, I presume."
