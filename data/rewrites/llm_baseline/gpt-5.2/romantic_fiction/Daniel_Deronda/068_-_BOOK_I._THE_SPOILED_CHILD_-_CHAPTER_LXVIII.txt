Deronda went again to Ezra Cohen’s lodging with a resolve that had the steadiness of hunger: not the coarse hunger that makes a man swallow without tasting, but that finer craving which sharpens the senses and will not be put off with trifles. Hans had spoken with honest bluntness—Mirah needed relief from jealousy—and the words had worked in Deronda like heat in dough, making what had been folded and hidden begin to rise and insist on its own shape.

If Mirah would take his love, he could meet the rest—her father’s perilous nearness, Ezra’s fragile peace, whatever disputes and humiliations might come—as a man meets weather when he has a roof to build for another. He had watched Lapidoth these days without seeing open signs of fresh turbulence; yet foreboding lingered with him like an undertone in music, something you might not name but could not stop hearing. He felt there would be a struggle, a mortification, some intolerable wrenching of domestic quiet; and that he might be called to save Ezra and Mirah from being helpless under it.

He did not know what was gathering in Lapidoth’s mind.

To Ezra, Lapidoth’s restlessness was a small daily torment—the shifting attention, the half-listening, the perpetual sense of a man only waiting for his own moment to escape. But to Lapidoth it was worse than torment: it was restraint, the clipped wing, submission made barely tolerable by the prospect of a later, better liberty. He had come at first meaning to wait for a chance of money—a real chance, some considerable sum from Deronda; meanwhile he watched with the stealthy curiosity of appetite, trying to learn where Mirah kept her keys, where she laid her earnings. The gambling desire in him was like a restless animal that never slept; it ran under every other thought and made a web of imagining that caught the world and dragged it into its service.

Yet Mirah, practical and clear-eyed, had defended the promise she had given Ezra. She kept by her only what she must spend at once, and carried the rest to Mrs. Meyrick’s care, as if her coins were loaves to be stored in a neighbor’s safe cupboard where a hungry hand could not reach. Lapidoth found himself supplied in kind—food, shelter, attendance—with the irritating completeness of a place arranged to defeat him, like an asylum where every sharp edge has been covered.

He felt injured by it. If he could once have taken what he wanted without being forced to ask, he would have called it no disgrace. People who boast their delicacy often draw their line of liberty with other people’s property in a way that is curiously precise until law begins; and Lapidoth’s mind made fine distinctions, as if a theft at home were only a kind of family tasting, like taking an extra slice from the tart before it is served. He had had of Mirah’s apple-tart when she brought it in with shy dutifulness; why should he not have of her other earnings? Still, he remained outwardly submissive; the strongest temptation in him was not to wrangle with Mirah, but to appeal to Deronda.

He had learned, as many clever men with nothing else to sell learn, that absence can be priced. He imagined his family would breathe more freely without him, and that Deronda might be willing to pay handsomely for the comfort of getting rid of him. Yet even his hardihood had a seam of fear: Deronda’s quiet authority, his look that seemed to see without staring, had kept Lapidoth from venturing too soon.

But this day Deronda came with a light in him that could not help showing itself—an amenity as unmistakable as the way a kitchen changes when the bread is nearly done, warm, expanded, ready to be broken and shared. Lapidoth was at a crisis of discontent and longing; Deronda’s new brightness fed his schemes, and his mind became so occupied that he could not even keep up his usual show of interest. Presently he went out, saying he would smoke and walk in the square.

The room grew easier at once, as if an ill-tuned instrument had been taken away and the remaining notes could ring true.

Mirah was not at home, but she was expected soon. Deronda’s eyes kept turning—half unconsciously—to the door, with a secret anticipation that made his pulse quicken. He fancied that when she came he would see on her face some sweetness of recognition that had been hidden from him before, as flavor is hidden until the right moment of heat brings it forth.

He sat with Ezra among the books and papers in that double room which was still too narrow for Ezra’s breath. The air lay heavy—London’s close heat, holding the smell of stone, dust, and old cloth—broken now and then by the faint drift of outdoor sounds: wheels on the square’s hard edge, a distant call, the thin song of a bird that persisted as if it would stitch something living into the city’s dull fabric. Deronda, sensitive to these small acoustics, felt how the room pressed on Ezra—not only with its walls but with that muffled urban roar which never fully ceased, a low industrial tide that modern life keeps rolling even into sickrooms.

“This little room is too close for you, Ezra,” he said, breaking off from the reading. “Our week’s heat is worse than Genoa’s, where one can sit in the shaded coolness of large rooms. You must have a better home now. I shall do as I like with you, being the stronger half.” There was play in his voice, but under it a firm tenderness.

Ezra answered, “I am straitened for nothing except breath. But you, who might be in a spacious palace with the wide green country round you, find this a narrow prison. Nevertheless, I cannot say, ‘Go.’”

“Oh, the country would be banishment while you are here,” Deronda said, rising and walking what little promenade the room allowed, fanning himself with his handkerchief. “This is the happiest room in the world to me. Besides, I will imagine myself in the East, since I am getting ready to go there some day. Only I will not wear a cravat and a heavy ring there.”

He paused, and with an emphatic gesture unfastened the cravat and slipped off the ring he had been wearing, laying both upon a small table behind Ezra—dark mahogany that showed the ring’s pale fire as plainly as a drop of oil on a cutting-board. “I’ve worn my memorable ring ever since I came home,” he went on, reseating himself. “But I’m such a Sybarite that I constantly put it off as a burden when I’m doing anything. I can understand why the Romans had summer rings—if they had them. Now then—I shall get on better.”

They returned to their work. Deronda read a piece of rabbinical Hebrew and Ezra corrected and commented, their voices making a kind of inward music against the city’s outer din. In that exchange there was also something more—the sense of a language carrying centuries in its consonants and breath, and of how easily, when no one listens, a tongue can die like a song drowned by machinery. Deronda felt the words in his mouth with care, aware how meaning is never fully carried over, how what Ezra heard in them could not be translated whole.

When Lapidoth re-entered, they scarcely noticed at first. He took a seat somewhat in the background. But his rambling eyes did not ramble long before they found the ring, detached and within reach, throwing back a small, tempting sparkle.

During his walk his mind had been busy with a fiction—an opening abroad needing ready money; he would communicate it to Deronda privately; Deronda would ask, inevitably, how much; and there lay the difficult art—ask too much and you risk refusal, ask too little and regret gnaws you. Lapidoth’s desire had no limit, and he had no measure of Deronda’s willingness.

Now, in the midst of that airy calculation, the ring lay before him, solid and immediate. Its value was far below the sums he had been balancing, but it was here, ready for a hand. Desire leapt, not yet into intention but into pictured act: pocket it, go out, and present restraint would be ended at once. He told himself it would be safe. Any property of Deronda’s, available without formal consent, was—so his mind argued—one with his children’s property; and no child would prosecute a father. He had never committed larceny; but larceny was what law punished, and this, he insisted inwardly, was no larceny—only taking from one who would have given more if pressed.

He rejected it—again. He would go below, he decided, and watch for Deronda’s departure; then ask to join him in his walk and boldly carry out the plan of a larger gift. He rose and stood looking out of the window; but what he saw, like a refrain repeating itself, was not the square—it was the short passage to the door by the table where the ring lay.

He meant to go down. And yet—without a distinct change of will, rather by the dominance of desire, like the drunkard’s thirst that overtakes resolution—when he passed the table his fingers fell noiselessly on the ring. The action was so quick it seemed to have happened before it was chosen. He found himself in the passage with the ring in his hand.

After that the rest followed as naturally as a man putting on his coat when he has decided to leave. He took his hat and quitted the house. The possibility of returning to throw himself again upon Ezra and Mirah receded, becoming a vague future that might never arrive. Before he was even out on the square, his haste had narrowed to one urgent line: sell the ring, get on shipboard, be free.

Deronda and Ezra were only just aware of his exit; nothing more.

By-and-by Mirah came in and made a real interruption—quiet footfall, the faint rustle of her dress, the cooler breath of outdoors following her as if she carried a small pocket of air with her. She had not taken off her hat, and there was a look on her face like someone who has been hurrying with an errand and does not know whether she brings help or trouble. When Deronda rose and advanced to shake hands, she said, with a confusion that was at once unaccountable and vexing to herself, “I only came in to see that Ezra had his new draught. I must go directly to Mrs. Meyrick’s to fetch something.”

“Pray allow me to walk with you,” Deronda said, urgency breaking through his usual restraint. “I must not tire Ezra any further; besides my brains are melting. I want to go to Mrs. Meyrick’s. May I go with you?”

“Oh—yes,” Mirah answered, blushing more, feeling something altered in him, as one feels a change in a room not by sight but by the way the sounds fall—his voice held a new warmth, a new nearness, as if it had moved closer to her ear. She turned away to pour out Ezra’s draught. Ezra, meanwhile, threw back his head with his eyes shut, still full of the ideas the reading had stirred—old words, old griefs, and the ache of meanings that cannot be laid into another language without losing their living pulse.

Deronda stood for a moment thinking of nothing but the walk with her, as if the world had narrowed to the short distance between Ezra’s lodging and the Meyricks’ door. Then Mirah turned round again with the draught; and suddenly he remembered his cravat. “Pray excuse my dishabille—I did not mean you to see it,” he said, and went to the little table to take up the cravat.

His hand found only the cloth.

A violent impulse of surprise seized him. “Good heavens—where is my ring gone?” He began searching on the floor, as if it must have rolled into shadow and lodged itself where the patterned carpet could hide its glint.

Ezra looked round the corner of his chair.

Mirah, quick as thought, went near where Deronda was seeking. “Did you lay it down?”

“Yes,” Deronda answered, still visited only by the idea that it had fallen. He moved small pieces of furniture, peered under edges, searched in places where reason said it could not be, but the eye still hopes.

Another explanation had come to Mirah like a chill. The color left her cheeks. She bent to Ezra’s ear and whispered, “Was my father here?”

Ezra bent his head in reply. Their eyes met—his full of terrible understanding, hers of dread that became certainty.

She darted back to Deronda, who still cast his eyes down in that hopeless exploration we persist in after we know we have already examined in vain. “You have not found it?” she said, hurriedly.

He met her frightened gaze and caught alarm from it. “I perhaps put it in my pocket,” he said, feeling there, trying—by that small feint—to spare her.

She watched him, and her voice sharpened with a painful clearness that would not let him pretend. “It is not there?—you put it on the table.” And immediately she rushed out of the room.

Deronda followed. She had gone into the sitting-room below, looking for her father, opening the bedroom door, glancing where his hat usually hung—searching with the swiftness of someone trying to outrun a shame that is already upon her. Then she turned, clasping her hands tight, lips pale, and stared out of the window as if the square might show him vanishing among strangers’ footsteps and wheels.

She looked up at Deronda. She could not utter a word. The look itself seemed her confession, her submission to humiliation in his presence—an unspoken “This is what I come from; this is what I cannot prevent.”

Deronda did not speak at once. He took her clasped hands between both his with a tenderness that was like reverence, as if he held something sacred and wounded. His voice, when it came, had the depth of a vow.

“Mirah—let me think that he is my father as well as yours—that we can have no sorrow, no disgrace, no joy apart. I will rather take your grief to be mine than I would take the brightest joy of another woman. Say you will not reject me—say you will take me to share all things with you. Say you will promise to be my wife—say it now. I have been in doubt so long—I have had to hide my love so long. Say that now and always I may prove to you that I love you with complete love.”

The change in Mirah did not come like a door flung open. It was gradual, as warmth spreads through a cold room, as broth turns from mere hot water into something that sustains. At first she felt only solemn comfort, attributing Deronda’s goodness to his feeling for Ezra—his loyalty to the house as a whole, not to her alone. But as his words went on, the assurance of an unhoped-for good took possession of her frame, and the shame that had made her white began to melt under a new radiance.

Her face glowed as he bent over her; yet her eyes kept their intense gravity—the same grave gratitude with which she had once acknowledged that he had thought her “worthy of the best.” When he had finished, she could say nothing. Speech, with its public weights and its imperfect translations, failed her. She only lifted her lips to his and kissed them—simply, as if that were the plainest “yes” she could give.

They stood then only looking at each other, his hands still holding hers—too happy to move, meeting so fully in their new consciousness that any other sign might have seemed to set them apart again. The city’s distant noise went on outside, indifferent and grinding; but in that small domestic space, where humiliation had just entered, love had made a shelter.

At last Mirah whispered, “Let us go and comfort Ezra.”
