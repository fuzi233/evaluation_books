The grey twilight of the Arno offered no comfort, only a suffocating blanket of indifference. On the opposite bank, where the city walls retreated to leave a desolate expanse of warehouses and granaries, a solitary figure sought refuge in the damp. This was Baldassarre, driven not by commerce or crime, but by a hollow, gnawing desperation. He sat amidst the long, waterlogged grass, bending over the swift, channelled flow of the river, his eyes fixed on the water as if it held the answers to a prayer he could no longer articulate.

His presence there was born of a singular, irrational hope. Years prior, a kind-hearted runlet—a small boatman—had occasionally tossed him scraps of bread, raw carrots, and apple parings. These fleeting acts of charity had created a habit of vigilance; Baldassarre returned daily to this secluded spot, hoping for another miracle. It was preferable to the silent, humiliating begging on the church-steps beyond the Porta San Frediano. Baldassarre despised the act of begging with a visceral hatred; he would have welcomed death a thousand times over than to lower his head and plead for alms. Yet, he lived. Why? Because a vague, formless possibility had taken root in his mind, clinging to him like a parasite. It was the same futile hope that drives a woman to stare at a horizon, waiting for a ship known to be lost for decades.

After his release from the convent hospital of San Miniato, where the monks had tended him during his period of total incapacity, Baldassarre had searched for the wife who was meant to be his anchor. She had vanished, swallowed by the pestilence that seemed to rot the very air of Florence. Without her, and stripped of his knife, the instrument of his intended vengeance, he was reduced to a shattered, bewildered relic of a man. He was too physically broken to earn a living, too mentally fractured to devise a plan, even if he possessed the blade. Yet, he wanted to live. He waited for something undefined, a phantom stimulus that caused his heart to hammer against his ribs with the intensity of waking from a nightmare.

While Baldassarre sat in the mud, plotting a future that existed only in his fevered imagination, the Compagnacci executed their own grim agenda. Left largely unchecked by a paralyzed Signoria, these bands of armed mercenaries split into two converging streams. One mass headed for the Ponte Rubaconte, the other for the Ponte Vecchio, united by a single, brutal directive: to sack the house on the Via de' Bardi. The common rabble required no explanation; the thrill of destruction was sufficient fuel. The leaders, however, understood the tactical precision of their mission. They knew exactly where to converge, anticipating finding the master of the house asleep in his bed, vulnerable and unsuspecting.

Tito, the master, was not sleeping. The events of the previous day had ignited a panic in him that sleep could not quell. Investigations loomed, delays threatened, and a pervasive dread of danger permeated his thoughts. While falsehoods flourished in Florence, fear had grown alongside them, evolving from a specific terror of Baldassarre into an undying, habitual paralysis. Tito felt unsafe within the city's confines. He was preparing to flee. His household was in motion: Maso was handing over the keys to the new occupant, horses awaited in San Gallo, and his family was being readied in the Borgo outside the gates.

Dressed in his finest attire, nearly as resplendent as the day he had first walked into this house and made the fatal error of loving Romola, Tito descended the stone steps. The mistake was corrected now; the old life was discarded. He turned toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, intending to cross the Ponte Rubaconte, but a sudden shift in the ambient noise halted him. Sounds of commotion drifted from the Oltrarno district. Annoyed by the loss of privacy, he altered course, opting for the safer route via the Ponte Vecchio. Unpleasant sensations prickled his skin as he pulled his mantle tight, accelerating his pace.

In the grey gloom, unseen by the few, the sounds grew louder, more chaotic. A mob was approaching. Tito spun around, fleeing in the opposite direction, convinced the rioters were crossing the bridge. He hesitated briefly to verify, only to confirm the terrifying reality: the crowd was pouring onto the Via de' Bardi from the opposite end. Panic seized him. He abandoned the thought of returning home; they wouldn't attack *him* specifically, surely? But he carried valuables, and mobs operated on chaos, not reason. Necessity forged a temporary courage. He marched toward the Ponte Vecchio, the roar of trampling feet and confused shouts drowning out his own thoughts.

Reaching the end of the street, he collided with the advancing tide. Before he could process the sudden shout rising around him, he was surrounded—not by unarmed thugs, but by the armed Compagnacci. His hat was knocked from his head, and he was shoved violently into the throng, pushed along the narrow passage of the bridge. The cacophony erupted: *"Piagnone! Medicean! Piagnone! Throw him over the bridge!"* Strong hands tore at his mantle, threatening to choke him, until the fibula gave way. Someone lunged for his *scarsella* (purse), but the grab missed; it still hung at his side.

Shouts, yells, and motiveless curses hammered into his ears, spreading like wildfire among those who hadn't yet identified him. Tito's worst nightmare crystallized: he feared being struck down or trampled before reaching the open arches of the bridge. His only salvation lay in being thrown overboard before his strength was broken. Every fiber of his being focused on this slim chance.

Yes, they were at the arches. In that critical moment, Tito experienced a surge of primal instinct. With a desperate, tearing effort, he unclasped his belt and hurled it, along with the *scarsella*, toward a patch of clear space against the parapet. *"There are diamonds! There is gold!"* he cried in a ringing voice.

The hold on him loosened instantly as the mob surged for the purse. Seizing the opening, Tito threw himself onto the parapet and launched himself into the void. He plunged with a great splash into the dark river below. It was his chance. He had survived drowning before; his powerful strokes propelled him upward. Rising to the surface, gasping, he felt a profound sense of deliverance. The current aided him. If he could swim past the Ponte alla Carrara, he might reach a remote section of the city, perhaps even San Gallo. Life remained possible. Above, the idiot mob continued to shout and bellow, convinced he was already dead. Peering over the parapet, they saw only the drifting blackness of floating hair and velvet sleeves. Only from a distance could a pale olive face be seen, white against the dark water—a face with a square forehead, long arched brows, and lustrous, agate-like eyes.

Onward he drifted, passing the bridge of Santa Trinita. Should he risk landing now? No. Yells pursued him, and the terror of human malice outweighed the uncertainty of the river. He swam on, panting and straining, fueled by a fear of his fellow men rather than the abstract dangers of the water. Exhaustion began to cloud his edges, pulling him toward unconsciousness, but he pushed through. The final bridge passed. He felt the shift, a vague certainty that he was safe, though the current carried him blindly. Where was he? The dreamy haze of exhaustion took hold.

Then, eyes caught sight of him. Aged, sharp eyes scanning the distance. Baldassarre, staring blankly from his post among the rushes, saw a white object emerging from the broader stream. Could this be fortune? He strained, watching until the shape resolved. A start of recognition jolted him. He leaned forward, his eyes filling with a new, fierce light. Yet, he remained motionless. Something was being delivered to him.

Suddenly, a body was cast violently onto the grass mere yards from him. Baldassarre sprang forward like a panther, grabbing the velvet tunic as he collapsed onto the stranger. He flashed a look into the man's face. Dead? The eyes were rigid. But no—death was rarely so absolute. Men feigned death; life could return. Baldassarre did not feel feeble. He knew exactly what he could do. He wedged his large fingers into the neck of the tunic, kneeling beside the body, watching the face intently.

A fierce hope ignited in his heart, mingled with trembling. All the slow-burning embers of his remaining vitality leaped into flame. Rigid. Still rigid. Those eyes, half-lidded, were locked against vengeance. *Could* he truly be dead? Time was meaningless; it felt long enough for hope to freeze into despair. Surely, the eyelids were quivering now. The rigidity was breaking. A vibrating light flickered in them; they opened wide.

Tito recognized him, but confusion warred with memory. Did he recognize life or death? It might be death, and death meant the eternal chill of the hideous past looming over him. But now, Baldassarre's sole dread was that the young limbs would slip away. He pressed his knuckles against the round throat, kneeling on the chest with the full force of his aged frame. *Let death come now!*
