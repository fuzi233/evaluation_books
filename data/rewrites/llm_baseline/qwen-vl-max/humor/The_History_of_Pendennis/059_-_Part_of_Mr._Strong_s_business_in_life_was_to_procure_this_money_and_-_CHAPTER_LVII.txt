Worthy Major Pendennis, ever the gentleman of conscience and occasional moral compass, fulfilled his promise to Warrington—just enough to satisfy his own良知, which is always a very lenient judge when it comes to family matters. He reassured poor Helen that Arthur’s connection with the odious little gatekeeper (a position she had long suspected was more of a romantic entanglement than a janitorial one) was officially over. No more secret meetings! No more clandestine glances across the garden! The romance was dead—killed by virtue, propriety, and probably a well-timed cough from the butler.

Arthur, meanwhile, once he’d recovered from the shock of realizing Fanny Bolton wasn’t going to die dramatically in his arms (a fate he’d secretly hoped for, if only for the dramatic flair), felt a great weight lifted. He could now enjoy life without fearing an impromptu proposal or a sudden marriage certificate being thrust into his hands by a distraught young woman. Freedom! At last!

And so, with hearts light and wallets slightly lighter (though not too much—Pendennis was no fool), the party set off on their grand Continental tour. Arthur Pendennis, rentier extraordinaire, accompanied Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell (who, though technically not French, did her best to act like one, speaking English with exaggerated elegance and occasionally referring to “the charming little village” when they were clearly in a town with a population of 10,000). George Warrington, particulier, age 32, height 6 feet (Anglo-Saxon standard), hair black, beard idem, etc., took charge of logistics—passports, baggage, and making sure no one got lost in Bruges while trying to find the nearest café serving coffee that wasn’t made from boiled shoe polish.

They crossed from Dover to Ostend, where they paused briefly to admire the sea, the wind, and the fact that they were finally out of England. Then onward they went—Bruges, Ghent, Brussels, and eventually the Rhine, where the scenery was so picturesque it made you want to write poetry… or at least take a photograph. But since photography hadn’t been invented yet, they settled for sighing dramatically and pretending they were characters in a novel.

Now, we don’t intend to bore you with every detail of this thoroughly unoriginal tour. We won’t describe Laura’s first encounter with a canal boat (she nearly fainted when she saw how low it sat in the water). We won’t dwell on Helen’s fascination with Beguine convents—where she spent hours staring at nuns in black veils, arms outstretched like they were auditioning for a particularly solemn ballet. She found the whole thing deeply unsettling. Barefoot friars? Crowned saints? People bowing down to images of the Virgin Mary like they were auditioning for a religious reality show? It was all so *foreign*. And then there were the theatres open on Sundays—blasphemy! The idea of dancing on a holy day? Unthinkable! Yet here they were, doing it, and looking rather pleased about it.

When the men returned from their evening walks or drives, they’d find Helen quietly reading her devotional books—psalms, hymns, and other things that didn’t involve modernity. Laura would watch her, eyes full of concern, wondering if her mother-in-law-to-be (if things ever came to that) was holding up under the strain of foreign customs and emotional turmoil. Poor Pen, meanwhile, was devotedly attentive to his mother, though there was something missing—a kind of invisible wall between them. She loved him fiercely, but also felt the sting of betrayal: once, her son had poured out his soul to her; now, he kept secrets. She missed those days when Arthur’s heart was hers alone—when he’d tell her everything, even what color socks he thought looked best with his waistcoat.

Laura, observing this quiet agony, once said softly, “If Pen had loved me as you wished, I’d have gained him—but I’d have lost you, mamma. And I’d rather have you love me best.” To which Helen sighed and agreed—though she firmly denied the part about Pen loving Laura. But let’s be honest: Miss Laura was probably right on both counts. Women, after all, are built for love—not just in moments, but in hours, days, years. Men? They’re more like oaks. Standing tall. Hands in pockets. Occasionally offering shade. Women? They’re the ivy. Clinging. Twining. Kissing the bark. Devoting themselves entirely to the tree—even if it doesn’t know it’s being worshipped.

Meanwhile, old Pendennis had only seen them off at the dock before vanishing into the mist of European diplomacy. He was off to visit a nobleman friend—someone important, someone whose name appeared in the Morning Post alongside “Marquis of Steyne,” which was quite a thrill for the Major. He’d earned a break after years of tending to sick family members, though he did admit he missed the partridges at Stillbrook. Still, the pheasants were still around, and so was the noble owner, so he headed straight to the hospitable mansion and began enjoying himself—shooting, dining, and generally behaving like a man who’d forgotten he was nearing seventy.

He was a treasure in a country house. He told risqué stories in the smoking room (which the young men laughed at, mostly because they didn’t understand half of them). He flattered the ladies in the morning rooms. He showed new arrivals around the park, pointing out the best view of the lake, the spot where the old road used to go, and the exact location where Lord Lynx caught Sir Phelim O’Neal praying on his knees before the lady of the house—*very* dramatic, though possibly apocryphal. He knew everyone’s names—lodge-keepers, gardeners, the cook’s nephew—and had a word for each, usually something mildly critical. He was invaluable. And he enjoyed every minute of it.

Back on the Continent, Warrington became the de facto leader of the expedition. He spoke German fluently (unlike Pen, who had forgotten most of it during his illness). He handled money, managed luggage, and ensured the group never ended up in a hotel with no hot water. He found the English church, walked with Helen and Laura to services (with proper decorum, naturally), and even escorted the widow on her donkey rides—though he once almost fell off when the animal startled at a passing goat.

At the baths, many a dashing Prussian or French dandy cast longing glances at Laura, the fresh-faced English girl beside the pale widow. They’d have given anything to dance with her—except, of course, Warrington, who had never learned to waltz. Not that he minded. He’d have liked to learn—if only to dance with *her*. But alas, such thoughts were forbidden territory. He was a bachelor, after all. A rough diamond with no intention of becoming a polished gem. Or so he told himself.

Yet he stayed. Day after day, he watched over Helen, attended to her needs, fetched her ‘Galignani’ (a newspaper she read with intense interest), and arranged comfortable seats under lime trees during the afternoon promenades. He was so attentive, in fact, that people began to wonder if he was actually her son. Or perhaps an adventurer hoping to marry her fortune. Maybe both. After all, this was a *selfish story*, remember? Everyone has their reasons. Even the generous ones.

Laura, meanwhile, grew more fond of Warrington with each passing day. She greeted him with bright smiles and eager conversation. He found himself talking to her in ways he never thought possible—sharing thoughts, feelings, even jokes. He started noticing things: crow’s feet near his eyes, silver hairs in his beard, the way his boots pinched his toes. He envied the young bucks—the Germans with their tight waists, the Frenchmen with their lacquered mustaches, the English dandies with their cool superiority. He felt old. Outdated. But still… he couldn’t leave.

Every night, he left the group with growing reluctance. Alone in his lodgings, he felt lonelier than ever. And Helen? She saw it. She saw the way he looked at her, the care he took with her, the quiet devotion. She understood why Major Pendennis had insisted Warrington join them—because he, too, was watching. Watching over her. Watching over her son. Watching over *everything*.

Pen, however, remained blissfully unaware. He didn’t see what was happening. Didn’t care. Or maybe he did—but chose not to interfere. After all, he had his own concerns: his health, his mother’s fragile state, and the lingering guilt over Fanny Bolton.

Which brings us to the moment when a letter arrived from London. Delivered by Morgan, the Major’s valet, who carried it like it was a sacred relic. Inside was a note from Fanny Bolton—written in trembling hand, filled with sorrow and devotion.

She’d come to his rooms, she said. Found them empty. Left no message. And when she’d written to him, asking only for one kind word, her letter had vanished. She’d cared for him when he was ill, cut off his hair (a shocking intimacy!), and kept a lock of it—just a small token. Could she keep it? Would his mother demand it back?

Arthur read the letter and turned red. Then white. Then red again. He stormed into the Major’s room, letter clutched in hand.

“Do you know anything about this?” he demanded.

The Major, fresh from his bath and smelling faintly of eucalyptus and regret, blinked. “What letter?”

“The one from Fanny Bolton!”

“I know nothing about it,” the Major said sharply. “I’ve never met the girl. Never spoken to her. Never sent her away.”

“Then it was my mother!” Arthur shouted. “She did it!”

“Perhaps,” said the Major, shrugging. “But let’s not argue. I’ve got a napoleon to lose at the casino later.”

Arthur didn’t listen. He stormed out, hat in hand, muttering about justice and dignity and how his mother had treated a poor, innocent girl like dirt.

Back at the lodging, he found Warrington waiting for the ladies to attend church. He threw the letter across the table.

“Look at this,” he said. “She saved me. She loved me. And they treated her like trash.”

Warrington frowned. “If your mother did it…”

“It makes it worse,” Arthur snapped. “She should have protected her. Not rejected her.”

“Hush,” Warrington whispered. “They can hear you.”

But it was too late. The storm had begun. And somewhere, deep in the heart of Rosenbad, a tiny spark had ignited—leading, inevitably, to disaster.
