ON BEING IDLE. Now, this is a subject on which I flatter myself I am truly *au fait*. The gentleman who, when I was young, baptized me at the fountain of wisdom for nine guineas a term—no extras charged—used to claim he'd never known a boy capable of doing less work in more time. Meanwhile, my poor grandmother, while casually instructing me on the proper use of the Prayer-book, observed that it was highly improbable I would ever do much that I shouldn't, though she felt convinced beyond a shadow of a doubt that I would leave undone practically everything I should. I fear I've partially vindicated her prophecy. Heaven help me! I have done quite a few things I shouldn't have, despite my laziness. Yet, I have fully confirmed her judgment regarding the vast array of duties I neglected. Idling has always been my forte. I take no credit for it—it is a rare gift. While there are plenty of lazy folks and numerous slow coaches, a genuine idler is a rarity. He isn't a man slouching about with his hands in his pockets. On the contrary, his most striking trait is that he is perpetually, intensely busy. One cannot enjoy idling thoroughly unless one has ample work to do. Doing absolutely nothing when you have nothing to accomplish offers no fun; wasting time then becomes merely an occupation, and a terribly exhausting one. True idleness, like fine kisses, must be stolen to remain sweet.

Many years ago, when I was a young man, I fell violently ill—I couldn't quite discern what was wrong, save for a beastly cold. Presumably, it was something grave, for the doctor remarked I should have sought him a month prior, warning that if the condition persisted another week, he wouldn't answer for the outcome. It is extraordinary, but I've never encountered a physician summoned to a case who didn't discover that a single day's delay would have rendered recovery impossible. Our medical guide, philosopher, and friend resembles the hero in a melodrama: he invariably arrives on the scene just, and only just, in the nick of time. It is simply Providence.

Well, as I was saying, I was very ill and prescribed a month-long stay at Buxton, with strict orders to do absolutely nothing whatsoever. "Perfect rest," the doctor insisted. This seemed delightful. "This man clearly understands my plight," I thought, envisioning a glorious four weeks of *dolce far niente*, seasoned with just enough illness to lend it poetic flavor. I imagined rising late, sipping chocolate, and eating breakfast in slippers and a dressing gown. I pictured lounging in a garden hammock, reading sentimental novels with tragic endings until they slipped from my listless grasp, then reclining to gaze dreamily at the azure sky, watching clouds float like white-sailed ships, while birds sang and leaves rustled. Should weakness prevent outdoor activity, I envisioned sitting propped against pillows at an open window, appearing wasted and intriguing, causing passing beauties to sigh. Twice daily, I would descend in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to drink the mineral waters. Oh, those waters! Ignorant of their nature then, I was charmed by the fashionability of the phrase "drinking the waters," sounding distinctly Queen Anne-esque. But alas! After the first three or four mornings... Sam Weller's description of them tasting like "warm flat irons" barely scratches the surface of their hideous nausea. Nothing could hasten a sick man's recovery like knowing he must consume a glassful daily until cured. I drank them neat for six consecutive days, nearly killing myself; thereafter, I adopted the strategy of following each dose with a stiff glass of brandy-and-water, finding immense relief. Later, eminent physicians informed me the alcohol likely neutralized the chalybeate properties entirely. Fortunate I discovered the remedy.

Yet, drinking the waters was merely a minor torment compared to the misery of that unforgettable month—the most wretched period of my life. For most of it, I adhered strictly to the doctor's mandate, doing nothing but wandering the house and garden, and venturing out for two hours daily in a Bath chair. This broke the monotony slightly. There is more thrill to Bath-chairing—particularly for the unaccustomed—than meets the eye. A constant sense of danger pervades the passenger's mind, convincing him every moment that disaster looms, a feeling intensifying near ditches or freshly macadamized roads. Every approaching vehicle seems destined to strike him; every ascent or descent triggers calculations on whether the frail driver will lose control. However, even this diversion eventually failed to enliven the soul, and the ennui became unbearable. My mind began to crack under the strain. Lacking a robust constitution, I deemed it unwise to push it further. Thus, around the twentieth morning, I rose early, ate a hearty breakfast, and marched straight to Hayfield, at the foot of Kinder Scout—a charming, bustling town nestled in a lovely valley, boasting two delightfully pretty women. At least, they were then; one crossed the bridge and perhaps smiled, while the other stood at an open door, offering unpaid kisses to a red-faced infant. Years have passed; surely they have grown stout and sharp-tongued since.

Returning, I spotted an elderly man breaking stones, sparking such a fierce desire to use my arms that I offered him a drink to swap places. Being a kindly soul, he agreed. I tackled those stones with the pent-up energy of three weeks, accomplishing in thirty minutes more labor than he had managed all day. Yet, it elicited no jealousy. Having taken the plunge, I ventured deeper into dissipation, embarking on long morning walks and attending the band in the pavilion every evening. Still, the days dragged on, and I was heartily relieved when the final day arrived, whisking me away from gout-ridden, consumptive Buxton to London, with its stern industry and vibrant life. As the carriage thundered through Hendon that evening, the lurid glow of the great city warmed my heart. Later, as my cab rattled out of St. Pancras station, the familiar roar swelling around me sounded sweeter than any music I'd heard in months.

I certainly did not relish that month of enforced idleness. I enjoy idling when I *shouldn't* be idling; not when it is the sole option available. Such is my stubborn nature. The moment I prefer standing with my back to the fire, tallying debts, is when my desk is piled high with correspondence demanding immediate replies. The time I savor dawdling over dinner is when a heavy workload awaits. And if urgency demands an early rise, it is precisely then that I cherish lying awake an extra half-hour. Ah! How delicious to roll over and drift back to sleep—"just for five minutes." Do humans exist, aside from the protagonist of a Sunday-school tale for boys, who ever rises willingly? Some men find waking at the appointed hour utterly impossible. If eight o'clock is the required time, they linger until half-past. If circumstances shift and half-past becomes acceptable, they don't rise until nine. They resemble the statesman noted for being punctually half an hour late. They devise endless schemes: purchasing alarm clocks (clever devices that trigger at the wrong moment for the wrong person), instructing Sarah Jane to knock and call, to which they grunt "awry" before sinking back into slumber. I knew one fellow who would endure a cold bath; yet, useless, he would immediately return to bed to warm himself. I believe I could stay out of bed indefinitely once I managed to extricate myself. The true difficulty lies in wrenching the head from the pillow; no amount of pre-dawn resolve eases this task.

After wasting the entire evening, I declare to myself, "Well, no more work tonight; I'll rise early tomorrow." Fully resolved, I intend to do so—until morning. Then, enthusiasm fades, replaced by the reflection that stopping earlier would have been preferable. Add to this the dread of dressing; the more one contemplates it, the greater the urge to postpone. Strange indeed is this bed, this mimic grave, where we stretch our weary limbs and sink quietly into silence and repose. "O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head," sang poor Hood. You are a kind old nurse to our fretful youths and maidens. Whether clever or foolish, naughty or good, you cradle us all in your maternal lap, hushing our wayward cries. The burdened man, the sufferer, the little maiden mourning a faithless lover—we lay our aching heads on your white bosom, and you gently soothe us to sleep. Our sorrow is profound when you refuse comfort. How agonizingly slow the dawn approaches when sleep eludes us! Oh, those dreadful nights tossing in fever and pain, lying like living corpses amidst darkness drifting toward light. And oh, those even more terrible nights sitting vigil beside another in agony, startled by falling embers from the low fire, hearing the clock's tick sound like a hammer striking out the life we watch fade.

Enough of beds and bedrooms. I have dwelt on them too long, even for an idle fellow. Let us step outside for a smoke. This wastes time equally well and looks less suspicious. Tobacco has been a boon to us idlers. Hard to imagine what the civil-service clerks before Sir Walter Scott's era found to occupy their minds. I attribute the quarrelsome spirit of medieval youth entirely to the absence of this soothing herb. With no work and no smoking, they fought endlessly. If peace held, they ignited deadly feuds with neighbors; if spare moments remained, they debated whose sweetheart was fairest, wielding battle-axes and clubs as arguments.
