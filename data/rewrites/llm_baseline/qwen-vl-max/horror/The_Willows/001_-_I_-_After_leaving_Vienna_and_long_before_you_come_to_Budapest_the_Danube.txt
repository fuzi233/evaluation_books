After leaving Vienna, and long before you reach Budapest, the Danube slips into a realm of profound solitude—a place where the river forgets its name and becomes something else entirely. Here, it splits into a thousand channels, none dominant, all whispering secrets as they snake through a vast, waterlogged plain. For miles on end, there is no shore, only a sea of willows—low, dense, and endless—bending under the wind like a living tide. On maps, this region is brushed with pale blue, fading toward the edges, labeled in bold, looping script: _Sümpfe_—marshes.

In flood, the waters rise so high that even the highest mounds are nearly submerged. But now, at mid-July, the river is neither full nor low—just restless. The willows sway in silvered waves, their undersides flashing like armor when the sun hits them, their tops trembling as if caught in some unseen breath. They never grow tall enough to be trees; they remain humble, rounded, soft—yet somehow, they move. Not just the branches, but the whole landscape seems to breathe, to pulse. The wind drives across them in gusts, sending ripples over the green field, turning it into a rolling sea of leaves. When the breeze shifts, the color changes—first deep green, then sudden white as the backs of the foliage catch the light. It’s as though the land itself is alive, dreaming.

The Danube, freed from the rigid banks of civilization, roams freely here. It carves new paths daily, tearing away sandbanks, swallowing islands whole, then spitting out others in their place. Whirlpools form without warning, rapids roar like beasts, and the current carries away entire clumps of willow as if they were nothing more than driftwood. Nothing lasts. No island remains unchanged for long. This is not a river—it’s a wild beast, growing, shifting, devouring.

We reached this part of the journey in our Canadian canoe, loaded with a gipsy tent, a frying pan, and dreams of the Black Sea. We had left Vienna at dawn, slipping past its sleeping streets while the sky bled crimson. By noon, we’d passed Fischeramend, Hainburg, Petronell—the old Roman city of Carnuntum—and finally, under the shadow of Thelsen, crossed into Hungary. The March joined us quietly from the left, and the frontier vanished behind us.

Twelve kilometers an hour carried us swiftly into Hungarian territory. The muddy water, thick with silt, signaled rising floodwaters. We scraped over shingle beds, spun helplessly in whirlpools, and were tossed like corks until Pressburg—Pozsóny—rose against the horizon. Then, with a surge, the canoe leapt forward, darted beneath the grey stone walls of the city, slipped past the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brücke ferry, turned sharply left, and plunged into the chaos beyond—the swamp, the islands, the endless willows.

And then, abruptly, the world changed.

It was like stepping from a film of cities into one of forests and lakes—without warning, without transition. Within minutes, there was no sign of human life. No boats, no huts, no smoke, no roofs. Just water, willows, and wind. A silence so complete it felt like being swallowed by nature itself. The isolation pressed down on us, heavy and real. We laughed, half-serious, that we must have needed a special passport to enter this hidden kingdom—one ruled not by men, but by water and wind and ancient spirits.

By late afternoon, the wind had grown fierce, beating us relentlessly. We searched for a place to camp. The islands shifted and blurred in the current, pulling us in and pushing us out. Willows snagged our hands as we tried to steady the canoe. Finally, a gust drove us sideways into a narrow backwater. With a spray of foam, we grounded the bow on yellow sand. Panting, we collapsed onto the beach—sheltered, hot, and drenched in sunlight. Above, the sky was a flawless blue. Around us, the willows danced, clapping their leaves like tiny hands, as if applauding our arrival.

“What a river,” I said, staring at the water.

“Won’t stand much nonsense now, will it?” my companion replied, dragging the canoe farther up the bank. He stretched out, already half-asleep.

I lay beside him, content. The elements surrounded us—wind, water, sun, sand—like a bath of pure sensation. I thought of the journey: from the Black Forest, where the Danube began as a timid stream, to this moment, where it roared like a god. And I thought of my friend—the Swede—quiet, practical, unimpressed by ghosts or legends. Yet even he could not deny the river’s power.

From the beginning, the Danube had seemed alive. Not merely flowing, but thinking. At night, it sang to the moon—a low, sibilant hum, like stones grinding beneath its surface. We knew its voices: the gurgle of whirlpools, the thunder of rapids, the constant rush beneath everything. When rain fell, it shouted. When the wind blew upstream, it laughed. It chattered when hills rose beside it, spoke with dignity through towns, whispered when the sun warmed its curves. It was never still. It played tricks—disappearing into the earth near Donaueschingen, reappearing on the other side of limestone hills, starting anew under another name. It refused to acknowledge tributaries, running alongside them for miles, ignoring their presence. Only after Passau did it yield—when the Inn arrived with such force that it couldn’t be ignored. Then the river fought, the canyon shook, and our canoe rode the waves like a child in a storm.

But now, after Pressburg, the Danube had changed. It grew serious. It was no longer a boy playing games. It was becoming something older, deeper. It split into three arms, each wide and powerful, converging only far downstream. There was no sign which path to take.

“I warn you,” said the Hungarian officer in Pressburg, “if you take a side channel, you may wake up forty miles from anywhere, stranded, with no food, no people. No fishermen. No help. The river is still rising. The wind will worsen.”

We didn’t fear the flood. But the idea of being trapped, high and dry, terrified us. So we stocked extra provisions. And indeed, the wind increased—steady, cold, relentless—until it became a gale.

We made camp earlier than planned. The sun was still hours from setting. I left my companion asleep and wandered the island.

It was small—less than an acre—a mere sandbar jutting into the river. The apex pointed toward the sunset, where the wind hurled spray like a curtain of fire. I stood there, watching the crimson flood roar down upon us, crashing against the bank as if trying to tear it apart. The water swirled past in two foaming streams, one on each side. The ground trembled. The willows bent and twisted, creating the illusion that the island itself was moving—drifting, breathing, alive.

Above, the river descended like a sliding hill, white with foam, leaping up in every direction. From the lower end, it looked dark, angry. Only the crests of the waves showed, streaked with foam, pushed forward by the wind. For a short stretch, it wound between islands, then vanished into the willows—swallowed whole, as if the trees were monstrous creatures gathering around a sacred spring.

It was a scene of eerie beauty. And yet, as I watched, something stirred within me—not fear, not exactly, but a quiet unease. A sense that we were not welcome here.

The river was powerful, yes—but that wasn’t it. The wind was wild, but I shared its joy. The flood was terrifying, but I admired its strength. No—this feeling came from the willows.

They were everywhere. Thick, endless, pressing close. They crowded the river, as if trying to smother it. Their ranks stretched mile after mile, silent, watchful, waiting. And in their number, in their stillness, in their endless motion, I sensed something unnatural. Something alien.

Mountains awe. Oceans terrify. Forests enchant. But these willows… they did not inspire wonder. They inspired dread. A slow, creeping dread. As if they were not plants, but beings—sentient, ancient, guarding something forbidden.

I tried to shake it off. I walked the island, collecting driftwood. The Swede climbed the bank, searching for fuel. “By Jove!” he called suddenly. I ran to see what he had found.

He was looking across the river, pointing.

“Good heavens—it’s a man’s body!” he cried.

A black shape rolled in the foam, flipping over and over. Twenty feet out, it lurched and turned—eyes reflecting the sunset, glowing yellow in the dimming light. Then it plunged beneath the surface.

“An otter!” we both said at once, laughing.

It was an otter—alive, hunting. But for a moment, it had looked like a drowned man. A real man. A dead man.

We turned back, arms full of wood. And then—another thing.

A boat. A small, flat-bottomed craft. And a man standing in it, steering with a long oar. He was on the opposite shore, moving fast, heading downstream. The slanted light distorted his shape. The water shimmered, refracting the sun. I couldn’t focus. But I saw him—upright, facing us, gesticulating wildly.

His voice came across the water—shouting, furious—but the wind tore the words apart. I couldn’t hear a single syllable.

Then I saw it—he was crossing himself. Making the sign of the Cross.

“He’s crossing himself!” I shouted.

The Swede shaded his eyes. “You’re right. He’s scared.”

The man disappeared quickly, melting into the willows where the sun struck them red and gold. Mist rose from the water. The air grew thick.

“What in the world is he doing here at dusk?” I asked, half to myself. “What did he mean by those signs? Was he warning us?”

“He saw our smoke,” the Swede said, laughing. “Thought we were spirits. These Hungarians believe in all sorts of nonsense. Remember the shopkeeper at Pressburg? She said no one ever lands here because it belongs to beings outside human law. Fairies, elementals—maybe demons. That peasant saw people on an island for the first time in his life. It scared him.”

His tone was light, but I noticed something—his usual calm was gone. His voice sounded strained. His laughter didn’t reach his eyes.

“If they had imagination,” I said, louder than necessary, trying to fill the silence, “they might populate this place with gods. The Romans built shrines here. Sacred groves. Elemental deities.”

I smiled. But inside, the unease remained. The willows watched. The river breathed. The wind whispered. And somewhere, deep in the marsh, something waited.
