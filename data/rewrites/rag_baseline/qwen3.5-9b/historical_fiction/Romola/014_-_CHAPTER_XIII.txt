The afternoon of September seventh moved with a languid, oppressive heat, two months since the confession that bound Romola and Tito together. Inside Nello's shop, the atmosphere was thick with the dust of idle days. Tito stepped down from the threshold to find the barber sprawled across the bench, a cap pulled low over his eyes. One leg was drawn up, the other slipping toward the floor, dragging a manuscript volume of verse behind it; the pages lay crushed, a testament to the barber's surrender to sleep. In the corner, Sandro sat alone, engaged in a solitary game of *mora*, his left fingers responding to the arithmetical demands of his right with a solemn, rhythmic precision.

Tito moved with a silent tread, snatching up the lute. He bent over the sleeping man, plucking the strings softly as he sang Lorenzo de' Medici's carnival song: *"Quant' è bella giovinezza / Che si fugge tuttavia! / Chi vuol esser lieto sia, / Di doman non c'è certezza."*

*"Beauteous is life in blossom! And it fleeteth—fleeteth ever; Whoso would be joyful—let him! There's no surety for the morrow."*

Nello woke instantly, as easily startled as a bird from a branch. The cap flew from his eyes. "Ah, my Apollino!" he exclaimed, springing upright. "I seem to have overslept on this infernal heat. The fault lies in not sleeping naturally, but ingesting a potion of potent poetry. Listen to me matching my syllables to their initials, like a *Trovatore*. That is one of my afflictions: I fear the fine wine of my intellect is running off at the spigot of authorship, leaving me an empty cask smelling of dregs, like so many geniuses I know."

"What is it, my Orpheus?" Nello extended his arms fully, then curled them inward to grasp Tito's curls, pulling them out playfully. "What do you seek from your well-tamed Nello? I hear a coaxing strain in your music. Show me the needle's eye of your desire so I may thread it."

"That is merely a tailor's metaphor," Tito replied, his fingers drifting lightly over the strings. "Yet you have divined the source of my impatience. I require an extra measure of your artistry—not upon my chin, but upon the *zazzera*, which is as tangled as Florentine politics. Your comb works with adroitness, flattering the skin and stirring the animal spirits in that region. A touch of your delicate orange-scent would be fitting, for I am bound for the Scala palace. The young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici attends, accompanied by Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit moves so rapidly that I see no defense against it save the fragrance of orange blossoms."

Nello had already seized his comb, flourishing it before pushing Tito gently backward into the chair and wrapping a cloth around him. "Never speak of rivalry, *bel giovane mio*: Bernardo Dovizi is a keen youth who will never cast a net to catch the wind. Yet he bears the sharp-muzzled look of his brother, Ser Piero, the weasel Piero de' Medici employs to slip through small holes. No! You distance all rivals and may soon touch the sky with your forefinger. They claim you have carried enough honey to sweeten the sour Messer Angelo; he has pronounced you less of an ass than expected, given the good understanding between you and the Secretary."

"And between ourselves, Nello mio, Messer Angelo possesses more genius and erudition than all other Florentine scholars combined. It suits them to praise me now that Poliziano is bowed by grief or illness. I might attempt a flight with a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito, but Poliziano is a large-beaked eagle who would swallow me, feathers and all, without noticing the loss."

"I will not contradict your modesty," Nello said smoothly. "We clever Florentines do not repeat the same truths daily; we cry down Dante and cry up Francesco Cei simply for variety. If we hail you as a new Poliziano, heaven ensures the lie remains manageable. Besides, are you not a pattern of virtue in this wicked city? With your ears double-waxed against siren invitations that would lure you from the Via de' Bardi and the great work destined to astonish posterity?"

"Posterity," Tito murmured, "whom it will probably astonish as the universe does, by the impossibility of discerning the plan."

"Yes, similar prophecies circulated recently. Cristoforo Landino remarked that the excellent Bardo is one of those scholars who lie overthrown in their learning, like cavaliers in heavy armor, only to rage because they are over-ruled. A pithy observation, though it seems Messer Cristoforo is the very pearl of such commentary, feeding men with an empty spoon and gagging them with vain expectation. *Ecco!* You are perfect now."

Nello withdrew the cloth. "Impossible to add further grace! But love is not always fed on learning, is it? I shall have to dress the *zazzera* for the betrothal soon, shall I not?"

"Perhaps," Tito smiled, "unless Messer Bernardo recommends that Bardo require me to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the car of the Zecca before I can win my Alcestis. Yet I confess he is right; I am unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad that may grow dim by marrying any mortal."

"*Gnaffè*," Nello scoffed. "Your modesty is misplaced. Fate seems to have measured and chiseled you for the niche left empty by the old man's son. By the way, Cronaca tells me he is now at San Marco. Did you know?"

A faint electric shock passed through Tito as he rose, though he concealed it by stooping to flatten the crushed leaves of the fallen book. "No; I thought he was at Fiesole. Are you certain he has returned to San Marco?"

"Cronaca is my authority," Nello shrugged. "I do not frequent that sanctuary, but he does. Ah," he added, taking the book from Tito's hands, "my poor Nencia da Barberino! It jars your scholarly sensibilities to see the pages dog-eared. I was lulled by the well-rhymed charms of that rustic maiden—'prettier than the turnip-flower,' 'with a cheek more savory than cheese.' But to acquire such a well-scented notion of the *contadina*, one must lie on velvet cushions in the Via Larga, not attend the *Fierucoloni* stampeding into the Piazza della Nunziata this evening after sundown."

"And pray, who are the *Fierucoloni*?" Tito asked indifferently, settling his cap.

"The country women from the mountains of Pistoia and the Casentino, and wherever else, keeping vigil in the church of the Nunziata to sell their yarn and dried mushrooms at the *Fierucola*, as we call it. They make a queer spectacle with their paper lanterns, howling hymns to the Virgin on her nativity eve. If you had the leisure..."

"No?" Nello interrupted. "Well, I have had enough. There is wild work in the Piazza today. One may receive stones about the ears or shins without asking for them, and I have never favored such pressing attention. *Addio*."

Tito departed carrying a subtle unease. His visit ended sooner than anticipated due to the absence of the boy-cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, the youngest of the red-hatted fathers who would later present his broad dark cheek to posterity as Pope Leo X. Detained at his favorite pursuit, the chase, the Cardinal had failed to appear.

It was nearly half an hour before sunset when Tito left the Scala palace, intending to proceed immediately to the Via de' Bardi. He had not traveled far when, to his astonishment, he saw Romola advancing along the Borgo Pinti. She wore a thick black veil and mantle, yet her figure and gait were unmistakable. Beside her walked a short, stout form: Monna Brigida, recognizable despite her unusually plain attire.

Romola had not been raised for devotional observances outside the loggia on her father's roof, occasions so rare and heavily premeditated due to Bardo's jealousy that Tito assumed there must be urgent grounds for her absence unheard of the previous day. She spotted him through her veil and quickened her pace.

"Romola, has anything happened?" Tito asked, turning to walk beside her.

She remained silent initially. Monna Brigida interjected. "Ah, Messer Tito, you do well to turn round; we are in haste. Is it not a misfortune? We are obliged to circumvent the walls via the Via del Maglio because of the Fair. The *contadine* blocking the road to the Nunziata would delay us to San Marco by half the time."

Tito's heart hammered violently. "Romola," he whispered, "are you going to San Marco?"

They had emerged from the Borgo Pinti and were now beneath the city walls, where wide gardens lay to their left and all was quiet. Romola lifted her veil slightly to breathe, revealing a face of subdued agitation.
