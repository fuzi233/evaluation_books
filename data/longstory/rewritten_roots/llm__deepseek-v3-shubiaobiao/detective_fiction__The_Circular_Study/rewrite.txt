Mr. Gryce was weary—not just in body, but in spirit. Age had dulled the thrill of the chase, and the monotony of routine investigations had left him restless. He had even begun to consider retirement, imagining a quiet life on his farm in Westchester. But fate had other plans.

A telephone call shattered the stillness of the afternoon—a message from Carter’s drug store, relayed by a nervous boy. A woman, elegant and unknown, had sent him with a cryptic warning: *A strange crime has been committed in the old mansion on —— Street.*

The word *strange* lingered in Gryce’s mind, sparking a flicker of curiosity he hadn’t felt in years. He set out at once, the boy in tow, toward the stately brownstone that stood like a relic of another era amidst the bustling storefronts and crowded sidewalks.

At first glance, the house betrayed nothing—no signs of disturbance, no cries for help. Its heavy curtains were drawn, its door shut tight. Yet something in the air felt *wrong.*

An officer met him at the stoop, frustration etched on his face. “Can’t get in,” he muttered. “Rang every bell. Place seems empty—except for movement upstairs.”

Gryce’s instincts sharpened. “Break in through the rear,” he ordered. “And keep the boy out of sight.”

Once inside, the silence was unnerving. The halls were dim, the air thick with the scent of old wood and polished leather. Nothing seemed amiss—until they reached the study.

The door creaked open, revealing a circular room bathed in eerie rose-colored light. Tapestries adorned the walls, their intricate patterns twisting like shadows. Shelves of ancient books lined the lower half of the chamber, but Gryce’s gaze was drawn to the center—where a man lay sprawled on a bearskin rug, a dagger buried in his chest.

His face was frozen in an expression beyond fear or pain—something deeper, more haunting. A golden cross rested on his breast, its edges gleaming against the dark fabric of his robes. The scene was less a murder than a macabre tableau, as if staged for some unseen audience.

Gryce exhaled slowly. This was no ordinary crime.

And he was no ordinary detective.

The hunt had begun.

For a heartbeat, Mr. Gryce stood frozen. The dim glow of the ironwork lamp cast shifting shadows over the circular study, its crimson light painting the scene in hues of blood. The silence was absolute, broken only by the faint rustle of his own breath. Before him lay the body—a man of striking presence, even in death—his dark features sharp against the pallor of his skin, a cross resting upon his still chest like a silent accusation.

The detective exhaled sharply, forcing composure into his limbs. He had seen death in many forms, but never one so steeped in contradiction: the symbol of peace atop a wound driven by fury.

Kneeling, he pressed fingers to the cooling wrist, then lifted the man’s eyelids. The gaze that met him was fixed, unseeing, yet brimming with a reproach that made Gryce’s jaw tighten. No pulse. No breath. Only the lingering warmth of recent life.

The dagger’s hilt jutted from the victim’s ribs—an ornate thing, foreign, its blade buried at an angle that whispered not of suicide, but of another’s hand. Gryce left it untouched. Evidence first; theories later.

His eyes swept the room. No overturned furniture, no signs of struggle—only a scattering of rose petals near the body, as if the violence had been swift, efficient. The great table at the center stood undisturbed, its clutter of curiosities arranged with fastidious care: pipes, manuscripts, a scroll of music, Venetian glass. And there, half-hidden—a row of electric buttons.

Curious, Gryce pressed one.

The red light above flickered, then deepened into an eerie green, washing the room in a sickly pallor. He grimaced, stabbed another button, and the glow softened to white. *Childish,* he thought—until he looked again at the dead man’s face. No, not childish. Purposeful. Every line of those sharp features spoke of a mind that calculated, that *intended*.

His gaze moved to the walls, draped in tapestries and relics. One detail snagged his attention—a bent nail, its cord still clinging to the cross now laid upon the corpse. Someone had torn it down. Not the victim—not with that wound. Another hand, then. A gesture of remorse? Mockery?

A portrait dominated one wall—a young woman, her beauty haunting, her eyes alive with intelligence. Civil War era, judging by her dress. Beneath it hung a sword, epaulettes, a medal for valor. Gryce filed it away: *A soldier’s mementos. A connection?*

Then, the curtain.

Dark, nearly invisible against the wall, it concealed a narrow doorway. Gryce pushed through into a Spartan bedchamber—a bachelor’s quarters, precise and orderly. But disorder lurked: a silver comb on the floor, a pearl-handled parasol by the door. And there, glinting on the threshold—a spangle. Then another. And another.

A trail.

He followed it back to the study, to the table where the last sequin lay. His fingers brushed the wood near the inkstand, then paused. A smile, thin and knowing, flickered across his face as he lifted the inkwell and set it back *just so*.

Footsteps.

Styles, the officer, reappeared, urgency in his stance. *Upstairs,* he mouthed, jerking his chin toward the staircase. *The killer.*

Gryce hesitated, then nodded. They retreated into the bedchamber, the curtain a hair’s breadth ajar.

The steps descended—slow, labored. A figure emerged: a servant, gaunt and pale, balancing a tray with a single glass of water. The man halted, took in the scene—then the glass slipped from his fingers, shattering as a choked cry tore from his throat.

Styles tensed. *Watch.*

And Gryce did.

The servant’s face contorted. His body coiled, then *moved*—not in panic, but in grotesque reenactment. A hand stretched back, fingers clawed as if gripping a phantom blade. A lunge. A twist. The pantomime of a killing.

Then the collapse—the slow, shuddering descent to his knees, arms outstretched as if cradling an invisible weight. Horror etched his features. Remorse. Madness.

Styles whispered, *He did this upstairs too. The man is broken by his own deed.*

Gryce said nothing.

Some crimes left stains no light could cleanse.

The man who had just reenacted the violent scene before them now rose unsteadily to his feet, his expression shifting from frenzy to vacant confusion. As he bent to retrieve the fallen glass, Gryce’s voice cut through the tension like a blade.

“Hold there. That glass stays. We have questions first—starting with how your master ended up dead on this floor.”

The servant showed no reaction, moving as if deaf—until Styles seized his shoulder, forcing him to face the grim tableau: the sprawled corpse, the bearskin rug, the unblinking scrutiny of the detectives.

Then, something monstrous flickered in the man’s eyes.

A laugh tore from his throat—low, guttural, triumphant. It slithered up the walls, curling into the vaulted ceiling before fading into silence. Gryce’s jaw tightened. Even in madness, such glee was obscene.

“Enough,” the detective snapped. “You were here. Who killed him?”

The servant’s lips parted, but no words came—only choked, wordless sounds. His hands flew to his ears, then his mouth, shaking his head violently.

Styles recoiled. “Damn it—can’t you speak?”

The man’s only answer was a smile, sharp as a knife, before he launched again into his grotesque pantomime: the stabbing, the collapse, the mock grief. His performance was too precise, too *practiced*.

Gryce’s patience frayed. “Search him. Then scour the upper floors. If he’s involved, he’ll bear traces of it.”

But the search yielded nothing—no blood, no weapon, no evidence of guilt beyond that eerie reenactment. Frustrated, Gryce turned back to the body, studying the dead man’s frozen grimace. What had those lips sealed forever?

Then—a voice.

From the shadows above, a shrill cry pierced the stillness:

Gryce’s head jerked up. A starling, caged near the ceiling, cocked its head and screeched again, the words unnervingly clear.

Evelyn. A name, a ghost, a key? The bird’s timing was uncanny—as if it knew.

Before Gryce could react, Styles called from the anteroom: “He’s agitated—not violent, but restless. Like he’s forgotten something.”

“Let him go,” Gryce ordered. “But follow him. Every move matters now.”

As the mute servant hurried past, he thrust two objects into Gryce’s hands: his master’s hat and umbrella. A servant’s instinct? Or a clue? The detective’s fingers tightened around them, but the man had already retreated, sinking back into his chair, his face blank as a shuttered window.

Outside, the crowd’s clamor crescendoed—then ceased abruptly beneath a single, thunderous knock.

Time was up. The coroner had arrived.

And the starling’s cry still echoed in the silence.

Mr. Gryce found himself facing a puzzle unlike any he had encountered in years. The victim, Mr. Adams, had been a recluse—no family, no neighbors, no obvious ties beyond his mute servant. Even now, hours after the news of his death had spread, no one had come forward with questions or information. Unusual, given the man’s notoriety in the district. But then, everything about Adams had been unusual.

The absence of witnesses only sharpened Gryce’s interest. When the last of the reporters and officials finally cleared out near midnight, he seized the chance to pursue two lingering questions.

The first led him to rouse a dozing boy slumped in a hallway chair.

"Up, Jake," Gryce said. "I need you to show me exactly where that woman approached you today."

The boy rubbed his eyes, fumbling for his cap. "Can’t I see my mother first? She’ll be worried."

"She knows you’re here. I sent word hours ago. She vouched for you—said you’re honest."

Jake brightened. "She’s good to me. Wouldn’t do to lie to her." He jammed the cap onto his head. "Ready."

Gryce led him into the quiet streets, pausing before a shop window where two stuffed frogs dueled with tiny swords.

"Was she pretty?" Gryce asked.

Jake hesitated. "Scared me, more like. Fine clothes, though—real fine."

"Troubled?"

"Not crying, but… she gripped my arm hard. Dragged me to the drugstore. Would’ve run if she hadn’t paid me first."

Gryce moved on. *Not young. An old flame, then. Dangerous.*

Outside the drugstore, he stopped. "Show me where she stood while you went in."

Jake pointed. "She left before I came out. Headed for the curb, waved—not for a streetcar. Maybe a cab?"

Gryce’s brow smoothed. "Any cabs around?"

Jake shrugged. Gryce handed him off to an assistant and returned to the house.

Inside, he found Styles waiting. "Anyone for me?"

"Electrician. Name’s Hines. Parlor."

"Good. Keep others out."

Upstairs was empty. Gryce entered the parlor, exchanged brisk words with the young electrician, then led him to the study. The body was gone, but a single candle burned near the door.

Gryce pressed a button on the central table. "Blow out the candle."

Darkness swallowed the room—then erupted into violet light.

Hines blinked. "Slide mechanism?"

"Press another." The glow shifted—pink, green, white, a sickly yellow.

"Examine it," Gryce said.

Hines did, finding only amateur craftsmanship. "Works well, though."

Gryce frowned. "Self-taught?"

"With those books, yes."

Disappointed, Gryce gestured to a birdcage suspended high on the wall. "Another toy. Lower it."

Hines found a hidden button. The cage descended.

Inside, a starling ruffled its feathers but stayed silent.

Gryce set the cage down, pressed another button—then froze.

The doorway to the anteroom was sliding shut.

Hines chuckled. "Convenient, if you hate getting up."

Gryce didn’t laugh. The gap narrowed—a foot, an inch—then sealed with a metallic *click*.

Steel.

Hines’s smile faded. "No handle on this side."

They searched for a release. Nothing.

Gryce whistled sharply, then hammered the barrier with an alpenstock.

An hour passed before Styles heard. Five more before they chiseled an opening.

The room had been designed to trap.

And Gryce was beginning to suspect why.

The peculiar events of the night had sharpened Mr. Gryce’s resolve to unravel the mystery surrounding Mr. Adams’s death. Upon arriving at Headquarters, his first inquiry was for Bartow, the mute butler—had there been any change in his condition? The answer was discouraging. The man remained in the same unresponsive state, and the specialist consulted had given little hope for his immediate recovery.

Gryce frowned. Summoning the officer who had handled Bartow’s arrest, he asked how the mute had reacted to his confinement.

"Confused, sir. Shook his head, made some odd gestures—kept holding his hand back like *this*." The officer demonstrated the motion, one Gryce had seen before. Bartow repeated it incessantly, as though it held some meaning, some desperate plea for understanding.

"Does he show fear? Anger?"

"Both, sir. One moment he’s listless; the next, he’s pounding the walls like a madman. Needs constant watching."

"See that he gets it. Note every change. Even a mute’s movements can speak volumes."

Dismissing the officer, Gryce sat in contemplative silence. From his pocket, he drew a small envelope and spilled its contents onto his desk: five tiny black spangles, glinting dully under the lamplight. He had always thought best with some tangible object before him—something to focus the mind. These sequins, plucked from the scene of the crime, might yet yield answers.

After a long moment, he swept them back into the envelope, checked his watch, and strode into the outer office. His gaze settled on a young man among the waiting officers—one whose keen interest had not escaped his notice. With a gesture, he summoned him inside.

"You’ve been itching for real work, Sweetwater?"

The young detective’s face lit up. "Six months of routine, sir. I’d welcome a challenge."

"Good. Because I’m handing you an impossible one." Gryce leaned forward. "A woman—unknown, unseen, except for two details: she wore a dress heavy with black spangles, and she carried a parasol. Gray chiffon, pearl handle, pink ribbons. No name, no maker’s mark. She vanished near Hudson’s fruit shop on 14th Street around half-past four—just as the telephone warning came in."

Sweetwater absorbed this with the intensity of a bloodhound catching a scent. "And the only lead?"

Gryce raised his right hand in a deliberate motion. "She made this gesture before disappearing. That, and these five spangles, are all we have."

Sweetwater’s grin was undaunted. "Then I’ll start where a woman who loses sequins would go—to replace them."

——

By evening, Gryce had exhausted every conventional avenue. Bartow remained silent. No relatives, no associates of Adams had come forward. Even the landlord knew little—only that his tenant had been a reclusive scholar, his life sealed behind steel plates and darkened windows.

Then Sweetwater returned, breathless.

"I found her."

Gryce’s pulse quickened. "How?"

"The spangles. They’re from expensive trimming—the kind only a few shops sell. I staked out Arnold’s, and a maid came in for a small piece of matching net. Followed her to a brownstone on the Upper East Side." He paused. "The parasol was the test. When I showed it, a voice from upstairs demanded to see it. Then she said—*‘You’ve come from Gryce.’*"

Gryce stiffened. "She knew my name?"

"And demanded to see you. She’s waiting outside now."

Gryce moved swiftly, curiosity overriding caution. But when he stepped into the outer office and saw the woman seated there—erect, composed, unmistakably respectable—his composure faltered.

Not just any woman.

Of all people.

Her sharp eyes met his. "Well, Mr. Gryce? Shall we talk?"

Miss Butterworth’s usual composure was absent when Mr. Gryce met her gaze. There was an uncharacteristic unease in her expression—an admission of vulnerability that even a stranger might have found striking.

"You’ve caught me," she said briskly, her tone edged with sharpness. "That gray parasol you so kindly returned to my residence is not mine. But since you’ve deduced my presence in Mr. Adams’s study, I see no point in evasion. I was there. And now, here I am to confess it."

Mr. Gryce’s interest sharpened. He drew a chair closer and sat. "You were there," he repeated. "At what time? And—though I hesitate to pry—for what purpose?"

She lifted her chin. "I must explain, if only to mitigate my own disadvantage. I have no connection to Mr. Adams, nor any excuse for inserting myself into this affair, as I did in past matters. This time, I sought it out—a lapse in judgment I now regret. Had I gone to Jericho instead, it might have spared me this humiliation."

She paused, then continued with uncharacteristic hesitation. "Mr. Gryce, I never intended to involve myself in another murder. My past successes in deduction may have earned your approval, but they did not tempt me to repeat the experience. Yet here I am, entangled once more—not by your designs, but by circumstance."

Mr. Gryce waited, patient.

She exhaled. "I was passing the house—an old, notable residence I’ve often observed with curiosity. As I glanced up, the vestibule door burst open. A young man rushed out, carrying a woman in his arms. She seemed unconscious; he was in a state of violent agitation. Before the crowd could take notice, he set her on her feet and hurried her away. Then—I heard a cry from inside. A cry of terror."

Her voice lowered. "I acted on impulse. I entered, closing the door behind me. The house was silent, but that cry drew me forward. I found myself in the circular study—and there he lay. Mr. Adams, dying, the dagger in his heart, the cross on his breast. His last breath passed as I knelt beside him."

Mr. Gryce leaned in. "The young man you saw—describe him."

"Tall, well-dressed, in a checked suit. But it was his expression that struck me—a turmoil of passion, barely restrained. The woman was pale, almost lifeless in his grip."

"And the butler? Did you encounter him?"

"No. He was not in the study when I arrived. Yet I heard his cries from within—or perhaps it was the bird, shrieking in its cage. It called a name—'Eva. Lovely Eva.'"

Mr. Gryce’s eyes narrowed. "You examined the room?"

"I did. I moved the inkstand—carefully—searching for any scrap of writing. The woman had said, 'You’ve left that line of writing behind.' I thought it might hold the key."

"And did you find it?"

"No. But I noticed something else—the look on Mr. Adams’s face as he died. Not just pain, but resolve. A final, desperate decision."

Mr. Gryce studied her. "You have a theory."

She met his gaze. "I do. And I believe you’ll find it worth considering."

With that, she leaned forward and whispered in his ear.

His eyebrows rose. Then, slowly, he smiled.

"Madam," he said, standing, "your insight does you credit. We will act on it at once."

And with a bow, he left the room.

Miss Butterworth was not a woman prone to fidgeting. Raised in the strictest school of manners, she prided herself on her composure—whether sitting in stillness or moving with brisk precision. Yet now, alone after her unsettling interview with Mr. Gryce, she found her fingers twitching against the armrest of her chair, her foot tapping an impatient rhythm. The case had gotten under her skin.

When Gryce finally returned, his expression was almost cheerful. "Well, that’s settled," he announced. "Now we wait. Unless, of course, you’ve had another stroke of insight?"

She arched a brow. "You’d like another, would you? I’d prefer to see the first one proven before venturing a second. Let’s compare notes instead. For instance—why are we both so certain Bartow isn’t the killer?"

"I thought we’d exhausted that," Gryce mused. "Your suspicions lie with the fleeing couple, while mine… well, the mute’s pantomime suggests a witness, not an actor. He’s reenacting what he saw, not what he did."

"Precisely. And insanity is more often the result of witnessing horror than committing it."

"Unless the victim was dear to him. Bartow shows no grief for Mr. Adams."

"His mind is shattered. He may not remember grief."

"True. But if we trust his gestures as truth, we must accept all of them—including the unholy satisfaction on his face afterward. That’s not the look of a horrified witness. It’s triumph."

"Or mimicry. The killer might have rejoiced."

"The young man you described didn’t seem the rejoicing sort."

Gryce exhaled. "A puzzle, then. Let’s move forward. His first gesture—reaching behind him while leaning forward. What does that signify?"

Miss Butterworth frowned. "I didn’t see that. When I observed him, he was thrusting left-handed across his body—a vicious strike. Odd, given the doctors confirm Adams was killed by a left-handed blow."

Gryce’s eyes sharpened. "You see the contradiction?"

"Of course. Bartow mirrors what he saw. If the killer struck left-handed, Bartow would mimic with his right—like a reflection. Try it yourself." She raised her right hand; instinctively, Gryce mirrored with his left.

He chuckled. "Point taken. So either the killer wasn’t left-handed, or he wasn’t standing where we assume."

"Or the doctors are wrong," she countered. "But I doubt that. What we need is to observe Bartow in that study, free to move as he pleases. We’d learn more in minutes than we will in hours of speculation."

Gryce considered. "After the funeral, perhaps. The house is swarming now."

She nodded, then hesitated. "Mr. Gryce… might my presence at the scene draw the coroner’s attention?"

He gave her a sidelong glance. "If we find those young people first, the coroner may *overlook* your involvement."

The emphasis was deliberate, and she stiffened. But there was more to confess. "There was another person in the house. An old gentleman. I didn’t mention him earlier—he seemed irrelevant, but now…"

Gryce leaned forward. "An old man? Who knew Adams?"

"Apparently. He arrived just as I was leaving—expectant, almost jovial, until he saw the body. Then…" She hesitated. "His reaction wasn’t just shock. There was hatred. Terror. And something worse—a kind of glee. It vanished when the bird screeched *‘Remember Evelyn!’* He recoiled, muttered, *‘Amos’s son, not Amos…’* then left laughing. A cruel, mocking laugh."

Gryce’s fingers drummed the table. "‘Amos’s son.’ Adams’s full name was Felix Adams. Felix, son of Amos? That’s a thread to pull. This man must be found."

She rose. "Then I leave it to you. Though I fear my boa may have left traces on the study table—bloodstains, if I leaned too close."

Gryce’s smile was thin. "A detail, but a telling one. Goodnight, Miss Butterworth."

As she departed, he didn’t dwell on the ethics of extracting so much while revealing so little. Detectives, after all, were pragmatic creatures.

The following morning, Mr. Gryce received a brief missive from Miss Butterworth at nearly the same moment she found his own note awaiting her. Hers read:

*You were correct. As far as can be determined, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams’s study table after his death. I have since trimmed the ends of my boa.*

His reply was equally succinct:

*My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams’s jaws have been pried open. A scrap of paper was found clenched between his teeth. It has been recovered and will be presented at the inquest. Certain individuals—yourself among them—may be permitted to examine it beforehand.*

Of the two, his letter naturally stirred greater excitement in its recipient. Miss Butterworth’s satisfaction was palpable, though its source remained a mystery to her household, prompting no small degree of speculation.

Meanwhile, at Police Headquarters, the floodgates of public testimony had opened. The second day’s reports brought forth a deluge of would-be witnesses—each eager to recount their sightings near Mr. Adams’s home on the fateful afternoon. Yet their accounts dissolved into vagueness when pressed for detail.

One elderly woman swore she had seen a lady of refined appearance admit an elderly gentleman in a greatcoat. A fashionable young woman, breathless with urgency, claimed a pale young lady on a gentleman’s arm had collided with her outside the house at the very hour of the murder. The descriptions, however, were frustratingly indistinct—blurred impressions of gray feathers and pallid faces.

By nightfall, little progress had been made. The mystery remained stubbornly intact.

---

The next day saw Mr. Adams laid to rest. With no kin or intimate friends to claim him, his landlord arranged the funeral, while his banker played the role of mourner. Detectives mingled with the crowd as the casket was borne away, but their vigilance yielded nothing—neither at the house nor at the cemetery.

The solution, it seemed, would have to be pieced together from the fragments already in hand.

That evening, Mr. Gryce excused himself from Headquarters and made his way alone to the darkened house. With a key, he let himself in, then methodically lit every room. The study had been restored to order—the cross rehung, the birdcage returned to its hook—save for the shattered door casings, their splintered edges still gaping. The steel plate had been pushed back into place, but its glint through the broken wall was an eyesore. With a housekeeper’s practicality, Mr. Gryce tugged two rugs from the parlor and draped them over the gaps.

A glance at his watch told him it was nearly nine. He positioned himself behind the parlor curtains and waited.

Soon enough, a carriage drew up. A lady descended, and he moved to greet her.

Miss Butterworth.

"Your punctuality matches my own," he remarked. "Have you dismissed your coachman?"

"Only to the corner," she replied. "He will await your signal."

"Excellent." He led her inside. "Would you mind waiting here while I watch for the others? At present, we are the only ones in the house."

"If I were prone to fear, I would not have come," she said dryly. "Though I must say, the lighting is quite dramatic. You’ve chosen crimson for the study?"

"Because crimson was burning when Mr. Adams died."

Miss Butterworth started. "The bird! That’s not the cry it gave before. Last time, it was *'Eva! Lovely Eva!'* Do you suppose *Eva* and *Evelyn* are the same?"

"Madam, we have riddles enough without adding that one. I expect Mr. Adams’s valet shortly."

"You mean Bartow will be here?" Her eyes sharpened. "I confess, I dreaded being excluded from this experiment."

"You’ve earned your place in it. Bartow has been given a key. He will enter as he always did, unaware of observation. Our task is simply to watch."

"In this room? I’d rather not meet him face to face."

"You won’t. He must move freely, without distraction. We’ll observe from concealment." He gestured to the parlor. "This room was rarely used. He’s unlikely to notice us here."

"Then the gas must be extinguished. An unfamiliar light would draw his eye."

"Precisely." He turned off the lamp, then adjusted the portières, leaving two small gaps—one for her eye, one for his. "A simple trick, but effective."

Miss Butterworth suppressed a sniff.

The front door creaked open. A hush fell as shuffling footsteps approached. Bartow entered the antechamber, set down his hat, and moved toward the study portière—only to pause, as if halted by an unseen force. He did not lift the drape. Instead, with mechanical resignation, he retrieved his hat and retreated upstairs.

Mr. Gryce whispered, "I’ll follow. Don’t be alarmed—this whistle will summon help instantly."

"I am *not* afraid—"

But he was already gone. He returned moments later, frowning. "He’s gone to bed. The experiment may fail."

Leading her into the study, he idly pressed a button on the table. The light shifted to violet. Miss Butterworth’s eyes widened at the sudden glow, but before she could speak, footsteps sounded again.

Bartow descended, this time with purpose. He entered the study, hesitated briefly at the threshold, then proceeded with his nightly routine: fetching wine, setting out slippers, straightening the bedroom.

When he reached the study’s center, he froze. His gaze fixed on the empty chair, then dropped to the rug where his master had lain. Memory seemed to strike him like a blow. His body tensed, his hands clenched—and then he reenacted, with eerie precision, the violent motions they now understood mirrored the killer’s.

A moment later, he was gone.

Miss Butterworth exhaled. "A futile exercise. The man is lost in his routines."

But Mr. Gryce was already pressing another button. The light deepened to blue.

Nothing happened.

"You think he can see through walls?" he murmured.

Then—footsteps.

Bartow reappeared, this time with sluggish uncertainty. He approached the table, groping for something that wasn’t there—perhaps the books he once reshelved. Confusion flickered across his face before he collapsed into a chair, drowsing.

Miss Butterworth’s lips curved. "You see? He expected books. But how does he know the light has changed?"

Mr. Gryce frowned. "There’s no line of sight from upstairs."

"Ah, but there is." Her tone turned sly. "When I went upstairs before, I noticed something—something small. You must have seen it too."

"I searched every room—"

"And the halls?"

"And the halls."

"Then you overlooked the floor."

"The *floor*?"

"Follow me."

Upstairs, she stopped at the landing. "Observe the wear here—the frayed carpet, the scuffed wood. Why would anyone linger in this spot?"

Mr. Gryce’s gaze traveled upward—to a painting hanging askew. He tugged it aside.

A small opening. A beam of blue light spilled through.

"No window," Miss Butterworth said softly. "But a peephole into the study."

His eyes gleamed. "You’ve sharp vision, madam. I wonder you didn’t investigate this sooner."

"I was in a hurry that day. And the painting wasn’t crooked then."

Below, the bird shrieked: *"Poor Eva!"*

Bartow, visible through the hole, started violently. His face twisted in fury—a fist raised toward the unseen bird.

Miss Butterworth recoiled. "He *hates* that creature."

"Which is likely why it was hung so high," Mr. Gryce mused. "Shall we return? I assure you, you’re safe."

She eyed the demented valet. "His arm may be weak, but his rage is not."

"And mine," said Mr. Gryce, offering his own, "is not weak at all."

At the foot of the stairs, Mr. Gryce paused, excusing himself briefly before summoning the men stationed outside. With quiet efficiency, he directed them to remove the valet, ensuring the scene was undisturbed before leading Miss Butterworth back into the study.

When silence settled once more, Mr. Gryce turned to her, his expression contemplative. "One thing, at least, is now clear—Bartow had no hand in the crime."

"Then we may focus entirely on the young pair," Miss Butterworth replied. "I assume you’ve uncovered nothing further?"

"Nothing."

"And the old man who laughed—no trace of him either?"

"None."

Miss Butterworth’s lips tightened in frustration. "I had hoped that scrap of writing you found would offer some direction. If it was worth swallowing in his final moments, surely it held significance."

"Unfortunately, it’s as cryptic as the rest. A hurried scrawl, reading: *‘I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!’* Signed, *‘Amos’s son.’*"

"Amos’s son—that would be Mr. Adams himself."

"So we believe."

"Strange. And yet the paper was clenched between his teeth as though his life depended on it. Was the handwriting verified?"

"Yes, it matches his signature in his books."

"The mystery deepens. To go to such lengths to hide those words—even in death—suggests they held a truth too dangerous to leave exposed. And the girl—her first words upon waking: *‘You have left that line of writing behind.’*" Miss Butterworth leaned forward. "Mr. Gryce, those words are the key. Repeat them again, line by line."

"Very well. First: *‘I return your daughter to you.’*"

"So—Mr. Adams had someone’s daughter in his keeping. But whose? Not the young man’s, unless she were a child. And if the message wasn’t meant for his assailant, why conceal it so desperately?"

"A valid point. I’ve puzzled over the same."

"Then perhaps it was intended for the old gentleman who entered later?"

"Possible. Especially given the next line: *‘She is here.’* The only woman present before the old man’s arrival was the fair-haired girl carried out."

"True. But why assume the message was freshly written? It could be an old fragment, tied to some past secret."

"Unlikely. The paper was torn from a sheet on the desk, the pen left askew—you noted its placement, I’m sure."

"I did, though I hadn’t grasped its significance. So the words were written that day, connected to the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn, or he wouldn’t have repeated the phrase *‘Remember Evelyn!’*" Miss Butterworth’s gaze drifted to the large portrait on the wall. "Shall we call her Evelyn for now? Such sorrow in that face suggests a family tragedy. But how does the young man fit into this? His violence is absent from these lines."

"A mystery indeed, madam. One that may defy our efforts."

"Yet I cannot let it go. The final words—*‘Neither she nor you will ever see me again.’* Followed by *‘Remember Evelyn!’* Could it be—was it a suicide?"

"You have the right to ask. But Bartow’s pantomime showed an attack, not self-infliction. Still, the state of Mr. Adams’s affairs—the missing documents, the lack of personal papers—suggests he anticipated death. Perhaps not like this, but he was prepared."

Miss Butterworth exhaled sharply. "There’s more you haven’t told me."

"A confession, then. This study—it conceals a secret. One I discovered in a rather startling manner. The room can be sealed off entirely, turned into a tomb. This steel plate"—he gestured to the wall—"slides across the doorway. I was trapped behind it myself; these holes were drilled to free me."

"Good heavens! A medieval death trap in modern New York?"

"Some men carry the past with them. Mr. Adams was such a man—a romantic, a mechanist, a lover of enigma. Even his death bore the mark of it. And the cross on his breast—placed by another’s hand, surely. A priest or a woman stood by as he died."

"Yet he was no dreamer when it came to practicality. His devices worked flawlessly."

"A contradiction, like the rest of him." Miss Butterworth rose, restless. "I wish his spirit *would* haunt this room. Perhaps then we’d grasp the truth."

"There’s only one path forward," Mr. Gryce said. "We must find the young couple. Without them, the case is hopeless."

"Then we’ve come full circle," she murmured. "Lost in the woods."

As they prepared to leave, Miss Butterworth’s gaze lingered on the walls. "Look there—the gap where the stair landing’s painting was removed. From this angle, it blends into the tapestry. Nearly invisible."

"Yes. With the picture restored, no one would suspect."

"One must keep their eyes moving in a case like this. That painting was shifted more than once while we were here, yet we never noticed."

"Because we didn’t look high enough," Mr. Gryce mused. "High and low, Miss Butterworth. What happens at eye level is plain to all."

His smile, as he escorted her out, held less irony than melancholy. Was it age that dulled his once-keen sight? Once, he would have missed nothing—neither the heights nor the depths. Now, even the keenest mind could falter.

"A blonde, you say?"

"Yes, Sweetwater. Not the usual sort—delicate, almost ethereal. The kind you wouldn’t suspect of any connection with crime. And him? According to Miss Butterworth, he’s the opposite—tall, powerfully built, the sort of man who draws eyes wherever he goes. Together, they’d stand out. But what makes you think we’ll find them together now?"

"How were they dressed?"

"Like people of means. He wore a brown-checked suit, freshly tailored. She was in dove gray with white trim. The parasol matches her hat—white plumes. Both young. And both, according to Miss Butterworth, seemed unused to violence. She was near collapse when he carried her out. And him? Even with every reason to keep his head, he lost control—so visibly shaken that he’d have been stopped and questioned if he hadn’t set her down in the vestibule and vanished into the crowd. Think you can find them, Sweetwater?"

"You’ve given me nothing but this parasol and a handful of dead rose petals."

"That’s all."

Sweetwater turned the parasol over in his hands, letting the dried petals scatter. "Not much to go on. If Miss Butterworth weren’t the sort to notice details, I’d call it hopeless. But I’ll try—starting where no one else would think to."

"Where’s that?"

"A florist’s. Those petals might tell us what kind of roses they were. And if I can trace the bouquet, I might trace the couple who bought it."

Gryce shrugged. "Every man has his methods. Try yours."

---

Three days later, Sweetwater returned, triumphant.

"I’ve found them. By luck, maybe—but found them. The florist said the petals could be from bride roses. That got me thinking. A woman in gray and white, a man in a new suit—it had the look of a wedding. So I checked the marriage records."

"Clever."

"Eva Poindexter. Married two weeks ago. Eva—a name tied to the house on —— Street. I followed it."

"To the groom?"

"Thomas Adams. Tall, well-built, brown-checked suit—just as described. They wed in a hotel parlor. Private affair. Her father was there."

Gryce frowned. "Her father?"

"Yes. Odd, isn’t it? But the real twist? The groom’s name—Adams. Same as the dead man’s."

---

A pause. Then Gryce leaned forward. "Tell me everything."

Sweetwater did. The couple had registered at the Waldorf, left abruptly on the day of the murder, and never returned. Their belongings were collected later by an older man—her father.

"But here’s the strangest part," Sweetwater added. "I went to Montgomery, Pennsylvania—where they came from. The Poindexters and the Cadwaladers were once close. Amos Cadwalader had a son—Felix. And a daughter, Evelyn, who died young. The same Evelyn named in those lines clenched in the dead man’s teeth."

Gryce’s fingers drummed the desk. "A thirty-year-old tragedy tangled up in this. And now an Eva marrying a man who shares the victim’s name."

"We’ve got them, sir. They’re in Belleville. The postmaster confirmed it."

"Then we move. But carefully, Sweetwater. This isn’t just a chase—it’s a minefield. One wrong step, and the whole thing blows up in our faces."

---

As Sweetwater left, Gryce penned a note to Miss Butterworth:

*Care to take a trip to Belleville? Be at the Bedell House by five. Bring a novel. If I set aside my book for a newspaper, keep one eye on yours—and one on the guests. Close the book if you recognize them. I’ll know what it means.*

The game was on.

The well-dressed woman seated on the far end of the Belleville Hotel piazza might have been engrossed in her book—had the story been less demanding. But her sharp, observant eyes, betraying both intelligence and a shrewd curiosity, strayed far more often to the lively scene before her than to the pages she mechanically turned.

The view was certainly diverting—the shimmering expanse of the sea, dotted with sails; the beach alive with brightly dressed figures; the village street bustling with activity; the lawn where young couples strolled, rackets and golf clubs in hand. Yet her attention seemed drawn less to the cheerful chaos than to the occasional solitary figures—particularly those of a certain imposing stature.

At the opposite end of the veranda, an elderly gentleman appeared wholly absorbed in his novel, his lips occasionally quirking at some unseen humor. Yet, just as abruptly, he snapped the book shut and unfolded a newspaper, shielding himself behind its broad sheets.

A shift in the air. The woman—Miss Butterworth, as discerning readers might guess—suddenly stiffened. Two men had emerged from the hotel doors, their measured steps cutting through the idle chatter of the afternoon. One was older, his posture rigid with disciplined restraint; the other, younger, carried himself with a tension that bordered on anguish.

Miss Butterworth’s reaction was immediate. She snapped her own book closed, rose with uncharacteristic haste, and retreated to the lawn, where she busied herself with a small, unimpressed terrier. Her back remained resolutely turned, though her ears strained for the murmured exchange now taking place between the two men.

*"It’s no use,"* the younger man muttered, his voice raw. *"I’m wretched beside her, and worse away. I should go back—she watches, she wonders—"*

His companion’s reply was too low to catch, but the despair in the young man’s face deepened. Miss Butterworth’s fingers tightened unconsciously on the dog’s collar, earning a sharp yelp.

She barely noticed. Her attention had been stolen by two chattering young women nearby, their gossip painting a portrait of another guest—*Mrs. Adams*, a woman too ill to leave her room, her husband too devoted to leave her side.

*"She fainted right in the middle of the piazza,"* one girl whispered. *"Just collapsed. And he—he barely moved at first. But now he won’t leave her."*

Miss Butterworth’s lips thinned. A shadow crossed her path—Sweetwater, the detective’s man, murmuring just loud enough for her to hear:

Her stomach twisted. Upstairs, in a room at the end of the hall, a fragile voice keened through the transom:

And then—silence. A faint, choked gasp. The rustle of skirts. A maid’s hurried footsteps.

Below, in the smoking room, three men now stood in a tableau of tension. The detective, Gryce, had delivered his blow with quiet finality.

The young man—*Tom Adams*—had gone ashen. His father-in-law, Poindexter, stood like a statue, his face unreadable.

A knock. A maid’s voice, urgent: *"Mrs. Adams has fainted again—she’s asking for you!"*

Tom Adams’s knees buckled. *"She knows,"* he whispered. *"She suspects. Don’t—don’t let her see this."*

Gryce’s voice was firm, but not unkind. *"You will come with me. But you may bid her farewell—briefly."*

A pause. Then, with a sudden, steeling breath, the young man straightened. *"No. I won’t have her remember me like this."* His voice hardened. *"Take me now. Quickly."*

A nod. A signal. The door opened, and a discreet escort stepped forward.

As the trio vanished, Miss Butterworth remained frozen on the stairs, her heart aching for the woman who would wake to an empty room—and a storm about to break.

Mr. Gryce was a man who understood the value of subtlety. Though he had announced his intention to follow Mr. Adams, he did so with such immediacy that he not only boarded the same train but chose a seat in the same car. His purpose was clear: to observe the young man’s demeanor when he believed himself unwatched.

What he saw intrigued him. The man who had earlier been wracked with visible anguish now sat motionless, his face a mask of unnatural calm. His eyes, though open, seemed to look inward, as if lost in some private torment. Was it the stillness of despair, or simply the detachment of a mind too burdened to register the world around him? Gryce couldn’t decide.

When they reached Brooklyn, he rejoined his prisoner, who barely acknowledged his presence. Within the hour, they stood in Police Headquarters, and the interrogation began.

To Gryce’s surprise, Adams—or rather, Cadwalader—was not reticent. He spoke with a grim urgency, as though eager to unburden himself of a truth too heavy to carry any longer. But the confession that followed was not what the detective had expected.

***

"My name," the man began, "is Thomas Cadwalader. Adams was an alias my brother Felix adopted—for reasons of his own. My father was a Scotsman who settled in Pennsylvania years ago. Felix was his son by an earlier marriage; I, the product of a later union. After my mother’s death, I was sent abroad, only returning this year to find my father on his deathbed and Felix a man of wealth—wealth he had no intention of sharing."

His voice grew bitter. "When I pressed him, he offered nothing but empty promises. Then, in Montgomery, I met a woman—Evelyn Poindexter." Here, his tone softened, then faltered. "I loved her. But I had nothing to offer. Still, I dared to hope Felix might relent if he saw her, if he understood the life I wished to build. So I married her in haste, concealing my poverty from her and her father."

He swallowed hard. "Two days after the wedding, I brought her to Felix’s home. I thought—foolishly—that even he could not resist her grace. But he was colder than I imagined. He sneered at love, at marriage, at the very idea of sentiment. And then—"

Thomas Cadwalader’s hands clenched. "We argued. I said things I should not have. He… he seized a dagger from the table. ‘You want my money?’ he snarled. ‘Then take it!’ And before I could stop him, he drove the blade into his own heart."

Silence filled the room. Then, quietly, he finished: "That is the truth. I did not kill my brother. But his blood is on my hands all the same."

The detectives exchanged glances. It was a tale almost too strange to believe. Yet the raw agony in Thomas’s voice left little room for doubt.

The question now was: would anyone else believe it?

The room was thick with tension, the air heavy with disbelief. Every man present, whether seasoned officer or hardened detective, felt the weight of the young man’s words—words that clashed violently with the silent testimony of Bartow’s pantomime. Thomas Adams stood before them, his broad shoulders slumped, his defiance crumbling under the weight of their skepticism.

“I will neither add to nor take from what I have said,” he repeated, his voice low and resolute. “Believe it or not, that is my account.”

The inspector exchanged a glance with Mr. Gryce, whose keen old eyes missed nothing. With a slight nod from the detective, the questioning resumed.

“Mr. Adams,” Gryce began, his tone measured, “if your brother took his own life as you claim, why did you flee without raising an alarm? Why conceal your relation to him?”

A bitter smile twisted the young man’s lips. “Incriminate myself? No—that’s not what I fear. Look at me. You already think me guilty. What proof do I have? None.”

The raw despair in his voice gave even the inspector pause. “You forget,” the man countered, “your wife was with you. She can verify your story.”

Adams’s face darkened. “My wife?” His voice cracked. “She is ill—fragile. Dragging her into this would destroy her. She loves me, but even she… even she cannot bring herself to believe me.”

The admission hung in the air, heavy with implication.

The inspector pressed on. “You’ve read the papers. You know your brother was found with a cross on his chest. How did it get there?”

“I placed it there,” Adams admitted. “Felix was a devout man. I thought—I hoped—it might bring him peace.”

“And the words he wrote before he died? The scrap of paper left behind?”

At this, Adams stiffened. His breath came faster, his hands trembling. The inspector held up the fragment—a silent accusation.

The young man’s resolve shattered. “Enough!” he burst out. “I lied. I killed him. But I will not say why. Hang me if you must.”

A gasp rippled through the room. Then—

A sound at the door. A figure, pale as death, swayed on the threshold.

Evelyn Adams, her frail form wrapped in a shawl, her eyes burning with desperate resolve, stepped forward.

“No!” Her voice, though weak, cut through the silence like a blade. “He lies to protect me. *I* killed Felix Cadwalader.”

The room froze.

Adams lunged for her, but she held up a hand, her gaze steady. “It’s too late, Tom. They must know the truth.”

She turned to the stunned assembly. “I did it. My hand held the dagger. But do not mistake me for a monster. There are things—terrible things—you do not know.”

Her legs buckled. Adams caught her before she fell, cradling her against his chest. When he looked up, his face was transformed—not by guilt, but by a fierce, unshakable love.

“Hear me,” he said, his voice raw. “Before you judge her—before you condemn us—listen.”

And so, with his wife unconscious in his arms and the eyes of the law upon him, Thomas Adams began his tale.

Young Adams drew a steadying breath before speaking. "What I’m about to tell you will seem both familiar and strange," he began. "Earlier, I twisted the truth—now I need to start from the beginning, as if I’d said nothing at all."

"Hold on," interrupted Mr. Gryce, raising a hand. His voice carried the weight of authority. "We’ll hear you in a moment." Leaning toward the inspector, he murmured a few quick words, then pulled out a pencil and scribbled down a series of notes. These he passed to the inspector, who studied them as Adams—no, *Cadwalader*—continued his story.

The notes, crisp and deliberate, outlined the unresolved questions that demanded answers:

1. **The Comb & The Fury** – Why did a woman composed enough to adjust her hair mid-conversation erupt into violence against a man she seemingly held no prior grudge against? *(See: the comb found in the bedroom.)*

2. **The Note’s Riddle** – What did Felix Adams mean by writing: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"* before his death?

3. **The Pronoun’s Weight** – Why did Felix refer to himself as "I" in relation to Evelyn, his brother’s bride? What history justified such a claim?

4. **The Dying Act** – Why, when attacked, did Felix try to *swallow* the very note he’d written—dying with it clenched between his teeth?

5. **The Face of the Dead** – If killed in a fit of rage (*"as monsters do,"* per his killer’s words), why did Felix’s expression show sorrow, not hatred? Where was the panic of a man taken by surprise?

6. **The Crimson Light** – With strength enough to reach for the note, why didn’t Felix switch the lamp to summon help? Why leave it blazing red—the signal for *"Stay away"*?

7. **The Steel Plate** – What purpose did the hidden mechanism in the doorway serve, and why wasn’t it triggered during the attack?

8. **Poindexter’s Timing** – How did the old man arrive so swiftly? His muttered words—*"It’s Amos’s son, not Amos!"*—suggested shock at the victim’s identity. Had he expected someone else?

9. **The Left-Handed Blow** – Bartow’s pantomime pointed to a left-handed killer. Was the woman left-handed? And why did she hold her other arm outstretched behind her?

10. **The Bird’s Cry** – Why did the parrot shift between *"Remember Evelyn!"* and *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* Had Felix known Evelyn long before her marriage?

11. **Bartow’s Madness** – If innocent, why did the servant show *joy* at the crime? What truth drove him to madness?

12. **The Lamp’s Code** – Experiments with Bartow revealed the lamp’s signals:
- *White* – Water needed.
- *Green* – Fetch coat and hat.
- *Blue* – Return books.
- *Violet* – Prepare study for night.
- *Yellow* – Wait for next signal.
- *Red* – *"Nothing needed. Keep away."*
The red light burned during the murder. Did Felix *want* no one to interfere?

With these questions hanging, the truth of the crime—and the tangled past that led to it—would finally unfold.

Thomas Cadwalader’s story was not one told in full, but rather pieced together through fragments—hints and shadows of a past that refused to stay buried. To recount it plainly would rob it of its weight, its depth, the slow-burning fire of vengeance that had smoldered for decades before igniting into tragedy. This was not merely a tale of death, but of a wound inflicted long before Felix Adams drew his last breath—a wound carved into the heart of a family, festering until it demanded blood.

And so we begin not with the end, but with the beginning—with the birth of a hatred that outlived its victims.

Thomas had never known his mother. She had faded from the world before his memory could grasp her. His father, too, was a stranger, a specter whispered about but never seen. He had been raised in France by a stern old Scotsman, a lawyer with tight lips and colder eyes, who spoke little of the past and never of the man who had sent his son across an ocean to be forgotten.

Until the day the lawyer placed a book in Thomas’s hands.

*"Read these,"* he said, his voice as dry as the pages. *"Romances, tales of grand betrayals and bloody justice. But remember—none of them, no matter how thrilling, will ever match the truth of your father’s life."*

*"My father?"* Thomas had whispered, the word unfamiliar on his tongue.

The old man had only smiled—a thin, knowing thing—and walked away.

That was the first spark.

From that moment, Thomas’s life became a pursuit of shadows. He was molded into something polished, something lethal—educated at the Sorbonne, trained in music, swordsmanship, every art that might make him formidable. His guardian watched, corrected, refined. *Too abrupt with men. Too timid with women.* How had his father known? How had he seen these flaws from across the sea?

The answer came like a blade between the ribs: *You have a brother.*

A brother. A living piece of the father he had never known. A brother who had walked the same streets as Thomas, who might have brushed past him in a crowd, who had watched him unseen—judging, assessing, reporting back.

It was then Thomas understood: he was being shaped for a purpose.

When the summons finally came, he left France without hesitation. His father was dying. There was no time for farewells, no time for doubt. He boarded a ship, then a train, then a carriage that rattled through the storm-lashed night toward a house that stood like a tomb against the sky.

The door was opened by a crone with a candle, her face a map of wrinkles and old sorrows.

*"The master’s been crying for ye,"* she muttered, her voice trembling like the flame in her hand.

The house was a ruin. Bare floors, cracked walls, the stench of damp and decay. This was not the home of a man of means. This was the lair of a ghost, a man who had waited too long for vengeance to care for comfort.

And then—the voice.

It came from the dark, a rasping, desperate sound.

Thomas climbed the stairs, his pulse hammering in his throat.

The room was a cell. A bed, a lamp, a figure swallowed by blankets—a face like a skull, eyes burning with something beyond fever.

*"At last,"* the dying man whispered.

Thomas fell to his knees.

What followed was not a reunion, but an indictment. His father’s hands clutched his, not with love, but with the fervor of a man delivering a mission.

*"You are not my only child,"* the old man gasped. *"There was a sister. Evelyn."*

The name hung in the air like a curse.

Then the story spilled forth—a tale of betrayal, of trust turned to poison. John Poindexter, his father’s oldest friend, who had taken in his children while Amos Cadwalader fought in the war. John Poindexter, who had watched Evelyn blossom into beauty—and then destroyed her.

*"She was buried before I returned,"* the old man hissed. *"They told me she had wasted away. But I knew. Felix knew. She had been murdered—not by blade or poison, but by shame."*

Thomas felt the world tilt beneath him.

And then—a new voice. Smooth, calm, cutting through the storm like a knife.

Thomas turned.

There, in the doorway, stood a man who could only be his brother. Felix. His face was a mask, his eyes dark as the night outside.

*"It is Evelyn’s story you need to hear,"* Felix said. *"And it is Evelyn’s death you must avenge."*

The wind howled. The candle guttered.

And Thomas knew—this was why he had been made.

A heavy sigh escaped Amos Cadwalader’s lips, weighted with grief that had festered for thirty years. Felix, sensing the depth of his father’s pain, pressed on without pause.

"It was dusk when I told my story. From nightfall until dawn, we sat in silence, eyes locked—neither sleeping nor resting. Then we sought out John Poindexter."

The man had not fled. He met them with cold composure, his indifference a blade to their fury. His words still echoed in Felix’s memory: *Spare yourselves the theatrics. The girl is dead; I cannot undo it. Enjoy what life remains—why dwell on old sorrows?* His final taunt had been worse: *When a man cares for nothing, curses mean nothing.*

That was the heart of it. Poindexter felt nothing—not for others, not even for himself. The brothers had been powerless against such emptiness. But before leaving, they swore an oath: one day, somehow, they would make that frozen heart bleed.

Three decades had passed. The oath remained unfulfilled.

Felix fell silent. Thomas lifted his head, but Amos silenced him with a raised hand.

"Some men forget. I never have. Nor has Felix. When you were born—after I remarried, hoping for some shred of joy—I felt, inexplicably, that Evelyn’s avenger had come. And when we learned that Poindexter, after eighteen years of marriage, had finally been given a child of his own, I looked at you—your bright eyes, your strong spirit—and knew fate had delivered my weapon."

Thomas recoiled, but Amos gripped his arm with feverish strength.

"You grew. You became everything I hoped—clever, charming, resilient. When your mother died, I no longer hesitated. Do you remember the day I took you to the edge of his estate? The road was dust, the sky darkening. I lifted you among the gravestones, pointed to his house, and swore you would destroy whatever happiness blossomed under his roof. You remember. And now—*now*—the time has come. Poindexter’s daughter is a woman."

Thomas flinched. Amos mistook his horror for hesitation.

"Woo her. Win her. They don’t know you—you’ll be Thomas Adams to them, not Cadwalader. Take her heart, and then—" His voice turned to venom. "*Crush it.* He must love her. Through her, we’ll twist the knife in his soul. Make her suffer. Let him watch. If he flinches, know I am watching from my grave—and be glad, for you will have done what I could not."

The old man’s breath faltered, but his will was iron. Thomas felt it coil around him, bending him to a hatred not his own.

"I am dying," Amos rasped. "I won’t live to see Poindexter’s ruin. But I’ll know. My spirit will rise when he screams. And when he stumbles into the dark after me—" His grip tightened. "*Remember Evelyn.*"

The words were a brand. Thomas had never known such fury. It scorched through him, drowning his protests. He raised a hand—not in refusal, but in helpless acknowledgment.

Amos misread the gesture. "You hesitate? After all I’ve done—all you’ve been given—you *dare*—?"

"He won’t fail you," Felix cut in, smooth as oil. "I’ve watched him. I know his heart. He won’t fail."

Thomas turned. Felix stood framed by a curtain of dark green, his presence as commanding as Amos’s rage. He was not a large man, but his will was a vise. His gray eyes pinned Thomas where he stood.

"You hesitate because you never knew Evelyn," Felix murmured. "Then *see* her."

He gestured. The curtain fell away—not to shadow, but to light.

A woman stood before them. Not a ghost, not a painting, but a vision so vivid Thomas felt her breath. Her eyes held sorrow deeper than any he had known.

He dropped to his knees. "Evelyn—"

"Swear," Amos demanded, arms outstretched like a cross.

"Swear," Felix echoed, laying his hand over Amos’s.

Thomas placed his beside theirs. The storm outside howled like the dead.

"I swear," he vowed, voice raw, "to win Eva Poindexter’s heart. And when it is mine—to break it at her father’s feet."

A sigh, distant as the grave. Then darkness swallowed him whole.

***

Amos Cadwalader died that night—but not before one last vision gripped him.

At midnight, his eyes flew open, fixed on the door.

"Ah," he rasped, lips twisting. "A visitor. John Poindexter. Come to beg forgiveness before I depart."

The mockery in his voice turned Thomas cold. Amos saw what they could not.

"Does he fear my meeting with Evelyn?" Amos hissed. "Then let him hear this: I forgive you, Poindexter. I forgive you my daughter’s ruin, my wasted years. But know this—" His hand shot out, seizing Thomas’s wrist. "*My son does not forgive.*"

With those final words, the oath sealed in blood, Amos Cadwalader died.

Felix had inherited none of his father’s financial ineptitude. In the two decades since Thomas had last been abroad, Felix had amassed a fortune—one their father had refused to share but had eagerly directed toward their shared vengeance. There was weight, then, in Felix’s parting words to Thomas as he set off for Montgomery:

Thomas inclined his head. *The laborer is worthy of his hire,* he thought. "And you?" he asked, glancing around the sparse walls of the cabin, now hollow without their father’s presence. "Will you stay here?"

Felix’s reply was sharp. "No. I leave for New York tomorrow. I’ve taken a house there—under the name Felix Adams. That’s how you’ll reach me. The name *Cadwalader* dies here. And remember—my face is known in Montgomery. That’s why this burden falls to you." His lips twisted in a cold smile, aware of his own advantage in looks. "Now—those introductions I asked for. You have them?"

---

I’ve arrived. I’ve seen her. She is, as you described, a pale blonde. Tomorrow, I present myself to John Poindexter. The reception, so far, promises to be favorable.

—*Thomas*

I couldn’t tell Felix the truth of today. Why? And why must I write it here? To purge the thoughts gnawing at me, perhaps.

I’ve seen her. That much I admitted to Felix—but not where, or how. How could I? Would he understand that a Poindexter could perform an act of grace? Or that I, steeped in vengeance, could feel my purpose waver at the sight of her face—the face I swore to ruin?

Before even seeking lodging, I went to the cemetery. I needed to stand where my father once held me, a child of four, and swore revenge over Evelyn’s grave. I found the weathered stone, its inscription half-lost to time, and bent to trace the letters—when the rustle of skirts broke the silence.

A young woman approached, bathed in the last light of sunset, carrying an armful of white lilies. Her gaze was fixed not on me, but on Evelyn’s grave. I stepped back into the shadows and watched as she knelt, laying the flowers—symbols of purity—over the earth.

Who was this girl who honored my sister’s memory?

When she rose, I couldn’t stay silent. I stepped forward, masking my intensity behind polite curiosity. *"Who rests here?"* I asked. *"And why do you bring lilies to a grave so old?"*

Her answer struck like a blow:

*"I bring them because she shared my birthday. She died in my father’s house, no older than I am now. And since I’ve grown old enough to understand the tragedy of youth lost, I’ve made it a tradition to share my birthday flowers with her. They say she was a lily in spirit as well as face."*

It was Eva Poindexter.

And she had been laying flowers on Evelyn’s grave.

---

I’ve shaken John Poindexter’s hand. To win the daughter, I must first charm the father—or at least hold his attention. I believe I’ve managed it.

—*Thomas*

I am no longer the man I was. John Poindexter is stern, unyielding—but he does not inspire the loathing I expected. Is it because he is Eva’s father? Has her radiance cast a halo around him? If so, Felix would curse me, and our father would—

---

My introductions have opened every door. The town has wealth, society, and she is its jewel. As for Poindexter—yes, he remains its king.

—*Thomas*

She is beautiful—not just in feature, but in essence. Delicate as a lily, yet with a strength that surfaces when duty calls. Could Felix have imagined such a Poindexter? I can’t reconcile her with the sin that doomed her family in our eyes.

In her presence, my past—the vengeance, the oath—fades. I dream of light, not darkness. Is it her smile? Her voice? Or have I, in my blindness, mistaken a saint for a woman?

---

Rides, dances, trivialities. My interest in her is noted by all—except, it seems, by her. I wonder if our plan will fail through her innocence. Today, I rode twenty-five miles to fetch her a trifle she fancied.

—*Thomas*

This will not be as simple as Felix believes. Eva Poindexter is no provincial fool. Grace alone won’t win her. Do I have what she truly values? And if I do—will my hidden purpose poison it all?

A new terror grips me: *I need her.* Not for revenge, but for herself. I’ve forgotten my oath in the hunger to possess her heart. God help me.

---

She may not love me yet—but she loves no one else. John Poindexter himself assures me of this. He seems eager to aid my suit.

A warning: if she ever does love, it will be with the force of a deep nature. The pale blonde has fire beneath the ice.

—*Thomas*

This is more than passion. It’s possession. I see through her eyes, hear through her ears. I want her happiness more than my own.

Felix, you should have chosen a harder man.

---

Evelyn’s name is never spoken in that house. Does he even remember her? And yet—her daughter does.

Must the blow fall on her? Should I pray instead that she never loves me? That she sees through me?

But no man prays against his own heart.

---

Send jewels from Tiffany’s. Her father expects gifts. If she accepts them, you’ll have your answer about her feelings.

—*Thomas*

I can’t bear a day away from her. If Felix saw my devotion, he’d applaud my dedication to the plan. *"You do not disappoint us,"* he’d say.

*Us.* As if our father still watched.

But if he does, he sees deeper than Felix. He sees that with every step closer to Eva, I recoil from the end we plotted.

I am a traitor to my oath.

---

This morning, I passed beneath Evelyn’s window—to test my own resolve. It still burns.

Eva accepted the brooch. The simplest of your gifts.

—*Thomas*

I hate John Poindexter. But not his daughter. Only Felix could confuse the two.

But must I hate her? Am I not, in some twisted way, meant to love her?

I could ask Felix. But he’d never understand. He’d come here. The thought of him watching her—*judging her*—sickens me.

---

A mountain picnic. I escorted Eva down a treacherous slope. No words of love were spoken—but I’ve gained ground.

—*Thomas*

I’ve touched her. Held her. On those lonely slopes, we might have been the only two souls alive. Yet I dared no more than if all Paris watched.

How I love her. How distant now seems that old crime, that oath sworn over my father’s dying hand—

---

Felix is here.

A note commanded me to meet him at Evelyn’s grave—midnight.

I dread it. He relies too much on theatrics. But the time for such tricks is past. I’ll show him that neither place nor hour can sway me now.

Not with her glove on my table, and her face in my dreams.

Evelyn is sacred. But the dead must not rule the living.

---

I wouldn’t have recognized Felix in daylight. In the thin moonlight, he was a stranger—voice altered, manner hesitant.

He didn’t ask the questions I feared. Didn’t rage. Only said coldly: *"Shorten the courtship. Two weeks. Then it ends."*

Then he turned to Evelyn’s grave, tracing her name on the mossy stone. His hands shook. His body was cold as the slab he leaned against.

Was it grief? If so, it was the first honest thing between us.

As I turned to leave, he seized my arm. *"You’ve seen the girl. She’s changed. Some might call her beautiful."* His grip tightened. *"But beauty must not blind you. Remember John Poindexter. His debt. Our vengeance."*

His voice dropped. *"The sweeter she is, the deeper the knife will cut him."*

The old spell gripped me. I said nothing.

*"I return to New York tomorrow,"* he said. *"In two weeks, you’ll telegraph your readiness to join me."*

Then, clutching his chest, he added, *"You may hear from me again."*

He vanished into the dark. I hadn’t spoken a word.

---

No word from you. Are you well?

No progress to report. Oddly, John Poindexter has taken to advising me on courtship. What do you make of that?

—*Thomas*

Felix hasn’t left. He’s watching.

I’ve glimpsed him—lurking in Poindexter’s garden as Eva and I talked by the window. Skulking in church shadows as we sat together.

He’s thinner. The strain wears on him.

Should I end this? Admit my betrayal? Declare I’ll marry her, oath be damned?

---

Eva has confessed she cares for me.

Are you satisfied now?

—*Thomas*

She loves me.

I held her, pressed until the words came. Now let Felix do his worst.

What is John Poindexter to me? Her father.

What is Amos Cadwalader’s hatred? A ghost.

Life—*life with her*—is all I want.

I’ll write Felix tomorrow.

---

I didn’t write Felix.

I couldn’t.

---

A dream last night:

My father and Evelyn meeting in some shadowed beyond. Light followed her steps; darkness trailed his.

As they parted, he cried:

I woke before her reply. But the question haunts me.

---

Felix came again.

This time, the truth was spoken.

I’d been pressing Eva to name the wedding day. We stood together—John Poindexter, Eva, and I—when a groan came from the garden. A faint thud.

Felix.

He’d been watching. Heard me speak of marriage.

I found him by the old sundial, pale as death in the starlight.

*"You play your part too well,"* he said. *"Marriage? That was never the plan."*

I forced the words out: *"I love her. I will marry her. Then—"*

I couldn’t finish.

Something inhuman passed between us. A gust of wind? A trick of light? For an instant, I saw his face clearly—and the agony there was worse than any rage.

He said nothing.

I babbled excuses—her goodness, her purity. The darkness returned, mercifully hiding him.

At last, desperate, I cried: *"You’ve never loved a woman, Felix! You don’t know what this is!"*

He remained silent. A statue of grief.

Then, just as I turned to leave, his voice came, hollow:

And he was gone.

Beneath the cold precision of Felix Cadwalader’s exterior raged a tempest no one could see—a war waged in silence, in the shadowed corners of a mind that allowed no witness. The evidence of it? Fragments of letters, torn and hidden, as if even the act of writing them was a betrayal. They surfaced later, tucked beneath the floorboards of Bartow’s room, their origins as obscure as the man who had hoarded them.

* * *

I saw Eva Poindexter again. And now, heaven and hell duel within me. *Eva.* The girl I dismissed as mere leverage, the one I handed to my brother like a pawn in our game. But she is no pawn. She is sunlight breaking through a storm—too radiant for him, too pure for any man who has not lived untouched by love until this moment.

Is this love? Can *I*, Felix—whose heart has been iron, whose purpose has never wavered—harbor such weakness? Impossible. And yet.

Her eyes, half-lowered yet piercing. Her lips, parted in unguarded honesty. The way she carries herself, tenderness woven with unyielding strength. She is ruin dressed in grace. I should flee. I should never look upon her again.

Thomas does not waver. He must never see that I do.

If only he had not been molded in Paris, perhaps he too would—but no. I alone am weak. A curse upon my—

Did I swear to leave? I cannot. Not yet. One more glimpse—to prove this madness is real, that it is *her* and not some flaw in my own mind. Perhaps yesterday was illusion. Perhaps I mistook radiance for the ordinary. I will stay. I will—

Thomas will not. *Shall* not—

Poindexter’s daughter.

Hate. Hate for Thomas.

* * *

My pride is salvaged. I saw them together. His gaze held devotion—how could it not?—and I played my part. I was stone. She loves him—or he believes she does—and the plan moves forward. But I will not watch it unfold. No. No.

Last night, I told him my story disguised as another man’s past. How the devils must laugh at us. Does my father, in whatever hell he inhabits, laugh too?

Home now, where silence amplifies the torment. I pace. I whisper her name like a prayer, even beneath Evelyn’s portrait. There are moments I consider writing Thomas—forbidding him—

Every nerve screams it. But no one will know. Thomas least of all. Let him never guess how she consumes me, how the thought of her is a blade twisting in my ribs.

I wait for his letters—brief, cold—like a condemned man awaits the gallows. Does she truly love him? Or does a soul like hers crave something fiercer, a will unbroken, a—

Once, in the depths of despair, I imagined my father rising from the grave to curse me. Let him. If only—

* * *

I thought no man knew my secret. But Bartow knows. Deaf, mute, yet he *sees*. His sidelong glances, his restless discontent—the jealousy of a servant toward his master’s weakness. He was never jealous of Evelyn. Her name once filled these rooms; my eyes fixed only on her portrait, that face of sorrowful beauty. But now?

A new horror: the starling, trained to murmur Evelyn’s name, now shrieks *"Eva! Eva!"* My first instinct—to crush its throat. My second—to press it to my chest. I did neither. I am still a man.

Bartow will kill that bird if I don’t stop him. This morning, I caught him hovering by the cage, murder in his eyes. He cannot hear the name, but he knows it is *hers*. Should I move the cage beyond his reach? His obedience, his isolation, his animal devotion—I need them now. But the bird—

Is this love or hate? Salvation or damnation? Love would seek to spare her. But I do not. Not now. Not when she has given her heart to Thomas. Not when he—

This is no longer about Evelyn. This is *mine*. The sleepless nights. The unraveling of my own nature. The agony of conceding their happiness while I choke on bitterness.

They will not escape.

I have planned it all—every step, every consequence. Time, money, cunning—it has cost me everything. Let them cross the threshold of my circular study. They will bend to my will or perish there. With me.

They will not live happily. Thomas will not. John Poindexter will not.

I might have forsaken the oath sworn over my father’s corpse. But I will not forget this torment. This humiliation.

And still—*Eva. Eva. Eva.*

Thomas’s confession unfolded with grim precision before the detectives:

True to my word, I brought my young bride to Felix’s home at the appointed hour. The house loomed before us, its entrance guarded by a deaf-mute servant—Bartow—whose presence sent an uneasy tremor through Eva. But I steadied her with a reassuring smile, masking my own creeping dread as we followed the old man through the dim, oppressive halls.

Felix awaited us in his study, a chamber so bizarre it bordered on surreal—gold-lit vaults, grotesque masks leering from the tapestries, the air thick with the scent of incense. At its center stood Felix, pale as marble, his eyes burning with a cold intensity. Eva, unprepared for the force of his presence, faltered—but only for a moment. She stepped forward, graceful as ever, offering him a smile meant to disarm.

It did not.

His gaze locked onto her, unreadable, relentless. When I asked for a private word, he led me aside with a mocking courtesy that stung like a lash. "Your wife will wait *here*," he said, ushering her into a small adjoining room before sealing us in his domain.

Alone with him, I made my plea. "Felix, I cannot keep my oath. Not now. Not after knowing her."

His expression hardened. "I anticipated this. And I have prepared."

The room seemed to tighten around us. My eyes darted to the high, slit-like windows, then to the door—now the only exit. Felix’s hand hovered near a button on the table.

"Understand this," he said, his voice glacial. "If you defy me, that door will seal. Permanently. The house is already being locked down. Bartow leaves in minutes. No one will hear you. No one will come."

A bird’s voice—harsh, sudden—cut through the silence: *"Remember Evelyn!"*

I recoiled. Felix barely flinched. "A starling," he said dismissively. Then, relentless: "John Poindexter will arrive soon. He’ll find your note—or he’ll find *nothing*." He slid a paper toward me. *Sign it*, his silence demanded.

The words were a knife to my soul:

> *I return you back your daughter. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!*
>

I refused. "Better death for us all than this."

His finger twitched toward the button—

Then Eva stepped into the room.

The light shifted, staining her cheeks crimson. She had heard enough. "What is this?" she demanded, her voice steady despite the terror in her eyes.

Felix turned to her. "A choice. Part in shame, or perish together."

For a heartbeat, she wavered. Then—swift as thought—she seized a dagger from the table and drove it into his side. "We cannot part. We cannot die."

Felix staggered. Blood darkened his sleeve. Yet he did not fall—not yet. His gaze locked onto Eva with something beyond fury, beyond hate.

*"Eva! I love Eva!"* screeched the bird overhead.

The truth struck me like lightning. *He loved her too.*

His knees buckled. As he collapsed, his final words were a whisper: "Save her. She did not know."

Eva stood frozen, her hands trembling. "I’ve killed him," she breathed.

I acted fast. Snatching the crucifix from the wall, I pressed it into Felix’s slack fingers. Then I lifted Eva—limp with shock—and fled.

Outside, the world moved on, oblivious. I bundled her into a carriage, my mind already racing ahead—to silence, to survival.

She wanted to confess. I would not let her. Neither would her father, when he learned the truth. John Poindexter’s pride was armor no grief could pierce.

But Felix’s last words haunted me: *She did not know.*

Perhaps none of us had.

A heavy silence filled the room as the weight of Thomas Adams’s confession settled over the gathered investigators. The tension was palpable—each person present grappling with the implications of what had been revealed.

Inspector Reynolds, after a moment of quiet deliberation, returned the annotated memorandum to Mr. Gryce. The once-mysterious questions now bore the marks of resolution, each line crossed with the decisive verdict: *Answered.*

1. **The Comb & The Fury** – The woman’s initial composure, pausing to arrange her hair, clashed with the violent rage that followed. Now, the truth was clear: the confrontation had escalated beyond control, her fury ignited by revelations too devastating to bear.

2. **The Final Words** – *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"* These words, once cryptic, now carried their full weight. They were a farewell—and a confession.

3. **The Pronoun "I"** – Felix Adams had not been a stranger to Evelyn. His claim over her was personal, intimate. The bond between them had been severed by her marriage, yet his final act was to acknowledge that loss.

4. **The Swallowed Secret** – Even in death, Felix had sought to hide the damning words, clutching them between his teeth. A final, desperate act to shield the living from the full truth.

5. **The Face of Sorrow** – His expression in death had not been one of rage, but of sorrow. He had expected violence—perhaps even welcomed it—but not from the hand that struck him.

6. **The Crimson Light** – The red glow, signaling *"Nothing more required. Keep away,"* had been deliberate. Felix had ensured no interruptions. He had chosen his fate.

7. **The Steel Plate** – A barrier meant for protection—yet it remained unused. Had he disabled it? Or had he simply accepted that no defense could shield him from this reckoning?

8. **Poindexter’s Shock** – The old man’s cry—*"It is Amos’ son, not Amos!"*—spoke of mistaken expectation. He had come anticipating another, only to find the son fallen instead.

9. **The Left-Handed Blow** – Thomas’s reenactment provided the missing piece: his grip had restrained her right hand, forcing the fatal strike with her left. The unnatural posture Bartow had mimed now made terrible sense.

10. **The Bird’s Cry** – *"Remember Evelyn!"* and *"Who would strike Eva?"* The parrot’s words were echoes of Felix’s own torment. He had loved her—and in the end, that love had destroyed them both.

11. **Bartow’s Madness** – The servant’s twisted joy was no longer a mystery. His hatred for Felix had festered in silence, and witnessing his master’s downfall had been his vengeance.

12. **The Signal Lights** – The red glow had been no accident. Felix had ensured solitude in his final moments.

Only one question lingered—Bartow’s reaction, the depth of his madness—but even that, in time, would find its answer.

As Mr. Gryce marked the last line with *"Answered,"* the room exhaled. The truth, though brutal, was complete.

Justice, at last, had its story.

A tense silence lingered in the air as Mr. Gryce stepped into the outer room, his sharp eyes scanning the assembled faces. A single question burned in his mind—one he hoped the scene before him might answer. And there, poised with the quiet assurance of a woman who knew far more than she let on, stood Miss Butterworth.

He approached with a knowing smile. "You’ve had a hand in this, I presume?"

She met his gaze with an unflinching lift of her chin. "I was merely present when events unfolded. When the young Mrs. Adams discovered her husband had vanished into the night with two strangers, it became clear she needed someone at her side who understood the gravity of her situation. That someone, as fate would have it, was me."

"Ah," Gryce murmured, "you do have a habit of appearing where you’re most needed. So you’ve heard her confession?"

"I have. And once I did, there was no question of what must be done. Tell me, Mr. Gryce—will she face trial for what happened? Will the law punish a woman who acted in desperation?"

His expression softened. "Twelve reasonable men would never condemn her for defending her own life. No charges will stand. You may set your mind at ease."

Relief flickered across Miss Butterworth’s usually composed features. "Then my work here is done. These hours have worn on me more than I care to admit. Would you be so kind as to see me to my carriage?"

Gryce suppressed a smile. If exhaustion weighed on her, it did little to diminish her formidable presence. Still, he obliged, offering his arm as she swept past the murmuring onlookers.

Before she departed, however, he had one final question. "And her father—Poindexter? Why was he absent when his daughter needed him most?"

A shadow crossed her face. "That man has ice in his veins. He accompanied us to the city but refused to step foot in this place. Some men, Mr. Gryce, are incapable of feeling even the sharpest blade of justice. Do not speak his name to me again." With a regal tilt of her head, she added, "Now, if you would direct my driver to Gramercy Park? My days of meddling in police affairs are over—quite decisively over."

Yet as she settled into the plush interior of the carriage, there was something in the set of her mouth—a quiet, undeniable satisfaction.

Gryce watched the carriage roll away, his own lips quirking briefly before he schooled his features back into solemnity. Would fate ever conspire to cross their paths again in such a manner? He doubted it. And yet, the world was full of mysteries.
