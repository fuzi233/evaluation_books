It was, as Mrs. Archer said with her smile that never lost its edge, a great event for a young couple to give their first big dinner.

Since the Newland Archers had set up their house there had been, as the phrase went, “a good deal of company”—three or four friends dropped in to dine, a familiar clink of glasses, a joint carved without ceremony, May’s bright readiness set like a napkin across the whole table. Archer liked such evenings. They had the homely mercies of routine: the servants moved with less self-consciousness, the silver seemed less on parade, and the talk, if it did not become truer, at least grew less rehearsed. Yet even in those small gatherings he had felt how little of May’s impulse belonged to herself, and how much to the pattern pressed on her by mothers, aunts, traditions—by the old New York conviction that marriage was a social duty performed in public, like attending church or sending one’s card on New Year’s Day.

But a big dinner was another matter. A hired chef meant a stranger in the kitchen giving orders in a tongue the household did not speak; two borrowed footmen meant borrowed hands carrying borrowed importance; Roman punch meant the whole ceremonial unrolled like a carpet—canvas-backs or terrapin, two soups in procession, a hot sweet and a cold, shoulders bared under gaslight, and guests of that rank which required you to be careful not only what you served, but what you implied.

It was always curious to watch a young pair launch their first invitations in the third person, their names printed as if they were an institution. The summons was seldom refused. Still, it was triumph enough that the van der Luydens, at May’s request, should have stayed over to be present at her farewell dinner for the Countess Olenska.

The two mothers-in-law sat in May’s drawing-room on the afternoon of the great day, as if presiding over a rite older than either family. Mrs. Archer wrote out menus on Tiffany’s thickest gilt-edged bristol, her sharp gold pen scratching with the dry certainty of one who knew exactly how many syllables a dish might have without seeming pretentious. Mrs. Welland superintended palms and standard lamps, moving furniture a fraction of an inch as though a corner wrongly created might shift the moral balance of the evening.

Archer, coming late from the office, found them still at it. Mrs. Archer had turned from the menus to the name-cards; Mrs. Welland was considering whether the large gilt sofa ought to be brought forward to make a new “corner” between piano and window.

May, they told him, was in the dining-room inspecting the mound of Jacqueminot roses and maidenhair in the centre of the long table, and the placing of the Maillard bonbons in openwork silver baskets between the candelabra. On the piano stood a large basket of orchids Mr. van der Luyden had sent from Skuytercliff—blooms whose perfume had the thick, expensive languor of a conservatory in winter. Everything was, in short, as it should be when a household meant to show that it knew how to do what must be done.

Mrs. Archer ran thoughtfully over the list, checking off each name as if she were ticking the beats in a score.

“Henry van der Luyden—Louisa—the Lovell Mingotts—the Reggie Chiverses—Lawrence Lefferts and Gertrude—(yes, I suppose May was right to have them)—the Selfridge Merrys, Sillerton Jackson, Van Newland and his wife. How time passes! It seems only yesterday he was your best man, Newland—and Countess Olenska—yes, I think that’s all.”

Mrs. Welland surveyed her son-in-law with that affectionate approval which made admiration feel like supervision. “No one can say, Newland, that you and May are not giving Ellen a handsome send-off.”

“Ah, well,” said Mrs. Archer, “I understand May’s wanting her cousin to tell people abroad that we’re not quite barbarians.”

“I’m sure Ellen will appreciate it. She was to arrive this morning, I believe. It will make a most charming last impression. The evening before sailing is usually so dreary,” Mrs. Welland continued, as if dreariness were a thing one could banish with hot soup and well-timed orchids.

Archer turned toward the door. “Do go in and have a peep at the table. And don’t let May tire herself too much,” his mother-in-law called after him. He affected not to hear. He sprang upstairs to his library.

The room looked back at him like an alien face arranged into polite features. It had been ruthlessly tidied, prepared by a judicious distribution of ash-trays and cedar-wood boxes for the gentlemen to smoke in later. Even the air had been scoured of any trace of private life, as if the day had demanded not a dinner but a small surrender.

“Ah, well,” he thought, “it’s not for long—” and went on to his dressing-room.

Ten days had passed since Madame Olenska’s departure from New York. In those ten days there had been no sign from her except the return of a key, wrapped in tissue paper, sent to his office in a sealed envelope addressed in her hand. It might have been, in another story, a move in a familiar game; Archer chose to read it as something else. She was still fighting, he told himself, not against him but against what claimed her. She was going to Europe, and she was not returning to her husband. Nothing, therefore, would prevent his following her; and once he had taken the irrevocable step—once she had seen that it could not be undone—he believed she would not send him away.

That confidence steadied him. It kept him from writing to her, from betraying by any sign his misery and mortification. He felt, absurdly, that in the deadly silent game between them the trumps were still in his hands, and he waited.

Yet there had been moments he had had to pass through as one walks through a room in which everyone has stopped talking. The day after Madame Olenska’s departure, Mr. Letterblair had sent for him to go over the details of the trust Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to create for her granddaughter. For two hours Archer examined the terms of the deed, hearing the lawyer’s voice as if it came from behind a screen, and all the while feeling that he had been summoned for something other than cousinship, and that the real purpose would appear at the end.

“Well, the lady can’t deny that it’s a handsome arrangement,” Mr. Letterblair had summed up at last, mumbling over the settlement. “In fact I’m bound to say she’s been treated pretty handsomely all round.”

“All round?” Archer echoed, with a touch of derision. “Do you refer to her husband’s proposal to give her back her own money?”

Mr. Letterblair’s bushy eyebrows rose a fraction. “My dear sir, the law’s the law; and your wife’s cousin was married under the French law. It’s to be presumed she knew what that meant.”

“Even if she did—what happened subsequently—” Archer began; but he paused. The lawyer had laid his pen-handle against his big corrugated nose and looked down it with the expression virtuous elderly gentlemen put on when they wish their youngers to understand that virtue is not synonymous with ignorance.

“My dear sir, I’ve no wish to extenuate the Count’s transgressions; but—but on the other side… I wouldn’t put my hand in the fire… well, that there hadn’t been tit for tat… with the young champion…” Mr. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and pushed a folded paper toward Archer. “This report, the result of discreet enquiries…”

Archer made no effort to glance at it, no effort to deny. The lawyer, a little flatly, continued: “I don’t say it’s conclusive, you observe; far from it. But straws show… and on the whole it’s eminently satisfactory for all parties that this dignified solution has been reached.”

“Oh, eminently,” Archer had said, pushing back the paper.

A day or two later, when Mrs. Manson Mingott sent for him, the ordeal had been more trying because it was dressed in affection.

He found the old lady depressed and querulous, sunk in her chair as in a featherbed of grievances.

“You know she’s deserted me?” she began at once; and without waiting for his reply: “Oh, don’t ask me why! She gave so many reasons I’ve forgotten them all. My private belief is that she couldn’t face the boredom. At any rate that’s what Augusta and my daughters-in-law think. And I don’t know that I altogether blame her. Olenski’s a finished scoundrel; but life with him must have been a good deal gayer than it is in Fifth Avenue. Not that the family would admit that: they think Fifth Avenue is Heaven with the rue de la Paix thrown in. And poor Ellen, of course, has no idea of going back to her husband. She held out as firmly as ever against that. So she’s to settle down in Paris with that fool Medora… Well, Paris is Paris; and you can keep a carriage there on next to nothing. But she was as gay as a bird, and I shall miss her.”

Two tears, the parched tears of the old, rolled down her puffy cheeks and vanished in the abysses of her bosom.

“All I ask is,” she concluded, “that they shouldn’t bother me any more. I must really be allowed to digest my gruel…” She twinkled a little wistfully at Archer, as if even gruel, properly digested, were a kind of dignity.

It was that evening, on his return home, that May announced her intention of giving a farewell dinner to her cousin.

Madame Olenska’s name had not been spoken between them since the night of her flight to Washington. Archer looked at his wife with surprise, as if a drawer had suddenly slid open in a room where all the furniture had been counted.

“A dinner—why?” he asked.

Her colour rose. “But you like Ellen—I thought you’d be pleased.”

“It’s awfully nice—your putting it in that way. But I really don’t see—”

“I mean to do it, Newland,” she said quietly, rising and going to her desk. “Here are the invitations all written. Mother helped me—she agrees that we ought to.”

She paused, embarrassed and yet smiling; and in that instant Archer saw, more clearly than he had ever seen it, the embodied image of the Family—its collective will dressed in a girl’s gentleness.

“Oh, all right,” he said, staring with unseeing eyes at the list of guests she put into his hand.

When he entered the drawing-room before dinner May was stooping over the fire, coaxing the logs to burn in their unaccustomed setting of immaculate tiles. The sound of the poker on clean brick struck him as loud as a hammer in a church: the house, for all its velvet and gilt, had an acoustic of restraint, every noise sharpened by the expectation of silence.

The tall lamps were all lit. The rosy shades gave the room a tropical glare, and the palms made soft, faint rustlings—leaf against leaf—whenever a door opened and the draught moved through. Mr. van der Luyden’s orchids had been conspicuously disposed in modern porcelain and knobby silver. Mrs. Newland Archer’s drawing-room was generally thought a great success: the gilt bamboo jardinière crowded with primulas and cinerarias punctually renewed, the pale brocade sofas grouped about little plush tables densely covered with silver toys, porcelain animals, and efflorescent photograph frames. It was a room that smelled not of people but of flowers trained to bloom on command; and even their perfume seemed disciplined.

“I don’t think Ellen has ever seen this room lighted up,” May said, rising flushed from her struggle, sending about her a glance of pardonable pride.

The brass tongs she had propped against the side of the chimney fell with a crash that drowned her husband’s answer. The sound rang out—an honest clang in a room built for soft footfalls—and then, before he could restore them, Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden were announced.

The other guests quickly followed, for it was known the van der Luydens liked to dine punctually. Voices blended, then separated; silk made its small, dry murmurs; the servants’ movements had the measured hush of trained steps. Archer found himself showing Mrs. Selfridge Merry a small, highly-varnished Verbeckhoven “Study of Sheep,” which Mr. Welland had given May for Christmas, when he became aware that Madame Olenska stood at his side.

She was excessively pale, and her pallor made her dark hair seem denser and heavier than ever. Perhaps it was that, or the fact that she had wound several rows of amber beads around her neck—amber with a resinous, forest smell if one warmed it in one’s palm—that reminded him suddenly of the little Ellen Mingott he had danced with at children’s parties when Medora Manson first brought her to New York.

The amber was trying to her complexion, or her dress was unbecoming: her face looked lustreless, almost ugly, and he had never loved it as he did at that minute. Their hands met. He thought he heard her say, “Yes, we’re sailing tomorrow in the Russia—”; then came the unmeaning noise of opening doors, and after an interval May’s voice:

“Newland! Dinner’s been announced. Won’t you please take Ellen in?”

Madame Olenska put her hand on his arm. He noticed the hand was ungloved, and remembered how his eyes had kept coming back to it in the little Twenty-third Street drawing-room. All the beauty that had forsaken her face seemed to have taken refuge in the long pale fingers and faintly dimpled knuckles resting on his sleeve; and he said to himself, with a kind of physical ache: If it were only to see her hand again I should have to follow her.

It was only at an entertainment ostensibly offered to a “foreign visitor” that Mrs. van der Luyden could suffer the diminution of being placed on her host’s left. The fact of Madame Olenska’s foreignness could hardly have been more adroitly emphasized than by this farewell tribute; and Mrs. van der Luyden accepted her displacement with an affability that left no doubt as to her approval. There were things that had to be done, and if done at all, done handsomely and thoroughly; and one of these, in the old New York code, was the tribal rally around a kinswoman about to be eliminated from the tribe.

There was nothing on earth the Wellands and Mingotts would not have done to proclaim their unalterable affection for the Countess Olenska now that her passage for Europe was engaged; and Archer, at the head of his table, marveled at the silent, untiring activity with which her popularity had been retrieved, grievances silenced, her past countenanced, her present irradiated by family approval. Mrs. van der Luyden shone on her with the dim benevolence that was her nearest approach to cordiality, and Mr. van der Luyden, from his seat at May’s right, cast down the table glances plainly intended to justify all the carnations he had sent from Skuytercliff.

The meal itself moved like a practiced procession. The soup arrived with its faint steam and its careful garnish; the fish followed, then the canvas-backs, their rich, dark meat giving off that unmistakable savor of wild feeding—marsh and grain and distance—lifted and intensified by the sharpness of currant jelly. The Roman punch came in with its cold sting and its sweetened fire, a frosted promise of release that dissolved on the tongue and left only a clearer awareness of the room’s heat and of the crowd’s controlled breathing. Archer could hear the thin chiming of forks on china, the muffled slide of chairs on carpet, the murmur of voices trained never to rise too high; and beneath it all, like a second, secret music, the pulse of implication—what was not said but was heard, what was not looked at but was seen.

He felt himself assisting at the scene in a state of odd imponderability, as if he floated somewhere between chandelier and ceiling. As his glance traveled from one placid well-fed face to another he saw all the harmless-looking people engaged upon May’s canvas-backs as a band of dumb conspirators, and himself and the pale woman on his right as the center of their conspiracy. And then it came over him in a vast flash made up of many broken gleams: to all of them he and Madame Olenska were lovers—lovers in the extreme sense peculiar to foreign vocabularies.

He guessed himself to have been for months the center of countless silently observing eyes and patiently listening ears. He understood that by means unknown to him the separation between himself and the partner of his guilt had been achieved; and that now the whole tribe had rallied about his wife on the tacit assumption that nobody knew anything—or had ever imagined anything—and that the evening’s entertainment was simply May Archer’s natural desire to take an affectionate leave of her friend and cousin.

It was the old New York way of taking life without effusion of blood: the way of people who dreaded scandal more than disease, who placed decency above courage, and who considered that nothing was more ill-bred than scenes, except the behavior of those who gave rise to them.

As these thoughts succeeded each other Archer felt like a prisoner in the center of an armed camp. Over the Florida asparagus—pale spears smelling faintly of earth and forced warmth—they were dealing with Beaufort and his wife.

“It’s to show me,” he thought, “what would happen to me—” and a deathly sense of the superiority of implication and analogy over direct action, and of silence over rash words, closed in on him like the doors of the family vault.

He laughed, abruptly, and met Mrs. van der Luyden’s startled eyes.

“You think it laughable?” she said with a pinched smile. “Of course poor Regina’s idea of remaining in New York has its ridiculous side, I suppose;” and Archer muttered: “Of course.”

At that moment he became conscious that Madame Olenska’s other neighbor had been engaged for some time, and that the lady on his right had been left without the expected civilities. He saw May, serenely enthroned between Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Selfridge Merry, cast a quick glance down the table—a glance that was not reproachful, only arranging. It was evident that the host and the lady on his right could not sit through the whole meal in silence.

He turned to Madame Olenska. Her pale smile met him, and in it he read a kind of weary courage: Oh, do let’s see it through.

“Did you find the journey tiring?” he asked; and his own voice surprised him by its naturalness, as if the words had been ladled out by habit like soup.

“On the contrary,” she answered, “I’ve seldom traveled with fewer discomforts. Except—the dreadful heat in the train.”

He remarked that she would not suffer from that particular hardship in the country she was going to.

“I never,” he declared with intensity, “was more nearly frozen than once, in April, in the train between Calais and Paris.”

She said she did not wonder, but remarked that one could always carry an extra rug, and that every form of travel had its hardships. He returned abruptly that he thought them all of no account compared with the blessedness of getting away.

She changed color. He heard his own voice rise as if the room’s enforced hush made every sharp note louder.

“I mean to do a lot of traveling myself before long.”

A tremor crossed her face. He leaned over to Reggie Chivers and cried out—too loudly, as if the desire for distance had to be shouted above the walls:

“I say, Reggie, what do you say to a trip round the world: now, next month, I mean? I’m game if you are—”

Mrs. Reggie piped up that she could not think of letting Reggie go till after the Martha Washington Ball she was getting up for the Blind Asylum in Easter week; and Reggie, placidly, observed that by that time he would have to be practicing for the International Polo match.

Mr. Selfridge Merry caught the phrase “round the world,” and, having once circled the globe in his steam-yacht, seized the chance to send down the table several striking items concerning the shallowness of Mediterranean ports. It didn’t matter, he added, for when you’d seen Athens and Smyrna and Constantinople, what else was there? Mrs. Merry said she could never be too grateful to Dr. Bencomb for making them promise not to go to Naples on account of the fever.

“But you must have three weeks to do India properly,” her husband conceded, anxious to be understood as no frivolous globe-trotter.

At that point the ladies went up to the drawing-room, the drift of skirts and the soft closing of the door altering the room’s sound at once: fewer voices, more smoke, and the relieved scrape of chairs.

In the library, in spite of weightier presences, Lawrence Lefferts predominated. The men settled into their places, glasses touched, cigars were cut; the air thickened with toasted leaf and the sweet, heavy scent of Madeira. Archer could hear the faint crackle of the fire downstairs, as if the house itself were eating slowly.

The talk, as usual, veered around to the Beauforts; and even Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Selfridge Merry, installed in the honorary arm-chairs reserved for them, paused to listen to Lefferts’s philippic.

Never had Lefferts so abounded in the sentiments that adorn Christian manhood and exalt the sanctity of the home. Indignation lent him a scathing eloquence. It was clear that if others had followed his example and acted as he talked, society would never have been weak enough to receive a foreign upstart like Beaufort—no, sir, not even if he’d married a van der Luyden or a Lanning instead of a Dallas. And what chance would there have been, Lefferts wrathfully questioned, of his marrying into such a family as the Dallases if he had not already wormed his way into certain houses, as people like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers had managed to worm theirs in his wake? If society chose to open its doors to vulgar women the harm was not great, though the gain was doubtful; but once it got in the way of tolerating men of obscure origin and tainted wealth, the end was total disintegration—and at no distant date.

“If things go on at this pace,” Lefferts thundered, looking like a young prophet dressed by Poole and not yet stoned, “we shall see our children fighting for invitations to swindlers’ houses, and marrying Beaufort’s bastards.”

“Oh, I say—draw it mild!” Reggie Chivers and young Newland protested. Mr. Selfridge Merry looked genuinely alarmed, and pain and disgust settled on Mr. van der Luyden’s sensitive face.

“Has he got any?” cried Mr. Sillerton Jackson, pricking up his ears; and while Lefferts tried to turn the question with a laugh, the old gentleman twittered into Archer’s ear: “Queer, those fellows who are always wanting to set things right. The people who have the worst cooks are always telling you they’re poisoned when they dine out. But I hear there are pressing reasons for our friend Lawrence’s diatribe:—typewriter this time, I understand….”

The talk swept past Archer like some senseless river running and running because it did not know enough to stop. He saw on the faces about him expressions of interest, amusement, even mirth. He listened to the younger men’s laughter, and to the praise of the Archer Madeira which Mr. van der Luyden and Mr. Merry were thoughtfully celebrating, as if the wine’s age were a moral guarantee.

Through it all he was dimly aware of a general attitude of friendliness toward himself, as if the guard of the prisoner he felt himself to be were trying to soften his captivity; and that perception increased his passionate determination to be free.

When they joined the ladies again he met May’s triumphant eyes and read in them the conviction that everything had “gone off” beautifully. She rose from Madame Olenska’s side, and immediately Mrs. van der Luyden beckoned the latter to a seat on the gilt sofa where she throned. Mrs. Selfridge Merry bore across the room to join them, and it became clear to Archer that here also a conspiracy of rehabilitation and obliteration was going on.

The same silent organization that held his world together was determined to put itself on record as never having questioned for a moment the propriety of Madame Olenska’s conduct, or the completeness of Archer’s domestic felicity. All these amiable and inexorable people were resolutely engaged in pretending to one another that they had never heard of, suspected, or even conceived possible, the least hint to the contrary; and from this tissue of elaborate mutual dissimulation Archer once more disengaged the fact that New York believed him to be Madame Olenska’s lover.

He caught the glitter of victory in his wife’s eyes and for the first time understood that she shared the belief. The discovery woke a laughter of inner devils that reverberated through all his efforts to discuss the Martha Washington ball with Mrs. Reggie Chivers and little Mrs. Newland; and so the evening swept on, running and running like a senseless river that did not know how to stop.

At length he saw that Madame Olenska had risen and was saying good-bye. He understood that in a moment she would be gone, and tried to remember what he had said to her at dinner; but he could not recall a single word, as if the night’s talk had been swallowed by the room’s thick upholstery and disciplined air.

She went up to May, and the rest of the company made a circle about her as she advanced. The two young women clasped hands; then May bent forward and kissed her cousin.

“Certainly our hostess is much the handsomer of the two,” Archer heard Reggie Chivers say in an undertone to young Mrs. Newland; and he remembered Beaufort’s coarse sneer at May’s ineffectual beauty, and the way the world rewarded one kind of face and punished another without ever admitting it did either.

A moment later he was in the hall, putting Madame Olenska’s cloak about her shoulders. The hall smelled of cold air and fur and the faint metallic scent of wet carriage-steps. Through all his confusion he held fast to the resolve to say nothing that might startle or disturb her. Convinced that no power could now turn him from his purpose, he had found strength to let events shape themselves as they would.

But as he followed her out he felt a sudden hunger to be alone with her for one instant at the door of her carriage—one instant with only the night sounds outside, the honest jingle of harness, the hoof-beats, the driver’s muffled call—sounds not trained to politeness.

“Is your carriage here?” he asked.

At that moment Mrs. van der Luyden, who was being majestically inserted into her sables, said gently: “We are driving dear Ellen home.”

Archer’s heart gave a jerk. Madame Olenska, clasping her cloak and fan with one hand, held out the other to him. Her fingers were cold, and for an instant he felt as if all the heat of the dinner, all the clamor of unsaid words, had been condensed into that chill touch.

“Good-bye,” she said.

“Good-bye—but I shall see you soon in Paris,” he answered aloud—it seemed to him that he had shouted it in the guarded hush of the house.

“Oh,” she murmured, and her voice was so low it was like a breath against his ear, “if you and May could come—!”
