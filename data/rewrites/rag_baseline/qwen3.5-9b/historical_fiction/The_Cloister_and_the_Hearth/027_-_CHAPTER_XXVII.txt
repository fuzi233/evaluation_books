The worthy physician retreated to his sanctuary, confessing to his housekeeper that he suffered from a grievous burn—a phrase chosen with surgical precision. In the economy of human deceit, we often cauterize our neighbor's digits while searing our own fingers; he had merely burned his own. Yet, the housekeeper, wielding remedies as mild as milk, treated him with the deference due a lamb submitting to experience. While he feigned recovery, his mind was already drafting bills for shattered phials and orchestrating the immediate imprisonment of his guests. He swore before a magistrate that these strangers, owing him money, plotted instant flight from the township. Alas, it was his unluckiest day. His sincere, honest endeavor to commit perjury was thwarted by a circumstance he had never foreseen, nor deemed possible: he had spoken the truth. And in an affidavit!

When the officers arrived at "The Silver Lion," they discovered the birds had flown. Descending to the river, they learned from local intelligence and launched a hot pursuit up the bank. This fleeting escape owed everything to Denys's sharp sense and keen observation. After a peal of laughter that served as a cordial tonic, and after venting his watchword thrice, Denys abruptly grew grave. "Gerard Dusseldorf is no place for us," he declared. "That old fellow went off unnaturally silent for such a babbler. We are strangers here; the bailiff is his friend. Within five minutes, we shall lie in a dungeon for assaulting a Dusseldorf dignity. Are you strong enough to hobble to the water's edge? It is hard by. Once there, you have but to lie down in a boat instead of a bed; and what is the odds?"

"The odds, Denys? Untold, and all in favor of the boat," Gerard replied, his voice trembling with anticipation. "I pine for Rome; for Rome is my road to Sevenbergen. Then we shall lie in the boat, but *on* the Rhine—the famous Rhine, the cool, refreshing Rhine. I feel its breezes coming; the very sight will cure a little hop-o'-my-thumb fever like mine. Away! Away!"

Finding his excitable friend in this fervent mood, Denys hastily settled accounts with the landlord, and they hurried to the riverbank. Inquiry revealed dismay: the public boat had departed an hour prior, and no others would launch that afternoon. Yet, by dint of relentless questioning and gathering a crowd, they secured an offer of a private vessel from an elderly man and his two sons. A bystander ridiculed the arrangement. "The current is too strong for three oars."

"Then my comrade and I will help row," the invalid insisted.

"No need," the old man countered kindly. "Bless your silly heart, he owns the other boat."

A powerful breeze filled the sails from astern; the boatmen set them broad and began rowing, propelling the vessel at a spanking rate. "Are ye better, lad, for the river breeze?"

"Much better. But indeed the doctor did me good."

"The doctor? Why, you rejected all his cures."

"No, but I mean—you will say I am naught—but knocking the old fool down somehow soothed me."

"Amiable dove! How thy little character opens more and more every day, like a rosebud. I read thee all wrong at first."

"Nay, Denys, mistake me not. I trust I bore with his idle threats, though in sooth his voice pierced my poor ears. But he was an infidel, or next door to one, and such I have been taught to abhor. Did he not as good as say we owed our inward parts to men with long Greek names, and not to Him, whose name is but a syllable, yet whose hand is over all the earth? Pagan!"

"So you knocked him down forthright—as a good Christian."

"Now, Denys, you will still be jesting. Take not an ill man's part. Had it been a thunderbolt from Heaven, he had met but his due; yet he took but a sorry bolster from this weak arm."

"What weak arm?" Denys inquired, his eyes twinkling.

"I have lived among arms, and by Samson's hairy paw, I never saw one more like a catapult. The bolster wrapped round his nose, and the two ends kissed behind his head, and his forehead resounded. Had he been Goliath, or Julius Caesar, instead of an old quacksalver, down he had gone. St. Denys, guard me from such feeble opposites as thou! And above all from their weak arms—thou diabolical young hypocrite."

The river wound through the landscape, occasionally shifting the wind from astern to their side. Then, all hands moved to the weather side to prevent the boat from heeling dangerously. Among them was a child of about five years old, the grandson of the boatman and his darling. This urchin had slipped aboard at the moment of departure; being too light to affect the boat's trim, he existed above, or rather below, the strict laws of navigation. They sailed merrily on, little conscious that they were pursued by a whole posse of constables armed with the bailiff's writ, closing in rapidly. For if the wind was strong, so was the current.

Suddenly, Gerard remembered that this was a fine road to Rome, but a fatal one to Burgundy. "Oh, Denys," he said, with an almost alarmed look, "this is not your road."

"I know it," Denys replied quietly. "But what can I do? I cannot leave thee till the fever leaves thee; and it is on thee still, for thou art both red and white by turns. I have watched thee. I must e'en go on to Cologne, I doubt, and then strike across."

"Thank Heaven," Gerard exclaimed joyfully. Adding eagerly, with a touch of self-deception, "'Twere a sin to be so near Cologne and not see it. Oh, man, it is a vast and ancient city such as I have often dreamed of, but ne'er had the good luck to see. Me miserable, by what hard fortune do I come to it now?"

"Well then, Denys," the young man continued, less warmly, "it is old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century of grace, sacked by Attila the barbarous, and afterwards sore defaced by the Norman Lothaire. And it has a church for every week in the year, besides chapels and churches innumerable, convents and nunneries. Above all, the stupendous Minster, yet unfinished, wherein, but in their own chapel, lie the three kings that brought gifts to our Lord: Melchior with gold, Gaspar with frankincense, and Balthazar, the black king, who brought myrrh. Over their bones stands the shrine, the wonder of the world—it is of ever-shining brass, brighter than gold, studded with images fairly wrought and inlaid with exquisite devices, brave with colors. Two broad stripes run to and fro, of jewels so great, so rare, each might adorn a crown or ransom its wearer at need. Upon it stand the three kings curiously counterfeited: two in solid silver, richly gilt, bareheaded; but he of Aethiop, ebony-skinned, bears a golden crown. In the midst, our Blessed Lady, in virgin silver, holds Christ in her arms. At the corners, in golden branches, four goodly waxen tapers burn night and day. Holy eyes have watched and renewed that light unceasingly for ages, and holy eyes shall watch them in saecula. I tell thee, Denys, the oldest song, the oldest Flemish or German legend, found them burning, and they shall light the earth to its grave."

"And there is St. Ursel's church, a British saint's, where lie her bones and all the other virgins, her fellows. Eleven thousand were they who died for the faith, being put to the sword by barbarous Moors on the twenty-third day of October, two hundred and thirty-eight. Their bones are piled in the vaults, and many of their skulls are in the church. St. Ursel's is in a thin golden case, standing on the high altar, but shown to humble Christians only on solemn days."

"Eleven thousand virgins!" cried Denys. "What babies German men must have been in days of yore. Well, would all their bones might turn to flesh again, and their skulls sweet faces, as we pass through the gates. 'Tis odds but some of them are wearied of their estate by this time."

"Tush, Denys!" said Gerard. "Why wilt thou, being good, still make thyself seem evil? If thy wishing-cap be on, pray that we may meet the meanest she of all those wise virgins in the next world, and to that end let us reverence their holy dust in this one. And then there is the church of the Maccabees, and the cauldron in which they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat swine's flesh."

"Oh, peremptory king! And pig-headed Maccabees! I had eaten bacon with my pork liver rather than change places at the fire with my meat."

"What scurvy words are these? It was their faith."
