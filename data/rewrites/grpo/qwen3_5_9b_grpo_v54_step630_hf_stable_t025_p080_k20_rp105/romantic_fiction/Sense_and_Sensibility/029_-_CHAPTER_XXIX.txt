The fire had not yet been kindled, and the January morning remained a cold, grey prison of gloom. Marianne, barely clad, knelt before the window, clutching a letter as if it were the only thing keeping her anchored to reality. Tears flowed continuously, blurring the page, yet she wrote with a frantic, desperate speed. Elinor, roused from sleep by the sharp intake of her sister's breath and the rising tide of sobs, watched in silent, aching anxiety.

"Marianne," Elinor whispered, her voice gentle, "may I ask—"

"No, Elinor," Marianne cut in, her voice trembling with a terrifying, fleeting calm. "Ask nothing. You will know soon enough."

That calm shattered the moment the words left her lips, replaced instantly by the crushing weight of grief. Minutes stretched into an eternity before she could lift her pen again. Each pause, each fresh burst of weeping that forced her to stop, confirmed the terrible truth: she was likely writing her final farewell to Willoughby.

Elinor offered her every ounce of quiet support, yet Marianne, driven by a nervous irritability that bordered on madness, begged her not to speak a word. In such a storm of emotion, silence was the only mercy. Marianne could not remain still; she wandered the house until breakfast, a ghost avoiding the sight of everyone, her mind too restless for solitude or stillness.

At the table, she ate nothing. Elinor, seeing her sister's devastation, turned her attention entirely to Mrs. Jennings, diverting the older woman's gaze from Marianne's hollow eyes. The meal dragged on, a favorite ritual for Mrs. Jennings, until a knock brought a letter to Marianne. She snatched it from the servant's hand, her face draining of color until she looked deathly pale, and fled the room.

Elinor saw the direction immediately. Her heart seized, a sickness rising in her throat that made holding her head upright a struggle. She sat trembling, fearing Mrs. Jennings would notice her distress. Instead, the good lady, busy measuring wool for her rug, mistook the scene for a romantic comedy.

"Upon my word," Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, laughing, "I never saw a young woman so desperately in love! My girls were nothing to her, but Miss Marianne is quite an altered creature. I hope he doesn't keep her waiting much longer; it is grievous to see her so ill. Pray, when are they to be married?"

Elinor, though her spirit was heavy, felt compelled to answer. She forced a smile, her voice steady despite the turmoil within. "And have you really, Ma'am, persuaded yourself that my sister is engaged to Mr. Willoughby? I thought it a joke, but such a serious question implies more. Please, do not deceive yourself. Nothing would surprise me more than to hear of their marriage."

"For shame!" Mrs. Jennings retorted, her laughter fading into indignation. "How can you talk so? We all know it must be a match. They were over head and ears in love from the first moment they met. Did I not see them together in Devonshire every day? Did I not know your sister came to town specifically to buy wedding clothes? Come, come. Because you are so sly, you think no one else has sense. But it has been known all over town for ages. I tell everyone, and so does Charlotte."

"Indeed, Ma'am," Elinor said, her tone grave. "You are mistaken. You are doing a very unkind thing spreading such a report, and I am sure you will find yourself wrong."

Mrs. Jennings laughed again, dismissing Elinor's plea, while Elinor, drained of spirit, hurried away to their room. She pushed open the door to find Marianne stretched across the bed, almost choking on her grief, clutching one letter while two or three others lay scattered nearby.

Elinor approached slowly, saying nothing. She sat beside her sister, taking her hand and kissing it repeatedly. Then, the dam broke. Elinor wept, her tears nearly as violent as Marianne's. Marianne, unable to speak, felt the tenderness of the gesture. After a long period of shared, silent agony, she handed all the letters to Elinor. Covering her face with a handkerchief, she screamed with such raw agony that it filled the small room.

Elinor, knowing this pain must run its course, waited until the initial shock subsided. Then, she turned to the letter from Willoughby.

*I have just had the honour of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behaviour last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional. I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled. It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honoured from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me.*

The indignation that surged through Elinor was profound. She had suspected his inconstancy, but she had not anticipated such cruelty. To announce his betrayal with such cold, polite detachment, to deny any special affection while returning her tokens of love, was a violation of every honorable feeling. It was a letter of impudent cruelty, every line an insult that proclaimed its writer to be deep in hardened villainy.

She read it again and again, her abhorrence growing with each perusal. The bitterness of her feelings was so intense that she dared not speak, terrified that trying to comfort Marianne by framing this as an escape from a bad match would only wound her sister deeper. Marianne needed to know this was a loss, not a deliverance.

Lost in her meditation on the depravity of the mind that could dictate such words, Elinor forgot her sister's immediate distress, the unread letters on her lap, and the passage of time. Only when the sound of a carriage driving up to the door startled her did she realize Mrs. Jennings's chariot had arrived early. Determined not to leave Marianne alone in her despair, Elinor excused herself to the older woman, citing her sister's indisposition. Mrs. Jennings, ever good-natured, accepted the excuse readily.

Once the carriage departed, Elinor returned to find Marianne attempting to rise. Elinor caught her just in time to prevent a fall; her sister was giddy and weak from days of hunger and sleeplessness. Now that the fever of suspense had broken, the physical toll was evident: an aching head, a weakened stomach, and a general nervous faintness. Elinor fetched wine, which helped settle Marianne enough for her to whisper, "Poor Elinor! how unhappy I make you!"

"I only wish," Elinor replied, "there were anything I *could* do to comfort you."

Those words were too much for Marianne. "Oh! Elinor, I am miserable, indeed," she sobbed, her voice failing.

Elinor could no longer watch this torrent of unresisted grief in silence. "Exert yourself, dear Marianne," she cried. "If you would not kill yourself and all who love you. Think of your mother; think of her misery while *you* suffer: for her sake you must exert yourself."

"I cannot, I cannot," Marianne wailed. "Leave me, leave me, if I distress you; leave me, hate me, forget me! But do not torture me so. Oh! how easy for those, who have no sorrow of their own to talk of exertion! Happy, happy Elinor, *you* cannot have an idea of what I suffer."

"Do you call *me* happy, Marianne? Ah! if you knew!—And can you believe me to be so, while I see you so wretched!"

"Forgive me, forgive me," Marianne threw her arms around her sister's neck. "I know you feel for me; I know what a heart you have; but yet you are—you must be happy; Edward loves you—what, oh what, can do away such happiness as that?"

"Many, many circumstances," Elinor said solemnly.

"No, no, no," Marianne cried wildly. "He loves you, and only you. You *can* have no grief."

"I can have no pleasure while I see you in this state."

"And you will never see me otherwise. Mine is a misery which nothing can do away."

"You must not talk so, Marianne. Have you no comforts? no friends? Is your loss such as leaves no opening for consolation? Much as you suffer now, think of what you would have suffered if the discovery of his character had been delayed to a later period—if your engagement had been carried on for months and months, as it might have been, before he chose to put an end to it. Every additional day of unhappy confidence, on your side, would have made the blow more dreadful."

"Engagement!" Marianne gasped. "There has been no engagement."

"No engagement!"

"No, he is not so unworthy as you believe him. He has broken no faith with me."

"But he told you that he loved you."

"Yes—no—never absolutely. It was every day implied, but never professedly declared. Sometimes I thought it had been, but it never was."

"Yet you wrote to him?"

"Yes: could that be wrong after all that had passed? But I cannot talk."

Elinor said no more. Turning to the three remaining letters, her curiosity sharpened into a need for truth. She read them quickly. The first, sent upon their arrival in town, expressed excitement and expectation. The second, written after the dance at the Middletons', pleaded for an explanation of his absence and demanded a personal assurance of his presence. The third, following the incident at the ball, was a desperate plea for clarity, questioning his behavior and demanding a justification or a return of her letters and hair.

That such letters, so full of affection and confidence, could be answered with such cold dismissal, Elinor found hard to believe. Yet, she condemned the imprudence of writing them in the first place. As Marianne watched her finish, she remarked that the letters contained nothing anyone wouldn't write in her situation.

"I felt myself," Marianne added, her voice trembling, "to be as solemnly engaged to him, as if the strictest legal covenant had bound us to each other."

"I can believe it," Elinor said softly. "But unfortunately he did not feel the same."

"He *did* feel the same, Elinor—for weeks and weeks he felt it. I know he did. Whatever may have changed him now, (and nothing but the blackest art employed against me can have done it), I was once as dear to him as my own soul could wish. This lock of hair, which now he can so readily give up, was begged of me with the most earnest supplication. Had you seen his look, his manner, had you heard his voice at that moment! Have you forgot the last evening of our being together at Barton? The morning that we parted too! When he told me that it might be many weeks before we met again—his distress—can I ever forget his distress?"

For a moment, she could say nothing. When the emotion passed, she spoke in a firmer tone. "Elinor, I have been cruelly used; but not by Willoughby."

"Dearest Marianne, who but himself? By whom can he have been instigated?"

"By all the world, rather than by his own heart. I could rather believe every creature of my acquaintance leagued together to ruin me in his opinion, than believe his nature capable of such cruelty. This woman of whom he writes—whoever she be—or any one, in short, but your own dear self, mama, and Edward, may have been so barbarous to bely me. Beyond you three, is there a creature in the world whom I would not rather suspect of evil than Willoughby, whose heart I know so well?"

Elinor did not argue. "Whoever may have been so detestably your enemy, let them be cheated of their malignant triumph, my dear sister, by seeing how nobly the consciousness of your own innocence and good intentions supports your spirits. It is a reasonable and laudable pride which resists such malevolence."

"No, no," Marianne cried. "Misery such as mine has no pride. I care not who knows that I am wretched. The triumph of seeing me so may be open to all the world. Elinor, Elinor, they who suffer little may be proud and independent as they like—may resist insult, or return mortification—but I cannot. I must feel—I must be wretched—and they are welcome to enjoy the consciousness of it that can."

"But for my mother's sake and mine—"

"I would do more than for my own. But to appear happy when I am so miserable—Oh! who can require it?"

Silence fell between them again. Elinor walked thoughtfully from the fire to the window, unaware of the warmth or the view.
