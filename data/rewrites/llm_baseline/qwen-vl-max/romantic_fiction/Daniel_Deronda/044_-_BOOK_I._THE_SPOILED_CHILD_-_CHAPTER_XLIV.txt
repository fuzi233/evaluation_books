Fairy folk a-listening
Hear the seed sprout in the spring.
And for music to their dance
Hear the hedgerows wake from trance,
Sap that trembles into buds
Sending little rhythmic floods
Of fairy sound in fairy ears.
Thus all beauty that appears
Has birth as sound to finer sense
And lighter-clad intelligence.

Gwendolen was thinking of Deronda more than he was thinking of her—often wondering what his thoughts were about life, about meaning, about the world beyond her own narrow orbit. It was like a lap-dog imagining the grand adventures of dogs everywhere: utterly impossible. She could not fathom that Deronda’s life might be shaped by something vast and ancient—the destiny of a people long forgotten, or a calling no one else could see. Nor could she picture him riding away on a golden horse into the sky, disappearing like a star. Her mind, though sharp and quick, had never reached such heights.

Yet even with the deep sense of inferiority that had settled over her since marriage, she still believed herself central in his thoughts. It is natural, perhaps, for young hearts to assume that others feel as they do—especially when loneliness makes every glance, every word, seem charged with significance. Gwendolen, isolated and yearning, clung to the idea that Deronda saw her differently. That he valued her. That he noticed her—not just as Mrs. Grandcourt, but as someone whose soul stirred beneath the surface.

She often asked herself: *What would he tell me to do?* He had said she must care more for others, learn more, seek out the best things in life. But how? Where should she begin? She imagined him recommending books—great minds, towering intellects—and smiled at the thought of asking him if those unreadable volumes were really “medicine for the mind,” as some called them. Then, ashamed of her teasing, she would quietly gather a selection—Descartes, Bacon, Locke, Butler, Burke, Guizot—names she knew were respected, authors she assumed he had read. She hoped, by dipping into them quickly, she might catch a glimpse of the world through his eyes.

But time slipped away. The demands of being Mrs. Grandcourt left little room for quiet reflection. Every moment was watched, measured, judged. Grandcourt, ever watchful, had found new reasons to tighten his grip—making sure the marriage served his purposes, and serving them completely. And Gwendolen, though rebellious within, could not afford to fail in her performance. She could not bear the thought of giving any sign—any look, any word—that would betray her inner turmoil. Not yet. Not while the world still looked on her with admiration.

Around Diplow, during those early weeks of the year, she was seen as triumphant. Dressed in fine silks, riding with confidence, receiving guests with grace, she wore her title like a crown. “She hides it under an air of indifference,” Mrs. Arrowpoint remarked. “A stranger might think she has condescended rather than risen. I’ve always sensed that duality in her.”

To her mother, above all, Gwendolen wished to appear content. Poor Mrs. Davilow, deceived by the distance now between them, took it as a sign of changed affection—though Grandcourt had done nothing but provide for her. Visits were brief: lunch, dinner, a quick call with the husband waiting nearby. No real intimacy remained.

The truth was, when Gwendolen had once suggested inviting her mother with the Gascoignes, Grandcourt had hesitated, then drawled, “We can’t be having _those people_ always. Gascoigne talks too much. Country clergy are always bores—with their confounded fuss about everything.” The words chilled her. To have her family dismissed so casually—her mother included—was a wound. Yet she could not explain. She could not say, *He wants nothing to do with you. And I don’t want you to see how unhappy I am.*

So she kept silent, brushing aside hints gently. When her mother mentioned possibly moving near Ryelands, Gwendolen replied, “It wouldn’t be as nice as staying near the rectory, mamma. We’ll likely be away from Ryelands much. You’d miss Aunt and Uncle.” All the while, her heart swelled with a secret longing—for her uncle’s warmth, for her family’s presence, for anything that felt like home.

And perhaps, without knowing it, she was growing. Not through books, which demanded silence and focus she couldn’t give, but through memory, through love, through the ache of absence. She began to see her uncle not as a distant figure, but as someone who had cared, who had tried—even when he was wrong. It was a kind of quiet strength she hadn’t known before.

One day, the unexpected joy came: Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne were at Offendene, and Gwendolen rode up alone—without Grandcourt, only her groom beside her. The whole household, including the four girls and Miss Merry, saw her approach. Even the older ones felt a spark of romance, as if the beautiful sister on the proud chestnut horse had stepped out of a storybook—Harriet Byron reborn, or Miss Wardour returning from happiness ever after.

Her uncle met her at the door. She leapt down with a lightness that seemed to promise joy. Today, she was determined to comfort her mother—to show her there was still peace, still love. The seven kisses were no longer a burden. “Mr. Grandcourt is out,” she said, laying down her hat and sitting beside her mother. “So I decided to come visit.” Then, with a playful scolding tone, “You’re punished for not wearing better lace. You didn’t think I’d come and catch you—so careless-about-yourself, mamma!” She touched her mother’s hair tenderly.

“Scold me, dear,” Mrs. Davilow whispered, her face flushing with pleasure.

“I wish there was something you could eat after your ride,” she added. “Not just these scraps. Let Jocosa make you chocolate—your old way. You used to like that.”

Miss Merry rose immediately, though Gwendolen waved her off. “No, just bread or a biscuit. I’m not hungry. I’m here to say good-bye.”

“What! Going back to Ryelands?” asked Mr. Gascoigne.

“No,” said Gwendolen, breaking bread but not eating. “We’re going to town.”

“Early to go to town,” said Mrs. Gascoigne. “And Mr. Grandcourt isn’t in Parliament.”

“Oh, there’s just one more day of hunting, and Henleigh has business with lawyers, I think,” Gwendolen said. “I’m glad. I’ll enjoy being in London.”

“You’ll see your house in Grosvenor Square,” said Mrs. Davilow, her eyes shining.

“Yes,” Gwendolen agreed. “And there’s so much to see and do.”

Then Mr. Gascoigne spoke, with the weight of advice: “I wish you’d use your influence with Mr. Grandcourt to encourage him to enter Parliament. A man of his standing should make his voice heard. The ministry may need to appeal to the country soon. And he’s at the age when public duty becomes essential. A wife has great influence over her husband. Use yours, my dear.”

To Gwendolen, the speech tasted like bitter irony. If she had been laughing, she might have burst out. Her uncle had just admitted he didn’t know Grandcourt’s political views. And *influence*? Once, she had believed in her power to shape things. Now, she knew better. But she answered carefully: “I’d be glad, uncle. But I think Mr. Grandcourt wouldn’t want the trouble of an election—unless it could be without speeches. Don’t candidates always speak?”

“Not necessarily,” said Mr. Gascoigne. “A man of position needs few words. Tell him I say so.”

“Here comes Jocosa with my chocolate,” Gwendolen said, relieved to escape further conversation. The rector, feeling he had fulfilled his duty, sat back with the air of a wise magistrate offering counsel.

Mrs. Gascoigne, less composed, hinted at discomfort: “Your uncle and Anna will likely be in town by Easter. Rex hopes to come out with honors—perhaps a fellowship. Lord Brackenshaw has been very kind since returning to the Castle. He may invite them.”

“I hope my uncle brings Ann to stay in Grosvenor Square,” Gwendolen said, risking a small hope for the moment—though in truth, she dreaded bringing anyone near Grandcourt again.

“We mustn’t rejoice too soon,” said the rector, pleased with the topic. “But I’ve spoken with impartial judges—they hold high hopes for Rex. He’s clear-headed, principled. He’ll be a great lawyer someday.”

“How very nice,” Gwendolen murmured, her voice flat with sarcasm. Lawyers, she thought, were rarely *nice*.

“Lord Brackenshaw has been so kind,” Mrs. Davilow said. “He invited me to stay here until I find another house. And now one has come up—Mr. Jodson’s place. Small, but pleasant. Just a mile from the Rectory. You remember the low white house hidden by trees?”

“Yes,” said Gwendolen, her voice softening. “But you have no furniture, poor mamma.”

“Oh, I’m saving,” Mrs. Davilow said, touching her daughter’s hand. “You know who made me rich, dear. And Jocosa manages so well—it’s wonderful.”

“Oh, please let me go upstairs and fix my hat,” Gwendolen said suddenly, raising a hand to her hair. Her heart was heavy. She had to hide the truth: her mother would have been worse off without Grandcourt’s money. But the thought of losing this place, this family, this quiet warmth—made her nearly weep.

“I suppose I’ll never see this again,” she whispered, entering the black-and-yellow bedroom. She sank into a chair before the mirror, groaning softly, as if drained. She refused to cry. Her face turned pale.

“You’re not well, dear?” Mrs. Davilow asked.

“No,” Gwendolen said, lifting her hand. “That chocolate made me sick.”

“I’d come to you if you were ill,” her mother said, pressing her hand to her chest.

“I’ll be fine,” Gwendolen said, leaning her head against her mother. “I’m strong. Don’t fret. Make yourself happy with the girls. They’re better children than I’ve been.”

She smiled up at her mother. “You’ve always been good to me.”

“Why, what did I ever do that was good?” Gwendolen asked, rising with a sudden playfulness. “Except marry Mr. Grandcourt? And I wouldn’t have done that unless it pleased me.”

She tossed her chin, picked up her hat. “God forbid, child! I wouldn’t have had you marry for my sake. Your happiness is half mine.”

“Very well,” Gwendolen said, arranging her hat with care. “Then you’ll please consider yourself half happy—which is more than I’m used to seeing.”

She turned, smiling as before. “Now I’m ready. Oh, mamma—Mr. Grandcourt gives me money, expects me to spend it. But I can’t. I hate charity cases. Here—thirty pounds. Give it to the girls. Let them buy little things for themselves when you move.”

She placed the notes in her mother’s hands and hurried toward the door.

“God bless you, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said.

“It will please them so much,” Gwendolen said, turning back with a nod. “They’re troublesome—but they don’t trouble me now.”

She didn’t understand why she gave the money, but she didn’t want it taken seriously. She was glad to leave the room without showing more emotion. As she rode away, she thought sarcastically: *I think I’m making a very good Mrs. Grandcourt.*

She believed Grandcourt had gone to Gadsmere that day—had guessed it, as she had long guessed who lived in the “dog-hutch” he described. And yet, the thought of him going there—of him caring for those people—sent a fire through her. Why should it hurt her that he showed concern for those whose suffering she herself regretted?

Hadn’t she promised before marriage to act on their behalf? And wasn’t it good that he was making arrangements for his will, keeping conscience alive?

Yet now, as a wife, the knowledge that he was at Gadsmere burned like a brand. She had brought this upon herself—the humiliation of silence, the terror of being discovered. She had married him knowing the truth, and now she must endure it. As she told Deronda: *I must go on.*

After moments of fierce hatred, the old pressure returned—the spiritual force that made submission inevitable. She dared nothing except impulsive acts. Not even the faintest plan for freedom. For any attempt would bring deeper shame.

Still, the worst result of her marriage was not the pain, but the spectacle. She feared being seen as broken. And her humiliation was eased by believing only Mrs. Glasher knew the truth—the secret of the Whispering Stones, the letter, the threat. She had never told anyone Lush was involved. In her mind, Mrs. Glasher held the secret—and the fear that she too would dread exposure.

And there was another secret—her own suppressed rebellion. One she dreaded more than any other. Grandcourt could not truly imagine how she felt. He saw only what suited his will. But he sensed her resistance, magnified it—even as it deepened his satisfaction in controlling it.

And so, Gwendolen rode away, a queen in silk and sorrow, carrying her secrets like stones in her heart.
