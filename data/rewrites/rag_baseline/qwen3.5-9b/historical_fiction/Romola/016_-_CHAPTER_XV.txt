The heavy oak doors of San Marco groaned shut, sealing the noise of the city outside. Inside the cloister, the air was still and cool, smelling of wax and old stone. Monna Brigida retreated into the adjacent church, leaving Romola alone with the Frati. She was ushered to the threshold of the chapter-house, the boundary line where the sanctity of the enclosure ended and the infirmary began. No woman was permitted to cross further.

The door swung open, revealing a dim chamber illuminated only by the grey twilight filtering through the lattice and the flickering glow of two tapers set behind a narrow bed. Lying there was Fra Luca. His face was a map of emaciation, the skin stretched tight over bone, crowned by the golden tonsure. His hazel eyes, sunken deep into hollow sockets, were fixed on a small crucifix held tremblingly in his hand. He was propped upright, a fragile silhouette against the gloom. As Romola stepped forward, casting aside her veil, she registered the presence of another monk standing silently beside the bed, his black cowl pulled low, retreating toward the exit as she entered. Behind them, high on the frescoed wall, loomed the painted agony of Christ, pale and staring, with sorrowful faces peering down from the haloed heads of the apostles.

Then, Romola's gaze locked with Fra Luca's. From the crucifix, the monk's eyes lifted to meet hers. A sudden, sharp pang of recognition struck her, binding the emaciated figure before her to the memory of her fair, vibrant brother.

"Dino!" The word escaped her throat as a low cry of anguish. Yet, she did not lean in. She remained rigid, holding herself erect at a distance of two yards. An unconquerable repulsion surged within her. This monastic guise felt like a brand of dastardly ingratitude, the mark of a groveling superstition that could twist such betrayal into piety. Her father, a man of proud sincerity and simple living, had been one of the few frank pagans of his age. He had raised Romola to ignore the Church's claim to regulate the minds of rational beings. In her view, the Church belonged to the mixed multitude of the ignorant, separate from the cultivated intellect. Consequently, she viewed her brother's path not with religious awe, but with incurious, indignant contempt. Still, the loving current of her soul clung to the image of the boy she had known. Even as she stood immovable, a yearning search haunted her eyes, seeking something too faint to grasp.

But there was no corresponding warmth in the monk's face. He regarded his younger sister, returned to him in her full womanly beauty, with the distant gaze of a spirit revisiting a lost world.

"My sister," he whispered, his voice feeble yet distinct, broken by labored breaths. "It is well thou hast not delayed longer. I have a message to deliver, and my time is short."

Romola took a tentative step closer. She expected a confession of affectionate penitence directed at their father. Her heart began to soften; nothing could erase the long years of abandonment, but a guilty conscience looking back on irreparable harm often summoned pity. Perhaps, at the very end, there would be understanding. Dino would pour out natural filial feelings, asking about his father's failing sight, the darkness of his days, the life in the home where he had been fed. The final words from dying lips would surely be ones of tenderness and regret.

"Romola," Fra Luca began, "I have had a vision concerning thee. Thrice in the last two months, and each time it has grown clearer. Therefore, I came from Fiesole, convinced this was a mandate from heaven. And I gather a promise of mercy in this, that my breath is preserved..."

The difficult breathing cut off his sentence.

Romola felt her heart chill anew. A vision? One of those spectral intrusions her father had cited with bitter disdain? Indignation flared instantly.

"Dino, I thought you had words for my father. You deserted him when his sight failed; you made his life desolate. Do you care for that? Never repent? What is this religion of yours that places visions above natural duty?"

The deep-sunken eyes turned slowly toward her, resting in silence for several moments, as if weighing the cost of an answer.

"No," he said finally, his tone low and passionless, sounding less like a dying man and more like a spirit speaking through mortal instruments. "No. I have never repented fleeing the stifling poison-breath of sin that choked me, threatening to drown my senses like besotting wine. My father could not hear the voice calling me night and day; he knew nothing of the demon-tempters dragging me back. He lived amidst human sin and misery without believing in them, like one busy picking shining stones in a mine while a world died of plague above him. I spoke, but he listened with scorn. He told me the studies I desired were either childish trifles or must be warmed by worldly ambition and fleshly lusts—the very substances of the poetry and history he demanded I consume."

"Has my father not led a pure and noble life?" Romola burst out, unable to endure the implied accusation. "He sought no honors; he was truthful; he denied himself all luxuries. He lived like an ancient sage. He never wished you to serve worldly ambitions; he wished you to live as he did, according to the purest maxims of philosophy, which he instilled in you."

She spoke partly by rote, as ardent youth often do, but with intense conviction. A pink flush burned her cheeks, and she quivered from head to toe. Her brother was slow to respond, regarding her passionate face with strange, impassive eyes.

"What were the maxims of philosophy to me? They commanded strength when I felt weakness; when I was ready, like Saint Benedict, to roll among thorns and court wounds as deliverance. For the Divine Love had sought me, penetrating me, creating a vast need within me, like a seed demanding space to grow. Brought up in careless ignorance of the true faith, I had not studied its doctrines, yet it seized me like a rising flood. I perceived a life of perfect love and purity for the soul—a existence free of hungry craving, tormenting doubt, or fear of suffering. Before knowing the history of the saints, I foresaw their ecstasy. The same truth permeated pagan philosophy: that it is bliss to die to mortal needs and live in the life of God as the Unseen Perfection. But to attain it, I must forsake the world. No affection, no hope could wed me to that which passes away. I must relate to my fellow-beings only as souls connected to the eternal. This need urged me constantly. It overwhelmed me in visions when my mind grew weary of the vain words recording dead men's passions; it seized me after I was tempted into sin and turned away with loathing from the scent of the emptied cup. In these visions, I saw the meaning of the Crucifix."

He paused, breathing heavily for a minute or two. Romola did not prompt him. Contact with this unearthly brother's mind was futile, as useless as trying to grasp a shadow. When he spoke again, his heaving chest was calmer.

"I felt whom I must follow. But I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who claimed to have renounced the world, my soul would suffocate in the fumes of hypocrisy, lust, and pride. God did not choose me, as He chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil within the Church and the world. He called upon me to flee. I took the sacred vows and I fled—to lands where danger, scorn, and want bore me continually, like angels, to repose on the bosom of God. I lived as a hermit, ministering to pilgrims. But my task was short: the veil dividing me from my everlasting rest has worn very thin. I came back to Florence that—"

"Dino, you *did* want to know if my father was alive," Romola interrupted, the image of his suffering life stirring a desperate desire for union and forgiveness.

"—That before I die, I might urge our brethren to study the Eastern tongues, as I failed to do, and pursue greater ends. I find them already bent on this work. And since I arrived, Romola, I feel I was sent partly to thee—not to renew earthly bonds, but to deliver the heavenly warning conveyed in this vision. I have seen it thrice. Through all the years since the Divine Voice first called me, while I was still in the world, I have been guided by visions. For in the painful linking of our waking thoughts, we cannot be sure we have not mingled our own errors with the light we pray for; but in visions and dreams, we are passive, our souls mere instruments in the Divine hand. Therefore, listen, and speak not again—for the time is short."

Romola's mind recoiled from listening. Her indignation had subsided, only because she sensed the widening chasm between them. Just as Fra Luca spoke, the figure of the other monk entered, standing on the opposite side of the bed, his cowl drawn low.

"Kneel, my daughter," a strong, rich voice declared, startlingly contrasting with Fra Luca's frailty. "For the Angel of Death is present, waiting while the message of heaven is delivered. Bend thy pride before it is bent for thee by a yoke of iron."
