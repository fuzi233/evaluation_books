"Eli," Catherine commanded, her voice cutting through the quiet of the room. "Answer me one question like a man, and I shall ask no more today. What is wormwood?"

Eli looked momentarily helpless under the sudden demand upon his wits, but he quickly rallied. "It is a herb that tastes most bitterly," he replied.

"That is a fair answer, my man, but not the one I seek," she countered sharply.

"Then tell it yourself."

"And shall I? Wormwood is—two people in the house doing nothing but waiting for your shoes and mine," Eli groaned, the weight of his own idleness pressing down on him.

The barb struck home. "I believe waiting for their best friend's coffin, combined with having nothing to do, is enough to make them far worse than nature intended," Catherine declared. "Why not set them up elsewhere, giving them a chance?"

"I am willing," Eli admitted, though fear clouded his resolve. "But I dread they will drink and gamble their very shelves away before they earn a penny."

"Nay," Catherine retorted, her eyes flashing. "Do you take me for a simpleton? Of course, I intend to watch them closely at the start, driving them with a loose rein, as the saying goes."

"But where did you think of going? Not here; we must divide our own custom."

"Not likely," she dismissed the idea firmly. "I say Rotterdam against the world. There I could start them."

Oh, self-deception! The true motive behind this bold plan was merely to draw herself nearer to little Gerard. Following many heated discussions and earnest promises of reform from Cornelis and Sybrandt on these specific terms, Catherine traveled to Rotterdam to hunt for a shop, taking Kate with her for a change of air.

They soon located a suitable space in a respectable street, though it was sadly out of order. Yet, the dilapidated state worked in their favor; they secured it at a bargain price. Instantly, they set to work, scrubbing the floors until they shone, fitting proper shelves for the business, and rendering the dwelling habitable once more.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson was constantly asking Margaret what he could do to assist her. Her usual response came in a tone heavy with sorrow. "Nothing, Luke, nothing."

"What, you who are so clever? Can you think of nothing for me to do for you?" he pleaded.

"Nothing, Luke, nothing," she repeated, her heart breaking at his persistence.

Finally, however, she softened her reply. "If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate about."

The slave of love consented joyfully. Soon, Luke had crafted a small cart, cushioned it with care, and even yoked himself into it. At eventide, he drew Kate out of the town and along the pleasant boulevard, while Margaret and Catherine walked beside them. To an outside observer, it appeared a happier party than reality warranted.

Kate, for one, enjoyed the moment keenly. Little Gerard was placed in her lap, and she doted on the child with such intensity that he seemed a cherub carried by a little angel, or a rosebud resting in the cup of a lily.

Yet, the vulgarity of the crowd followed them. They jeered, asking Luke how a thistle tasted and mocking whether his mistress could not afford a horse with four legs instead of a man. Luke paid no heed to their taunts, but Kate suffered deeply for him.

"Thou hast made the cart for me, good Luke," she said gently. "'Twas much. I did ill to let thee draw me too; we can afford to pay some poor soul for that service. I love my rides, and carrying little Gerard, but I would rather ride no more than thou be mocked for it."

"Much do I care for their tongues!" Luke shouted back, his pride flaring. "If I did care, I'd knock their heads together. I shall draw you until my mistress says 'give over.'"

"Luke, if you obey Kate, you will oblige me," Margaret interjected softly.

"Then I will obey Kate," Luke declared.

An honorable exception to the prevailing mockery was Jorian Ketel's wife, Joan. She watched the scene with a knowing, pitying gaze. "That is strength well laid out, to draw the weak," she remarked. "And her prayers will be your guerdon; she is not long for this world; she smileth in pain."

These were the words of Joan, spoken with prophetic clarity. Single-minded Luke, however, offered a different defense. "I do not want the poor lass's prayers," he insisted. "I do it to please my mistress, Margaret."

After that, Luke often pressed Margaret again to give him something substantial to do, without success. But one day, seemingly tired of his constant importuning, she turned on him. With a look and an accent she knew he would struggle to interpret correctly, she spoke three devastating words:

"Find me my boy's father."
