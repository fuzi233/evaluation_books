A Wonderful Likeness

That evening, a grim figure emerged from Gratz, a pale, dark-complexioned man burdened by a horse and cart heavily laden with two massive packing cases, each brimming with canvases. The journey from our small capital spanned ten leagues, and whenever a courier traversed the distance, the inhabitants of our secluded halls would swarm around him, eager to devour the latest tidings. This arrival, however, sent a ripple of unease through our quiet domain rather than joy. The heavy cases were left standing in the hall, and the messenger was ushered away by the servants to consume his supper before returning.

Soon, flanked by assistants and armed with hammers, chisels, and turning screws, the man returned to the hall where we had gathered to watch the unveiling. Carmilla sat listlessly amidst the bustle, her gaze fixed on the floor, while the old portraits—nearly all of them, and mostly inherited from her side of the family through my mother's ancient Hungarian lineage—were carefully brushed free from decades of grime and obscurity.

My father held a ledger, reading off numbers as the restorer dug through the layers of varnish and dust. I confess I knew little of art, but these were undeniably ancient relics, some possessing a peculiar, haunting curiosity. For the most part, I beheld them for the first time; the smoke and time of centuries had nearly erased their features, revealing them only now.

"There remains a portrait I have not yet seen," my father remarked, his voice dropping slightly. "In the upper corner, though faded, I can discern the name 'Marcia Karnstein,' dated 1698. I am curious to see how it has survived."

A memory surfaced instantly. It was a small canvas, roughly eighteen inches tall and nearly square, unframed. In its current state, it was a blot of ink-blackened age, unreadable to the eye.

The artist produced it now, his movements betraying a sudden, intense pride. As he lifted it, the darkness shattered. It was breathtaking, jarring, and undeniably alive. It was the effigy of Carmilla herself.

"Carmilla, dearest!" my father exclaimed, his tone shifting from casual to awestruck. "Here is an absolute miracle. Look at her! She lives, she smiles, she seems ready to speak. Is it not beautiful, my girl?" He turned to me, his eyes wide. "And notice, even the tiny mole upon her throat is preserved."

My father chuckled, a sound that felt too bright for the somber air. "Undoubtedly, it is a wonderful likeness." Yet, as he spoke, his gaze drifted away, and strangely, he appeared less moved by the revelation than I was. To my astonishment, he continued conversing with the picture cleaner—who possessed a genuine intellect regarding his craft—as if discussing mundane matters of restoration, while I became increasingly entranced, staring at the painted girl until the room spun.

"Father," I asked, my voice trembling slightly, "may I hang this picture in my chamber?"

"Certainly, dear," he replied, a smile playing on his lips. "I am delighted you find it so striking."

"I'm sure it must be prettier than I imagined," he added, addressing me as if I were a child.

Young Carmilla made no response to his polite words, nor did she appear to hear them. She leaned back in her chair, her long-lashed eyes fixed upon me with an intensity that bordered on madness, a smile of rapturous ecstasy curving her lips.

"Now," she murmured, her voice thick with emotion, "you can read the name clearly inscribed in the corner."

"It is not Marcia," she corrected softly, tracing the gold leaf with her finger. "It reads Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. And here, beneath the date of 1698, lies a tiny coronet." She looked up, her expression languid yet piercing. "I am descended from the Karnsteins, just as Mother was."

"Oh!" the woman sitting beside her sighed, her voice heavy with a strange, hollow resonance. "Then I am a descendant of a very long line, a very ancient bloodline. Do any Karnsteins still walk the earth?"

"No one bears the name, I believe," I answered, feeling a cold prickle run down my spine. "The family was ruined in some civil wars centuries ago. Only the ruins of the castle remain, mere three miles from here."

"How fascinating!" she breathed, glancing toward the open door where the night air drifted in. Her eyes widened as if captivated by something unseen. "Look! What beautiful moonlight!"

"It resembles the night you first arrived," I whispered, the realization striking me with force.

She sighed, a sound that seemed to vibrate within my chest, and smiled. Rising, she took my arm, drawing me close, and together we stepped out onto the cobblestones. In a heavy, suffocating silence, we walked slowly down toward the drawbridge, where the landscape unfolded like a painting under the silver glow.

"And so," she whispered, her breath warm against my ear, "you were thinking of the night I came?"

"Were you glad I came?" I asked, unable to tear my gaze from her face.

"Delighted, dear Carmilla," she answered, though the word tasted like ash. "And you asked for this picture, so alike to me, to hang in your room?" She paused, sinking her head upon my shoulder, her arm tightening around me with a grip that felt too strong. "How romantic you are, Carmilla. Whenever you tell me your story, it will surely be woven from some great, tragic romance."

She pressed a kiss to my neck, silent and chilling.

"I am certain, Carmilla, that you have loved someone. That there is, right now, an affair of the heart consuming you."

"I have loved no one," she whispered, her voice barely audible, "and never shall... unless it be with you."

God, how beautiful she looked in the moonlight! Yet, there was a terrifying vacancy behind those eyes. With a shyness that felt unnatural, she buried her face in the crook of my neck and hair, emitting tumultuous sobs that sounded like gasps for air. Her hand clutched mine, trembling violently. Her soft cheek burned hot against mine.

"Darling, darling," she murmured, her voice dripping with a sickening sweetness. "I live inside you. And you would die for me, I love you so."

Suddenly, I started back as if struck by lightning.

She was gazing at me now, her eyes empty of all fire, all meaning, drained dry. Her face was colorless, mask-like, and utterly apathetic.

"A chill in the air, isn't it, dear?" she said, her voice drowsy and slurred, as if she were floating underwater. "I almost shiver. Have I been dreaming? Let us go inside. Come, come, hurry inside."

"You look ill, Carmilla. You look faint. You must drink some wine," I said, panic rising in my throat.

"Yes," she agreed, nodding slowly. "I will. I feel better already. I shall be quite well in moments. Yes, please, give me a little wine."

As we approached the door, I hesitated. "Let us linger just a moment longer. Perhaps it is the last time I shall see the moonlight with you."

"How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you truly better?" I asked, my voice shaking.

A dread began to coil in my stomach. Was she suffering from that strange epidemic rumored to sweep through the countryside, leaving victims catatonic or dead?

"If Father thought you were even slightly unwell," I stammered, "without telling us immediately, he would be grief-stricken. We have a highly skilled physician staying with us, the same one who visited Father today."

"I am sure he is excellent," she said, her gaze drifting past me. "I know how kind you all are. But, dear child, I am perfectly well again. Nothing has ever troubled me but a slight weakness. People say I am languid, incapable of effort, able to walk no farther than a toddler. Every now and then, this little strength falters, and I become... as you saw. But after all, I am easily revived. In a moment, I am myself. See how I have recovered."

Indeed, she had. The terror vanished from her countenance, replaced by a vibrant, animated liveliness. We talked endlessly, and the remainder of the evening passed without another recurrence of those terrifying, infatuated looks that had so unsettled me.

Yet, that night, an event occurred which shifted my thoughts entirely and seemed to jar Carmilla's lethargic nature into a flash of startled energy.
