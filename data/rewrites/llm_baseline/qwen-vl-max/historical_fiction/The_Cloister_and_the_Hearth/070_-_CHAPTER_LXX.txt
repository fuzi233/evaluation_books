Among the many vows Gerard had taken upon himself as a novice, one stood apart: to treat Lodovico with kindness, though in his heart he saw the man as venomous as a serpent. Yet this act of self-denial proved more powerful than he imagined. What began as a burden soon became a quiet joy—so much so that the penance ceased to feel like sacrifice at all.

At first, Lodovico’s pride was almost comical—his gaze fixed upon the very life he had once been paid to end, now preserved by his own hand. There was something absurd in the way he looked upon Gerard, as if he had earned salvation through mere survival. But even such vanity carried within it a seed of grace. For when a soul opens a door to mercy, other things follow—quietly, inevitably.

Lodovico found himself drawn to preserving life rather than taking it. Then came hesitation. Then regret. And then, slowly, something close to true repentance. Teresa, too, played her part. She warned him—again and again—that unless he abandoned his old ways, she would leave him. Her words were not gentle, but they struck deep. The nuns of the convent also lent their support, offering shelter and guidance.

Together, Lodovico and Teresa were sent by sea to Leghorn, where she had kin and friends. There, Lodovico settled into work as a porter—a humble post, far removed from the shadows of assassination. At first, he complained bitterly. "It's dull," he grumbled. "No excitement, no danger." But perhaps that dullness was the very thing he needed. Better to endure the slow grind of honest labor than to face the violent end that awaited him—the death by the Princess Claelia’s men, whom he had escaped only because fate had exiled him.

Meanwhile, the princess, thwarted in her vengeance, fell into deep sorrow. She dismissed her former confessor and sought out another—Fra Jerome, famed throughout Italy for his piety and unyielding truth. It was a stroke of divine irony that she should fall into his hands.

Fra Jerome listened to her tale without flinching. When she finished, he rose and spoke with the force of thunder. He did not spare her; he stripped away her illusions as one might shake dust from a cloak. To him, duty was above all—no prince’s anger could sway him, no title blind him.

“You are the devil,” he said, “who used your wealth to tempt a man into murder. And now, blinded by pride, you seek more blood—though none among those you wish to destroy is half as guilty as you.”

She resisted at first, offended that her confessor should speak to her so harshly. But Fra Jerome spared no effort. He threatened her with eternal damnation, tore the veil from her eyes, and left her trembling before him.

“Ah, holy father!” she cried, falling to her knees. “Have mercy on me! I am weak, lost—I see now how deceived I have been. Help me save my soul!”

“Doff your jewels,” he commanded.

“I will. I will.”

“Remove your silks and velvets. Dress as your lowest servant does. Go barefoot to Loretto—now.”

“I… I will.”

“No shoes. Only a sandal.”

“No father…”

“Wash the feet of pilgrims—both coming and going. And if any of them be holy friars, confess your sin to them, and accept their counsel.”

“Oh, holy father,” she begged, “let me wear my mask. My face is known across Italy.”

“Ay,” he replied grimly. “Beauty is often a curse. You may cover your eyes—but no more.”

She seized his hand, intent on kissing it in gratitude. But he pulled it away sharply.

“What would you do?” he snapped. “That hand touched the sacred host but an hour ago. Is it fit for a sinner like you to touch?”

“No,” she whispered. “But please—give your daughter your blessing.”

“Wait until you return from Loretto,” he said. “Then you may ask.”

And so it was. The events by the Tiber left lasting marks upon each who had passed through them. As prodigies do, they transformed lives.

Lodovico, softened by the life he had saved, turned from the dagger to the porter’s staff. The princess, humbled and broken, walked barefoot to Loretto, weeping and washing the feet of strangers—even those born beneath her. And Gerard—once brought to the convent on the brink of suicide—was now seen as a young saint among the brothers.

Eyes both loving and discerning watched over him. His probation was shortened. He was ordained priest sooner than most. Soon after, he took the solemn vows of the Dominican order and became a friar.

In dying to the world, he died also to his name. The man named Gerard vanished. In his place arose Brother Clement—a new identity, severed from all earthly ties. Here, the old life ended. And here, the monk began.
