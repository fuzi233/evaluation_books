The lad sank into a sweetness of sleep, as though the very house had put a hand upon his forehead and hushed him. All night the air had been full of kindly household sounds—soft footfalls on boards rubbed smooth by generations, the faint clink of a spoon set down, a shutter eased to against the moths, and, floating through it all like a thread of balm, the last hymn of evening sung in a clear young voice. He woke with the sun strong at the casement, a warm square of light lying across the counterpane; and there, smiling at his bed-curtains as if they had watched beside him, stood his host and hostess.

Hunger came on him like a fever of its own—ravenous, shameless, hearty. His doctor, who had bled him soundly and left him with that light swimming weakness which follows, examined the bandage and declared that appetite was the surest sign of mending.

“You shall eat, then,” said he; “and you shall eat what will knit you. No beef for you yet. A mess of chicken, with rice, and a little of the broth it was dressed in. My wife will see to it.”

Mrs. Lambert, pleased with her patient’s eagerness as if it were a compliment to her care, told him, as she drew the curtain a little more to give him the full cheer of the morning, that the dish had been prepared by one of her daughters’ own hands.

“One of her daughters?” The words, simple as they were, struck a spark upon the half-burnt tinder of his memory. A faint picture rose—two young faces with red cheeks and black waving locks bending over him, smiles that had looked like sunshine through leaves, and then the quick withdrawing of them when he came a little to himself. And on the heel of that came another remembrance—of a female indeed lovely, but older; certainly much older; and with f—.

Oh, horror and remorse! He turned upon the pillow with a groan as though his wounded shoulder had been stabbed anew. An immense gulf of time seemed to yawn between him and what had been last night and what had been long ago: pearls he had once been told were false, locks that were pinchbeck gold—boyhood warnings returned now like a bell heard in a fog. He had slept from a draught, had been empty of food, had bled, had fever in him; and the mind, loosened by such physic, will make phantoms and call them realities.

“What is it, my dear child?” cried kind Mrs. Lambert, frightened by his start.

“Nothing, madam,” said he, mastering himself. “A twinge in my shoulder. I— I speak to my host and hostess? Sure you have been very kind to me.”

“We are old friends, Mr. Warrington,” she answered with a look which made her kindness feel like a right. “My husband, Colonel Lambert, knew your father; and your mamma and I were girls together at Kensington. You were no stranger to us when your aunt and cousin told us who you were.”

“Are they here?” Harry asked; and for the first time the simple question had a blankness in it, as if he feared what the answer must bring.

“They must have lain at Tunbridge Wells last night,” said Mrs. Lambert. “They sent a horseman from Reigate yesterday for news of you.”

“Ah! I remember,” said Harry, and his eyes went to his bandaged arm as if it were a seal to the whole strange passage.

“I have made a good cure of you, Mr. Warrington,” the Colonel said, coming up with that blunt cheer which belongs to men who have seen wounds in earnest. “And now Mrs. Lambert and the cook must take charge of you.”

“Nay; Theo prepared the chicken and rice, Mr. Lambert,” said the lady, with a sort of pride that would not be hidden. “Will Mr. Warrington get up after he has had his breakfast? We will send your valet to you.”

“If howling proves fidelity,” said Mr. Lambert, “your man must be a most fond, attached creature.”

“He let your baggage travel off after all in your aunt’s carriage,” Mrs. Lambert continued. “You must wear my husband’s linen, which, I dare say, is not so fine as yours.”

“Pish, my dear! my shirts are good shirts enough for any Christian,” cried the Colonel.

“They are Theo’s and Hester’s work,” said mamma; and the Colonel’s eyebrows rose as if that fact were both pride and reproof. “And Theo hath ripped and sewed your sleeve to make it quite comfortable for your shoulder,” she added, as though the stitch itself were a virtue.

Harry, whose senses had returned with hunger, took note of everything about him with a new, bodily gratitude: the clean scent of linen warmed by sun, the faint sharpness of vinegar from the sick-room washing, and—on the toilet-table, beneath a japan-framed glass—a great China vase of roses, their perfume spilling into the air like a draught.

“What beautiful roses!” he cried, for the sight almost hurt him with its living colour.

“My daughter Theo cut them this morning,” said Mrs. Lambert quickly. “Well, Mr. Lambert? She did cut them!”

The Colonel, I suppose, thought his wife named Theo too often, for he trod on her slipper or gave some little nudge toward discretion; but Mrs. Lambert’s pleasure in her child’s services was not to be stifled.

“And I fancied I heard some one singing the Evening Hymn very sweetly last night—or was it only a dream?” Harry asked.

“Theo again, Mr. Warrington!” said the Colonel, laughing. “My servants said your negro man began it in the kitchen as if he was a church organ.”

“Our people sing it at home, sir,” Harry said; and the sound of his own voice saying it seemed to carry him far across the sea. “My grandpapa used to love it very much. His wife’s father was a great friend of good Bishop Ken, who wrote it; and—and my dear brother used to love it too.” His words fell away, and there was an instant in which the room’s cheerful noises—the clock’s tick, a distant creak of a pump-handle in the yard, the muted coo of pigeons—seemed to grow tender with his grief.

Mrs. Lambert looked as if she could have kissed him then, not from any forwardness, but because maternal pity will put its mouth upon sorrow as if to soothe it by touch. His mishap, his weakness, the simple pieties of the house, the readiness of their care, had softened him. For some weeks past he had breathed an air tainted with worldliness and corruption, where even kindness carried a price, and the talk of men had the ring of coin in it; here was a different climate, where attention was given as bread is broken—because it is right that one should not be hungry.

They left him to his breakfast, and the Colonel sat by the bed while the dish was brought. It was no grand entertainment; it was only a plain country mess, but prepared with that patient accuracy which makes homely food memorable. The chicken had been simmered, not fiercely boiled, so the flesh held its sweetness and did not shred into dryness. The broth had been skimmed till it shone, with a mild savour of parsley and onion that had yielded without asserting themselves. The rice lay in it plump and separate, not clotted, each grain softened through but still keeping its own shape—such a thing is not done without a cook’s attention and a young housekeeper’s pride. A little salt, and a squeeze—no more—of lemon, made the whole taste clean and restorative, as if it were meant to put the blood back in a man.

Gratitude and that comfortable repletion which comes of wholesome food set Mr. Warrington in a genial mood. He was more ready to speak than was his habit; for, unless some strong feeling stirred him, he was commonly cautious and taciturn, and no great maker of fancies. Books had been neglected with him, and of such as he had seen he spoke without cleverness; but of horses and dogs and the plain business of living he could talk freely, because those things came to him by eye, ear, and hand.

Colonel Lambert—who had shrewdness enough for men, and experience enough for both cattle and books—took his measure quickly. In their talk Harry learned, for the first time, that Mrs. Lambert had been early friend to his mother, and that Lambert’s father had served with Harry’s grandfather, Colonel Esmond, in Queen Anne’s wars. The sense of being among his own sort—of finding old ties in a strange land—eased him more than any medicine.

“My boys are both away,” said the Colonel, “or they would have shown you the country when you got up, Mr. Warrington. Now you can only have the company of my wife and her daughters. Mrs. Lambert hath told you already about one of them—Theo, our eldest—who made your broth, cut your roses, and mended your coat. She is not such a wonder as her mother imagines her: but little Theo is a smart housekeeper, and a very good and cheerful lass—though her father says it.”

“It is very kind of Miss Lambert to take so much care for me,” Harry said, and meant it, thinking of the steady hand that had sewn his sleeve and the careful skimming of that broth.

“She is no kinder to you than to any other mortal, and doth but her duty.” The Colonel smiled as he said it. “I laugh at their mother for praising our children, and I am as foolish about them myself. The truth is, God hath given us very good and dutiful children, and I see no reason why I should disguise my thankfulness. You have never a sister, I think?”

“No, sir. I am alone now,” Harry answered; and the words were heavier than he intended.

“Ay, truly, I ask your pardon for my thoughtlessness. Your man hath told our people what befell last year. I served with Braddock in Scotland, and hope he mended before he died. A wild fellow, sir, but there was a fund of truth about him, and no little kindness under his rough swagger. Your black fellow talks very freely about his master and his affairs. I suppose you permit him these freedoms as he rescued you—”

“Rescued me?” cried Harry, lifting himself with sudden heat.

“From ever so many Indians on that very expedition. My Molly and I did not know we were going to entertain so prodigiously wealthy a gentleman. He saith that half Virginia belongs to you; but if the whole of North America were yours, we could but give you our best.”

Harry coloured. “Those negro boys, sir, lie like the father of all lies. They think it is for our honour to represent us as ten times as rich as we are. My mother has what would be a vast estate in England, and is a very good one at home. We are as well off as most of our neighbours, sir, but no better; and all our splendour is in Mr. Gumbo’s foolish imagination. He never rescued me from an Indian in his life, and would run away at the sight of one, as my poor brother’s boy did on that fatal day when he fell.”

“The bravest man will do so at unlucky times,” said Lambert quietly. “I myself saw the best troops in the world run at Preston, before a ragged mob of Highland savages.”

“That was because the Highlanders fought for a good cause, sir.”

“Do you think,” asked Harry’s host, “that the French Indians had the good cause in the fight of last year?”

“The scoundrels!” Harry cried, clenching his fist. “I would have the scalp of every murderous redskin among ’em! They were robbing and invading the British territories, too. But the Highlanders were fighting for their king.”

“We, on our side, were fighting for our king; and we ended by winning the battle,” said the Colonel, with a laugh that took the edge off contention.

“Ah!” cried Harry; “if his Royal Highness the Prince had not turned back at Derby, your king and mine now would be his Majesty King James the Third!”

“Who made such a Tory of you, Mr. Warrington?” asked Lambert.

“Nay, sir, the Esmonds were always loyal!” Harry answered hotly. “Had we lived at home, and twenty years sooner, brother and I often and often agreed our heads would have been in danger. We certainly would have staked them for the king’s cause.”

“Yours is better on your shoulders than on a pole at Temple Bar. I have seen them there, and they don’t look very pleasant.”

“I shall take off my hat, and salute them, whenever I pass the gate,” cried Harry, carried away, “if the king and the whole court are standing by!”

“I doubt whether your relative, my Lord Castlewood, is as staunch a supporter of the king over the water,” said Colonel Lambert, smiling: “or your aunt, the Baroness of Bernstein, who left you in our charge. Whatever her old partialities may have been, she has repented of them; she has rallied to our side, landed her nephews in the Household, and looks to find a suitable match for her nieces. If you have Tory opinions, Mr. Warrington, take an old soldier’s advice, and keep them to yourself.”

“Why, sir, I do not think you will betray me!” said Harry.

“Not I; but others might. You did not talk in this way at Castlewood? I mean the old Castlewood you have just come from.”

“I might be safe amongst my own kinsmen, surely, sir!” Harry cried, stung.

“Doubtless. I would not say no. But a man’s own kin can play him slippery tricks at times, and he finds himself none the better for trusting them. I mean no offence to you or any of your family; but lacqueys have ears as well as their masters, and they carry about all sorts of stories. For instance, your black fellow is ready to tell all he knows about you, and a great deal more besides, as it would appear.”

Harry’s face flamed. “Hath he told about the broken-kneed horse?”

“To say truth, my groom seemed to know something of the story, and said it was a shame a gentleman should sell another such a brute—let alone a cousin. I am not here to play the Mentor to you, or to carry about servants’ tittle-tattle. When you have seen more of your cousins, you will form your own opinion of them; meanwhile, take an old soldier’s advice again, and be cautious with whom you deal, and what you say.”

Soon after, with Gumbo’s help, Harry rose. He promised his valet for the hundredth time a sound caning if ever he heard that Gumbo had ventured to talk of his affairs again in servants’ hall. Gumbo swore with solemn eyes that he would obey for ever; and, I dare say, had his mouth running even then, for news travels in kitchens as briskly as fire along dry straw, and servants will feed one another on secrets as eagerly as on ale and dripping. Mrs. Lambert, at any rate, came by a number of stories concerning her guest which she could not prevent her housekeeper from repeating; though of course one would not accuse any worthy lady—man or woman—of undue curiosity. Yet how is one to forbid listening, when talk comes unasked, like a cart rumbling past your gate?

Mr. Lambert’s house stood on the outskirts of the little town of Oakhurst, upon the road between Farnham and Reigate. Madame Bernstein’s people, in their confusion, naturally pulled at the first bell at hand when the accident befell; and so, instead of carrying the wounded gentleman a few hundred yards farther to the long street where a tavern’s swinging tuns promised hospitality and a surgeon’s blazing gilt pestle and mortar promised physic, they brought him into the hands of one who could have tended him no more cleverly had he taken a fee, and could have made him no more welcome if he had been kin.

Two tall gates, each topped by heraldic monsters, led from the highroad to a neat broad stone terrace on which stood Oakhurst House—a square brick building with stone-faced windows, high chimneys, and a tall roof with a fair balustrade. Behind it stretched a garden roomy enough for cabbages as well as roses; and before, separated by the highroad, lay a field where cows and horses grazed with that steady tearing sound of contented beasts, which, when the wind is right, is as soothing as the sea. Over the centre window was a carved shield supported by the same monsters and crowned with a coronet; for the house had once been the jointure-house of Oakhurst Castle, whose turrets and chimneys showed over the surrounding woods now dark with full summer leaf. Oakhurst House was the greatest in the town; but the Castle was of more importance than the town put together. The Castle and jointure-house had been friends for years: their fathers had fought side by side in Queen Anne’s wars. Two small pieces of ordnance stood on Lambert’s terrace, and six before the Castle; all had been taken from the same privateer which Lambert and his kinsman and commander, Lord Wrotham, had brought into Harwich from Flanders with despatches from the great Duke.

When Gumbo had dressed his master’s fair hair and set him neat, and when the wounded shoulder was supported by a handkerchief hung from his neck, Harry made his way out, preceded by his host. They went down a broad oak stair round which hung pikes and muskets of ancient shape, and so into a square marble-paved hall from which the rooms branched. Arms were everywhere—pikes and halberts, pistols and jack-boots more than a century old, that had served in Cromwell’s wars; a tattered French guidon borne by a gendarme at Malplaquet; and a pair of cumbrous Highland broadswords which, having been carried as far as Derby, had been flung away on the fatal field of Culloden. Breastplates and black morions of Oliver’s troopers gleamed dully, and portraits of stern men in buff jerkins and plain bands looked down with cropped hair and uncompromising mouths.

“They fought against your grandfathers and King Charles, Mr. Warrington,” said Lambert, noticing the boy’s troubled gaze. “I don’t hide that. They rode to join the Prince of Orange at Exeter. We were Whigs, young gentleman—and something more. John Lambert, the Major-General, was a kinsman of our house, and we were all more or less partial to short hair and long sermons. You do not seem to like either?”

Harry’s face indeed showed no pleasure at the company of Parliamentary heroes. The house itself, with its relics and its words, spoke a dialect of loyalty different from his own; and in that difference there was a quiet power. A man might swear one thing in a drawing-room, and find it translated another way in a kitchen. Even names—Whig, Tory—were like spices: in one mouth they warmed, in another they bit.

“Be not alarmed,” Lambert continued, good-humoured. “We are very good Churchmen now. My eldest son will be in orders ere long. He is now travelling as governor to my Lord Wrotham’s son in Italy. And as for our women, they are all for the Church, and carry me with ’em. Every woman is a Tory at heart. Mr. Pope says a rake, but I think t’other is the more charitable word. Come, let us go see them.”

He flung open the dark oak door, and led Harry into the parlour where the ladies were gathered.

“Here is Miss Hester,” said the Colonel, “and this is Miss Theo—the soup-maker, the tailoress, the harpsichord-player, and the songstress who set you to sleep last night. Make a curtsey to the gentleman, young ladies! Oh, I forgot—Theo is mistress of the roses you admired awhile since in your bedchamber. I think she has kept some of them in her cheeks.”

Miss Theo, making a curtsey as deep and ceremonious as the times required, blushed in good earnest. She was not a particular beauty, nor did Harry fall headlong at first sight, faithless to that other person who had lately bewitched him. But she had kind eyes and a sweet voice; a ruddy freckled cheek; a round white neck from which, beneath a little cap such as misses wore, fell rich curling clusters of dark brown hair. She was no delicate, sentimental creature: her bare arms from the elbow were jolly and red with work and health; her feet were not so miraculously small but that one might see them without a telescope; her waist had nothing waspish about it. She was sixteen and looked older, as girls who manage household things often do. Yet she blushed as if she had done something bold in bringing a bowl of broth to a stranger, and rose from her curtsey with the innocence of one who has been praised too openly.

As she came up, her father tapped her under the chin—there was a hint of a double chin there, honest and wholesome—and hummed, laughing, a French line he had read that day. “Eh bien! que dites-vous, ma fille, de notre hôte?”

“Nonsense, Mr. Lambert!” cried mamma, shocked at his freedom.

“Nonsense is sometimes the best kind of sense in the world,” said Colonel Lambert. Harry looked puzzled, as if the language itself had slipped past him.

“Are you fond of nonsense?” the Colonel asked him, watching his face with a kindly mischief. “We consume a vast deal of it in this house. Rabelais is my favourite reading. My wife is all for Mr. Fielding and Theophrastus. I think Theo prefers Tom Brown, and Mrs. Hetty here loves Dean Swift.”

“Our papa is talking what he loves,” said Miss Hetty.

“And what is that, miss?” asked the father of his second daughter.

“Sure, sir, you said yourself it was nonsense,” she answered, with a saucy toss.

“Which of them do you like best, Mr. Warrington?” asked the Colonel.

“Which of whom, sir?”

“The Curate of Meudon, or the Dean of St. Patrick’s, or honest Tom, or Mr. Fielding?”

“And what were they, sir?”

“They! Why, they wrote books.”

“Indeed, sir. I never heard of either one of ’em,” said Harry, hanging down his head. “I fear my book-learning was neglected at home. My brother had read every book that ever was wrote, I think. He could have talked to you about ’em for hours together.”

At this, Mrs. Lambert’s eyes went to her daughter, and Miss Theo cast hers down and blushed anew—so quickly do households keep their own private histories, and so silently do certain names and losses pass between women like a thing understood. The room, full of sunlight and polished wood and the faint smell of yesterday’s beeswax, seemed to hold also a shadow of that absent brother—present by the very mention of him, and absent by the untranslatable gap between what one man can feel and another can say.

“Never mind; honesty is better than books any day, Mr. Warrington!” cried the jolly Colonel. “You may go through the world very honourably without reading any of the books I have been talking of; and some of them might give you more pleasure than profit.”

“I know more about horses and dogs than Greek and Latin, sir. We most of us do in Virginia,” Harry said.

“You are like the Persians; you can ride and speak the truth.”

“Are the Prussians very good on horseback, sir? I hope I shall see their king and a campaign or two, either with ’em or against ’em,” Harry remarked.

Why did Miss Theo glance at her mother, and why did that good woman’s face take on a sadness that was like a cloud crossing the sun? Because young lasses in humdrum country towns are not born without dreams. Because they wear caps and curtsey deep and have never quitted mother’s apron, do you suppose they have no inward story, no private language of the heart? The world’s great trumpet calls—war, travel, glory—sound to them even through garden walls and dairy doors. They hear it in the crack of a distant whip on the road, in the post-horn’s faint note, in the roll of a cart over stones, and they translate it into their own dialect of longing.

In fairy tales, you understand, the blessed hour always comes: the ivory horn is blown at the castle gate; and far off in her bower the princess starts up and knows it is the right champion. He is always ready; giants’ heads tumble as he gallops on his white charger, falchion flashing. How should a maiden locked in an inaccessible fortress, who has never seen any man not eighty, or humpbacked, or her father, know such beings as young men existed? Yet she does. There is an instinct; there is a season; there is that watchful expectancy which sits like a bird at the window of the breast.

Sister Ann, Sister Ann, do you see him? Surely it is a knight with curling mustachios and silver armour. Oh no—it is only a costermonger with his donkey and pannier of cabbages. Sister Ann, Sister Ann, what is that cloud of dust? Only a farmer’s man driving pigs from market. Sister Ann, Sister Ann, who is that splendid warrior in scarlet and gold? He nears the gate; he lifts the hammer; he knocks twice—ah me, it is only the postman with a double letter from Northamptonshire.

So we make false starts in life. I do not believe there is any such thing remembered as first love—no more than one remembers one’s christening. You fancy your spotless maiden never cared for any but you, and she tells you so? Oh, you innocent! When she was four years old she had a tenderness for the Buttons who carried coals up to the nursery, or the little sweep at the crossing, or the music-master, or never mind whom. She had a secret longing toward her brother’s schoolfellow, or the third charity boy at church; and had occasion served, the comedy enacted with you might have been performed with another. Not that she confessed it; only that she had it. Lay down this page and think how many times you were in love before you chose the present Mrs. Jones as partner of your name and affections.
