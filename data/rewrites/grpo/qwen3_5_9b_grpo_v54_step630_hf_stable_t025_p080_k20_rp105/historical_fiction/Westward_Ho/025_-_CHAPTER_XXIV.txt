"Let us alone. What pleasure can we have
To war with evil? Is there any peace
In always climbing up the climbing wave?
All things have rest, and ripen toward the grave
In silence; ripen, fall, and cease:
Give us long rest or death, dark death, or dreamful ease."

Alexander von Humboldt, in his travels, once observed a group of Indians squatting around their fires, smeared with grease and paint, eating ants and clay. He naively remarked that without the comforting doctrine of science—which claims such behavior is merely the crude starting point of humanity—he might have viewed them as the final, degraded remnants of a fallen race. It is a pity the great traveler did not succumb to that temptation, choosing instead to cling to the dogmas of modern anthropology rather than the older, more honest biblical truth: that man in a state of nature is a fallen being, destined for death. It is far more hopeful to believe these poor souls were once nobler and have declined, than to believe they have always been blockheads incapable of improvement.

Our voyagers, however, cared little for such philosophical debates. Accepting the Bible as their guide, they sided with Humboldt's common sense rather than his scientific theories. To them, these new hosts were immortal souls, "captivated by the devil at his will," lost in the pathless forests and likely to remain lost forever. And indeed, the facts seemed to support their fears. While these Indians had not sunk quite as low as the Guahibas they had encountered lower down the river, the signs of the evil spirit were unmistakable.

Upon landing, they found a scattered village of palm-leaf sheds with hammocks strung between trees. Patches of cassava and indigo plants dotted the forest floor, suggesting a level of neatness superior to the average settlement. Yet, immediately flanking the landing place were four or five stout fellows wielding tall drums and long earthen trumpets. The moment the strangers stepped ashore, a deafening cacophony of howls, groans, and thumps erupted, fully justifying Yeo's remark, "They are calling upon their devil, sir." Cary, with a touch of reason, added that the Indians were unlikely to be disappointed, for "none but Sir Urian would ever come to listen to such a noise."

"And you mark, sirs," said Yeo, "there's some feast or sacrifice toward. I'm not overconfident of them yet."

"Nonsense!" said Amyas. "We could kill every soul of them in half an hour, and they know that as well as we."

Yet a great demonstration was clearly underway. The forest children were arrayed in two lines, men in front and women behind, bedizened with arnotto, indigo, and feathers. Suddenly, a figure leapt into the center of the clearing—a personage who could not have complained if anyone had mistaken him for the devil himself. Dressed in a jaguar-skin with a long tail, grinning teeth, horns, a plume of black and yellow feathers, and a huge rattle, he produced a hideous yell.

"Here's the Piache, the rascal," said Amyas.

"Ay," said Yeo, "in Satan's livery, and I've no doubt his works are according, trust him for it."

"Don't be frightened, Jack," said Cary, stepping back to support Brimblecombe. "It's your business to tackle him, you know. At him boldly, and he'll run."

The men laughed, and the Piache, realizing his solemn impression had failed, recovered himself. He smote a musician over the head with his rattle to procure silence and began a harangue, to which Amyas listened patiently, cigar in mouth.

"What's it all about, boy?"

"He wants to know whether you have seen Amalivaca on the other shore of the great water?"

Amyas, accustomed to this inquiry regarding the mythic civilizer who carved the inland cliffs before vanishing beyond the ocean, answered by praising Queen Elizabeth. The Piache replied that she must be one of Amalivaca's seven daughters, some of whom he had taken back while breaking the legs of the rest to prevent escape. Amyas corrected him, stating that his queen's legs were certainly not broken, for she was a model of grace and the best dancer in her dominions. More importantly, he asked if the tribe would provide cassava bread and allow them to rest on the island before fighting the Spaniards across the mountains.

The Piache, capering and howling, beckoned Amyas and his party to follow him. Seeing the Indians were unarmed and in high spirits, they complied. The Piache led them to a carefully closed hut, crawling up to it on all fours in an abject fashion to consult the "Daughter of the Sun."

"Ask what he is about, boy."

The lad asked the old cacique, who replied that the Piache was consulting the Daughter of the Sun.

"Here is our mare's nest at last," quoth Cary, as the Piache rose from whines to screams, gesticulating violently until he foamed at the mouth and collapsed, exhausted.

"As good as a stage play."

"The devil has played his part," said Jack. "And now, by the rules of all plays, Vice should come on."

"And a very fair Vice it will be, I suspect; a right sweet Iniquity, my Jack! Listen."

From within the hut rose a low, sweet song. The simple Indians bowed their heads in reverence, and the English were hushed in astonishment. The voice was not shrill or guttural like an Indian's, but round, clear, and rich, like a European's. As it swelled, displaying a compass and power extraordinary for any singer, the wild wanderers were rapt in new delight. The notes leaped and ran, perfect in melody like a bird on a bough, causing the Indians to welcome them as messengers from a higher world. Finally, one triumphant burst rang out, followed by dead silence. The Piache, suddenly restored, jumped upright and recommenced his preaching.

"Tell the howling villain to make short work of it, lad! His tune won't do after that last one."

The lad grinned, informing Amyas that the Piache signified their acceptance as friends by the Daughter of the Sun. Whereupon the Indians screamed with delight, and Amyas, rolling another tobacco leaf, asked, "Then let her give us some cassava."

The door opened, and the Indians prostrated themselves as a fair apparition emerged—the same maiden they had seen on the island, now decked in feather-robes of every imaginable hue. She walked slowly and stately to Amyas, glancing proudly at her prostrate adorers. Pointing gracefully to the trees, gardens, and huts, she conveyed by expressive signs that all was at his service. Taking his hand, she lifted it gently to her forehead.

At this sign of submission, a shout of rapture rose from the crowd. As the mysterious maiden retired, the English were pressed by adoring Indians who marveled at their swords, bows, and beast trophies. Women hastened to bring fruit, flowers, cassava, and calabashes of intoxicating drink. The English sat beneath the trees, feasting merrily while drums made hideous music and lithe young girls performed uncouth dances that scandalized Brimblecombe and Yeo. Persuaded to beat an early retreat, Amyas returned to the island-fortress, racking his brains to identify the maiden.

Amyas settled in his mind that she was one of the lost Inca race, perhaps a descendant of the fair girl, wife of the Inca Manco, whom Pizarro had tortured and killed forty years prior. That evening, after their customary psalm and catch, twenty mellow voices rang out above the roar of the cataract. Once or twice they thought they heard an echo, but it was only until Cary returned from a walk that they realized the truth.

"The sweet Iniquity is mimicking us, lad."

They went to the river's brink and heard plainly the same voice repeating snatches of the airs they had sung. The effect was strange and solemn: the men's deep voices answered out of the dark forest by those sweet treble notes. They stood long listening to the eddies swirling golden in the moonlight, seeing nothing beyond the black wall of trees, before returning to dream of Incas and nightingales.

Over the next week, the maiden appeared rarely, keeping her distance as haughtily as a queen. When Amyas questioned the cacique about her, the old man made an owl's face and intimated she was a very strange personage, a "child of the Sun," and that the less said about her the better. Cary, however, threatened to blight all the cassava plants unless the cacique explained that they were also children of the Sun, sent to inquire how the Indians had treated their step-sister.

The cacique, taking them in his canoe a mile down the stream, told them in a rhythmic chant how his tribe had once been a mighty nation in Papamene until the Spaniards drove them forth. Wandering northward beneath the cone of Cotopaxi, they had found this fair creature, about the size of a seven-year-old, wandering in the forest. Wondering at her white skin and delicate beauty, they worshipped her as a god and led her home. Realizing she was human, their wonder scarcely lessened; how could such a tender being survive the forests and escape the jaguar? She must be under Divine protection, a daughter of the Sun, one of the mighty Inca race whose fall had reached even these lonely wildernesses. They believed she would rise again to power when bearded white men came to restore them to their ancient throne.

As she grew, she was tended with royal honors by the tribe's conjuror to propitiate the Sun. She became a prophetess as well as an object of fetish-worship, more prudent in council, valiant in war, and cunning in the chase than all the elders. Her strange, sweet songs were full of mysterious wisdom about birds, animals, and flowers, taught her by the Sun and the Good Spirit. She remained unmarried, despising Indian youths and forbidden by the conjuror to mingle with the race of the Sun.

Cary remarked that the conjuror made a very good thing of it, but Amyas was silent, full of dreams about Manoa. Perhaps the Incas had moved eastward to escape the Spaniards, leaving this girl behind. He sighed at the hopelessness of searching with his diminished band, yet he hoped to learn the truth from the maiden herself. If he could get home safely, many gallant gentlemen, including Raleigh, would join him in a fresh search for the Golden City of Guiana.

The cacique entreated them to pray to the Sun for his descendant and begged them not to take her away, lest the Sun forget the poor Omaguas. Amyas had no wish to stay longer than necessary to retrieve the sick men from the Orinoco, a journey likely to take months.

Cary volunteered to undertake the adventure with half a dozen men and Indian help, but obtaining the latter was difficult. The tribe feared the fierce Guahibas, and every Indian tribe viewed others of different languages as natural enemies. Amyas and his party attributed this strange fact to the delusion of the devil, the divider and accuser.

Whether pride or shyness kept the maiden aloof, she eventually conquered it. She gave the English to understand that none of them was to be her companion but Amyas. Ere a month was past, she was often hunting with him in the forest, accompanied by a train of chosen nymphs who had persuaded their peers to spurn the dusky suitors. This fashion, common among Indian tribes where women escape male tyranny, convinced the English they were near the land of the famous Amazons. Amyas had no doubt that, as a descendant of the Incas, the maiden preserved the tradition of the Virgins of the Sun and their austere monastic rule.

A harmless friendship sprang up between Amyas and the girl, which soon turned to good account. Upon hearing he needed a crew for Cary's expedition, she consulted the Piache, assembled the tribe, and retired to her hut to commence a song. Unless the Piache lied, this song was a command to furnish young men for the expedition under penalty of the sovereign displeasure of an evil spirit with an unpronounceable name. The argument succeeded on the spot, and the canoe departed on its perilous errand.

John Brimblecombe doubted whether a venture started by the direct patronage of the fiend would succeed. Amyas, disliking the humbug, told Ayacanora it would be better to claim the deed pleased the Good Spirit.

"Ah!" said she, naively, "they know better than that. The Good Spirit is big and lazy; he smiles and takes no trouble. But the little bad spirit is so busy—here, and there, and everywhere. He is the useful one to have for a friend!"

The Piache much approved this sentiment. He told Brimblecombe sharply that if the Indians began asking the Good Spirit for what they wanted, who would bring him cassava and coca for keeping the bad spirit quiet? This argument, though forcible, did not stop Jack's preaching or the morning and evening services in the island camp. The Indians, attracted by the singing, attended in such numbers that the Piache found his occupation gone and vowed to put an end to Jack's Gospel with a poisoned arrow.

Blinded by his master, Satan, the Piache attempted to impart this plan to Ayacanora, expecting her partnership in tithes and offerings. He was exceedingly astonished to receive a box on the ear and a storm of abuse. Afterward, Ayacanora went to Amyas and proposed that the Piache be thrown to the alligators and Jack installed in his place, declaring that whatever the bearded men said must be true and whoever plotted against them should die.
