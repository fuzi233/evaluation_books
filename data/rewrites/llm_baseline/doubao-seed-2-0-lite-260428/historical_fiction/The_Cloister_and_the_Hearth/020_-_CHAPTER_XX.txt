The courage of ordinary men, much like their natural talent, runs within a narrow, well-worn groove. Drag them but an inch outside the bounds of what they know, and it curdles into nothing.
Martin Wittenhaagen’s courage had never failed him before, not in all his rough life. He had faced down wild boars in the thicket, outwitted river pirates on the Meuse, stood frostbitten and hungry through a three-day blizzard in the dunes, and every familiar danger he met he bore with Spartan fortitude, almost unflinching indifference. But he had never been hunted by a bloodhound. He had never seen that brute’s unerring, unshakable instinct outrun man’s cleverest trickery. Now, novelty tangled with a creeping, unspoken sense of the supernatural, and the iron in his chest turned soft. He stumbled to a halt a few paces into the trees, leaned his weight on his gnarled yew bow, and felt all his energy, all his hope, seep out of him like water through a sieve.

Gerard, for his part, had thought the danger small for as long as it stayed distant, and he urged the older man to run, to put more miles between them and the hunt.
“What use is running?” Martin said, his voice heavy with despair. “If we break for the open fields, they’ll cut us down like stray dogs before we’ve run a mile. But here, close by, I know a spot where we’ll sell our lives dear, not cheap.”
“Ah, good Martin, don’t give up so fast!” Gerard cried. “There has to be some way out of this.”
“Wait,” Margaret cut in, her mind sharp while the two men hung limp with dread. “What if we split apart? It’s only Gerard’s life they want. Can we not draw the hound after the two of us, and let him slip away safe?”
“Girl, you do not know the nature of a bloodhound,” Martin sighed, not lifting his head from where it hung over his bow. “He does not track a man’s face, or his clothes. He tracks blood. I’d stake my life they took that cur straight to the spot where Ghysbrecht fell. From the old man’s spilt blood, that hound will follow the man who shed it, no matter if Gerard runs through a whole army or swims the full breadth of the Meuse.”

The hound’s deep, mellow bay rolled through the wood then, clear as a bell. No hunter’s horn in Crete, Sparta, or Thessaly ever called a pack to heel so sweetly. It was strange, was it not? that something so beautiful could be so deadly? The boa constrictor’s eyes, when it fascinates its prey, glow like no jewel in any royal crown: ruby red shot through with emerald green, and the deer that meets that gaze loses all power to move, trembling as it waits for death. Just so, that soft, ringing bay seemed to wrap around Martin, to freeze his limbs. He stood bewildered, unstrung, all his hard-won nerve gone to nothing.
Gerard was little better. Martin’s words had daunted him. He had struck an old man, shed his blood, and now that very blood had summoned a four-footed avenger on his heels. Could this not be the hand of Heaven, reaching out to punish him?

While the two men stood benumbed, Margaret’s brain raced faster than it ever had. The man she loved was marked for death.
“Give me your knife,” she said to Martin, quiet as a shadow.
He passed it over, half careless. “Twill do you no good, lass, soft as your hands are.”
Margaret turned her back to them both before they could see, drew the sharp blade across the soft flesh of her upper arm, and let the blood flow freely. She stooped, smeared the warm, dark liquid all over her wool hose, her leather shoes, dabbing at the grass beneath her heels to leave a strong, fresh scent trail, and never once let the two men notice what she did. When she was done, she seized Martin’s arm, hard.
“Come on, be a man!” she snapped. “Lead us to the thickest part of the wood, the kind that swallows numbers whole. There our foes’ numbers won’t do them any good.”
“I’m going,” Martin said, sullen as a man tramping to the gallows. “Hurry does no good. We can’t outrun the hound, and the place is but a furlong off.” He turned left, leading the way, and soon brought them to a dense hazel coppice, thick with thin, springy shoots, very like the thicket that had hidden them from the patrol earlier that morning.
“This is it,” he said. “It’s no more than a furlong across, but it’ll serve.”
“What’s the plan?” Gerard asked.
“Push through to the other side, wait there. When they come straggling out after us, I’ll shoot three with my bow, you’ll crack two heads with your cudgel, and the rest will swarm us and kill us dead.”
“That’s all you can think of?” Gerard said, shaking his head. “Then I’m taking the lead, for you’ve lost your wits. Can you obey a boy half your age, Martin Wittenhaagen?”
“Oh yes, do as he says!” Margaret put in at once. “He’s wiser than his years.”
Martin gave a sullen, grudging nod.
“Then copy exactly what I do,” Gerard said, drawing his big hunting knife. At every step he took, he cut one or two hazel shoots clean off at the root, then twisted the loose ends up breast-high through the standing, living stems, weaving a tangled, invisible barrier that would catch at clothes and slow a man’s steps to a crawl. Martin followed suit, moving slow, dogged, still convinced they were all going to die.

Halfway through the coppice, the hound’s bay grew louder, closer, no longer sweet and mellow, but sharp, harsh, thunderous. Margaret trembled so hard her teeth chattered. Martin dropped flat to the leaf litter, pressing his ear to the ground.
“I hear horse hooves,” he muttered.
“Not a horse,” Gerard said. “A mule. That cursed Ghysbrecht isn’t dead at all. No one else would hound me this hard.”
“Never strike an enemy unless you mean to kill him,” Martin growled.
“I’ll hit far harder this time, if Heaven lets me get close enough,” Gerard swore.

At last they pushed through the last of the hazel, and came out into an open stretch of old wood, with great gnarled oaks standing far apart, no cover worth the name. Now the hound’s bay was tangled up with a dozen voices, shouting and hallooing, the whole village out to hunt them down.
“The entire parish is on our trail,” Martin said.
“I don’t care,” Gerard whispered. “Listen. The path we wove will be smooth as silk for the hound’s nose, but tangled as a thorn bush for the men chasing him. The dog will outrun all the rest, and we will kill him the second he steps out of the coppice.”
“Kill the hound?” Martin said. “There’s more than one.”
“I only hear the one barking.”
“Aye, only the lead hound gives tongue. The rest run silent. If he loses the scent, the next will start baying. There’s at least two dogs here, devils in dog fur.”
“Then we kill two,” Gerard said. “The second they are dead, we dive back into the coppice, and run the whole length of it back the way we came, right past the men following the hound.”
Martin’s eyes flashed, a flicker of his old courage coming back. “That’s a sharp thought, lad.”
“Hush. The men are in the coppice now.”

Gerard gave his orders in a whisper. “You take your bow and hide in that ditch right at the edge of the trees. I’ll go a dozen paces out to that big oak, and hide behind it. The dogs will be straight on my scent. When they come bursting out, you shoot as many as you can, and I’ll bash the rest’s heads in before they know what’s happening.”
Martin nocked an arrow, his old sharp self again. The shouts drew closer, the rustle of young hazel stems crackling, and every few seconds the bloodhound gave a short, sharp bay, a death knell ringing minute by minute.
A trembling hand fell on Gerard’s shoulder, making him jump half out of his skin. “Martin says if we have to split, make for the tall ash tree at the edge of the wood where we first came in.”
“Go back, for Heaven’s sake!” Gerard hissed. “Don’t stay out here in the open!”

Margaret turned to run back to Martin, but before she could reach him, a great brindled hound burst out of the coppice. It stood stock still for a heartbeat, nose down, and then gave a deafening snarl and launched itself straight at Gerard’s oak. Before it could cover half the distance, Martin’s bow twanged, and the arrow caught it full in the chest, spitting it clean through. The hound rolled head over heels and dropped dead on the spot.
The second hound came bounding out right after, nosed its dead comrade, and Gerard charged at it with his cudgel. He didn’t get there in time: a streak of white movement, and the hound crumpled to the dust, screaming piteously, a knife wound deep in its flank. But before Gerard could finish it off, the coppice exploded with noise. Three men, who had outpaced the rest of the hunt, burst out of the hazel, and Gerard barely had time to gesture wildly at Martin to go back, before he turned and scrambled back into the tangled thicket on all fours. Margaret and Martin, their wits sharp now, followed the same path, crawling back along the line of the trail they had woven. The pursuers and the pursued passed within a few yards of each other, moving in opposite directions through the brush.
A loud cry went up, as the hunters spotted the dead and dying hounds. Voices babbled, swelling as more and more villagers piled onto the scene, all crowding around, exclaiming, wasting precious minutes.
“I don’t hear any more hounds,” Martin whispered to Margaret. His nerve was fully back now.
It was Margaret’s turn to tremble, to feel despair closing over her. “Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They will kill my Gerard, and I will not be there to help him.”
“Nay, nay, those fools haven’t the wit to catch him. You told him to meet us at the ash tree, aye?”
“I did,” Margaret said, brightening. “Bless you for remembering. To the ash tree, then!”
“Softly, no noise.”

They were almost at the edge of the coppice, when they heard shouts and hallooing behind them. The hunters had worked out the fugitives were still in the thicket, and were beating their way back across it.
“No matter,” Martin whispered to his trembling companion. “We can break for the far treeline, run hard, and lose them in the open fields before they see us.” He tensed to burst out of the brush, then froze.
Seated on his mule at the edge of the wood was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. A bloody bandage was tied across his broken nose, his eyes sharp and alert above it, and he had clearly heard their rustle, craning his neck to spot them. Martin muttered a vicious oath, strung his bow slow, nocked his last arrow. Margaret pressed her hands to her face, but did not make a sound. She knew one of them had to die, the burgomaster or Gerard. She peered through her fingers, her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears.
Martin drew the bowstring back, the arrowhead level with his eye, ready to loose, when a figure launched itself at Ghysbrecht out of the trees behind him, fast as a hawk stooping on a pigeon. A kerchief whipped over the burgomaster’s face, his head was muffled tight in an instant, and he was hauled off his mule, crashing hard to the dirt, groaning in terror. Gerard jumped down after him.
“Hist! Martin! Margaret! Over here!”
They stumbled out of the coppice, Martin gaping. “Run! Now, while they’re all busy in the thicket! We’re saved!”

But the rush of the fight, the loss of blood from the cut on her arm, caught up to Margaret all at once. She swayed on her feet, gasping. “Oh my love, go on without me. Leave me, I’m too faint to run.”
“Not a step without you,” Gerard said, catching her as she fell. “We live together or die together. The mule! Put her on the mule, Martin, I’ll run at her side.”
Martin swung himself up on the mule’s back in an instant, Gerard lifted the fainting girl, settled her safe in the saddle, and grabbed Martin’s bow from him.
“Treason! Murder! Help!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, rearing up off the ground.
“Silence, you cur!” Gerard roared, shoving his boot down hard on the burgomaster’s throat, the way a man crushes an adder under his heel. “You hold on tight there, Margaret! Ride for your life!”
The mule lurched forward, its hooves kicking up sparks from the flints as it broke into a canter. Gerard lifted his boot off Ghysbrecht’s throat to run, and that very second, Dierich Brower and his five men, who had circled back for new orders, came bursting roaring out of the coppice, straight at them.
