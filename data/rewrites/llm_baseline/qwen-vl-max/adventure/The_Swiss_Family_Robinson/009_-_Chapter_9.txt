At last, the storm passed. The wind calmed, the clouds broke apart, and golden sunlight poured down upon the island like a blessing. Rain ceased, and spring had arrived. Stepping out of our winter shelter, we felt as though we’d been set free after years in captivity. The world sparkled with fresh green life, birds sang from every tree, and the air was sweet and warm—alive with the promise of new beginnings.

Our crops were thriving. Seeds we’d planted months before now pushed through the damp earth, sprouting strong and fast. Nature herself seemed to be breathing again.

First, we turned our attention to our nest—the shelter we’d built high in the trees. It had suffered during the storms: leaves scattered, branches broken, the whole structure torn by the wind. We worked hard to repair it, patching holes and reinforcing weak spots. In just a few days, it stood once more as a safe, snug home.

My wife asked me to begin preparing flax for her spinning. I quickly built a drying oven and then crafted a beetle-reel and a spinning wheel. Soon, she and Franz were busy at work—she spinning thread, he winding it neatly onto the reel. The boy’s hands moved with growing skill, and his eyes shone with pride.

I worried about what might have happened to our stores at Tentholm. With Fritz, I made the journey there. What we found was worse than I feared. The tent had been blown flat, its canvas shredded, provisions soaked through, and two casks of powder completely ruined. But the pinnace remained safe, though our beloved tub-boat lay in splinters on the shore.

This loss made me determined: we needed a better shelter for the next rainy season. Something stronger, safer, and permanent. Fritz suggested carving a cave into the rock. At first, the idea seemed impossible—but I saw no reason not to try. Even if we couldn’t dig a full chamber, we could create a cellar to protect our most precious supplies.

A few days later, we loaded the cart with tools—spades, hammers, chisels, pickaxes, crowbars—and set off toward the cliff face near Falconhurst. On the smooth stone wall, I marked the size of the entrance with chalk. Then, with hearts full of hope, we began to chip away.

For six long days, we struck the rock without much progress. The hole barely widened. It looked as though even Master Knips, our smallest dog, wouldn’t fit inside. Yet we did not give up. Then, one morning, the stone beneath us softened. Our tools bit deeper. Progress came faster. Our spirits lifted.

On the tenth day, Jack, hammering fiercely with a crowbar, suddenly shouted:
“Father! It’s gone! My bar has gone through the mountain!”

Fritz laughed. “Run around and get it—maybe it fell into Europe! Don’t lose such a good tool.”

“No, really!” Jack insisted. “It went right through! I heard it crash inside! Come see!”

We rushed to him. I thrust my hammer into the hole. It met no resistance. I could turn it freely. Fritz handed me a long pole. I tested the depth—nothing touched the end. There was only a thin wall between us and a vast, hidden space.

With shouts of joy, the boys swung their tools harder. Rock crumbled. In minutes, the opening grew wide enough to crawl through.

I stepped close, ready to explore—when suddenly, a wave of thick, foul air knocked me dizzy. I gasped and called out:
“Stand back! This air is dangerous!”

When I recovered, I explained: “Stagnant air becomes poisonous. Without fresh oxygen, it can kill. The best way to clean it? Fire. Throw burning hay into the hole. If the flame dies instantly, the air is still too bad.”

We tried. The fire vanished as soon as it entered. We threw in bundles of blazing grass—no change.

Clearly, we needed something stronger. I sent the boys to fetch the chest of signal rockets we’d saved from the wreck. Dozens of fiery serpents shot into the darkness, whizzing deep into the cave. They lit up the walls like stars, revealing a glittering roof and soaring crystal pillars—like a palace carved by magic.

After waiting a while, I threw in lighted hay again. This time, it burned brightly. The air was now safe.

Fritz and I widened the entrance. Jack raced off on Storm, the buffalo, galloping back to Falconhurst with news so astonishing it would surely make everyone leap from their beds.

And indeed, they did. Jack’s story reached them fast. Soon, the bridge echoed with hooves, and the rest of us followed in the cart—excited, curious, eager.

We brought candles, arms, and torches. I led the way, probing the ground ahead with a long pole, careful not to fall into unseen pits.

Silence fell over us all—my wife, the boys, even the dogs—as we entered the cavern. It was breathtaking. A grotto of diamonds. A cathedral of crystal. The candlelight danced on walls that shimmered like gold, while towering columns rose from the floor, branching overhead like trees, their tips dangling with rainbows of stalactites.

The floor was dry, hard sand—perfect for living. I tasted a piece of the crystal: salt. Pure, white rock-salt. An endless supply!

But one thing troubled me: large crystals littered the ground, fallen from above. If more dropped, we’d be in danger. I examined them closely and realized they had broken recently. Likely, the shock of the rockets had shaken the roof.

To test this, we fired our guns from the entrance. Nothing fell. The cave was stable.

We returned to Falconhurst, hearts full of wonder and plans. Now, nothing else mattered but building our new home.

We decided Falconhurst would become a summer retreat. The cave would be our winter fortress—our true stronghold.

Work began at once. We cut windows into the rock, fitting them with frames from the officers’ cabins. The door from Falconhurst was installed. I covered the hollow in the tree trunk with bark, so wild animals or strangers wouldn’t notice it.

Inside, we divided the cave into four sections:
- The front room, open to the entrance, became our sitting, dining, and sleeping area.
- To the right: kitchen and workshop.
- To the left: stables.
- Deep in the rear: storehouse and powder magazine.

We learned from our past mistakes. No more smoke-filled rooms. We built a proper fireplace with a chimney. The stable was larger, the workshop spacious—ready for any project.

Our visits to Tentholm revealed more treasures. Giant turtles came ashore to lay eggs. We caught them by turning them over and tying them to stakes near the water. They stayed healthy for weeks, giving us fresh meat whenever we wanted.

Lobsters, crabs, and mussels lined the shore. But the greatest surprise came one morning.

As we approached Tentholm, the sea beyond the bay churned. The surface flashed like fire under the sun. Hundreds of birds circled, screaming, diving into the waves.

“What is that?” Franz asked.

“It’s a herring bank,” I said. “A massive school of fish, swimming together—thousands deep, stretching across miles. Fishermen call it a ‘bank.’ These herrings are hunted by sharks below and birds above. To escape, they rush to shallow waters where the big fish can’t follow.”

“But how do we catch them?” Jack asked.

“We’ll bale them out like water,” I replied.

Jack and Fritz stood in the surf with baskets. They scooped the fish from the water, tossing them to us on shore. We cleaned them quickly and packed them in casks—layer of salt, layer of fish—until we filled dozens.

We sealed the casks and rolled them into the cool vaults behind the cave.

Then came another gift. After the herring left, seals appeared—drawn by the scraps we’d thrown into the sea.

Though I doubted they’d taste good, we caught several. Their skins we tanned for harnesses and clothes. Their fat we boiled down into oil—useful for lamps, soap, and tanning leather.

These tasks slowed our work on the cave, but soon we returned with renewed energy.

I noticed the salt crystals rested on a base of gypsum—a material I knew could be used as plaster. I broke off pieces, heated them until they turned to powder, then mixed them with water. The result was smooth, white, and strong.

I used it to seal the herring casks—making sure no air could reach them. The rest I saved for later.

To cure more fish, we built a small hut of reeds and branches. We strung the herrings on lines across the roof. On the floor, we lit a fire of brushwood and moss. Thick smoke curled around the fish, curing them in just a few days.

About a month later, another shoal appeared. Jack spotted them first: “Whales! Young whales!” he shouted.

We ran to the river and saw the bay teeming with sturgeon, salmon, and trout—migrating upstream to spawn.

“Now those are real fish!” Jack grinned. “How do you preserve them?”

“First,” I said, “tell me how you’ll catch them.”

“Oh, easy!” he said, dashing off to Rock House.

He returned with a bow, arrow, and a ball of twine. He’d tied a barbed spike to the arrowhead and fastened the string to the shaft.

He fired. The arrow struck a giant sturgeon in the side.

“Help! Father, help!” he cried as the fish dragged him into the water.

I rushed to pull the line. Together, we fought the creature. It thrashed wildly, but we held on. Slowly, it weakened. Blood stained the water. Finally, exhausted, it lay still.

We hauled it ashore. It was magnificent. Fritz and Ernest arrived just in time—jealous of Jack’s triumph.

They didn’t stay idle. Fritz grabbed a harpoon. Ernest took a fishing rod. I armed myself with an iron trident—Neptune himself could not have looked more imposing.

Soon, the beach was littered with great fish. One by one, we pulled them from the water.

Then Fritz harpooned a sturgeon eight feet long. He couldn’t bring it in alone. We all pulled—but it wouldn’t budge.

“Storm!” my wife called. Jack galloped off, returned with the buffalo, harnessed him to the rope, and—pulling steadily—the beast dragged the monster ashore.

We cleaned the fish. Some we salted. Some we dried. Some we cooked in oil, like Mediterranean tuna.

From the sturgeon roe, I made caviar. I removed the membranes, washed the roe in vinegar, salted it, pressed out the moisture, and packed it tightly in small barrels—storing it in the cellar.

I also collected the bladders. When dried and boiled, they produced isinglass—clear glue that could be used as glass substitute. I strained it through cloth, spread it thinly on stone, and let it dry. The result was clear, strong, and perfect for window panes.

Meanwhile, our garden flourished. Peas, beans, wheat, barley, rye, and corn grew year-round. Cucumbers, melons, and vegetables burst forth without care. The success at Tentholm gave me hope for our maize field.

We visited it one morning. As we passed the potato patch, we saw it covered in barley, wheat, rye, and peas—growing wild.

“Where did this come from?” I asked.

“From the seed I sowed,” my wife smiled. “Franz and I scattered it. You tilled the soil. All we did was plant.”

I was amazed—and delighted. Our maize must be doing well too.

We hurried to the field. The crop stood tall and strong. But as we approached, a flock of birds erupted into the sky. Among them, Fritz spotted ruffed grouse. Without hesitation, he unhooded his eagle, launched it, and leapt onto his onager.

The bird soared, then dove, snatching a grouse mid-air. Fritz followed, rescued the bird, hooded it, and returned triumphant.

Jack hadn’t waited. He released Fangs, his jackal. The animal darted among quails and brought back a dozen—faithfully delivered to his master.

We returned to Falconhurst, where my wife served us a refreshing drink: crushed young corn stems, mixed with sugar-cane juice and water.

That evening, I proposed a new plan: establish a farm farther from Falconhurst, to house our growing herd. We loaded the cart with chickens, pigs, sheep, goats, and a pair of hens and a cock grouse. Fritz led the way on his onager.

We cut a path through the forest and emerged onto a wide plain. White flakes covered the ground and bushes.

“Snow!” Franz cried. “Let’s play snowballs!”

But I knew better. “No, son. That’s cotton.”

Fritz nodded. “It’s a dwarf cotton tree—soft wool in pods.”

We gathered armfuls in three bags. My wife rejoiced.

Crossing the field, we climbed a wooded hill. From the top, the view was stunning: rolling green hills, shaded trees, a sparkling brook, and beyond, the endless sea.

Perfect. This was the place.

We pitched our tent, built a fire, and prepared dinner. Fritz and I chose a spot for a shed. A cluster of trees formed a natural rectangle—ideal for posts. I cut deep mortises in the trunks, ten feet up and again higher. I inserted beams, laid laths for a roof, and covered it with bark secured by acacia thorns. Rain would never harm it.

While clearing debris, I noticed a strange smell. I picked up bits of bark—some from the terebinth tree, others from the American fir. The goats had found cinnamon too—fragrant and delicious.

“The fir gives us turpentine and tar,” I told the boys. “We can make pitch for boats, grease for wheels. The terebinth tree produces gum that hardens into amber-like resin. Burn it—it smells wonderful. Dissolve it in alcohol, and you have varnish.”

We had discovered a new treasure—and with it, a future richer than we’d ever imagined.
