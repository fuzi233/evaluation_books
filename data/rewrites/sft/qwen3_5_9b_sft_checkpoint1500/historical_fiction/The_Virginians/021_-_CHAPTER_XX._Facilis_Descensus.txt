While the virtuous Bishop of Cambrai, in his recent romance, lamented Calypso's desolation at Ulysses' departure, I recall not whether he noted the sorrow of her handmaiden leaving Odysseus's own gentleman. Yet surely the servants must have wept in the kitchens while their masters embraced wildly in the drawing-room; they must have clung to one another in the forecastle as their principals shattered their hearts in the salon. When the final bell tolled and the mate roared, "Now! Any one for shore!" Calypso and her attendant stepped onto the same rotting plank, hearts pounding, eyes streaming. They waved handkerchiefs—of vastly different value and weave—to friends on the quay, while crowds on land and crew in the bows shouted "Hip, hip, hurrah!" in some ancient tongue. The truth remains: if Calypso could not console herself, her maid could not either. They traversed the same plank of grief, feeling the identical sting of separation. Upon returning home, their handkerchiefs might differ in texture, but the tears shed in marble halls and the servants' quarters alike were equally salty and abundant.

Harry Warrington departed Castlewood a captive of love, yet so did Gumbo, fleeing the same premises driven by a delightful passion. Though Gumbo's wit, accomplishments, humor, and skills in dance, cooking, and music endeared him to the entire female domestic circle, jealousy among the men was a distant concern. In those days, no such objections existed against black men as plague modern whites. Their condition was not equal, yet they possessed a sufferance and a grotesque sympathy from all, particularly from women, whose kindness toward them was boundless and generous. When Ledyard and Parke faced persecution in Blackmansland, did they not find black women pitiful and kind? Women, it seems, always cherish our sex. What mental negroes do they not adore? What moral hunchbacks, lepers, idiots, or misshapen monsters do they not fondle and cuddle? Gumbo was treated with the same gentleness afforded to many far superior beings; it was only the men in the servants' hall who rejoiced at the Virginian lad's departure. I long to witness his farewell: I picture Molly, the housemaid, slipping to the terrace gardens at grey dawn to gather a wistful bouquet, and Betty, the kitchen maid, snipping a thick lock of chestnut ringlets to trade for a woolly token from young Gumbo's head. Naturally, he claimed descent from Ashantee royalty (*regum progenies*), boasting of ancestors who once ruled vast realms in Caffraria, Connaught, and elsewhere.

At the appointed hour for Madame de Bernstein's departure, the numerous domestics of Castlewood swarmed the doors and passages. Some sought a final glimpse of her ladyship's men and the captivating Gumbo; others to bid farewell to her maid; all to intercept the Baroness and her nephew for parting fees, a customary largesse distributed that day. Generously, both bestowed gifts upon the liveried staff—the gentlemen in black and ruffles, the swarm of female attendants. Castlewood was the Baroness's youth, and as for her honest Harry, who had lived at free charge, won horses, and secured promises of money from his cousin and the unlucky chaplain, he was naturally generous. He reasoned that his mother, knowing the full extent of his generosity, would expect him to treat the retainers of the Esmond family liberally. Thus, he scattered gold pieces right and left, acting as if he were as wealthy as Gumbo proclaimed. From the major-domo to the shoeblack, Harry offered a peace-offering to all. To the grim housekeeper in her still-room, the feeble old porter in his lodge, he distributed tokens of remembrance. When a man loves one woman in a family, he strangely becomes fond of every soul connected to it. He ingratiates himself with maids, is bland with the butler, interests himself in footmen, runs errands for daughters, advises and lends to the young son at college, pats dogs he would otherwise kick, smiles at old stories he would otherwise yawn through, drinks sweet port he would otherwise curse, endures the cantankerous old maiden aunt, beats time for little Fanny's piano recitals, and smiles when wicked little Bobby spills coffee over his shirt.

For a brief time, Harry Warrington, following the customs of that age, had bestowed these artless testimonies of affection upon the Castlewood family, having recently declared and gained acceptance from one of them. Cousin Will should have reclaimed his money and welcome, or at least some of Harry's own, yet the lad, though a lover, remained shrewd, keen, and fond of sport and fair play—a true judge of a good horse. Having gambled away all of Will's money, alongside numerous autographs from Mr. Esmond, Harry was delighted to secure Will's brown horse—the same quadruped that had nearly thrown him into the water on his first evening at Castlewood. Having gazed upon the stars shining over his mistress's window and extinguished her candle, he retired to his dormitory. There, undoubtedly, he ruminated on his Maria and his horse with youthful satisfaction, dreaming of making the tour of the island on such a splendid animal with a pair of white arms encircling his waist. He fell asleep blessing his Maria a million times, anticipating at least a week more of her company.

In the early morning, poor Chaplain Sampson dispatched his little black mare via his groom, footman, and gardener, who wept and kissed the beast's white nose as they handed her over to Gumbo. Both Gumbo and his master were moved by the fellow's sensibility; the negro servant wept openly, while Harry produced two guineas to astonish and swiftly comfort the chaplain's boy. Then Gumbo and the former groom led the beast to the stable, receiving orders to return with Mr. William's horse after breakfast, at the hour the Baroness's carriages were scheduled.

So courteous was Harry to his aunt, or so grateful for her departure, that the master of the house appeared at the morning meal to bid farewell to his guests. The ladies and the chaplain were present; the only absentee was Will, who left a note stating, in exceedingly poor spelling, that he was obliged to depart for Salisbury Races that morning but had left the horse Harry had won the previous night. Tom, Mr. Will's groom, would deliver it to Mr. Warrington's servant. Will's absence did not hinder the remaining party from drinking tea amicably. Soon, the carriages rolled into the courtyard, servants packed them with the Baroness's multiplied luggage, and the moment of departure arrived.

A large open landau carried the stout Baroness and her niece, flanked by two men-servants on the box with pistols and blunderbusses ready for highwaymen. Another carriage held their ladyships' maids, while a third guarded the trunks, vast and numerous as they were, dwarfed only by the enormous baggage-train accompanying a lady of the present time. Mr. Warrington's modest valises were placed in the second carriage under the maid's guardianship, and Mr. Gumbo proposed to ride by the window for most of the journey.

My Lord, with his stepmother and Lady Fanny, accompanied their kinswoman to the carriage steps, bidding farewell with many dutiful embraces. Her Lady Maria followed in a riding dress, which Harry considered the most becoming costume in the world. A host of servants stood around, begging Heaven to bless her ladyship. The Baroness's departure was known throughout the village, and scores of folk waited under the trees outside the gates, cheering and waving hats as the ponderous vehicles rolled away.

Gumbo had gone for Mr. Warrington's horses while My Lord, with his arm under his young guest's, paced the court. "I hear you carry away some of our horses out of Castlewood?" My Lord asked.

Harry blushed. "A gentleman cannot refuse a fair game at the cards," he replied. "I never wanted to play, nor would have played for money had not my cousin William forced me. As for the chaplain, it went to my heart to win from him, but he was as eager as my cousin."

"I know—I know! There is no blame to you, my boy. At Rome you can't help doing as Rome does. I am very glad you have been able to give Will a lesson. He is mad about play—he would gamble his coat off his back—and I and the family have had to pay his debts many times. May I ask how much you have won of him?"

"Well, some eighteen pieces the first day or two, and his note for a hundred and twenty more, and the brown horse, sixty—that makes nigh upon two hundred. But, you know, cousin, all was fair, and it was even against my will that we played at all. Will ain't a match for me, my lord—that is the fact. Indeed he is not."

"He is a match for most people, though," said My Lord. "His brown horse, I think you said?"

"Yes. His brown horse—Prince William, out of Constitution. You don't suppose I would set him sixty against his bay, my lord?"

"Oh, I didn't know. I saw Will riding out this morning; most likely I did not remark what horse he was on. And you won the black mare from the parson?"
