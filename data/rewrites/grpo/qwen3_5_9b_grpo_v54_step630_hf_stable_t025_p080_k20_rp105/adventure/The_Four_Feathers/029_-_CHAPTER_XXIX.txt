The fever took Colonel Trench with the suddenness of a thief in the night. Three days after their arrival, the two men had fallen asleep with the same words on their lips: "Three more days." But dawn broke on a changed scene. Trench woke gasping, his skin burning with a fever that rapidly consumed him. By afternoon, the heat had stolen his reason, leaving him light-headed and delirious. The thousand nights spent in the House of Stone had finally completed their work, yet the timing was no accident. It was the very moment the door of his prison swung open, the moment his soul soared with the terrifying joy of impending freedom, that his body collapsed under the strain. The prospect of escape, the crowning trial he could not endure, had shattered his exhausted frame.

"I will be well in a few days," Feversham insisted, his voice tight with a desperate denial. "It is nothing."

"It is *Umm Sabbah*," corrected Ibrahim, shaking his head grimly. He spoke of the terrible typhus that had swept through the infected gaol, claiming victims on the seventh day.

Feversham refused to accept the diagnosis, clinging to his denial with a passionate obstinacy. Yet, beneath his words, a new anxiety took root: *Will the men with the camels wait?* Each day he walked to the Nile, he saw Abou Fatma in his blue robe standing at his post, making the silent signal. Each day, Feversham offered no answer. Meanwhile, with Ibrahim's aid, he nursed Trench through the agony of the illness. The boy arrived daily with food, fetching tamarinds, dates, and roots from which Ibrahim brewed cooling draughts. Together they carried the suffering colonel from shade to shade as the sun traversed the zareeba. Assistance was also found for Idris's starving family, leading to the removal of the heavy forty-pound chains that bound Trench. He was fed vegetable marrow soaked in salt water, his mouth packed with butter, and his body anointed and swaddled in camel-cloths.

On the seventh day, Ibrahim declared, "This is the last. Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham replied, his voice trembling with a fierce refusal. "That is impossible. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.' Not here, no." He spoke with the same stubborn intensity, no longer thinking of the man in the blue robe or the fading chances of escape. The fear that the third feather would never reach Ethne, or that she would lose the chance to return the fourth freely, was banished from his mind. Even the great hope of "the afterwards" vanished. He thought only of Trench and the few awkward words exchanged under the sky on their first night together. "No," he repeated, "he must not die here." Through that long, hard battle between life and death, he watched by Trench's side. At moments, it seemed the three years of the House of Stone would claim victory; at others, Trench's wiry constitution held firm.

For that night, they prevailed. The struggle was prolonged, but the dangerous seventh day passed. By the thirteenth day, Trench slept without rambling, and when he awoke, his mind was clear. He found himself alone, swathed in camel-cloths that pinned him to the ground, unable to stir. The heat of the day had broken, and the shadow of the House of Stone lay black upon the sand. He lay wondering idly how long he had been ill, until he heard the shouts of the gaolers and the cries of prisoners near the river. The gate opened, and the prisoners flocked in. Feversham was among them, walking straight to Trench's corner.

"Thank God!" Feversham cried, collapsing in fatigue beside him. "I would not have left you, but I was compelled. We have been unloading boats all day."

"How long have I lain ill?" Trench asked weakly.

"Thirteen days."

"It will be a month before I can travel," Trench said, his voice thin. "You must go, Feversham. You must leave me here while you still can. Perhaps when you reach Assouan you can do something for me. I cannot move now. Will you go tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham answered firmly. "I should not go without you in any case. As it is, it is too late."

"Too late?" Trench repeated, slowly grasping the meaning. He was reluctant to be disturbed by the sound, wishing simply to lie idle in the cool of the sunset. But gradually, the import of the words forced itself into his mind.

"Too late? Then the man in the blue gown has gone?"

"Yes," Feversham confirmed. "He spoke to me yesterday by the river. The camel men would wait no longer. They feared detection and intended to return whether we went with them or not."

"You should have gone with them," Trench said, his tone devoid of despair. For the moment, he did not care if he lived in the prison all his life, so long as he could lie quietly where he was. "So our one chance is lost."

"No, deferred," Feversham replied. "The man in the blue gown brought me paper, a pen, and wood-soot mixed with water. He dropped them by my side as I lay on the ground. I hid them beneath my *jibbeh*. Last night, under the moon, I wrote to a Greek merchant who keeps a café at Wadi Halfa. I gave him the letter this afternoon. He has gone. He will deliver it and receive money. In six months, or at the latest a year, he will be back in Omdurman."

"Very likely," Trench said skeptically. "He will ask for another letter for more money, and again promise to return in six months or a year. I know these people."

"You do not know Abou Fatma," Feversham countered. "He was Gordon's servant before Khartum fell; he has been mine since. He came with me to Obak, waited while I went to Berber, and risked his life to reach Omdurman. Within six months, he will be back. You may be very sure."

Trench did not press the argument. His gaze wandered until it settled on a pile of freshly turned earth in a corner.

"What are they digging?" he asked.

"A well," Feversham answered.

"A well?" Trench fretted. "And so close to the Nile! Why? What is the object?"

"I don't know," Feversham admitted, though a great fear gnawed at his heart. He suspected the reason for the well in the prison enclosure but withheld it until Trench was strong enough to bear the disappointment. Soon, his suspicion was proven true. It was announced that a high wall was to be built around the House of Stone. Too many prisoners had escaped in their fetters along the riverbank; henceforth, they were to be confined within the wall from year's beginning to year's end. The prisoners built it themselves of sun-dried mud-bricks. Feversham took his share of the labor, and Trench, as soon as he could stand, joined him.

"Here's our last hope gone," Trench muttered. Though Feversham did not openly agree, his own heart began to consent.

They piled the bricks, mortising them together. Each day the wall rose a foot. With their own hands, they closed themselves in. When finished, the wall stood twelve feet high—smooth, strong, and seamless, with no projection on which a foot could rest. It could not be broken through in a night. Trench and Feversham contemplated it in despair. The palm trees of Khartum were now hidden from their eyes. Their world was limited to a square of bright blue by day, dark blue by night, jeweled with points of silver and flashing gold. Trench covered his face with his hands.

"I daren't look at it," he said in a broken voice. "We have been building our own coffin, Feversham, that's the truth of it." Then he cast up his arms and cried aloud, "Will they never come up the Nile, the gunboats and the soldiers? Have they forgotten us in England? Good God! have they forgotten us?"

"Hush!" Feversham replied. "We shall find a way of escape, never fear. We must wait six months. Well, we have both waited years. Six months—what are they?"

Though he spoke stoutly for his comrade's sake, Feversham's own heart sank within him.

The details of their life during those six months need not be dwelt upon. In that pestilent enclosure, only the myriad vermin lived lives of comfort. No news filtered in from the outside world. They fed upon their own thoughts, so that the sight of a lizard upon the wall became an occasion for excitement. They were stung by scorpions at night and flogged by their gaolers by day. They lived at the mercy of the whims of Idris-es-Saier and the peculiar spirit Nebbi Khiddr, who always reported against them to the Khalifa just when Idris needed money most. Religious men were sent by the Khalifa to convert them to the only true religion, and the long theological disputations in the enclosure became events they eagerly anticipated. At times, they were freed from heavy shackles to sleep in the open; at others, without reason, those privileges were withdrawn, and they struggled for their lives within the House of Stone.

The six months ended. The seventh began; a fortnight passed, and the boy who brought Feversham food could never cheer their hearts with news that Abou Fatma had returned.

"He will never come," Trench said in despair.

"Surely he will—if he is alive," Feversham replied. "But is he alive?"

The seventh month passed. One morning at the beginning of the eighth, two of the Khalifa's bodyguard arrived at the prison to speak with Idris. Idris advanced to the two prisoners.

"Verily God is good to you, you men from the bad world," he said. "You are to look upon the countenance of the Khalifa. How happy you should be!"

Trench and Feversham rose from the ground in no very happy frame of mind. "What does he want with us? Is this the end?" The questions started clear in both their minds. They followed the guards out through the door and up the street toward the Khalifa's house.

"Does it mean death?" Feversham asked.

Trench shrugged and laughed sourly. "It is on the cards that Nebbi Khiddr has suggested something of the kind," he said.

They were led into the great parade-ground before the mosque and then into the Khalifa's house, where another white man sat in attendance on the threshold. Inside, the Khalifa sat upon an *angareb*, with a grey-bearded Greek standing beside him. The Khalifa remarked that they were both to be employed in the manufacture of gunpowder, with which the armies of the Turks were shortly to be overwhelmed.

Feversham was on the point of disclaiming any knowledge of the process, but before he could speak, he heard Trench declare in fluent Arabic that there was nothing connected with gunpowder he did not know. Upon these words, they were both told they were to be employed at the powder factory under the supervision of the Greek.

For that Greek, both prisoners will entertain a regard to their dying day. There was in the world a true Samaritan. Out of sheer pity, knowing the two men herded in the House of Stone, he suggested their employment to the Khalifa, and the same pity taught him to cover the deficiencies of their knowledge.

"I know nothing whatever about the making of gunpowder except that crystals are used," Trench admitted. "But we shall leave the prison each day, and that is something, though we return each night. Who knows when a chance of escape may come?"

The powder factory lay in the northern part of the town, on the bank of the Nile just beyond the limits of the great mud wall and at the back of the slave market. Every morning, the two prisoners were let out from the prison door. They tramped along the river-bank on the outside of the town wall and came into the powder factory past the storehouses of the Khalifa's bodyguard. Every evening, they returned by the same road to the House of Stone. No guard accompanied them, since flight seemed impossible. On each journey, they looked anxiously for the man in the blue robe. But the months passed, and May brought the summer.

"Something has happened to Abou Fatma," Feversham said. "He has been caught at Berber, perhaps. In some way he has been delayed."

"He will not come," Trench said flatly.

Feversham could no longer pretend to hope. He did not know of the sword-thrust Abou Fatma received as he fled through Berber on his return from Omdurman. Recognized by an old gaoler in that town, he had escaped cheaply with a single thrust in the thigh. From that wound, he had been slowly recovering in the hospital at Assouan for most of the year. Though Feversham heard nothing of Abou Fatma, towards the end of May, he received news that others were working for his escape.

As Trench and Feversham passed in the dusk of one evening between the storehouses and the town wall, a man in the shadow of a narrow alley whispering to them to stop. Trench knelt on the ground, examining his foot as though a stone had cut it. As he knelt, the man walked past and dropped a slip of paper at their feet. He was a Suakin merchant with a booth in the grain market of Omdurman. Trench picked up the paper, hid it in his hand, and limped on, with Feversham at his side. There was no address or name on the outside. Once they had left the houses behind, with only the wall on their right and the Nile on their left, Trench sat down again.
