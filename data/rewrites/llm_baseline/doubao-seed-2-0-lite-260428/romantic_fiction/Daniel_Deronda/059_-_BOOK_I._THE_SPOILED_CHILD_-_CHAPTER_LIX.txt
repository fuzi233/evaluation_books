“I count myself in nothing else so happy
As in a soul remembering my good friends.”

Sir Hugo Mallinger had not rushed to Genoa with the same urgent haste that had carried Mr. Gascoigne to his niece’s side, and Daniel Deronda would never have dreamed of departing before he could meet the baronet face to face. So much hung unspoken between them: the sudden, shocking drowning of Grandcourt, yes, but far more, the cataclysm that had torn through Deronda’s own world these past weeks—the spectral arrival and swift, painful departure of the mother he had never known, whose existence rewrote every line of the story he had always been told about his birth. He could never have laid that raw, tangled truth out in a letter.

It was not until the fifth evening that Deronda lingered on the Genoa station platform, waiting under the hum of arriving trains for Sir Hugo’s carriage, due between eight and nine. Even after all the recent tragedy that hung over him like a damp veil, a faint, soft smile tugged at the corner of his mouth when he pictured the baronet’s familiar, ruddy face—part of every happiest memory of his boyhood. It was a warm, private amusement, thinking of Sir Hugo’s quiet glee at being finally free of Grandcourt’s endless meddling with the Mallinger estates, free to leave his property to his daughters, or as the old whimsical way of thinking about inheritance went, save the best of it for a grandson, a proper heir to carry the name.

What a sour, small world we would live in, Deronda thought, if we could not take joy in our dearest ones’ small, imperfect pleasures, just because they do not line up exactly with our lofty ideals of how life ought to be. We would go through life with permanently pursed lips, frozen, unkind eyes, never able to make space for the soft mess of human imperfection. Even if Sir Hugo had done wrong, hiding the truth of his birth all these years, Deronda’s heart ached to excuse him, not condemn him. He had never known the sharp, private pain Deronda had carried as a boy, wondering who he was, why he never fit, never guessed at the quiet struggles his ward had endured. To rage at that gentle, well-meaning ignorance as if it was deliberate cruelty would be a far worse unkindness. Ten days of bearing witness to other people’s hidden, tangled griefs had softened Deronda even more, made him loath to rush to sharp indignation where affection had lived for decades. So when he saw Sir Hugo’s broad, familiar figure step down from the railway carriage, all the new, sharp knowledge of the secrecy that had wrapped his whole life melted away under the flood of lifelong, deep affection.

“Well, Dan,” Sir Hugo said, his voice thick with unspoken feeling, squeezing Deronda’s hand hard. There was no light, trivial greeting, no quip—for a long moment, the two men just stood there, the weight of all that had happened hanging between them, no words needed. The baronet gave his courier quick orders with a wave, then suggested they walk slowly through the mild, perfumed evening air, no rush at all to get to their hotel.

“Travel went easy, I’m in top form,” he said, as they stepped out under the faint starlight, the last pale gold glow of the lingering sunset still gilding the edges of the dark sky. “I took my time setting off, wanted to dig into a few details first, and I read your letter to Lady Mallinger before I left. Now—how is the widow holding up?”

“Calmer, day by day,” Deronda told him. “She escaped the dangerous fever we all feared after she was plunged into the sea, and all that terrible, shattering excitement. Her mother and uncle arrived two days ago, so she’s well cared for now.”

“Any chance she’s carrying an heir?” Sir Hugo asked, glancing sidelong at him.

“From what Mr. Gascoigne said, I don’t think so,” Deronda replied. “He spoke as if the only real prospect of her holding the estates for her lifetime is if there’s no Grandcourt heir born to her.”

“Hard to imagine her grieving that brute of a husband much, isn’t it?” Sir Hugo said.

“His death was so sudden it knocked her completely off her feet,” Deronda said gently, evading the question, not wanting to speak of the ugly unhappiness of Gwendolen’s marriage to anyone else.

“Wonder if he ever told her what was in his will?” Sir Hugo mused.

“Do you know the contents, sir?” Deronda parried.

“I do, worse luck,” the baronet huffed. “If there’s no legitimate heir, he’s left every last inch of the property to that boy he had with Lydia Glasher. You never heard of that affair, I suppose? She was as good as his wife for years, they have three little girls before the boy came. The lad’s supposed to take his name: Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. That Mallinger name won’t do him a bit of good, but the little rascal will have more than enough to live on while he’s still a minor. The worst of it is, he gets that fifty thousand I sank into Diplow that Grandcourt never had any right to, in the first place. Meanwhile, our beautiful young widow gets a paltry two thousand a year, and Gadsmere House—of all places. That’s a soft little banishment for her, if she chooses to shut herself up there, which I don’t for a second imagine she’ll do. Mrs. Glasher has been living there for years, for God’s sake. I’m so disgusted with Grandcourt I could spit. Just because he drowned doesn’t mean I’m going to pretend he was any better than he was, though I will admit, nothing he did his whole life served him half as well as dying.”

“I don’t fault him for leaving his land to his son,” Deronda said, a little dry. “I fault him for marrying Gwendolen in the first place, knowing what he did to her.”

“Right you are, about the rest,” Sir Hugo agreed. “He should have set her up properly, given her four or five thousand a year for life, and the London house, after he married her. That’s what any decent man would have done, put her in a position fitting the rank he dragged her up to. She was penniless, so her family couldn’t push for a proper settlement, and that fool let him get away with murder. Men always hide their worst petty cruelties in their wills, don’t they? Grandcourt clearly meant to snuff her whole light out the second he died, if she didn’t pop out an heir for him.”

“And if she had given him a son, he would have snuffed out all the Glasher children’s claim, instead?” Deronda said, a sharp edge of scorn in his voice.

“Exactly. Then it would be Gwendolen getting Gadsmere and two thousand a year, not the boy. Funny, that. One headache out of this: Grandcourt named me an executor. But he was my own brother’s son, so I can’t refuse the job. And it won’t be so bad if it means I can look out for the widow. Lush swears she knew all about his other family, what was in that will. Says the two of them never got along, at all. But you, eh Dan? You’re the one who spent all that time with her, who knows what she really feels?”

Sir Hugo wasn’t teasing, for once. His voice was low, curious, earnest. Deronda knew evasion would only lead to misunderstanding. “She was never happy. The two of them were completely wrong for each other,” he said gravely. “But as for the property? I’ve spent enough time with her to know she’ll be perfectly content with what he left her.”

“Then she’s one of a kind, unlike the rest of her sex,” Sir Hugo shrugged. “Though I suppose she must be extraordinary. Lady Mallinger said the exact same thing when that telegram came: ‘Isn’t it strange that Daniel is the one sending word of Grandcourt’s death?’ I’ve had that kind of weird coincidence happen to me before, once. I was staying at a hotel abroad, heard a lady got stranded there by her husband, no money at all. Turned out to be an old flame of mine, fool enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and no loyalty to speak of. I wasn’t playing knight errant then, just happened to be there for my own reasons, same as you weren’t chasing the Grandcourts when you came to Genoa.”

The two of them walked on in silence for a minute, the distant glint of the Genoa port fading in the dark. Sir Hugo had started with the Grandcourt business because it was easier, less loaded, than the secret hanging between them, the secret that had brought Deronda all the way to Italy. But both of them wanted to finally lay all the cards on the table, no more hiding. The baronet paused, turning to get one last look at the port glinting far off, his voice softening.

“And about your own business here in Genoa, Dan? I hope nothing you learned cut too deep. Nothing that’s going to change your place in the world, right? You know whatever happens to you, it matters more to me than almost anything.”

“I want to be completely honest with you, sir, repay all your kindness with full truth,” Deronda said. “I can’t say yes or no to that, though. Much of what I learned about the past hurt deeply, and it pained me to meet my mother, ill and suffering, and have to say goodbye. But knowing who my parentage is? That’s not pain. That’s clearing away all the old doubts I carried my whole life, and I’m grateful for it. Nothing will ever change how grateful I am to you, for the fatherly care you gave me all these years. But finding out I was born a Jew? That’s going to change the whole course of my life, in ways I can’t even fully explain to you yet.”

Deronda held his breath, he had never said the words out loud to the man who raised him, and he knew their two very different ways of seeing the world were about to reveal themselves more clearly than ever before. Sir Hugo shot him a quick, startled glance, and started walking on again, thinking hard for a few long moments.

“I always knew you were going to do something remarkable, Dan,” he said at last. “But for God’s sake, don’t go chasing after any silly eccentricities, all right? I don’t care what a man believes, but he doesn’t have to act like a lunatic to make people pay attention. This is the modern world, if you want people to take you seriously, you steer clear of melodrama. I’m not saying you’re going to go mad, far from it. But you’ve always had this soft spot for people everyone else is throwing stones at, Dan. I feel sorry for them too, but making a habit of their company is never a good idea. But don’t mind me, no rush to tell me anything until you’re ready. And if whatever path you choose needs money? I’ve got sixteen thousand pounds of your own that’s been piling up, over and above the income you’ve been drawing all these years. Now that I’m here, I suppose you’re eager to get back to England as soon as possible?”

“I have to go to Mainz first,” Deronda told him. “To collect an old chest that belonged to my grandfather, and maybe find a friend of his. It’s been sitting there for twenty years, but I have this silly, overanxious urge to get it under my own care, like something bad might happen to it if I wait. And I don’t regret lingering here after my mother left, not at all. If I hadn’t, Mrs. Grandcourt would have had nothing but servants to rely on, in the worst moment of her life.”

“Right, right,” Sir Hugo said, a little flippant, to cover the quiet vexation he felt at the news of Deronda’s new path. “I just hope you aren’t going to put the wishes of a dead Jewish man above the needs of a living Christian woman who’s depending on you.”

Deronda’s face flamed hot, and he bit back the sharp retort that rose immediately to his throat. Just then, they turned and stepped under the warm, glowing gas lamps of the Hotel Italia.
