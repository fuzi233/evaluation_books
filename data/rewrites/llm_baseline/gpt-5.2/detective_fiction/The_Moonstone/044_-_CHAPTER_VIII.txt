Late that evening, when the city had quieted to the thinner music of wheels and distant calls—when even my lodging-house seemed to listen, as a kitchen listens between courses—I was surprised by Mr. Bruff.

There was a change in him that struck me like a familiar dish made wrongly. His usual brisk assurance was gone; the man looked as if some sourness had got into his blood. He took my hand—he had never done that in his life before—and for a moment said nothing.

“Are you going back to Hampstead?” I asked, merely to break the silence.

“I have just left Hampstead,” he answered. “I know, Mr. Franklin, that you have got at the truth at last. But I tell you plainly, if I could have foreseen the price that was to be paid for it, I should have preferred leaving you in the dark.”

“You have seen Rachel?”

“I have come here after taking her back to Portland Place; it was impossible to let her return in the carriage by herself. I can hardly hold you responsible—considering that you saw her in my house and by my permission—for the shock that this unlucky interview has inflicted on her. All I can do is to provide against a repetition of the mischief. She is young—she has a resolute spirit—she will get over this, with time and rest to help her. I want to be assured that you will do nothing to hinder her recovery. May I depend on your making no second attempt to see her—except with my sanction and approval?”

The words were calm; but there was that underlying sharpness in them which is the rasp of a knife on a board when you would rather hear the gentle hiss of fat meeting heat.

“After what she has suffered, and after what I have suffered,” I said, “you may rely on me.”

“I have your promise?”

“You have my promise.”

He visibly eased, as a man does when a pot at last comes to the boil and proves it will not boil over. He set down his hat and drew his chair nearer.

“That’s settled!” he said. “Now, about the future—your future, I mean. To my mind, the result of the extraordinary turn which the matter has now taken is briefly this. In the first place, we are sure that Rachel has told you the whole truth, as plainly as words can tell it. In the second place—though we know that there must be some dreadful mistake somewhere—we can hardly blame her for believing you to be guilty, on the evidence of her own senses; backed, as that evidence has been, by circumstances which appear, on the face of them, to tell dead against you.”

“I don’t blame Rachel,” I put in. “I only regret that she could not prevail on herself to speak more plainly to me at the time.”

“You might as well regret that Rachel is not somebody else,” he returned. “And even then, I doubt if a girl of any delicacy, whose heart had been set on marrying you, could have brought herself to charge you to your face with being a thief. Anyhow, it was not in Rachel’s nature to do it. In a very different matter to this matter of yours—which placed her, however, in a position not altogether unlike her position towards you—I happen to know that she was influenced by a similar motive to the motive which actuated her conduct in your case. Besides, as she told me herself, on our way to town this evening, if she had spoken plainly, she would no more have believed your denial then than she believes it now. What answer can you make to that? There is no answer to be made to it. Come, come, Mr. Franklin! my view of the case has been proved to be all wrong, I admit—but, as things are now, my advice may be worth having for all that. I tell you plainly, we shall be wasting our time, and cudgelling our brains to no purpose, if we attempt to try back, and unravel this frightful complication from the beginning. Let us close our minds resolutely to all that happened last year at Lady Verinder’s country house; and let us look to what we can discover in the future, instead of to what we can not discover in the past.”

“Surely you forget,” I said, “that the whole thing is essentially a matter of the past—so far as I am concerned?”

He leaned forward, and his voice took that practical ring which lawyers carry like a set of keys.

“Answer me this. Is the Moonstone at the bottom of all the mischief—or is it not?”

“It is—of course.”

“Very good. What do we believe was done with the Moonstone, when it was taken to London?”

“It was pledged to Mr. Luker.”

“We know that you are not the person who pledged it. Do we know who did?”

“No.”

“Where do we believe the Moonstone to be now?”

“Deposited in the keeping of Mr. Luker’s bankers.”

“Exactly. Now observe. We are already in the month of June. Towards the end of the month (I can’t be particular to a day) a year will have elapsed from the time when we believe the jewel to have been pledged. There is a chance—to say the least—that the person who pawned it, may be prepared to redeem it when the year’s time has expired. If he redeems it, Mr. Luker must himself—according to the terms of his own arrangement—take the Diamond out of his banker’s hands. Under these circumstances, I propose setting a watch at the bank, as the present month draws to an end, and discovering who the person is to whom Mr. Luker restores the Moonstone. Do you see it now?”

I did see it; and yet I disliked it. It was like being told to watch a loaf rise, knowing hunger, knowing that you cannot touch it until the yeast has finished its quiet work.

I admitted—unwillingly—that the idea was new.

“It’s Mr. Murthwaite’s idea quite as much as mine,” said Bruff. “It might have never entered my head, but for a conversation we had together some time since. If Mr. Murthwaite is right, the Indians are likely to be on the lookout at the bank, towards the end of the month too—and something serious may come of it. What comes of it doesn’t matter to you and me except as it may help us to lay our hands on the mysterious Somebody who pawned the Diamond. That person, you may rely on it, is responsible (I don’t pretend to know how) for the position in which you stand at this moment; and that person alone can set you right in Rachel’s estimation.”

“I can’t deny,” I said, “that the plan you propose meets the difficulty in a way that is very daring, and very ingenious, and very new. But—”

“But you have an objection to make?”

“Yes. My objection is, that your proposal obliges us to wait.”

“Granted. As I reckon the time, it requires you to wait about a fortnight—more or less. Is that so very long?”

“It’s a life-time, Mr. Bruff, in such a situation as mine. My existence will be simply unendurable to me, unless I do something towards clearing my character at once.”

“Well, well, I understand that. Have you thought yet of what you can do?”

“I have thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff.”

“He has retired from the police. It’s useless to expect the Sergeant to help you.”

“I know where to find him; and I can but try.”

Bruff considered, as if weighing a dose.

“Try,” he said. “The case has assumed such an extraordinary aspect since Sergeant Cuff’s time, that you may revive his interest in the inquiry. Try, and let me hear the result. In the meanwhile,” he added, rising, “if you make no discoveries between this, and the end of the month, am I free to try, on my side, what can be done by keeping a lookout at the bank?”

“Certainly,” I answered—“unless I relieve you of all necessity for trying the experiment in the interval.”

He smiled, took up his hat, and paused with his hand on it, as if locking away the last of his counsel.

“Tell Sergeant Cuff,” he said, “that I say the discovery of the truth depends on the discovery of the person who pawned the Diamond. And let me hear what the Sergeant’s experience says to that.”

So we parted.

Early the next morning I set out for Dorking—the small town where Betteredge had told me Cuff had chosen to end his days. The journey had its own thin concert: rails clicking with mechanical regularity, stations clanging with porters’ cries, London’s harsher roar falling behind like a tide that withdraws but never quite leaves the ears. I caught myself longing for the remembered quiet of Lady Verinder’s house—quiet, now, made bitter by what it held.

At the hotel I asked my questions and got my directions. The cottage lay by a by-road a little out of the town, snug in its own plot of garden ground, protected by a good brick wall behind and at the sides, and by a high quickset hedge in front. The gate, with smartly painted trellis-work at the top, was locked. I rang, and peered through the lattice.

There were roses everywhere: the great man’s chosen preoccupation blooming like a private language. They climbed and clustered; they looked in at the windows; they overran the very doorway, as if they meant to bar out the old work of listening to lies and footsteps. The famous thief-taker, withdrawn from the city’s din, seemed to have given his remaining senses to scent and colour, letting the moral stink of London wash away in petal-soft air.

An elderly woman opened the gate, and with the first words out of her mouth broke the hope I had brought so carefully with me.

He had started—only the day before—on a journey to Ireland.

“Has he gone there on business?” I asked.

She smiled, as if at a child who thinks a man can carry two lives at once.

“He has only one business now, sir,” she said; “and that’s roses. Some great man’s gardener in Ireland has found out something new in the growing of roses—and Mr. Cuff’s away to inquire into it.”

“Do you know when he will be back?”

“It’s quite uncertain, sir. Mr. Cuff said he should come back directly, or be away some time, just according as he found the new discovery worth nothing, or worth looking into. If you have any message to leave for him, I’ll take care, sir, that he gets it.”

I gave her my card, and wrote on it in pencil: “I have something to say about the Moonstone. Let me hear from you as soon as you get back.” Then there was nothing left but to submit to circumstances and return to London.

In the irritable state of my mind, that failure acted like grit between the teeth. The noise of the streets—carts, calls, iron on stone—seemed louder for having been denied the garden’s hush. That day, on my return, I resolved that the next morning should find me on a new attempt—some attempt—at forcing my way through all obstacles from darkness to light.

What form was the next experiment to take?

If Betteredge had been with me, and if I had been foolish enough to let him overhear the disorderly kitchen of my thoughts, he would have said the German side of me was uppermost. In truth, I sat for most of the night smoking and building theories as a cook spoils a broth by adding one flavour after another until nothing can be tasted clearly. Each conjecture was more elaborate and more improbable than the last. When I slept, my fancies pursued me into dreams. I rose with Objective-Subjective and Subjective-Objective so knotted together in my head that I began the day by doubting—on purely philosophical grounds—whether anything, the Diamond included, existed at all.

How long I might have wandered in that fog, I cannot tell. Accident saved me, and I bless it for the plain, domestic way it did so. I wore that morning the same coat I had worn on the day of my interview with Rachel. Hunting in one pocket for something else, my fingers met a crumpled paper. I drew it out—and there was Betteredge’s forgotten letter.

It seemed hard on the good old man to leave him unanswered. I went to the table and read again.

A letter that has nothing of the least importance in it is not always easy to answer. Betteredge’s came into that troublesome class. Mr. Candy’s assistant—Ezra Jennings—had told his master he had seen me; and Mr. Candy, in his turn, wished to see me and say something when next I was near Frizinghall. What could I reply that would be worth the ink? I sat idly, and on the very sheet I had promised to give Betteredge, began drawing likenesses from memory of Mr. Candy’s assistant—that odd man with the piebald hair, whose look and manner had lodged in me like a taste you cannot name but cannot forget. Then, as if the paper itself had provoked me, it suddenly struck me: here was Ezra Jennings again, intruding between me and my object.

I tore up a dozen portraits at least—every one with that same remarkable hair—and flung them into the waste-basket; and then wrote Betteredge a perfectly commonplace answer. It had one excellent effect. A few plain sentences in plain English cleared my mind as a draught of cool water clears the palate.

Now, returning to the puzzle with a steadier appetite for facts, I tried to deal with it practically. The events of the memorable night were still unintelligible; so I went back farther, searching the earlier hours of the birthday for any incident that might prove a clue.

Had anything happened while Rachel and I were finishing the painted door? later, when I rode over to Frizinghall? afterwards, when I went back with Godfrey Ablewhite and his sisters? later again, when I put the Moonstone into Rachel’s hands? later still, when the company came and we all assembled at dinner?

My memory answered readily enough until it reached the dinner-table—then it stopped dead, like a knife that meets bone. I could not even be sure of the number of guests who had sat with me.

That blank, and the suspicion it awakened, came together. When we ourselves become the subject of inquiry, we grow distrustful of what we cannot recall. I resolved to get the names of those who had been present, to set down what each could remember of the evening, and to compare that harvest of recollection with what had happened after the company had left. Unlikely as it seemed, I had groped—without knowing it—toward the root of the matter. I wanted only a hint to start me right; and before another day had passed, one of the guests would give it.

First, I needed the complete list. Betteredge could supply it. I determined to go back to Yorkshire that day and begin the investigation the next morning.

But I had missed the train that left London before noon. There was nothing for it but to wait nearly three hours for the next departure. Could I use the interval?

My thoughts returned—obstinate as hunger—to the birthday dinner. Though I could not marshal the guests in my mind, I remembered that most were from Frizinghall or its neighbourhood. But not all. I myself had been one of the few from outside; Mr. Murthwaite another; Godfrey Ablewhite a third. Mr. Bruff—no, business had kept him away. Had any ladies been there whose usual home was London? Only Miss Clack came back to me. Here were three people, at least, whom I ought to see before leaving town. I drove at once to Mr. Bruff’s office, not knowing their addresses and hoping he could direct me.

Bruff was too busy to spare me more than a minute; but in that minute he contrived to discourage every question I put. My newly conceived method of inquiry he dismissed as fanciful. Mr. Murthwaite, he said, was already on his way back to the scene of his past adventures. Miss Clack had suffered losses and, for economy, had settled in France. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite might or might not be discoverable in London. Suppose I tried his club? And suppose I excused Mr. Bruff while he returned to his business and wished me good morning?

Thus narrowed, my London work became one thing: find Godfrey. I took the hint and drove to his club.

In the hall I met a member—an old friend of my cousin’s, and an acquaintance of my own—who gave me Godfrey’s address and, besides that, two items of recent news.

Godfrey, it seemed, had not been discouraged by Rachel’s withdrawal. Soon afterward he had offered himself to another young lady, reputed a great heiress. The match had been considered settled, when it too had suddenly broken off—owing, people said, to a serious difference between the bridegroom and the lady’s father on the question of settlements.

As compensation for this second disaster, Godfrey had been remembered in money by one of his admirers: a rich old lady, highly respected at the Mothers’ Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society, and a great friend of Miss Clack’s (to whom she left nothing but a mourning ring), had bequeathed Godfrey a legacy of five thousand pounds. Having secured this addition to his resources, he had been heard to remark that he required respite from charitable labours, and that his doctor prescribed “a run on the Continent” as likely to benefit his health. If I wished to see him, I had better lose no time.

I went at once.

The same unlucky exactness that had made me a day too late at Sergeant Cuff’s cottage made me again a day too late. Godfrey had left London the previous morning by the tidal train for Dover; he was to cross to Ostend; his servant believed he was going on to Brussels. His return was uncertain; but he would be away at least three months.

I went back to my lodgings depressed. Three guests from the birthday dinner—three exceptionally intelligent people—were out of reach at the precise moment when I needed them. My last hopes rested on Betteredge and on such of Lady Verinder’s friends as might still be found near Rachel’s country house.

This time I travelled straight to Frizinghall, making it the centre of my inquiry. I arrived too late in the evening to see Betteredge. Next morning I sent a messenger in a fly with a letter, asking him to join me at the hotel as soon as he could.

During the interval—less than two hours, if no delay occurred—I resolved to begin with those I could reach. My relatives, the Ablewhites, and Mr. Candy. The doctor had expressed a special wish to see me, and lived in the next street. I went to him first.

After what Betteredge had told me, I expected to see illness in the doctor’s face. I was not prepared for the ruin that met me when he entered and shook my hand. His eyes were dim; his hair had turned quite grey; his face was wizen; his figure had shrunk. In memory he had been a lively, rattling little man, full of jokes and social indiscretions; now nothing seemed left of him but the old vulgar smartness in his dress. The man was a wreck; and in cruel contrast his clothes and jewellery were as gay and gaudy as ever, like bright sugar on spoiled fruit.

“I have often thought of you, Mr. Blake,” he said, “and I am heartily glad to see you again at last. If there is anything I can do for you, pray command my services, sir—pray command my services!”

He hurried the commonplace words with needless eagerness, and stared at me with a curiosity about my business in Yorkshire that he was childishly unable to hide.

I had foreseen that I must offer some personal explanation, if I meant to enlist the memories of Lady Verinder’s friends. I had arranged my phrases on the road, and now tried them on Mr. Candy.

“I was in Yorkshire the other day, and I am in Yorkshire again now, on rather a romantic errand,” I said. “It is a matter, Mr. Candy, in which the late Lady Verinder’s friends all took some interest. You remember the mysterious loss of the Indian Diamond, now nearly a year since? Circumstances have lately happened which lead to the hope that it may yet be found—and I am interesting myself, as one of the family, in recovering it. Among the obstacles in my way, there is the necessity of collecting again all the evidence which was discovered at the time, and more if possible. There are peculiarities in this case which make it desirable to revive my recollection of everything that happened in the house, on the evening of Miss Verinder’s birthday. And I venture to appeal to her late mother’s friends who were present on that occasion, to lend me the assistance of their memories—”

I got no farther. As plainly as if the change had been audible, I saw in his face that my attempt had failed. He sat restlessly picking at the points of his fingers while I spoke, his dim watery eyes fixed on me with a vacant, wistful inquiry that was painful to witness. Whatever his mind was doing, it was not following my words. The only way to recall him seemed to be to change the subject sharply.

“So much,” I said, forcing a lighter tone, “for what brings me to Frizinghall! Now, Mr. Candy, it’s your turn. You sent me a message by Gabriel Betteredge—”

He stopped picking at his fingers and suddenly brightened up.
