In a chamber overlooking St. James's Park, situated high above the bustle below, stood an ancient oak desk of substantial proportions. The room, spacious and tastefully appointed by a hand of discernment, bore the quiet, heavy mark of a bachelor. John Marlowe unlocked the sturdy cabinet and withdrew a long, stout envelope from its depths.

"I comprehend," he addressed Mr. Cupples, "that you have scrutinized this document."

"I first encountered it two days past," Mr. Cupples replied, seated upon a sofa and surveying the room with a benevolent gaze. "We have debated its contents at length."

Turning to Trent, Marlowe placed the envelope upon the table. "There lies your manuscript," he declared. "I have examined it thrice. Few men, I daresay, could have extracted as much verity from the truth as you have inscribed therein."

Trent, indifferent to the compliment, sat by the table regarding the fire with stony resolve, his long legs twisted beneath his chair. "You imply, of course," he murmured, drawing the envelope closer, "that further truths await disclosure. We are prepared to hear you whenever you deem fit. I anticipate a lengthy narrative; the protracted, the better it serves me. I seek total understanding. What both parties desire, I surmise, is a preliminary exposition of Manderson and your relationship with him. From the outset, the nature of the deceased appeared to me to be a pivotal element in this business."

"You were correct," Marlowe answered grimly. He traversed the room and settled upon a corner of the tall, cushion-topped fender. "I shall commence as you propose."

"Pray inform me beforehand," Trent intoned, fixing his gaze upon Marlowe, "that whilst I sit here to listen, I harbor no cause to doubt the conclusions I have articulated herein." He tapped the envelope. "It constitutes a defense you intend to mount—you grasp this?"

"Perfectly." Marlowe remained cool, composed, and entirely master of his faculties, a stark contrast to the weary, nervous specter Trent had recalled at Marlstone eighteen months prior. His tall, lithe frame was held with the perfection of muscle; his brow open and honest, his blue eyes clear, though they retained, as he paused to gather his thoughts, the unsettling quality that had plagued Trent at their initial encounter. Only the lines around his mouth betrayed his awareness of a precarious position, one he intended to confront.

"Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal intellect," Marlowe began, his voice quiet. "Many of the ultra-rich I encountered in America attained their status through abnormal greed, abnormal industry, abnormal personal force, or abnormal fortune. None possessed remarkable minds. Manderson, however, relished the accumulation of wealth; he labored incessantly; he was a man of dominant will; and he enjoyed his share of luck. Yet what rendered him unique was his cerebral capacity. In his homeland, they might attribute his singular traits to ruthless ambition, but hundreds would have executed his designs with scant regard for others had they conceived them.

"I maintain that Americans are not devoid of wit—they are tenfold astuter than we, as a nation—but I never met another individual displaying such a degree of sagacity and foresight, such gifts of memory and mental tenacity, such raw force of intelligence, as propelled every maneuver Manderson undertook in his financial career. They dubbed him the 'Napoleon of Wall Street' frequently in the press, yet few comprehended the truth of the epithet as deeply as I did. He seemingly never forgot a fact potentially useful to him. Systematically, he applied to business facts concerning coal, wheat, or railways what Napoleon, according to historical accounts, applied to military facts. He studied them in specialized digests, prepared at regular intervals, always within reach, enabling him to formulate bolder, cleverer strategies than any rival. The Street grew rattled when it was known Manderson was out with his gun; his opponents often surrendered as effortlessly as Colonel Crockett's raccoon in the fable. The scheme I am about to recount would have occupied most men indefinitely. Manderson could have plotted it, down to the minutest detail, while shaving."

"I had long speculated," Marlowe continued, "that his distant strain of Indian blood might contribute to his cunning and ruthlessness. Curiously, this knowledge was exclusive to himself and me. It was when he commissioned me to apply my penchant for genealogical inquiry to his obscure lineage that I discovered he harbored a fraction of the blood of the Iroquois chief Montour and his formidable French wife, a terrifying woman who governed the savage politics of the tribes in the Wilderness two centuries prior. The Mandersons were active in the fur trade on the Pennsylvanian frontier; numerous members intermarried with indigenous women. While other Indian bloodlines might have descended to Manderson through prior and subsequent unions, given the untraceable antecedents of many wives and the generations of pioneering preceding the nation's civilizing, my research led me to conclude that aboriginal blood permeates the genealogical fabric of the American people, widely dispersed. The newer families constantly intermarried with the older ones, many proud of the native strain. Yet Manderson viewed mixed blood as disgraceful—a sentiment, I suspect, intensified by the Negro Question following the War. He was thunderstruck by my revelations and eager to conceal them from every soul. Naturally, I never divulged them while he lived, nor did I believe he expected me to. However, I now entertain the notion that his mindset turned against me from that moment forward. It transpired approximately a year prior to his demise."

"Had Manderson," Mr. Cupples interjected, the question so abrupt it startled the others, "any defined religious stance?"

Marlowe pondered briefly. "None that I ever heard," he responded. "Worship and prayer were alien to him, as far as I could discern, and I never heard him invoke religion. I doubt if he possessed any real sense of God, or capability to know Him through emotion. Yet I understood that as a child he received a religious upbringing with a robust moral component. His private life, in the conventional limited sense, was blameless. He was almost ascetic in his habits, save for smoking. I resided with him for four years without ever witnessing him utter a direct verbal falsehood, despite his constant practice of deceit in other guises. Can you conceive the soul of a man who never hesitated to undertake actions calculated to hoodwink others, who employed every market trick to mislead, yet remained scrupulous in never uttering a direct lie on the slightest trivial matter? Manderson was thus constituted, and he was not alone. You might liken the state of mind to that of a soldier who is personally truthful yet sticks at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of the game permit it; the same holds for business, as many businessmen regard it. Only with them is it perpetually wartime."

"It is a lamentable world," observed Mr. Cupples.

"As you posit," Marlowe assented. "Now, I was stating that one could invariably accept Manderson's word when delivered in definitive form. The first instance I ever heard him utter a downright lie was on the night of his death; hearing it, I believe, saved me from execution as his murderer."

Marlowe stared upward at the light fixture above his head, and Trent shifted impatiently in his chair. "Prior to addressing that," Trent interjected, "will you delineate precisely the footing upon which you stood with Manderson throughout the years of your association?"

"We maintained excellent relations from inception to conclusion," Marlowe answered. "Nothing akin to friendship—he was not a man for cultivating friends—but the finest rapport between a trusted employee and his chief. I approached him as private secretary shortly after obtaining my degree at Oxford. I had intended to join my father's enterprise, wherein I currently reside, but my father advised me to traverse the globe for a year or two. Thus, I accepted this secretaryship, promising varied experience, allowing the initial period to elongate into four years before the end arrived. The offer materialized through the last conceivable qualification for a salaried post: chess."

At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a muttering exclamation. The others gazed at him in surprise.

"Chess!" Trent repeated, rising and approaching Marlowe. "Do you know," he said, "what was the first observation I made regarding you at our inaugural meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. I could not place it then, but I now recognize whence I had beheld them before. They belonged to no lesser man than the great Nikolay Korchagin, with whom I once shared a railway carriage for two days. I thought I should never forget the chess-eye after that encounter, yet I could not assign a name to it when I saw it in you. I beg your pardon," he concluded abruptly, resuming his marble-like posture in his chair.

"I have played the game since childhood, alongside competent adversaries," Marlowe stated simply. "It is an hereditary gift, if you will concede it such. At the University, I was nearly as proficient as anyone there, devoting most of my faculties to chess, the O.U.D.S., and general amusement. At Oxford, as you likely know, inducements to amuse oneself at the expense of education are endless and encouraged by authorities.
