Life and liberty are things we scarcely notice when they run smooth, as natural as breath itself. Only when the shadow of peril wraps around them do men grasp their true weight, and treasure every beat of unshackled hearts. This truth holds as true for them as it does for the sun—whose noon blaze is so familiar we barely pause to mark it, until dusk dips its light in topaz and spills it across the boughs of shadowed elms. The light is fainter then, yes, but the gloom gathered at its edge throws every spark into sharp, glowing relief.

So it was for Gerard and Margaret, creeping soft as shadows through the tangled woodland, every rustle of dry leaves making them flinch, every distant snap of twigs sending a jolt up their spines. The memory of baying hounds and the brutal shouts of their pursuers still hummed in their ears, but every step they took deeper into the quiet, sheltering trees wrapped them in a warm, throbbing joy they had never known in all their days of ease.

Presently Gerard spotted dark streaks of blood on Margaret’s bare ankles, and his blood ran cold. “Martin! Martin, quick—she’s hurt! The crossbow bolt struck her!” he cried, fumbling to steady her as she swayed. Margaret laughed soft to calm him, her cheeks flushed pink. “No, no, my love, not a scratch on me, I swear it.” Gerard’s hands trembled as he knelt to examine the stain. “Then what in God’s name is this red mark on your skin?”

Margaret blushed bright, twisting her hands together. “Don’t scold me, then,” she murmured. Gerard stared, bewildered. “When have I ever scolded you?” “Never, dearest Gerard. Well then—Martin said the hounds were tracking us by the scent of blood. I thought, if I smeared a little on my shoes, the hounds would follow my trail instead, and let you slip away free. So I took Martin’s knife and scratched my arm. Forgive me! Whose else could I use? Not yours, Gerard, never yours. You’ll forgive me, won’t you?” she pleaded, leaning into him, her voice soft and warm as a summer wind.

Gerard’s throat tightened so he could barely speak. “Let me see that scratch first,” he managed. He pulled back her sleeve, and his breath caught. “A scratch? This is a deep, cruel gash—you might have drained yourself for me!” He shuddered, his eyes swimming. The rough soldier Martin huffed a laugh from a few paces off: “Milksop, over a little nick and a drop of blood? Sicken at the sight of that, would you?” “Not a sea of blood,” Gerard gasped, “only hers. Oh Margaret, how could you be so reckless with your own life for me?”

Margaret smiled, her face soft with a love too big for words. “Silly Gerard,” she whispered, wrapping her cut arm tight around his neck. “As if I would not pour every drop of blood in my heart out for you, never mind a few from my arm.” The shock of how close they had come to being torn apart hit her then, and she wept into his shoulder, hot tears soaking his tunic, and he wept right there with her, clinging to her like he would never let go.

“And I must part from her,” he sobbed. “We two who love so dear—one to be in Holland, one in Italy. Ah me! Ah me!” Margaret cried fresh then, but silent, patient; her instinct for unselfishness kept her from voicing the grief that would only weigh heavier on Gerard when he had to leave.

Suddenly they stepped out onto a beaten bridle path, and Martin halted, hanging his head. “This is the road I told you of,” he said, nodding ahead. “The little hostelry is just over that rise.” Margaret and Gerard exchanged a terrified, heartbroken glance. Gerard drew Martin aside, his voice cracking. “Good Martin, watch over her for me. She is my wife, though I leave her behind. Here, take this gold—all the coin I set aside for my journey. She will never take it from me, but she will take it from you, won’t she? God, is that all I have to give her? Money? But poverty is a curse I will not let her bear. You will not let her want for anything, dear Martin? The burgomaster’s silver is all I need for the road.”

Martin clapped him on the shoulder, his gruff face softening. “Thou art a good lad, Gerard. No harm shall come to her, no want shall touch her. I care more for her little finger than the whole rest of the world, and even if she was nothing to me, I would be a father to her for your sake. Go with a stout heart, and God go with you, coming and going.” He turned his head away, no longer hiding how much the moment moved him.

Gerard stopped him before he could step back to Margaret. “Stay here, behind this thicket, I beg you. Turn your head away. I cannot bear anyone to see how I grieve to leave her.” And so Martin did not see the two young lovers kneel in the dust, renewing the sacred vows that cruel men had tried to tear asunder before Heaven. He did not see them cling together as if their two bodies were one, then try to step apart and fail, then cling again, like two drowning men clutching the last floating plank. But he heard Gerard sob, again and again, and Margaret let out soft, broken moans.

At last there was a hoarse, tearing cry, and the sound of boots pattering hard on the packed dirt road. Martin looked up. Gerard was running wild, both hands clasped over his head in desperate prayer, and Margaret tottered after him, palms outstretched as if begging for him not to go, her cheeks ashen, her eyes fixed on nothing. Martin caught her as she swayed, his arms around her, and spoke soft, comforting words—but her grief was too sharp to take them in, she only clung to his tunic tight, trembling all over at the sound of his voice. He lifted her onto the mule, wrapped his arm around her to hold her up, her once strong, boyish frame gone all soft and limp with grief, and led her slow and sad back home. She did not shed a tear, did not speak a single word. At the edge of the wood he helped her down, and bade her go across to her father’s cottage. She obeyed, numb as a statue. Martin set off for Rotterdam next—Sevenbergen was far too dangerous for him now.

Gerard, torn away from the woman he loved, moved like a man in a dream. He hired a horse and guide at the little wayside inn, and rode fast towards the German border. All his movements were mechanical, his senses blunted: trees, cottages, peasants passing by drifted past him like shapes seen through a thick veil. His guide spoke to him twice, and he did not answer. Only once he cried out, hoarse and raw: “Will we never be free of this hateful country?”

After hours of hard riding, they reached the brow of a steep hill, a small silver brook glinting at its foot. “Halt!” the guide called out, pointing across the valley. “That there is Germany, sir.” “Where?” “T’other side of that brook. No need to ride down, I reckon.”

Gerard dismounted without a word, and pulled the burgomaster’s purse from his girdle. As he fumbled to open it, the guide muttered, half-sulky: “Soon you’ll be out of that hateful country, like you said. Mind, the one you’re going to might not like you no better. But whatever you ran from, they can’t touch you once you cross that little brook.” A month before, those words would have earned him a sharp rebuke, maybe a cuff to the ear. Now they were idle wind to Gerard. He paid the guide in silence, and walked down the hill alone.

The brook babbled soft over glinting pebbles, varnished clear by the running water. He sat down, staring at them stupidly for a long minute, then cupped his hands and drank the cold water, then laved his hot, throbbing feet and hands in it. The cold woke him, sharp. He stood up, took a running leap, and cleared the brook, landing on German soil. The moment his boots touched the strange ground, he spun around to look back. “Farewell, ungrateful country!” he cried out. “But for her, I would leave you without a second thought—all my kin, my mother that bore me, my childhood playmates, my little home town. Farewell, fatherland! Welcome the wide world! Omne solum forti patria—every land is a home to the brave.” But the brave words died in his throat, his arms and legs turned weak as water, and he sank down on the foreign soil and sobbed bitter, tearing sobs.

When he had sat there bowed for a long while, he stood up, dashed the tears from his eyes like a man, and did not glance back once more to weaken his resolve. He stepped out onto the long road south. His great love, his heavy grief, left no room in his heart for small, petty fears. Compared to the agony of tearing himself away from Margaret, the thousand-mile walk to Italy in that rough, hard age felt like a small, trivial thing. He knew all nations welcomed strangers in their convents; thanks to his monk friends back home, and his own hunger for learning, he could speak almost every language he would need on that long journey. He told himself, “I will reach Rome soon, the sooner the better now.”

After he had walked a good league, he came to a crossroads, four country lanes winding and serpentine, that had confused many a local villager before. Gerard pulled out the small pocket dial Peter had given him, set it to the autumn sun, and steered straight for Rome, unhesitating, inexperienced as a young swallow flying south for the first time—only unlike the swallow, he was flying there all alone.
