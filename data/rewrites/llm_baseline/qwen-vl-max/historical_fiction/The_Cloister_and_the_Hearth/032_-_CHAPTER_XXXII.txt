In the refectory, where Gerard sat among the monks, conversation turned to the sudden death of the brother who had been entrusted with copying the monastery’s charter and rule. Gerard, hearing this, hesitated only a moment before offering his own services. He spoke softly, not out of pride but from a quiet desire to give something in return for the kindness he’d received during his travels. “Not for hire, my lords,” he said, “but for love—for a small token of gratitude for the many nights your order has sheltered a poor wanderer.”

A monk smiled, pleased by the gesture, yet added gently that the late brother had been an exceptional scribe—so skilled that only another master could continue his work. At once, Gerard drew from his wallet a piece of vellum, its back cleaned and inscribed with careful script as a sample of his hand. The monk stared, then quickly rose and hurried toward the high table, bowing low as he passed the fifth and tenth steps, presenting the vellum to the prior with eager words.

Instantly, a dozen eyes turned to it. A murmur spread through the hall. Then, without warning, the prior pointed—not at the writing, but at Gerard. The monk returned, beaming like a man who had just won a crown, bearing a savory crustade ryal—a rich game pie spiced and glazed—and a silver cup filled with pimentum, a sweetened wine.

Gerard took the cup, bowed deeply first to the distant prior, then to his companions, and drank. As he passed the cup around, the entire table erupted in cheer. “Brother,” they called, “you are no stranger to cloistered ways—confess it!”

He admitted it, thanking Heaven for the truth. Had he not been raised in such a place, he would have stood as awkward and ignorant as Sybrandt and Cornelis beside him. But now he was recognized, and welcomed.

“Yet how did you know?” he asked, puzzled.

“You drank from the cup with both hands,” two monks answered together, nodding as if revealing a sacred secret.

For some time, laughter had been rising from a table near the far end of the hall. Then came a burst so loud and prolonged that even the prior sent the sub-prior himself down to silence them—threatening penance upon every monk present. Gerard flushed with shame, for beneath the uproar he had heard the word “courage!” and the bold voice of Denys of Burgundy.

Soon after, Gerard was given feu Werter’s cell—furnished with wax lights, a tilting frame for writing, and all the tools of a calligrapher. But the task was too great for one night. He turned to Denys, wondering how to ask him to stay longer. To his surprise, Denys replied with unexpected grace: “A day’s rest will do us both good. Write, and I’ll pass the hours as I may.”

When the work was done, the monks praised it highly. They agreed that the monastery’s records had gained more than they had lost with Werter’s passing. The sub-prior pressed a rix-dollar into Gerard’s hand, along with brushes and pigments from the abbey’s ample stores.

As Gerard prepared to leave, his heart warmed. This was a sign—the kind of omen he needed. For it was by the pen he hoped to rebuild his life and reclaim his beloved.

“Come, Denys,” he said cheerfully, showing off his gifts. “See what these good men have given me. Try now to be fairer to them. I confess—I felt my friendship chill when I heard you call them ‘hypocrites.’”

Denys paused, then said, “I recant.”

“Thank you! Thank you, good Denys!”

“I was a scurrilous vagabond,” he admitted.

“Nay, say not so,” Gerard protested.

“But we soldiers are blunt and quick to judge,” Denys continued. “I speak plainly: I lie to myself. And I offer full esteem to those I misunderstood. It is unjust to defame thousands for the sins of a few.”

Now Gerard nodded. “You’ve thought on what I said?”

“Nay—it was their own doing,” Denys replied.

Gerard smiled, pleased to be proved right. Then, with interest, he listened as Denys began to tell how he had changed his mind.

“Well,” said Denys, “on the first day at dinner, a young monk next to me opened his mouth—and laughed. Not a dry chuckle, but a full, musical sound. ‘Ah,’ I said, ‘at last I’ve found a man, not a shorn ape.’ So I slapped his back and gave him my consigne—my salute. ‘Heaven forbid!’ he replied. I stared. The lad looked as solemn as Solomon. A better mime you never saw—even at a Mystery play.

‘I see war sharpens neither wit nor spirit,’ he said. ‘What are the clergy for? To fight the foul fiend. And what are monks for? “The fiend being dead, the friars are sped.” You may plough up the convents—we shall have nothing left to do but become soldiers… and bring him back to life.’”

At this, the whole table roared. “Well,” said Denys, “I told him, ‘You’re the monk for me.’ And he replied, ‘And you’re the crossbowman for me. I’ll wager you could tell tales that’d make our hair stand on end.’ ‘Excusez!’ I said. ‘The barber has taken care of that.’ And there—I had the laugh.”

“What dreadful jesting!” Gerard murmured.

Denys grinned. “Truth be told, I’ve seen funnier tricks with less noise.”

But he went on. “It was a great thing to rid myself of that old prejudice. ‘So,’ I said, ‘I can give you the chaire de poule—if that pleases you.’ ‘We shall see,’ they cried—and a signal passed round.”

Then he recounted, laughing still, how he had been led to a private cell instead of the dormitory, forbidden to sleep, and given a book filled with pictures of monks chasing women. Later, barefoot and wide-eyed, he was brought to the parlour, where a feast fit for a duke awaited him—twelve jovial friars, roaring louder than any troop he’d met in war or peace.

They drank, they joked, they played cards with a deck where Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, bedecked in gold, were the queens; black, white, grey, and crutched friars were the knaves. Rosaries were staked, and curses flew when someone lost.

Near midnight, a monk slipped out—but not alone. Others followed, watching as he crept into the garden, pulled a rope-ladder from the ivy, and climbed the ten-foot wall. Almost instantly, a russet kirtle appeared from outside—then, in the moonlight, a pair embracing. “Mew!” Denys cried.

The monks joined in. The lay visitor shrieked. Only Abelard remained calm. “What?” he called. “Are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? You’ll caterwaul to a tune tomorrow night. I’ll dress each one of you in a fardingale.”

That threat, absurd as it was, made Denys stay—by Gerard’s request.

Gerard groaned.

Meantime, unable to silence such a bold monk, and seeing the demoiselle begin to whimper, they danced in a circle, caterwauling, then blessed the two lovers at the wall, and carried the drunk and the weeping back to their cells.

“And so shut up in measureless content,” Denys concluded.

Gerard was horrified. “Such wretches! Corrupters of youth! Perverters of innocence! Were it not for you, Denys—who have been taught no better—oh, that God might strike down every one of them! Impious, abominable hypocrites!”

“Hypocrites?” Denys echoed, startled. “That was my word before I knew them. And you stood against me. Nay—they are sinners. All good fellows are. But by St. Denis’ skull, they are not hypocrites. They are jolly, roaring blades.”

“Denys,” Gerard said, grave now, “you little know the danger you ran that night. That church—where you feasted—is haunted. I heard it from an elder monk. The dead walk there. Their light feet have been heard patter across the stones.”

“Misericorde!” whispered Denys.

“Aye,” Gerard continued, lowering his voice. “Celestial sounds have come from the crypt beneath the chapel—the very vault you turned into a tavern. Voices of the departed have held unearthly communion. Music has been heard—notes struck by no mortal hand, chords rung by no mortal finger—echoing faintly among the dead.”

Denys looked pale. “Ugh! If I’d known, mules and ropes could not have dragged me there. And so…”—he sighed—“I missed a merry time.”

Whether they might have discussed the spirits further, none can say. For at that moment, a bearded brother rode up on a mule, waving a piece of vellum. It was the deed between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt.

Gerard paled—then laughed, relieved. He had nearly lost it. To Denys’s amusement, he not only gave the brother a coin, but kissed the mule’s nose.

“I’ll read it to you,” he said, “even if it’s twice as ill-written. And—to make sure I never lose it”—he sat down, took out needle and thread, and sewed the vellum to his doublet with delicate skill. His thoughts fled to Sevenbergen.

They reached their destination. Denys, in high spirits, doffed his hat to every woman, who curtsied and smiled. He fired his consigne at most men—some stared, some grinned, some both. Finally, he delivered Gerard to one of the promised Burgundian inns.

“It’s a small one,” he said, “but I know it well. Les Trois Poissons.”

“But what does it say?” he asked, pointing to a long inscription painted across the front in huge letters.

“Oh,” said Denys, reading carefully, “‘Ici on loge à pied et à cheval.’” He looked proud of himself.

Gerard looked—and read aloud:

“ON NE LOGE CEANS À CRÉDIT; CE BONHOMME EST MORT, LES MAUVAIS PAIEURS L'ONT TUE.”
