“My desolation does begin to make A better life.”
—SHAKESPEARE: *Antony and Cleopatra.*

For a full day after his first heart-thundering interview with the mother he had never known existed, Deronda waited on tenterhooks, the empty space of their unspoken bond hanging heavy in every corner of the home he had always shared with Sir Hugo. The only message that came was a brief note saying she was too worn thin by emotion to receive him again—until the third morning, when a crisp, urgent line arrived: I leave to-day. Come and see me at once.

He was shown into the same dim drawing room as before, its blinds drawn low and heavy curtains draped across the windows to soften the autumn light into something muted, almost sacred. The Princess was not there at first, but a moment later she stepped through the door, wrapped in a loose, flowing gown of soft dusky orange silk, black lace drifting like mist around her head, her arms bare from beneath the wide, billowing sleeves. In the dim gloom her face looked more arresting than ever: her eyes larger, the sharp lines of her maturity carved deep and vivid, as if she might have been a sorceress who could weave youth and joy for everyone else, but had long since wearied of brewing potions for herself, having drunk enough of life’s fire to last a dozen lifetimes.

She crossed the room in two strides, slipped her arms around his shoulders, and pressed soft kisses to both his cheeks before sinking back into her nest of velvet cushions, her bearing steady, regal, no trace of the fitful, fraying emotion that had shaken her during their first meeting. “Sit by me,” she said, and he obeyed, his chest tight with unspoke hope that they might yet find some common ground.

“You are feeling better, I trust?” he said gently.

“At peace, at last.” She tilted her head, regarding him with the calm authority of a queen rather than the soft fondness of a mother. “Is there anything more you wish to ask of me?”

“Can I find the old house in Genoa where you lived with my grandfather?” Deronda asked, the question he had turned over a hundred times as he walked the previous night, imagining the threshold he might cross to touch the roots of the identity he had only just uncovered.

A small, dismissing movement of her bare arm was his answer. “It is pulled down. Gone, no trace left. But every detail of our family, every place my father lived, all the old stories—they are all written down in the papers locked in the chest I told you of. Better than I could ever tell you. He was a physician, you know. My mother was a Morteira. I heard all those tales as a child, and never listened. I was born into that life without my consent, and I banished it all as soon as I was old enough to run free.”

Deronda bit back the sharp, pained flinch that threatened to cross his face. “Anything I could ever hope to hear from you would only be what you chose to give, what might ease your own heart, not satisfy a list of demands.”

“I have told you everything that could possibly be asked of me,” she said, her gaze distant and coldly meditative. She had steeled herself the night before: I have confessed all. I will not tear open those wounds again. I will not let agitation break me. But for Deronda, whose whole life of quiet filial yearning had been leading him to this moment, it felt like a cruel blow. It was as if he had walked for years on a pilgrimage to a sacred shrine, only to find the altars bare, no emblems of the love he had ached to find.

“Then are we to part,” he said, his voice trembling a little, “and I am never to be anything to you at all?”

“It is better so,” her voice softened, deeper, richer. “There would be nothing but hard, tedious duty for you if you tried to step into the place of the son I never let you be. You would never love me, truly. Don’t deny it.” She held up a hand, sharp and commanding, cutting off his protest. “I know the truth. You hate what I did. You think I robbed you. You are on your grandfather’s side, and you will carry a quiet condemnation of me in your heart forever.”

Deronda fell silent, pinned by her certainty. He stood, rather than stay seated so close to her, the ache in his chest too big to bear while he was forced to hold back every impulse to reach for her and argue that he did not judge her. But she looked up at him then, a new, warm spark of admiration lighting her dark eyes. “You are wrong to be angry. You are better for what I did.” She paused, then spoke abruptly. “What will you do now? What will this news of your birth change for your life?”

“Do you mean immediately, or the whole path ahead?” Deronda asked.

“Your future. Will you turn yourself into the perfect image of my father, the Jew he wanted you to be?”

“The education I was given can never be undone,” he said, his tone steady, firm with conviction. “The Christian sympathies I was raised with will never fade. But it is my duty—my deepest, most urgent impulse—to bind myself as closely as I can to my people, my heritage. If I find work I can pour my whole soul into for their sake, that is the path I will choose.”

She stared at him, her gaze sharp and curious, as if she was tracing the lines of his face to decode a language she did not fully understand. He held her gaze steady, rooted in the full weight of that new purpose. Then she leaned in a little, her voice sharp and certain. “You are in love with a Jewess.”

Color flooded Deronda’s cheeks, hot and unbidden. “My resolve has nothing to do with any such person.”

“I know better. I have seen how men are,” she said, dismissive, peremptory. “Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will not accept anyone who is not one of her own. There are a few such.” There was a faint, sharp edge of scorn in her tone.

Deronda felt the familiar, helpless frustration of speaking to someone whose mind was already made up, who would not be swayed by any explanation he could give. He could not bring himself to confirm or deny the secret, tender, unspoken longing he harbored for Mirah Lapidoth, so he stayed silent. She nodded, as if his stillness was confirmation. “You love her the way your father loved me. She pulls you after her, the same way I drew him.”

The words tugged at his imagination, softening his gaze, and she took that as assent, her voice rising with a rush of old, long-buried passion. “But I was leading him the other way. And now my father has his revenge through you.”

“Mother,” Deronda said, his voice gentle, pleading, “do not see it that way. I do not resent the education you chose for me. I would rather be grateful for all the good it gave me than dwell on any wrong you might have done. It would have been a gift to grow up knowing I was Jewish, but I will never regret that I was given such wide, open sympathies, such a broad view of the world. And now you have given me back my inheritance—events have restored to me more than you ever could have taken. You did not rob me of my duty, or my people of my service. Can you not let your heart soften to that?”

He paused, and she looked at him, listening, as if the cadence of his voice had wrapped around her and stolen her attention away from her old, hard grief. She shook her head slowly. “You told me you sought the best for me,” he pressed, more urgently. “Soft your heart to my grandfather, who sought the best for you, in his own way.”

“Not for me. Never for me.” She shook her head, more fiercely, folding her arms tight across her chest. “He never saw me as anything but an instrument. Because I had wants outside of his plans for me, he wanted to lock me in a frame and torture me. If that is the law the world runs on, I will not pretend to love it. If my acts were wrong—if God is making me give back what I stole, punishing me for deceiving my father, for breaking his trust—well. I have confessed all. I have done all I can. And your soul consents. That is enough. I turned out to be the instrument he wanted after all. ‘I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.’”

She narrowed her eyes, nodding her head slowly, her voice dropping to a low, chesty rumble, as if she spoke the words against her will.

“Were those his words?” Deronda asked, his throat tight.

“Yes, they are written down in the papers,” she snapped, a burst of the old passion flaring up again. “I wanted to thwart him.” Her tone softened, slow, bitter. “You want me to love what I have hated since I was no higher than”—she held her left hand a yard above the floor—“a small child. That can never happen. But what does it matter? His yoke has been on me no matter how much I hated it. You are the grandson he wanted. You speak like a wise young man, full of purpose. But what does it all mean?”

Her tone was sharp, scornful, but Deronda held fast to the bond of blood between them, refusing to let anger win. “Mother, I do not claim to be wise. We are all set in the middle of such messy, tangled difficulties. The only clarity I know of is to be truthful, to not hide the facts that carry obligation within them, that guide us toward what is right. No human’s selfish will can ever outrun the tides of generations, the things that are rooted deep in the sacred foundations of the world. Your will was strong, but the trust my grandfather held that you tried to break, that yoke you hated—it was stronger than you. You renounced me, you banished me as a son—but that greater Something has made me exactly the grandson you once tried to erase.”

She watched him fixedly, and admiration gathered in her face again. After a long silence, she patted the cushion beside her. “Sit down again.” He obeyed, and she rested her hand light on his shoulder. “You rebuke me. Well, I am the one who lost. And you are angry because I send you away. What could you do for me but wear out your own patience? I am a shattered woman now. My sense of life is almost nothing but the memory of what was, except for the pain that comes sharp and present. You grieve that I parted with you. I had joy enough without you back then. Now you have come back to me, and I cannot make you a joy. Do you carry that old Jewish curse in you? Can you not forgive me? Will you be glad to think I am punished for not being a good Jewish mother to you?”

“How can you ask me that?” Deronda said, stung. “I have begged you to let me be your son now. My grief is that you say I can never comfort you. I would give up every dear thing I own to soothe your pain.”

“You will give up nothing,” she said, hurrying, agitated. “You will be happy. You will let me think of you happy. I did you no harm. You have no reason to curse me. You will think of me the way people think of the dead they pray for—you will wish me free of all pain, all punishment. I will see your face instead of my father’s every time I close my eyes. If you think the Kaddish will help me, say it. Say it. You will stand between me and the dead. When you think of me, you will look as you do now—always a tender son, as if I was always a tender mother.”

Her hand trembled on his shoulder, and Deronda wrapped his arms around her, pressing her head gently to his chest, no words enough for the great, swelling compassion that filled him. They sat that way, quiet, for long minutes. Then she lifted her head, a great sigh heaving out of her, as if she had cast off a lifetime of heavy weight in that one breath. The moment of softness passed, and one of her swift, abrupt changes came over her.

“Is she beautiful?” she said.

Deronda flushed. “Who?”

“The woman you love.”

“There is no point in hiding it,” he said softly. “Yes. She is.”

“Not ambitious?”

“No, I do not think she is.”

“Not one who must carve a path all her own?”

“Her nature is not one to make great demands of the world.”

She pulled a jeweled miniature from her wallet, held it up before his face. It was her own portrait, painted when she was young, bright with all the fire and genius of her youth. “I had a right to be more than just a daughter, more than just a mother,” she said, her voice tight. “My voice, my talent, matched that face. Whatever else I did that was wrong, acknowledge I had a right to be an artist, even when my father’s will was against me. My nature gave me that charter.”

“I do acknowledge that,” Deronda said, his eyes moving from the miniature to her worn, pale face, which held a living force no painter could ever capture.

“Will you take it?” she said, her voice softer now. “If the girl you love is a kind woman, teach her to think of me kindly.”

“I will be so grateful for it,” Deronda said, “but I must tell you—there is no certainty that the woman I love will ever return my feelings. I have never dared speak of them to her.”

“Who is she?” the Princess asked, her tone sharp, almost a command.

“She was raised to be a singer for the stage,” Deronda said, reluctant. “Her father took her away from her mother when she was very small, and her whole life has been unhappy. She is only twenty. He tried to raise her to hate her Jewish heritage, but she has clung to the memory of her mother, and to her people, with every bit of her heart.”

“Ah, like you. Attached to a Judaism she barely knows,” the Princess said, dismissive. “Poetry, pretty enough to last the length of an opera. Is her singing any good? Does she love the artist’s life?”

“Her singing is exquisite. But her voice was never fit for the stage. Her life as a performer has been made bitter, hateful to her.”

“Why then, she is made for you. Sir Hugo told me you were dead set against ever marrying a singer, and I can see you would never have let yourself be swallowed whole by a wife, the way your father was by me.”

“I tell you again,” Deronda said, urgent, “there is no certainty we can ever be together. Painful roads may lie ahead of me. I have always made a point of preparing myself to renounce that hope, not cling to it. But I suppose that is how one should feel about all happiness. You should be ready to do without it, even if it never comes.”

“Do you truly feel that way?” She rested her hands on his shoulders, searching his face, speaking low and meditative, pausing between every word. “Poor boy. I wonder what would have happened if I had kept you with me. If you would have turned your heart to the old traditions against mine, and we would have fought and fought, with my father’s blood running in you, tangling up my life with your young roots from that old Jewish root.”

“I think my love for you would have outlasted every quarrel,” Deronda said, his heart heavy. “It would not have hampered you. It would have enriched your life, surely.”

“Not then. Not back then, when I wanted nothing but freedom.” A bitter, melancholy note crept into her voice. “I might be glad of it now, if I could be glad of anything.”

“Do you love my other siblings? Do they love you?” Deronda asked, anxious.

“Oh yes,” she said, as if he had asked if the sun rose in the east. Then her tone deepened, turning dark and honest. “But I am not a loving woman. That is the truth. To love is a talent. I do not have it. People have loved me, and I have let them. I know what love does to you—it is subjection. You take another person and make them larger than yourself, wrap your whole self around them.” She tapped a finger to her own chest. “I never would willingly be subject to any man. Men have been subject to me.”

“Perhaps the man who was subject to you was the happier of the two,” Deronda said, his face grave, sad at the great empty gap in her life that she had never let love fill.

“Perhaps. But I was happy, for a few bright years I was happy. If I had not been so afraid of failing, of losing, I might have kept that joy. I miscalculated. What of it? It is all over. People talk of another life as if it only starts after you die. I have been living in my second life for a very long time.”

She lifted her arms then, bare to the elbows, her brow creased in one deep, sharp line, her eyes closed, her voice smothered. Draped in that dusky, flame-colored gown, she looked like a spirit visiting from some far land of long-lost ghosts. Deronda’s chest ached so sharply he could not hold back a soft, audible sob. She opened her eyes, rested her hands back on his shoulders.

“Goodbye, my son. Goodbye. We will never see each other again, never hear from each other. Kiss me.”

He wrapped his arms tight around her neck, and they kissed. Deronda never remembered walking out of that room, or down the stairs, or across the park to his own lodgings. He felt years older than he had that morning. All the boyish, wandering yearning he had carried for a mother he never knew was gone. He had walked through a tragedy that would solemnize every day of his life from that point on, making every promise he made to the people he loved, every step he took toward his heritage, more sacred than he had ever imagined it could be.
