The new journal, *The Pall Mall Gazette*, launched with a bang that echoed through the literary circles of London. Rumors swirled like dust in a sunbeam: an influential political party stood behind the paper, and its columns boasted the names of titans. Whether these whispers were true remained a mystery, but the truth was far more theatrical. A fiery article on foreign policy, widely attributed to a high-born Lord with deep ties to the Foreign Office, was actually penned by Captain Shandon in the smoky parlor of the Bear and Staff public-house near Whitehall Stairs. There, tracked down by a printer's boy and temporarily residing alongside a literary ally named Mr. Bludyer, the Captain had crafted the piece with the ease of a man comfortable in his own skin. Similarly, a string of papers on finance, universally believed to be the work of a great Statesman of the House of Commons, were the brainchild of Mr. George Warrington of the Upper Temple.

There was likely some secret handshake between the *Gazette* and that powerful party. Percy Popjoy, whose father, Lord Falconet, belonged to the faction, could frequently be seen climbing the stairs to Warrington's chambers. The paper also published peculiar information that seemed to come from nowhere, alongside several poems signed "P.P."—feeble in thought but roaring with expression. These verses, along with outrageously enthusiastic praise for Popjoy's novel, filled the pages.

While Mr. Pen kept his distance from the political machinations, he became the paper's most energetic literary soul. The *Gazette* operated out of Catherine Street in the Strand, a hub where Pen would arrive with manuscripts tucked in his pockets, buzzing with the electric thrill of a debutant. To see his words in print felt like a novel sensation; he delighted in the idea that his writings were stirring the world.

It was here that Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, performed his magic with paste and scissors. As the supervisor of the Journal, Finucane possessed an eagle eye for the world of fashion. He ensured that no death or dinner party among the aristocracy escaped his notice, fishing out astonishing gossip from obscure provincial prints and distant Scottish and Irish papers. It was a comical sight for any philosopher to witness: Jack Finucane, Esquire, sitting on a stool with a plate of cheap meat and a glass of porter, recounting the grand feasts of the elite as if he had been seated at the head table. Dressed in tattered trousers and a dingy shirt, he cheerfully arranged the most brilliant fêtes of the world, his appearance a stark contrast to the high society he described. This incongruity amused Pen, who knew that Jack had rarely seen anything beyond the tavern parlors of his native village, yet his writing made him sound like a regular at White's. While errors occasionally slipped through, the *Ballinafad Sentinel*, where Jack served as correspondent, suffered more from them than the London *Gazette*, where the editors preferred his scissors to his pen.

Pen poured his heart into his reviews. Armed with a desultory but eager reading habit cultivated in his youth, he possessed a keen sense of fun. His articles delighted both his chief and the public, earning him pride and a paycheck. The *Gazette* was devoured at Fairoaks, enjoyed by the two ladies there, and welcomed at Clavering Park, where a young lady of literary tastes awaited it. Even old Doctor Portman, to whom the widow sent the paper after memorizing her son's articles, offered his seal of approval. "The lad has spirit, taste, and fancy," the doctor declared. "He writes, if not like a scholar, at least like a gentleman."

What truly astonished Major Pendennis, however, was hearing his clubmates at the Regent dissecting the latest issue. Among them were Wenham, Lord Falconet, and other gentlemen of fashion, all laughing uproariously over an article that savaged a recent book by the Countess of Muffborough. The book, a *Travels in Spain and Italy*, was a masterpiece of linguistic confusion, written indifferently in French and English. Pen's critique, signed anonymously, pounced on the blunders with delightful mischief. He danced around his subject with jocularity, exposing the noble lady's faults with mock gravity. Every word was polite, yet the Countess was thoroughly scarified. Wenham's bilious face puckered with malignant pleasure; Lady Muffborough hadn't invited him to her parties in a year. Lord Falconet giggled heartily, remembering his rivalry with Lord Muffborough. They all turned to Major Pendennis, praising his nephew. Until then, the Major had dismissed hints from Fairoaks about "Arthur's constant and severe literary occupations" as beneath his dignity, but now, seeing the acclaim, he changed his tune.

"When Wenham praises the boy," the Major mused, "and Lord Falconet approves of his genius, and even Lord Steyne laughs and calls it capital, saying the Muffborough will writhe like a whale under a harpoon..." The Major's voice trembled with pleasure. "By gad, the young rascal has some stuff in him! He will do something!" Trembling with joy, he sat down to write to the widow at Fairoaks, relaying the great folks' praise, and then addressed a letter directly to the young man. He invited Pen to eat a chop with him and announced he was commissioned to take him to dinner at Gaunt House, for Lord Steyne appreciated anyone who could entertain him, whether by folly, wit, or dullness. Pen tossed the letter to Warrington, perhaps expecting a reaction that didn't quite come.

Young critics possess prodigious courage. They climb the judgment seat and deliver verdicts on the most intricate works without hesitation. If Macaulay's *History* or Herschel's *Astronomy* had been placed before Pen at this stage, he would have glanced through them, smoked a cigar, and issued an august approval as if he were their superior patron. With the aid of the *Biographie Universelle* or the British Museum, he could summarize historical periods and allude to dates and facts with masterly ease, astonishing his mother at home who wondered where such knowledge came from. Only when reading his articles months later, having forgotten the sources, would he realize the extent of his bluffing. At twenty-four, Pen would not have hesitated to judge the greatest scholars or critique the *Encyclopaedia*. Fortunately, Warrington kept him in check with constant, wholesome ridicule. Without it, Pen might have become conceited beyond endurance. Shandon, the veteran, relished the dash and flippancy of his young aide-de-camp, preferring Pen's light flashes to the heavier metal of his elder coadjutor.

Yet, despite his impertinence and premature judgments, Pen was a perfectly honest critic—too candid for Mr. Bungay's liking. A dispute arose between Pen and his chief, Captain Shandon. "In the name of common-sense, Mr. Pendennis," Shandon asked, "what have you been doing—praising one of Mr. Bacon's books? Bungay was in a fury this morning seeing a laudatory article on the works of that odious firm."

Pen's eyes widened. "Do you mean to say," he asked, "that we are to praise no books Bacon publishes? Or that, if they are good, we must call them bad?"

"My good young friend," Shandon inquired, "for what do you suppose a benevolent publisher undertakes a critical journal, to benefit his rival?"

"To benefit himself certainly, but to tell the truth too," Pen insisted. "*Ruat coelum*, to tell the truth."

"And my prospectus," Shandon laughed, sneering. "Do you consider that was a work of mathematical accuracy?"

"Pardon me," Pen replied. "I had qualms of conscience about that prospectus and debated it with Warrington. We agreed, however, that because the prospectus was declamatory and poetical, and the giant painted on the show-board was larger than the original inside the caravan, we need not be too scrupulous about this trifling inaccuracy. We are the fiddlers, playing our tunes; you are the showman."

"And leader of the van," Shandon added. "Well, I am glad your conscience gave you leave to play for us."

"Yes, but," Pen said, asserting the dignity of his position, "we are all party men in England. I will stick to my party like a Briton. A fool quarrels with his own nest; I will hit the enemy as hard as you like—but with fair play, Captain. One can't tell all the truth, I suppose, but one can tell nothing but the truth. I would rather starve, by Jove, and never earn another penny by my pen—this redoubted instrument, now six weeks in use, which I hold with vast enthusiasm—than strike an opponent an unfair blow or rank him below his honest desert."

"Well, Mr. Pendennis, when we want Bacon smashed, we must get some other hammer," Shandon said with fatal good-nature, perhaps thinking, "A few years hence, perhaps the young gentleman won't be so squeamish." The veteran Condottiere was no longer scrupulous; remorse had long left him after fighting and killing on so many sides. "Gad," he muttered, "you've a tender conscience, Mr. Pendennis. It's the luxury of all novices, and I may have had one once. But that bloom wears off with the rubbing of the world. I'm not going to the trouble of putting on an artificial complexion like our pious friend Wenham or our model of virtue, Wagg."

"I don't know whether some people's hypocrisy is not better, Captain, than others' cynicism."

"It's more profitable, at any rate," said the Captain, biting his nails. "That Wenham is as dull a quack as ever quacked. You see the carriage in which he drove to dinner. Faith, it'll be a long time before Mrs. Shandon takes a drive in her own chariot. God help her, poor thing!"

Pen departed from his chief, pondering the Captain's tale. He saw a man stored with genius, wit, learning, and a hundred good natural gifts, yet wrecked by paltering with honesty and forgetting self-respect. *Wilt thou remember thyself, O Pen?* he thought. *Thou art conceited enough! Wilt thou sell thy honour for a bottle? No, by heaven's grace, we will be honest, whatever befalls, and our mouths shall only speak the truth when they open.*

A punishment, or at least a trial, awaited Pen. In the very next number of the *Pall Mall Gazette*, Warrington read aloud an article that failed to amuse Arthur Pendennis, who was busy drafting his own critique for the following week. In this piece, the Spring Annual was ferociously maltreated by an unknown writer. The person most cruelly mauled was Pen himself. His verses had not appeared under his own name in the Annual but under an assumed signature. Having refused to review the book, Shandon had handed it to Mr. Bludyer with instructions to dispose of it. Bludyer, a man of considerable talent belonging to a race extinct in modern journalism, possessed a reputation for savage humor. He smashed and trampled the poor spring flowers with no more mercy than a bull on a parterre. Having cut up the volume to his heart's content, he sold it at a bookstall and purchased a pint of brandy with the proceeds.
