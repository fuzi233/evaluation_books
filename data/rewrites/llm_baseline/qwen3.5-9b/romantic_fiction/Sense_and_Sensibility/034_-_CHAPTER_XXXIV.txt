Mrs. John Dashwood possessed such absolute faith in her husband's discernment that she waited until the very next day to pay calls upon Mrs. Jennings and her daughter. Her trust was richly rewarded; she discovered that even the former, the woman with whom her sisters were residing, was far from unworthy of her attention. As for Lady Middleton, she found her to be one of the most charming women in existence! Lady Middleton, in turn, was equally captivated by Mrs. Dashwood. Between them lay a certain cold-hearted selfishness that acted as a powerful magnet, drawing them together through a shared insipid propriety of manner and a mutual lack of genuine understanding.

However, the very qualities that secured Mrs. John Dashwood the high regard of Lady Middleton failed to win the favor of Mrs. Jennings. To the latter, she appeared merely as a somewhat proud-looking woman of uninviting address, who greeted her husband's sisters without affection and seemingly had nothing meaningful to say to them. Indeed, during the quarter-hour spent at Berkeley Street, Mrs. Jennings sat in silence for at least seven and a half minutes.

Elinor yearned intensely to inquire, though she chose not to ask aloud, whether Edward was currently in London. Yet, nothing would have prompted Fanny to volunteer his name before her until she could announce that his marriage to Miss Morton was finalized, or until her husband's hopes regarding Colonel Brandon were realized. She believed them still so deeply attached that they required constant vigilance to keep them apart in word and deed. Nevertheless, the information Fanny withheld soon surfaced from another source. Lucy arrived shortly thereafter to solicit Elinor's sympathy for her inability to see Edward, despite his arrival in town with Mr. and Mrs. Dashwood. He dared not visit Bartlett's Buildings for fear of being detected, and though their mutual longing to meet was palpable, they could do nothing but correspond. Edward confirmed his presence in town twice by leaving cards on the table at Berkeley Street. Elinor was pleased that he had called, and even more so that she had managed to miss him.

The Dashwoods were so extravagantly delighted with the Middletons that, despite rarely giving entertainments, they resolved to host them. Soon after forming their acquaintance, they invited the couple to dine in Harley Street, where they had recently leased a fine house for three months. Their sisters and Mrs. Jennings were cordially invited, and John Dashwood took care to secure Colonel Brandon. Always eager to be near the Miss Dashwoods, the Colonel received his enthusiastic courtesies with some surprise, but with even greater pleasure. They intended to invite Mrs. Ferrars, though Elinor could not ascertain if her sons would attend. The prospect of meeting *her*, however, filled Elinor with keen interest. Though she could now face Edward's mother without the intense anxiety that once plagued such introductions, and could view her with perfect indifference regarding her own self-worth, her desire to be in Mrs. Ferrars' company and her curiosity about her appearance remained as vivid as ever.

This anticipation was soon intensified, more powerfully than pleasantly, by news that the Miss Steeles were also to attend. Having won such favorable impressions from Lady Middleton, and rendered so agreeable by their assiduous efforts, that although Lucy lacked true elegance and her sister was scarcely genteel, they were as readily invited by Sir John to spend a week or two in Conduit Street. It proved particularly convenient for the Miss Steeles that their visit commenced a few days prior to the party, once the Dashwoods' invitation became known. While their connection as nieces of the gentleman who had long cared for her brother might not have secured them seats at Mrs. John Dashwood's table otherwise, as guests of Lady Middleton, they were welcome. Lucy, who had long desired to be personally acquainted with the family, to gauge their characters and assess her own predicaments, and to find opportunities to please them, had never been happier in her life upon receiving Mrs. John Dashwood's card.

For Elinor, the effect was starkly different. She immediately resolved that Edward, living with his mother, must be invited to a party hosted by his sister, and she dreaded the thought of seeing him for the first time since everything transpired, surrounded by Lucy! She struggled to comprehend how she could endure it. These fears, perhaps not entirely grounded in reason and certainly devoid of truth, were alleviated not by her own reflection, but by Lucy's goodwill. Believing she was dealing a severe blow, Lucy informed Elinor that Edward would certainly not be in Harley Street on Tuesday, hoping further to soothe her by convincing her that he was absent solely due to his profound affection for her, which he could not hide when they were together.

Tuesday finally arrived, the day destined to introduce the two young ladies to this formidable mother-in-law. "Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!" cried Lucy as they ascended the stairs together—for the Middletons had arrived immediately after Mrs. Jennings, and they all followed the servant simultaneously—"there is no one here but you who can truly feel for me. I declare I can hardly stand. Good heavens! In a moment I shall behold the person upon whose happiness my entire future depends—that is to become my mother!"

Elinor could have offered immediate relief by suggesting they might be about to see Miss Morton's mother rather than her own, but instead, she assured Lucy with great sincerity that she pitied her, to the utter astonishment of Lucy, who, though genuinely uncomfortable, had hoped at least to inspire in Elinor a feeling of irrepressible envy.

Mrs. Ferrars was a small, thin woman, upright to the point of formality in her bearing, and serious to the verge of sourness in her expression. Her complexion was sallow; her features were small, lacking beauty and naturally devoid of expression. However, a fortunate furrowing of the brow saved her countenance from the disgrace of dullness, lending it instead the strong marks of pride and ill-nature. She was not a woman of many words; unlike most, she measured her speech strictly against the number of her thoughts. Of the few syllables that escaped her lips, not one was directed toward Miss Dashwood, whom she regarded with a spirited determination to dislike her regardless of circumstances.

Elinor could no longer be distressed by such behavior. A few months prior, it would have wounded her deeply, but Mrs. Ferrars held no power to trouble her now. Moreover, the disparity in her treatment of the Miss Steeles—a difference seemingly engineered to humble them further—only amused Elinor. She could not help smiling at the graciousness displayed by both mother and daughter toward the very person whom, had they known the full extent of the situation, they would have sought most diligently to mortify. Meanwhile, Elinor herself, possessing comparatively little power to wound them, sat pointedly slighted by both.

As she smiled at this misplaced grace, she could not refrain from despising all four of them. She observed the mean-spirited folly from which it sprang and the calculated attentions with which the Miss Steeles courted its continuation. Lucy was in a state of pure exultation at being so honorably distinguished, while Miss Steele needed only to be teased about Dr. Davies to achieve perfect happiness.

The dinner was a grand affair, attended by numerous servants, signaling the mistress's penchant for display and the master's capacity to sustain it. Despite the ongoing improvements to the Norland estate and the fact that its owner had once been within a thousand pounds of selling at a loss, no trace of the indigence he had attempted to project appeared. No poverty existed anywhere, save for a distinct lack of conversation, which was considerable. John Dashwood had little of value to contribute, and his wife even less. Yet, there was no special shame in this, for it was common among their chief guests, who almost universally suffered from one or more disqualifications for being agreeable: a lack of natural or cultivated sense, a lack of elegance, a lack of spirit, or a lack of temper.

When the ladies retired to the drawing-room after dinner, this conversational poverty became especially apparent. The gentlemen had indeed supplied the discourse with variety—politics, enclosed lands, and broken horses—but that was all. Only one topic engaged the ladies until coffee was served: the relative heights of Harry Dashwood and Lady Middleton's second son, William, who were nearly the same age. Had both children been present, the matter might have been settled easily by measurement; but since only Harry was there, it devolved into conjecture. Everyone felt entitled to be equally positive in their opinions and to repeat them ad infinitum. The standings were as follows: the two mothers, though each firmly convinced her own son was taller, politely decided in favor of the other. The two grandmothers, with equal partiality but greater sincerity, were equally earnest in supporting their own descendants. Lucy, hardly less anxious to please one parent than the other, deemed both boys remarkably tall for their age and could not conceive of any difference whatsoever. Miss Steele, with even greater address, gave credit to each boy as quickly as possible.

Elinor, having once expressed her opinion in favor of William (thereby offending Mrs. Ferrars and Fanny further), saw no need to enforce it with additional assertions. Marianne, when called upon, offended everyone by declaring she had no opinion to offer, as she had never considered the matter.

Before departing Norland, Elinor had painted a very attractive pair of screens for her sister-in-law. Now freshly mounted and brought home, they adorned the current drawing-room.
