Sally surveyed the long table with satisfaction. Her farewell party—after a rocky start—had officially crossed over from "awkward lull" to "roaring success." The initial tension (undoubtedly caused by her brother Fillmore’s aggressively white evening waistcoat) had melted away, and the assorted boarders of Mrs. Meecher’s establishment were now in full, boisterous swing.

At her end of the table, the conversation had circled back to the only topic more thrilling than free food: Sally’s sudden windfall and how she ought to spend it. Nothing brings people together like spending imaginary money, and the suggestions were appropriately extravagant.

“Here’s what *I’d* do,” declared Augustus Bartlett, with the brisk authority of a man who had once shaken hands with a stockbroker. “Sink two hundred grand into rock-solid bonds—we’ve got a lovely issue at the office—then take the rest for a spin on something spicy. Multiple Steel’s the play. Rumor says it’ll hit a hundred fifty by Saturday.”

Elsa Doland, the doe-eyed actress beside him, rolled her eyes. “*Or* buy a theater and put on something that doesn’t make audiences weep for the wrong reasons.”

“And lose every dime,” countered a mild-voiced young man across the table. “If I had a few hundred thousand, I’d dump it all on Benny Whistler for the title fight. My barber’s cousin knows a guy who swears Battling Tuke’s been paid to take a dive in the seventh—”

“Hold up,” interrupted another guest. “Lemme tell you what *I’d* do with four hundred thousand—”

Elsa sighed. “If *I* had four hundred thousand, I know the *first* thing I’d do.”

“What’s that?” Sally asked.

“Pay last week’s rent.”

Sally shot up, darted down the table, and flung an arm around her friend. “Elsa, you’re *not* broke, are you? Because you know I’d—”

Elsa laughed. “You’re a saint, Sally, but no. Just back from tour, flush as a high roller. I only said it to watch you sprint.”

Relieved, Sally returned to her seat just as the party split into two factions: the cautious (Liberty Bonds and real estate) and the degenerate (Benny Whistler at 3-to-1 odds). Time to clarify.

“Your numbers are off,” she announced. “I’ve got twenty-five thousand. Not a penny more.”

A stunned silence fell. To this crowd of imaginary tycoons, the sum was practically loose change. Then, with admirable resilience, they recalibrated.

“If *I* had twenty-five thousand,” Bartlett mused, “I’d snap up Amalgamated—”

“If *I* had twenty-five thousand,” Elsa began.

“If I’d had twenty-five thousand in 1900,” muttered a gloomy spectacled man, “I could’ve overthrown Paraguay.”

Sally clapped her hands. “Here’s *my* plan: a month in France—assuming they let me in—then back to find a cozy little business to pour money into. Any objections?”

“Even a *grand* on Benny Whistler—”

“No. If I want to light money on fire, I’ll do it in Monte Carlo with a view.”

At the far end of the table, a chair scraped. Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house’s elder statesman, rose with the gravitas of a Shakespearean actor (which he had been, briefly, in 1893).

“Ladies,” he began, bowing, then fixed the fidgeting younger men with a glare, “*and* gentlemen. I cannot let this evening pass unmarked.”

No one was surprised. Mr. Faucitt had never encountered an occasion he felt should pass unmarked.

A heckle came from the Marvellous Murphys—acrobats, new arrivals, and general nuisances. “Who asked you?”

Unfazed, Faucitt pressed on. “I have been asked—though others here are worthier—to propose a toast to our hostess, the radiant Miss Sally Nicholas, and her brother, our own Fillmore.”

Fillmore acknowledged this with a regal nod—the sort reserved for peasants. Sally’s fingers twitched toward an orange.

Fillmore was proof that nature distributes beauty unevenly. At seven, he’d been angelic; at twenty-five, he was a study in self-satisfaction. Wealth had inflated him, literally and figuratively.

He stood, adjusted his waistcoat, and delivered his speech:

“You don’t want a long speech. Thanks for the toast.”

He sat.

The room exhaled. Faucitt looked as though he’d been slapped with a fish.

Sally, horrified, flung herself at the old man, hugging him. “My brother,” she hissed, glaring at Fillmore, “has *not* done justice to the moment. I can’t make speeches, but—I’ll *never* forget you all, and—”

Here, she kissed Faucitt’s cheek and burst into tears.

“There, there,” Faucitt murmured, patting her. Eloquence had failed, but the sentiment? Perfect.

---

“You,” Sally informed Fillmore, “are a *pig*.”

Fillmore inflated like a startled pufferfish. “What did I *do*?”

“You *know* what. Those people adore you, and you treated them like a bad smell.”

Fillmore smoothed his waistcoat. “Sally, be reasonable. That life is *over*. I’m moving in bigger circles now. One can’t be tied to the past.”

“One *can*,” Sally shot back, “unless one’s a *snob*.”

Fillmore drew himself up. “I’m going to be a *great* man.”

“You’re going to be a *wide* man.”

Fillmore ignored this. “I’ve got *vision*. You’ll see.”

“Oh, I’ll see,” Sally said darkly. “From the visitors’ gallery at your *debtors’ prison*.”

A lean figure approached—Gerald Foster, an English writer boarding at Meecher’s. Introductions were made. Fillmore, sizing him up, decided Foster had the look of a fellow who Got Things Done.

After pleasantries, Fillmore seized the chance to escape. “You’ll excuse me. Business.”

Sally watched him go. “*Ugh.*”

Foster raised an eyebrow. “Brothers?”

“Regrettably.”

He laughed. “I’ll walk you back.”

As they strolled, Sally’s mood lifted. The party had been a triumph, Fillmore’s nonsense aside. Ahead lay France, adventure, and freedom.

Life, she decided, was *good*.

By six o'clock the next evening, Sally had reluctantly accepted that Ginger Kemp would have to navigate life without the benefit of her well-intentioned lecture. She hadn’t seen him all day, and in an hour, she’d be on the seven-fifteen express to Paris, then Cherbourg, and finally her liner back to New York.

Still, hope lingered. At half-past six, after depositing her luggage with a cheerful porter, she made one last dash to the Casino Municipale—the serious gambler’s haunt, not the breezy boule-playing cousin on the Promenade.

And there he was.

Ginger’s fiery head gleamed above a crowd huddled around a roulette table, his face the picture of canine intensity—ears practically pricked, eyes locked on the wheel. A growing pile of counters sat before him. Just as Sally stifled a laugh (earning scandalized glares from the devout gamblers), the ball clattered into red. A communal groan rose like a revivalist’s lament.

Ginger, blissfully indifferent to his shattered streak, scooped up his winnings and barreled through the crowd toward her.

“I say, this is topping,” he beamed, steering her to a quiet corner. “I’ve been looking for you everywhere.”

“Funny, so was I.”

His grin faltered when she mentioned her train. “*Leaving?*”

“Back to America. Boat sails tomorrow.”

“Oh, my aunt!” Ginger turned crimson. He stared at a uniformed attendant like a man mustering courage. Then—

“Will you marry me?”

Sally blinked. “*Marry you?*”

“You know what I mean.”

“I *do*, but—isn’t this a bit sudden?”

“It’s *got* to be sudden. I thought you’d be here for weeks!”

Sally patted his hand. “Ginger, we’re practically strangers.”

“If I look at you properly,” he muttered, still addressing the attendant, “I won’t answer for the consequences.”

She laughed. “And *this* is the man I was going to lecture on ‘Enterprise.’”

“You’re the most wonderful girl I’ve ever met,” he burst out. “I know I’m not much, but I just won a pile, and—well, with you bucking me up—”

Sally cut in gently. “I’m already engaged.”

Ginger deflated. “Oh. Right.”

She softened. “You don’t really mean it.”

“Don’t I!”

“Love at first sight isn’t—” She stopped, recalling her own whirlwind romance.

Ginger sighed. “Suppose I couldn’t expect you to care. I’m not much of a chap.”

Seizing the opening, Sally launched into her pep talk—self-reliance, seizing opportunities, defying the family’s expectations. Ginger listened raptly.

“When you talk fast,” he mused, “your nose goes all squiggly. Ripping.”

Sally groaned. “Were you even *listening*?”

“You told me to get a job.”

“Close enough.”

As the clock struck departure time, they bolted for the station. Ginger, displaying unexpected athleticism, hurled her onto the moving train.

“Don’t forget!” she yelled from the window.

“Right ho!”

“Death to the family!”

“Right ho!”

She turned—and collided with the icy glare of Bruce Carmyle, Ginger’s cousin.

Dinner was awkward. Carmyle, initially civil, soured when Sally championed Ginger’s cause.

“He was right about Scrymgeour,” she insisted.

“I prefer not to discuss family matters,” Carmyle sniffed.

“Oh, *stop* being so pompous! You’re like an 18th-century portrait—all fishy eyes and disapproval.”

Carmyle’s roast beef met a violent end.

“If my eyes are fishy,” he said stiffly, “no one’s mentioned it before.”

“No sisters, I suppose. They’d have told you.”

She left before coffee, tossing a “Thank you for dinner” over her shoulder.

Bruce Carmyle sat, indignant—and oddly fascinated.

Some days later—days in which Ginger had successfully avoided thinking about his cousin Bruce Carmyle—fate, in its usual helpful fashion, arranged for them to collide in Piccadilly. Ginger, preoccupied with his own gloomy thoughts, nearly sprinted past before Carmyle snagged him with all the charm of a tax collector.

“Just the man I wanted to see,” declared Carmyle, as if this were a delightful coincidence and not the opening move of a chess game Ginger had no interest in playing.

“Oh. Hullo,” said Ginger, with the enthusiasm of a man spotting a dentist’s appointment in his calendar.

Carmyle, inexplicably cheerful, offered a cigarette. Ginger eyed the case like it might contain a hidden subpoena. This was unprecedented. In their long and strained acquaintance, Carmyle had never once been *pleasant*. Suspicion prickled.

“Been back long?”

“A day or two.”

“Ah. I heard you were at the club. By the way, thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas.”

Ginger nearly swallowed his tongue. *“What?!”*

“In the compartment at Roville. You more or less hurled her at me. We considered it an introduction. Charming girl.”

Ginger’s jaw tightened. Of course Carmyle would think so. The man had the predatory instincts of a seagull at a picnic.

“Very pleasant conversation,” Carmyle mused. “She didn’t, by chance, give you her address?”

Ginger’s protective instincts flared. Sally’s address was his treasure, hoarded like a dragon’s gold. “Why?” he demanded.

“Oh, I promised to send her some books. American editions are lacking—”

“Everything’s published in America,” Ginger countered, stubborn as a mule.

Carmyle’s patience frayed. “Not *these* books. Just give me the address.”

Reluctantly, Ginger surrendered it, feeling vaguely like he’d handed over a state secret under duress. Carmyle jotted it down in a notebook so sleek it probably had a trust fund.

A pause. Then Carmyle, geniality evaporating now that he’d gotten what he wanted, dropped the hammer.

“Saw Uncle Donald this morning.”

Ginger’s soul withered. Uncle Donald was the human equivalent of a blocked drain—necessary in some abstract way, but universally dreaded.

“He wants you to dine tonight. Bleke’s.”

Ginger recoiled. Dinner with Uncle Donald was bad enough. Dinner at *Bleke’s*—a place so grim even the ghosts looked bored—was cruel and unusual punishment.

“Tonight?” he stalled.

“Don’t be an idiot. You’re going.”

Ginger sighed. Resistance was futile. “Right. Seven-thirty.”

They parted ways, Carmyle off to terrorize the legal world, Ginger to wander aimlessly, his thoughts circling Sally like a moth around a flame. Hopeless love had turned him into a walking thundercloud. Even minor irritations—Uncle Donald’s soup-deflecting moustache, for instance—now sparked homicidal urges.

By the time he returned to his club, Ginger was a simmering cauldron of rebellion. Dressing for dinner, he wrestled with his tie like it was a personal enemy. Then—Fate’s final nudge—a telegram arrived.

Forwarded from the *Olympic*. Five words, explosive as a grenade:

Ginger sat heavily on the bed.

Outside Bleke’s, the taxi driver eyed his fare curiously. The young man stepping out had the look of someone either about to storm a castle—or burn one down.

Sally had grand plans upon arriving in New York. She would sweep into the St. Regis, bask in the gilded excess her newfound fortune allowed, and then—after a suitably dramatic pause—settle into a cozy, tasteful apartment befitting a woman of refined independence. But as she stood on the dock, hailing a taxi, the whole scheme suddenly struck her as unbearably *Fillmorian*. No, the noble thing—the *Sally* thing—was to return, just for a little while, to the familiar shabby embrace of Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house. After all, what was home if not the place where prunes were served with alarming frequency and old friends awaited?

The city itself seemed to agree with her decision. The morning was crisp, the sky a cheerful blue, and even the ash-cans had a certain rakish charm. Streetcars rattled past, packed with merry commuters. Policemen twirled their batons like vaudeville performers. The whole metropolis appeared to be celebrating her return—though, admittedly, they might just have been enjoying the weather.

Then Mrs. Meecher delivered the first blow.

"Gone to Detroit, he has," she announced, referring to Gerald Foster. "Miss Doland, too." She paused to chastise the handyman, who was using Sally’s trunk as a battering ram against the hallway wallpaper. "That play of his opens there Monday. They’ve been rehearsing ever since you left."

Disappointment flickered, but Sally refused to let it linger. Detroit was only a train ride away. She’d go tomorrow. There was something rather nice about having a journey to look forward to.

"Oh, is Elsa in the company?"

"Sure. And very good, I hear." Mrs. Meecher, a veteran of *Florodora* (though *not*, she would have you know, one of the original Sextette), prided herself on her theatrical connections. "Mr. Faucitt saw a rehearsal. Said Miss Doland was fine. And *he’s* not easily pleased."

"How *is* Mr. Faucitt?"

Mrs. Meecher brightened—tragedy being her favorite conversational seasoning. "Poor old gentleman, not at all well. Went to bed early last night with a headache. This morning, he looked downright peaky. Could be this Spanish influenza. *Lots* of people dying of it, if you believe the papers."

Sally’s heart lurched. "Good gracious! You don’t think—?"

"Well, he ain’t turned *black*," Mrs. Meecher admitted, somewhat regretfully. "But that might come later. Doctor’s due soon. He’s quite happy, though. Toto’s sitting with him."

Sally’s concern deepened. Toto—a small, yapping insult to the canine species—was the last creature a sick man needed. She imagined the poor old man lying there, besieged by that shrill, relentless noise.

"I must go up and see him."

"Sure. You know his room. Listen—you can hear Toto talking to him now." Mrs. Meecher beamed. "He wants a cracker. Toto *always* wants a cracker after breakfast."

Sally entered to find Mr. Faucitt’s weary eyes lighting up at the sight of her.

"Sally!"

"One moment. Here, Toto!"

The dog, momentarily silenced by the prospect of food, allowed himself to be lured into the hallway. Sally tossed the cracker like a grenade, slipped back inside, and shut the door.

Mr. Faucitt exhaled as if he’d been rescued from a firing squad.

"Sally, you come, as ever, as an angel of mercy. Mrs. Meecher means well, but she labors under the delusion that that creature is a combination of sick-nurse, tonic, and a seaside holiday. He has been yapping. *Relentlessly.* Each yap was like a hammer on sheet metal. You, my dear, shine like a good deed in a naughty world. When did you return?"

"Just now. Straight from the pier."

"And you came to see me at once? I am touched. Now tell me—how was Europe?"

"Moist. *Very* moist."

Mr. Faucitt sighed wistfully. "Ah, London. Those glorious brown mists, the pavements slick with rain, the glow of the Bodega’s lamps through the fog..."

"You *miss* that?"

"Deeply. But never mind. Where did you go?"

"Paris, mostly. Then the seaside. It was heavenly. I’d have stayed forever if I could. But I wanted to see everyone—and I *had* to be here for Gerald’s play. Mrs. Meecher says you saw a rehearsal?"

Mr. Faucitt’s expression darkened. "I witnessed what was *purported* to be a rehearsal. The play itself is excellent. The acting... less so."

"Oh dear. Mrs. Meecher said you praised Elsa."

"And rightly so. She has promise. But she’s wasted in a small part. The *lead*, however, is being *murdered* by Miss Mabel Hobson."

"Murdered?" Sally’s stomach dropped. She’d warned Gerald about this.

"Face of an angel, talent of a suet pudding. I’ve seen many a wealthy man’s lady friend thrust upon the stage, but Miss Hobson is in a class of her own."

Sally bit her lip. "Poor Gerald."

Mr. Faucitt sniffed. "I shed no tears for that young man. Selfish to the core. Reminds me of an actor I once knew—Billy Fothergill. Did I ever tell you about the time Billy—?"

Sally steered the conversation elsewhere. "Have you seen Fillmore lately?"

"Funny you should ask. Ran into him on Broadway just the other day. He was surprisingly cordial—not at all his recent stiff self. I suspect his fiancée is the softening influence."

Sally stared. "*Fiancée?*"

"You didn’t know? Ah, he must have wanted to tell you in person. Yes, a Miss Winch. In the profession, I believe. Seemed a very sensible girl."

Sally shook her head. "That can’t be right. Fillmore would *never* fall for someone sensible. Was she loud? Overdressed?"

"Quiet. Neat."

"There’s been a mistake. I’ll have to investigate. Honestly, I can’t turn my back for five minutes without brothers getting entangled and friends falling ill."

A knock interrupted them. Mrs. Meecher entered with the doctor—a small, spectacled man who promptly dispatched her on a fool’s errand for a glass of water.

"Now then," he said once she was gone, "let’s have a look at you."

The diagnosis was swift. "Spanish influenza. Mild case. Bed rest, warmth, and *someone* to keep our chatty landlady at bay." He eyed Sally. "Are you a nurse?"

"Of course I am," Sally lied smoothly. "I specialize in pillow-smoothing and dog-deterrence."

The doctor nodded approvingly. "Then the patient is in excellent hands."

Mr. Faucitt protested. "Sally, you can’t waste your time—"

"Nonsense. I’ll just pop out to send a wire, and then I’ll be back."

Five minutes later, Sally was in a telegraph office, informing Gerald she wouldn’t make it to Detroit for the opening.

Sally didn’t make it to Detroit until Friday. By Saturday morning, she was rolling up to the Hotel Statler in a taxi, her spirits somewhere around her ankles. She’d spent the last week playing nurse to poor, flu-ridden Mr. Faucitt, slept fitfully on the train, and now, to top it all off, Gerald had greeted her over the phone like she was a mildly inconvenient telegram.

Not exactly the romantic reunion she’d imagined.

Coffee helped. Men, she reminded herself, were practically comatose before noon. Surely a cold shower and a proper breakfast would resurrect the Gerald she knew—the one who might actually be pleased to see her.

She was halfway through her second cup when a familiar shape lumbered into the dining room, squinting around like a shortsighted bulldog.

Sally blinked. Her brother wasn’t supposed to be west of New York, let alone prowling a Detroit hotel lobby. Yet here he was, looking like a man who’d just realized he’d left the stove on.

“Fillmore?” she called.

He jerked like a startled rabbit, spotted her, and shuffled over with the air of someone approaching a dentist’s chair.

“Sally! What—uh—what are you doing here?”

“Seeing a play. Or I *was*, until the theatres closed. What’s *your* excuse?”

Fillmore coughed. “Well, you see, I’m—er—sort of involved with the production.”

“Involved?”

“Financially.”

Sally raised an eyebrow. “Since when do *you* finance Broadway plays?”

Fillmore puffed up slightly. “Cracknell—old college pal—invited me in. Big opportunity.”

“Ah. So you’re a theatrical impresario now.”

“Well, I wouldn’t say *impresario*—”

“I would. And while we’re on the subject, when were you planning to tell me you got engaged?”

Fillmore’s face turned the shade of a ripe tomato. “You *know*?”

“Mr. Faucitt spilled the beans. Who is she?”

“Gladys Winch. She’s in the play. Only a small part, but—” His voice took on the reverence of a man describing a religious experience. “Sally, she’s a *genius*. The way she says *‘Did you ring, madam?’*—it’s *art*.”

Sally fought a smile. “I can’t wait to meet her.”

Fillmore glanced at his watch. “I’ve got to find Bunbury. The producer. He’s probably upstairs.”

“Run along, then. Don’t let me keep you from your high-powered theatrical dealings.”

Fillmore retreated, and Sally turned her attention back to her coffee, feeling marginally better. At least Gerald’s gloom made sense now—no wonder he’d been terse.

When he finally appeared, he looked like a man who’d been handed a life sentence.

“Oh, Jerry,” she said sympathetically, “I just heard about the theatres closing. What rotten luck.”

Gerald stabbed at his grapefruit. “It’s a farce. They shut down theatres but let people cram into stores like sardines. And I don’t even *believe* in this Spanish flu nonsense.”

Sally bit back a retort. Mr. Faucitt had been genuinely ill, but Gerald wasn’t in the mood for perspective.

“The company’s falling apart,” he muttered. “Rehearsing for nothing, actors going stale. And that *Hobson woman*—”

“The star?”

“If you can call her that. She couldn’t act her way out of a paper bag. Elsa Doland would’ve been ten times better, but no, Cracknell insisted on Hobson. And now she’s throwing tantrums because she thinks someone might get an extra *line*.”

Sally sipped her coffee. “Sounds like a nightmare.”

“It *is*.” Gerald scowled. “And for God’s sake, don’t mention we’re engaged. I have to keep Hobson happy, and she’s the jealous type.”

Sally’s cup clinked sharply against the saucer. “If my being engaged to you is such a *handicap*—”

“Don’t be *ridiculous*,” Gerald groaned. “It’s just—everything’s a mess, and I can’t afford any more complications.”

She exhaled. Maybe she was just tired. Maybe *he* was just tired. Maybe the world was out of joint because she hadn’t had a bath yet.

She reached across the table and squeezed his hand. “I’m sorry. I *do* understand.”

Gerald gave her a look of wounded gratitude. “You’re the only thing keeping me sane.”

By the time Sally reached the theatre, the rehearsal was already in full swing—if *chaos* could be called “full swing.”

She slipped into a back row as Elsa Doland delivered a line to a man in a bowler hat, who responded with:

From the front row, a voice bellowed:

A walking stick sailed across the auditorium.

Sally blinked. This was *not* how she imagined rehearsals.

Enter Gladys Winch.

Sally leaned forward. *So this is Fillmore’s genius.*

Gladys was square-jawed, no-nonsense, and chewing gum with the focus of a philosopher contemplating the universe.

Mr. Bunbury, the orange-haired producer, erupted.

Gladys considered this. *“Bit o’ business. Maids chew gum.”*

*“Huh. Maybe you’re right.”* She brightened. *“Hey, how about Elsa scolds me, I snap back, we build a whole comic scene—”*

Before Bunbury could combust, a vision in blue velvet stormed onstage.

“*Listen here!*” Her voice could have shattered glass. “If *anybody’s* part gets ‘written up,’ I walk. *So quick you won’t see me go!*”

A nervous young man (presumably Cracknell) fluttered after her. *“Now, sweetie—”*

*“Oh, *can* it, Reggie!”*

Gladys shrugged. *“Just a thought. Working for the good of the show.”*

Bunbury massaged his temples. *“Can we *please* get back to rehearsal?”*

They tried.

It was a disaster.

Mabel Hobson was stunning—if the role had required standing still and looking expensive. But acting? No.

Then came the paper-knife incident.

*“How can I toy with it if it’s *not there*?”*

*“It’s *not*!”*

A furious pause. Then—

From the wings, like a man walking to his execution, shuffled Fillmore.

Sally’s jaw dropped.

Not a financier. Not an impresario. Just the guy who’d misplaced the paper-knife.

Fillmore stood under the spotlight, looking like he wished the stage would swallow him whole.

Sally’s heart twinged. *Oh, Fillmore.*

All his bluster at breakfast—*“big opportunity,” “Cracknell’s pal”*—had been a smokescreen. He was broke. Again.

And now he was getting yelled at in front of everyone.

She sighed. *Time for some first aid.*

If actors were stocks, their emotional volatility would make Wall Street look stable. One moment they’re slumped in despair, the next they’re soaring on the wings of optimism—no financial advisor required. Sally found it both baffling and amusing how her simple act of funding her brother Fillmore’s theatrical ambitions had turned the entire *Primrose Way* company into a pack of over-caffeinated optimists.

The transformation was nothing short of miraculous. The assistant stage manager, formerly a creature lurking in the shadows of unpaid invoices, had overnight become Fillmore Nicholas, *Proprietor and Visionary* (as the new posters declared in aggressively large font). The company, now freed from the soul-crushing tyranny of Miss Hobson, buzzed with rumors so cheerful they could have been bottled and sold as tonic.

Fillmore, meanwhile, celebrated his managerial ascension in classic Fillmore fashion: by expanding physically. Sally could have sworn he gained five pounds the moment the contract was signed. His waistcoat strained at the buttons, his cigars multiplied like rabbits, and his opinions on lunch menus grew alarmingly specific. Standing outside the theater, arms crossed, surveying his domain, he was less a man and more a monument to ambition—one that pedestrians had to navigate around like a particularly plush traffic island.

Yet, for all the infectious joy around her, Sally couldn’t shake a quiet unease. It lingered at the edges of her thoughts, a shadow she couldn’t quite pin down—until an afternoon drive with Gerald on Belle Isle forced it into the light.

Gerald, freed from Miss Hobson’s oppressive presence, was in peak form—charming, animated, and brimming with plans. The future, in his telling, was a glittering parade of triumphs waiting just around the corner.

*“One success—that’s all I need—then watch me!”* His voice was full of fire. *“I haven’t had a chance yet, but when I do—”*

Sally stopped listening. The sun had dipped behind the trees, and a sharp wind cut through the warmth of the day. And then, like a sudden gust clearing fog, she understood.

It wasn’t just Gerald. It was *all* of them. Fillmore, Gerald, every ambitious man she knew—they treated life like a race, sprinting toward some invisible finish line, blind to everything they passed along the way. Success wasn’t just a goal; it was an obsession, a hunger that left no room for anything else.

Gerald spoke of his future in terms of *plays* and *opportunities* and *audiences.* She listened and realized, with a hollow ache, that she was an afterthought in that future—a pleasant diversion, a supporting character in the grand drama of *His Career.*

The irony wasn’t lost on her. The very play she’d saved—the one now fueling Gerald’s dreams—was about the cost of ambition. And here he was, proving its thesis without a hint of self-awareness.

The wind bit deeper. Gerald, oblivious, rattled on about New York runs and producers clamoring for his genius. Sally stared out at the grey sky, the river’s chill seeping into her bones.

*Men and their victories,* she thought. *Women and their compromises.*

The car turned back toward the city, leaving the cold behind. But the weight of the realization stayed.

When Sally boarded the train from Detroit that Saturday, her earlier worries had melted away like morning fog. She’d convinced herself she’d been silly—of *course* Gerald was pouring his energy into his work. His success was hers too, wasn’t it? And success seemed to be the word of the hour.

Detroit had adored *The Primrose Way*. The theater, miraculously allowed to open after Teddy’s prediction, had been packed. Critics raved, calling Gerald the next big thing and Elsa Doland a rising star. Even Fillmore earned a nod as a promising manager—though he sulked for a solid hour when Gladys Winch’s name was misspelled (“Wunch!” he’d fumed. “A *talent* like hers!”).

Now, as the train rattled eastward, Fillmore radiated smug satisfaction from his seat across from Sally.

“Yes, do,” Sally said suddenly.

Fillmore blinked. “Eh?”

“Buy the fur coat. It’s practically your duty now.”

A faint pink tinged his cheeks. “I wasn’t—that is—”

“Astrakhan collar, definitely. And when you roll down Fifth Avenue, I’ll point and say, ‘That’s my brother!’ ‘*Your* brother?’ ‘Yes! The great Fillmore Nicholas! And I was there when he bought that coat—’”

“Oh, shut up,” Fillmore muttered, though his lips twitched. “I was actually thinking about what Ike Schumann said. He’s on the train—wants to talk Chicago for the show.”

“We’re on first-name terms with Ike now, are we?”

“Everyone calls him Ike,” Fillmore said loftily.

“Just like everyone will soon call *you* ‘The Man in the Fur Coat.’”

Fillmore gave up and fled to the smoking car, leaving Sally to her magazine—until a polished voice broke in.

“Miss Nicholas.”

Bruce Carmyle, impeccably tailored and just as stiffly formal as she remembered, slid into the seat across from her.

Sally nearly dropped her magazine. “Mr. Carmyle! What are you doing in America?”

“Traveling,” he said, as if that explained everything. “One ought to see the world.”

Sally eyed him. “And you just *happened* to take this train?”

A flicker of discomfort crossed his face. “I—ah—inquired at your address in New York.”

“You *what*?”

“My cousin Lancelot mentioned it.”

At the name, Sally sat straighter. “How *is* Mr. Kemp?”

Carmyle’s expression darkened. “We’ve no idea. He vanished.”

“*Vanished?*”

“After insulting his uncle Donald—a man whose goodwill was *essential* to his future.” Carmyle’s jaw tightened. “The family has disowned him.”

Sally’s pulse jumped. *Ginger had done it.* He’d actually rebelled. Pride warred with guilt—had she pushed him into disaster?

Fillmore reappeared, and introductions were made. Carmyle, ever the gentleman, excused himself shortly after, leaving Sally to her thoughts—which, by the time the train reached New York, had spiraled into full-blown panic. *Had she ruined Ginger’s life?*

By morning, her imagination had conjured Ginger penniless, sleeping on the Thames Embankment, eyeing the dark waters with tragic resolve—

“Ugh!” Sally shook the image away as she reached the boardinghouse.

Mrs. Meecher greeted her with news: Mr. Faucitt had inherited money and dashed off to England. Sally’s spirits lifted briefly—until she trudged upstairs, exhausted, and collapsed onto her bed.

Then she froze.

A shoe. A *man’s* shoe. Peeking out from under her bed.

Sally bolted upright. “*Who’s under there?*”

A sneeze. A shuffle. Then, inch by inch, a familiar figure emerged—dusty, disheveled, and grinning sheepishly.

Ginger Kemp blinked up at her. “Oh, hullo!”

The moment Sally saw Ginger Kemp standing in her room—hair tousled, nose smudged with what appeared to be chimney soot, and an expression of sheepish guilt on his face—she realized two things: first, that she had been quietly panicking for hours imagining him floating facedown in the Hudson, and second, that relief was an emotion best expressed via uncontrollable laughter.

She collapsed into a chair, howling.

Ginger shifted uneasily. "I say, you know," he ventured, eyeing her as one might a suddenly hysterical stranger on a train.

Sally wiped her eyes. "Oh, I am *glad* to see you."

"No, really?" Ginger perked up. "That's fine." Then, remembering his manners: "Awfully sorry about the, er, intrusion. Thought the room was empty. Unoccupied, you know. Not the sort of chap who makes a habit of lurking under beds."

"Unless you’re decorating it as a sort of avant-garde art piece," Sally observed, "you’ve got a smudge on your nose."

Ginger recoiled. "Oh, my aunt! Not *really*?"

"Would I lie about something so vital? The mirror’s over there, if you’re brave enough."

Ginger lunged for the dressing table. "Good Lord, you’re right." He scrubbed at his face with a handkerchief. "Hair’s a bit of a disaster too."

"Like a haystack after a hurricane."

"Take my advice," Ginger said gravely. "Never nap under furniture. It’s overrated."

"Which brings me to my next question—and don’t take this the wrong way—but *why* were you under my bed?"

"Ah." Ginger frowned, as if mentally retracing his steps. "Right. Hiding from Mrs. Meecher."

"Hide-and-seek champion, are we?"

"More like fleeing from forced canine companionship. She was after me to walk that infernal woolly rat of hers, Toto. Can’t stand the creature. Looks like a mop that’s been cursed by a witch."

Sally shook her head. "You mustn’t encourage her in these whims. It sets a bad precedent."

Ginger groaned. "It’s not that simple. I’m a bit behind on rent, you see, and when you owe a landlady money, you lose the moral high ground to refuse walking her mutant lapdog."

"But you’ve only been here a week!"

"Yes, and that’s the week I’m behind on."

Sally blinked. "What happened to the fortune you won at Roville?"

Ginger scuffed his shoe against the floor. "Went back to the tables. Then America happened. Money just... evaporated."

"And why *are* you in America?"

Ginger turned an impressive shade of pink. "Opportunities, and all that."

"Found any?"

"Well, I’m a waiter. Or was. Got docked for breaking things. Apparently, ‘graceful service’ is a requirement."

Sally gasped. "*Ginger Kemp, a waiter?*"

"Not a very good one."

"This won’t do. You’re coming with me to see Fillmore."

"Fillmore?"

"My brother. He’s in theater. He’ll give you a job."

"Doing what?"

Sally waved a hand. "His right-hand man."

"Never been one of those before."

"Neither has he had one. It’ll be an adventure for you both."

They were interrupted by a yapping in the hall. Ginger froze like a rabbit spotting a fox.

"Mrs. Meecher," he whispered.

Sally rolled her eyes. "Honestly, you’d think Toto was a Bengal tiger. Come on."

She steered him past a disappointed Mrs. Meecher ("*So* sorry, we’re late for a *terribly* important meeting!"), and they burst onto the sidewalk. Ginger exhaled as if he’d just escaped a firing squad.

"You’re *wonderful*," he declared.

"I know," said Sally. "Now, tell me everything. Starting with why your uncle disowned you."

Ginger winced. "Ah. Well. There was soup involved."

"Soup?"

"Mulligatawny. And a mustache. And some strongly worded opinions."

Sally grinned. "This I *have* to hear."

As they hailed a cab, Ginger launched into his tale of family discord, while Sally plotted his professional salvation. Fillmore would *have* to hire him. If only to spare the restaurant industry.

Sally had entered what she liked to think of as her Golden Age. Peace reigned on all fronts. The sun shone (metaphorically, at least), birds chirped (she hadn’t actually heard any, but it felt like they should), and life was so thoroughly ironed out that even the most dedicated pessimist would have struggled to find a wrinkle.

True, Gerald was still in Chicago, tinkering with *The Primrose Way* and rescuing some other poor playwright’s doomed comedy. This meant his triumphant return to New York was delayed, but Sally refused to let it dampen her spirits. Everything else was far too perfect. Fillmore was thriving, Ginger was gainfully employed (or at least drawing a paycheck), she’d found an apartment, her new hat was *divine*, and *The Primrose Way* was a hit so massive that Chicago had apparently forgotten about all other earthly concerns, including politics and possibly even food.

But the crowning achievement of this blissful era was Ginger Kemp’s gainful employment. Thanks to Sally’s maneuvering, he now occupied a chair (mostly) in the outer office of *Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.* (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas, who would have made the title longer if the brass plate had allowed it). Ginger’s official duties remained mysterious, even to him. Sometimes he intercepted visitors. Sometimes he read scripts. Sometimes he just read the evening papers. But whatever he did, he was doing it for fifty dollars a week, and he was *profoundly* grateful.

So grateful, in fact, that he had appointed himself Sally’s personal furniture-hauler in her quest to furnish her new apartment.

“I mean to say,” puffed Ginger, adjusting his grip on a stubborn armchair for the fourth time in ten minutes, “after you got me that job, the least I can do is—”

“Ginger, *stop*,” Sally ordered.

“But honestly—”

“If you thank me one more time, I’ll make you carry this chair into the bathroom.”

“Right-ho,” Ginger said gamely, though his hands were beginning to resemble raw steak. “Wherever you like.”

Sally frowned at the chair, then at the room, then back at the chair. Interior design was proving more complicated than she’d anticipated.

“No, the first spot was better.”

Ginger, ever the stoic, reversed course.

“You’re an angel,” Sally said, watching him with sympathy. “I’m giving you blisters for life.”

“Nonsense!” Ginger wiped his forehead. “The way I see it—”

“If the next words are *you got me that job*, I *will* make you haul this into the kitchen.”

Ginger wisely swallowed his gratitude and staggered onward.

By the time the chair was finally deemed acceptable (back by the window, naturally), Sally was exhausted just from supervising. She collapsed into it with a sigh, surveying her domain with satisfaction. The apartment was shaping up nicely—pictures hung, carpet laid, furniture arranged (and rearranged, and rearranged again). For the first time in years, she had a place that felt like *hers*. A proper home, where she could shut the door on the world and just *be*.

Her gaze drifted to the mantelpiece. Something was missing.

“Wait a second,” she said. “Where’s that photograph of me? I *swear* I put it there yesterday.”

Ginger’s face turned an interesting shade of red. “Oh! Uh. No photograph there.”

“I *know* there isn’t. But there *was*.”

“Perhaps you forgot?”

“Maybe.” Sally narrowed her eyes. “It was a *very* flattering one. Not a bit like me, but that’s what darkroom trickery is for. I liked it because it was the version of me I’d *like* to be.”

Ginger nodded solemnly. “I’ve never had a beautiful photograph taken of myself.”

“Cheer up.”

“Oh, I don’t mind. I only meant—”

“Ginger,” Sally interrupted, “as fascinating as this conversation is, this chair is *still* not right. Back to the first position, please. And then I’ll make you tea. *If* I have any tea. Or cups. Or milk.”

“There *are* cups,” Ginger said helpfully. “I broke two yesterday. I’ll nip out for milk.”

“Yes, please *nip*. All this supervising has left me *worn out*.”

Over tea, Sally turned interrogator.

“Ginger, I don’t want to cast aspersions on your work ethic—*especially* since I nobly secured your employment—but how is it you’re always free to haul my furniture around? Shouldn’t you be at your post, doing… whatever it is you do?”

Ginger stirred his tea thoughtfully. “Well, I sort of… *mess about*.” He frowned, searching for a more professional description. “I tell people Fillmore’s out. I take names. Sometimes I read scripts.”

“Does Fillmore consult you much?”

“He lets me read plays people send in. Mostly tosh. Sometimes he sends me to vaudeville.”

“As a reward?”

“To scout acts. For his revue.”

Sally’s teacup clattered onto its saucer. “*What* revue?”

“Oh, didn’t you know? He’s planning a *whacking* big one. Going to out-Folly the Follies.”

Sally’s Golden Age wobbled. *Of course* Fillmore would do this—just when everything was going smoothly, he’d decide to leap into the theatrical stratosphere.

“Ginger, that’s *insane*.”

“Well, revues *can* make pots of money—”

“Not when you’re Fillmore!” Sally groaned. “I’ll have to talk to him.”

“Right. But, uh—” Ginger eyed the armchair warily. “Before you do… this chair *still* isn’t right, is it?”

Sally sighed. “No. It *obviously* belongs by the window.”

Ginger, ever dutiful, prepared to heave it *again*.

Sally’s suspicions about Fillmore’s sanity were confirmed when she received a telegram from Gladys Winch in Chicago:

Cryptic, yes. But ominous. Sally abandoned her decorating and marched straight to Fillmore’s office.

Ginger’s usual chair was occupied by a pimpled youth who, upon hearing her name, ushered her in with reverence. Inside, Fillmore sat behind a desk strewn with costume sketches, looking every inch the Overworked Mogul.

“Ah, Sally!” he said, in the weary tone of a man besieged by greatness. “I’m *frightfully* busy—”

“Then you’ll be *frightfully* interrupted,” Sally said. “Fill, what’s this about a revue?”

Fillmore’s expression shifted to that of a child caught with his hand in the cookie jar.

“Oh! The revue! Well, you see—”

“It’s *madness*.”

Fillmore drew himself up. “Women never understand business.”

“I understand *you*, and that’s worse. You can’t afford a massive revue right now!”

“Nonsense! Money is no object.”

“Whose money?”

Fillmore hesitated. “…Bruce Carmyle’s.”

Sally’s stomach dropped. *Bruce Carmyle.* The man was like a bad penny—always turning up.

Before she could muster a response, the door burst open. Gladys Winch stormed in, radiating exasperation.

“Fillmore, you *lunatic*,” she announced, “stop picking straws out of your hair and *listen* to me.”

Fillmore stiffened. “I *must* protest—”

“You’re *dippy*,” Gladys clarified. She turned to Sally. “Do you know what this genius has planned now? He wants to make me a *star*.”

Fillmore waved a hand. “I have used my judgment—”

“Your *what*?” Gladys snorted. “I’m sitting in Chicago, minding my own business, when Ginger Kemp shows up and tells me to hightail it back to New York because Fillmore’s decided to launch me into the theatrical stratosphere.”

Sally blinked. “*You*?”

“*Me!*” Gladys spread her hands. “I can play maids. I can say ‘Yes, madam’ with the best of them. But a *lead*? The man’s *delusional*.”

Fillmore looked wounded. “You underestimate yourself.”

Gladys kissed him on the forehead. “You’re sweet, but *no*. I’m a *cook*, not an actress. My idea of fun isn’t being mauled by critics while your bank account hemorrhages.”

Fillmore slumped. His dreams of a second overnight star were crumbling.

Gladys turned to Sally. “And now this *revue*—”

“It is *not* off!” Fillmore exploded, leaping to his feet. “I *refuse* to be hamstrung! I am a man of *vision*! I *shape* my schemes—”

And with that, he stormed out, leaving a trail of dramatic silence in his wake.

Gladys sighed. “He’ll be back soon with candy. Probably the mushy kind, even though I *keep* telling him I prefer the chewy ones.” She shook her head. “The boy’s got ambition, I’ll give him that. But someone’s got to keep him from bankrupting himself before the wedding.”

Sally laughed. “You *do* love him.”

Gladys’s freckled face softened. “Like you wouldn’t believe. He’s a chump, but he’s *my* chump.” She grinned. “And if he keeps this up, I’ll have to lock him in a cupboard until he comes to his senses.”

If you had asked Ginger Kemp to list his finest qualities, he’d have scratched his head, chewed his pencil, and maybe—after a long pause—muttered something about *meaning well*. Tact and sensitivity? Not exactly his strong suits. Yet, against all odds, he displayed a surprising amount of both by giving Sally space in the days following her heartbreak.

Ginger was no stranger to life’s *nasty jars* (as he called them), and his own coping strategy had always been to sulk in solitude until the storm passed. So, assuming Sally wanted the same, he kept his distance. By Saturday, however, he decided it was time to re-enter her orbit—preferably in style.

The weather was practically *showing off*—one of those golden Indian Summer days that made even New York’s grubby rooftops look poetic. Standing atop Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house, dodging laundry lines like an obstacle course, Ginger squinted at the distant smokestacks of the *Mauretania* gliding down the Hudson. A sentimental sight, that ship—it had brought him over from England.

Inspiration struck. A drive! A *dinner*! He’d blow his week’s salary (what remained after Mrs. Meecher’s *strategic deductions*) on an open car, whisk Sally up the Boston Post Road, and cheer her up with bad jokes and good food.

Optimism carried him all the way to Sally’s apartment building—where it promptly crashed into the immovable object of Bruce Carmyle’s back.

Ginger’s mood curdled. His cousin—*here*? In *New York*? In *this hallway*? It was like spotting a cockroach in your soup. Worse, Carmyle was deep in conversation with the janitor, and the shiny hired car idling outside suggested he’d had the *same* idea about cheering up Sally.

Carmyle turned, his expression suggesting he’d just bitten into a lemon. "Hullo."
"Hullo," Ginger echoed.

The silence that followed was thick enough to carve.

"Here to see Miss Nicholas?" Carmyle finally said.
"Yeah."
"She’s not here."

Ginger blinked. "Not here?"

"Gone to England," Carmyle announced, as if personally offended by Sally’s audacity. "Sailed this morning on the *Mauretania*."

Ginger’s stomach performed a backflip. The *Mauretania*—the very ship he’d just been admiring like some kind of sentimental idiot. He’d *watched* her leave.

Carmyle, meanwhile, was busy dismissing his hired car with the air of a man who’d just been charged for a banquet he didn’t eat. The chauffeur, a philosopher in overalls, spat thoughtfully and declared, *"You’ll have to pay just the same."*

Ginger didn’t stick around for the financial negotiations. He wandered the streets in a daze, the city suddenly drained of color. Central Park? Boring. Sidewalks? Boring. Existence? *Deeply* boring.

Then—salvation. A letter.

Not just any letter. A *Mauretania*-crested, Sally-scented lifeline. Ginger carried it to his room like a sacred relic, set the stage (pipe lit, shoes off, stolen photograph of Sally propped against the washstand), and began to read.

*I’ve run away. I’m a coward, but I couldn’t stay. New York’s full of ghosts, and I’m not brave enough to face them. Mr. Faucitt’s in England, floundering in dresses and despair (his brother left him a fashion house—long story), so I’m fleeing to help him. Distraction, Ginger. That’s the plan.*

*Elsa Doland wrote me a gushing letter. Sweet of her, considering I financed the play that made her a star. Gerald wrote too. I burned his unopened. What’s the point? Explanations don’t un-break things.*

*Here’s the ugly truth: I’m not heartbroken because I lost him. I’m heartbroken because my *pride* got trampled. I was *cocky*, Ginger. Thought I had life figured out. Now I’m just another girl who didn’t see the punch coming.*

*Don’t you dare mess up your life while I’m gone. You’re my project. Stay employed. Stay out of trouble. And if you *do* make a hash of it, picture me glaring at you from your mantelpiece.*

Ginger set the letter down, exhaled a sound that was half growl, half sigh, and briefly fantasized about throttling Gerald Foster. Then, because there was nothing else to do, he picked up the letter and read it again.

Dear Ginger,

I’m alive. Shocking, isn’t it? Three months since my last letter, so by now you’ve probably assumed I’ve either joined a nunnery or been swallowed by a particularly ambitious London fog. Neither, as it happens—though the fog did try its best.

Mr. Faucitt is still clinging to this shop like a limpet to a very expensive rock. He keeps lunching with a whiskered gentleman who looks like a villain from a melodrama, so I assume negotiations for the sale involve coded messages in the soup. Poor dear, he’s desperate to flee to the countryside and become a duck tycoon. London has betrayed him. Every time he blinks, another landmark of his youth vanishes. The Empire’s a theatre now, the Tivoli’s rubble, and his old baker’s shop is peddling frocks instead of buns. The only thing that hasn’t changed is the fog, which still rolls in like a vengeful purple ghost.

As for me, I’ve become a professional smiler-and-nodder at Laurette et Cie. (I spent my first week convinced "Cie" was some dashing Frenchman I’d have to charm. Imagine my disappointment.) My job is to flutter at customers like a helpful moth, lie about the weather, and shepherd them toward people who actually know what they’re doing. It’s blissfully mindless, which is exactly what I need.

Oh! I saw a rugby match. For *you*, Ginger. Sacrificed my ribs to a mob of howling patriots just to understand what you used to do before I civilized you. The scrum-half—a brave, doomed soul—was buried under a mountain of Welshmen. *This* was your idea of fun? You’re lucky I rescued you from such barbarism. Office life may be dull, but at least no one tackles you at the water cooler.

Spotted Bruce Carmyle lurking in Piccadilly. Didn’t wave. New York memories are still on my no-fly list.

Ginger,

I did a foolish thing. I called at your club. Asked for you like some tragic heroine in a bad play. A grandfatherly doorman humored me, and a page boy went scampering off, piping “Mister Kemp!” like a tiny herald. It was absurd and awful and made my chest hurt. Don’t reply to this. Let me babble into the void. It helps.

Mr. Faucitt has sold the shop. He’s off to commune with ducks in Gloucestershire. I’m adrift.

Ginger,

Fate has a grudge against me. I fled New York. New York sent a search party. Who should I collide with in the Strand but Fillmore—now roughly the size of a modest bungalow—and Bruce Carmyle, who apparently finds my brother *fascinating*. They dragged me to the Savoy, where Fillmore bellowed Broadway gossip at me until I was dizzy with homesickness. The entire restaurant might as well have been Times Square.

Now I’m trapped in a stately home that belongs in a poem. Monk’s Crofton is *obscene* in its beauty. Rolling lawns, deer posing artistically, a lake glittering like a misplaced ocean. There’s even a rose garden with a wall so old it probably remembers the Wars of the Roses. But the catch? *Your family is here.* All of them. In bulk.

Dinner was an inquisition. Someone mentioned you, and the room turned Arctic. I made the mistake of suggesting you weren’t *entirely* a goblin. Cue horrified silence, followed by a chorus of uncles explaining how you’re a moral sinkhole. I’ve been under polite surveillance ever since. Sending this letter will require the stealth of a spy.

Disgraced. Banished. The Family has spoken. I’ll spare you the details, but let’s just say Uncle Donald’s moustache quivered with outrage. Fillmore’s already gone, blissfully unaware.

I’m coming back to New York. Hiding here was cowardice. The ghosts will just have to deal with me.

See you soon—assuming your club hasn’t disowned you too.

—Sally

Sally sat in her New York apartment, feeling like a swimmer who had just plunged into icy water—aching but exhilarated. She had made her choice, and now she was in the thick of it, ready for whatever came next. The pain of decision was sharp, but it was better than the dull ache of indecision. She was alive, defiant, and itching for action.

Unpacking done, her next move was obvious: *Find Ginger.* She needed his steady presence like a life raft. Why hadn’t she cabled him to meet her at the dock? The echoing customs shed had been lonely, and she’d felt adrift.

A glance at her watch told her it was still early. She could catch him at the office and drag him out to lunch. Hat on, she marched out the door.

The Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. had a new gatekeeper—a pimpled, suspicious youth who eyed her like she’d come to steal the stapler.

“I’d like to see Mr. Kemp,” Sally said.

The boy scratched his cheek with a ruler. “Don’t know any Kemp.”

Sally frowned. “Red hair? Works here?”

“Nope.”

A cold suspicion settled over her. Left unsupervised, Ginger had clearly bungled things *again.* Fillmore must have fired him.

“Where’s Mr. Nicholas?”

“Gone to White Plains.”

“White Plains? Why?”

The boy perked up. “To see Bugs Butler train.”

“Who’s Bugs Butler?”

The office boy looked at her like she’d asked who the president was. “He’s fighting Lew Lucas tomorrow. And let me tell you—”

Sally held up a hand. “Never mind. How do I find my brother in White Plains?”

“Train leaves in ten minutes. Just ask for the training camp.”

Sally bolted for the door. Behind her, the boy resumed balancing his ruler on his chin, dreaming of vaudeville.

Fillmore Nicholas was trying to escape.

He’d been cornered by Lester Burrowes, Bugs Butler’s excitable manager, who had spent the last fifteen minutes explaining why his fighter was *definitely* going to destroy Lew Lucas. Fillmore, starving and desperate, had just spotted an exit when a soft voice behind him nearly sent him through the ceiling.

“Hello, Fillmore.”

He spun around, goggling. “*Sally?!*”

She looked at him calmly. “Surprise.”

“But—you—*Monk’s Crofton!*”

“I left.”

Fillmore’s brain short-circuited. “You *turned down Carmyle?!*”

Sally’s cheeks flushed. “Let’s not talk about it.”

Fillmore sputtered. “But he’s *perfect!* Rich, well-bred, sensible—”

“I don’t care if he’s the King of England. I’m not marrying him.”

Fillmore clutched his stomach. “I need food.”

“Then go eat.”

He hesitated, torn between hunger and the need to interrogate her further—but before he could choose, Lester Burrowes swooped back in, buzzing like a hyperactive hornet.

“Mr. Nicholas! You ain’t leaving? Bugs is about to spar!”

Fillmore introduced Sally weakly. Burrowes lit up. “Pleasure, miss! Now, about Bugs—he’s a *wizard*, I tell ya—”

Sally cut in. “Can he make weight without being weak?”

Burrowes froze. His mouth opened and closed like a fish.

Fillmore seized his chance and fled.

Bugs Butler swaggered into the ring, bathrobe swinging, smirk in place. He was, Sally decided, the most unpleasant man she’d ever seen—all sneer and arrogance.

Burrowes hustled over with Bugs’ sparring partner in tow—a red-haired young man currently wrestling with a sweater.

Then the sweater came off.

Sally gasped.

Ginger Kemp stared back at her, face turning as red as his hair.

Burrowes clapped his hands. “Let’s go, boys! Time’s wasting!”

Ginger fumbled with his gloves, avoiding her gaze.

Sally stood frozen, torn between laughter and outrage.

Ginger Kemp—*Fillmore’s sparring partner?!*

This was going to be interesting.

If spreading happiness is the key to personal bliss, then Bugs Butler ought to have been floating on cloud nine. After all, his spectacular knockout at the fists of Lew Lucas in Jersey City had brought joy to countless souls.

Lew Lucas himself was practically doing cartwheels. His seconds were so thrilled that one of them planted an enthusiastic kiss on his sweaty cheek. The bettors in the crowd, who’d staked their rent money on the champ, were practically throwing a parade. Even the pimpled office boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. had whooped like a wild Indian in the subway upon reading the news—his meager savings had just doubled thanks to Bugs’ glass jaw.

And Sally? Sally was sipping her morning coffee with the serene satisfaction of a woman who had just witnessed karma in action. She wasn’t one to hold grudges, but Bugs Butler had carved out a special little niche in her heart labeled *Absolute Menace*. Lew Lucas, on the other hand, was a hero. In just three rounds, he’d turned Bugs into a human punching bag before sending him to dreamland with a right hook. Sally raised her cup in a silent toast: *To Lew Lucas, and may his mother brag about him forever.*

The telephone rang.

“Hullo?”

“Oh, hullo,” said a voice.

“Ginger!” Sally’s face lit up.

“I say, I’m awfully glad you’re back. I only got your letter this morning. Popped into the boarding-house and—”

“Ginger,” Sally cut in, “your voice is lovely, but I need to *see* you. Where are you?”

“At a chemist’s across the street. I was just wondering if—”

“Get over here *immediately*.”

“I say, really? I was just about to ask.”

“You absolute *worm*, why haven’t you come sooner?”

“Well, I haven’t been out much the last day. You see—”

“I *know*,” Sally said, softening. She shot another grateful glance at Lew Lucas’s triumphant grin in the newspaper. “You poor thing. How are you?”

“Oh, right as rain, thanks.”

“Good. Now *hurry*.”

A pause.

“I say—”

“What now?”

“I’m not exactly… presentable at the moment.”

“You never were. Stop stalling and *move*.”

“But I mean—”

Sally hung up.

She waited, drumming her fingers, until footsteps finally approached her door. The bell rang. She flung it open—and nearly screamed.

“Oh, *Ginger*!”

He hadn’t lied. His right eye was unharmed, gazing at her with its usual devotion. The left, however, was buried under a swollen purple lump. His cheekbone was a canvas of bruises, and his lips looked like they’d lost a fight with a beehive.

“It’s not as bad as it looks,” he assured her.

“It’s *worse*!” She clenched her fists. “I wish Lucas had *killed* him!”

“Eh?”

“Bugs Butler! That *animal*!”

“Oh, I don’t know,” Ginger said, ever the diplomat. “He’s not a bad sort, really.”

Sally gaped. “You’re telling me you *don’t* despise him?”

“Well, I see his side of it. Bit thick, a sparring partner suddenly trying to rearrange his face. Can’t blame him for losing his temper.”

“If someone mugged you, you’d apologize for being in their way.”

“Well… his wife—”

“Larking?”

“More like I was chasing her.”

Sally’s eyebrows shot up. “*Chasing* her?”

Ginger turned a shade redder than his bruises.

“She sneaked in and grabbed something I was looking at, refused to give it back, so I… sort of went after her. Just as I caught her, Fillmore walked in.” He winced at the memory. “Not his favorite sight—me manhandling his wife. Can’t blame him, really.”

Sally folded her arms. “What did she take from you?”

“Oh, just… a thing.”

“Ginger.”

“A photograph,” he muttered.

“Of?”

“…You.”

Sally’s jaw dropped. “I never *gave* you a photo!”

“Not *gave*, exactly. More like… appropriated.”

A memory clicked. “The one that went missing when I first moved in? You *stole* it?”

Ginger studied the carpet.

“You *fraud*! You helped me *search* for it!” She shook her head, torn between outrage and admiration. “You devious little weasel.”

He brightened slightly. “Not too angry, are you?”

“Flattered, actually. So *I* ruined your job prospects *again*? First your uncle, now Fillmore. I’m your personal curse.”

“Nonsense.”

Ginger pondered. “If I had capital…”

Sally perked up. “Ah! The entrepreneur speaks. What’s your grand scheme?”

“Breeding dogs. Selling them. Winning shows. There’s money in it.”

Sally’s eyes gleamed. “Ginger, you *genius*! You’re marvelous with dogs—I’ve seen it. How much to start?”

Ginger balked. “I can’t take your money!”

“It’s not a *gift*,” Sally snapped. “It’s an *investment*. Six percent interest, take it or leave it.”

After a brief skirmish over percentages, Ginger surrendered.

“Ring the kennel man *now*,” Sally ordered. “Before someone else snatches it.”

Ginger hesitated at the door.

“You’re *sure* about this?”

“*Go*.”

Sally settled by the window, half-reading the fight report, half-watching the street below. It was a perfect morning—sunny, lively, and utterly peaceful.

Until a taxi screeched to a halt outside.

Fillmore emerged, paid the driver, then… hesitated. He took three decisive steps toward the building, then three hurried steps back. He repeated this bizarre dance twice more before finally spinning on his heel and marching away.

Sally stared. *What on earth?*

The phone rang.

“Sally?” Fillmore’s voice was strained.

“I saw your performance,” she said. “Very interpretive. What was it—*The Tango of the Deeply Confused*?”

Fillmore groaned. “Gladys is coming to see you.”

“Good! I’ve missed her.”

“She’s… upset.”

“Why?”

“She’ll explain.” A pause. “It’s bad news.”

“What news?”

Silence.

“Fillmore—”

He’d hung up.

Sally frowned, unease prickling her skin. She busied herself washing dishes until the doorbell rang again.

Gladys stood there—same solid frame, same warm smile, but her eyes were troubled.

After a quick hug, Sally cut to the chase.

“What’s the bad news?”

Gladys hesitated. “Sally… who’s this Carmyle fellow in England?”

Sally stiffened. “Fillmore told you about him?”

“Said he was rich, crazy about you, and you turned him down.”

“True.”

“Any chance you’ve changed your mind?”

Sally’s temper flared. “No. And I don’t appreciate the sales pitch.”

Gladys held up her hands. “Not pushing. Just… it’s a pity.”

“Why?”

A heavy sigh. “Because Elsa Doland’s marriage just imploded.”

Sally’s heart lurched.

“Elsa?” Her voice wavered. “To… Gerald Foster?”

“Yep. Total disaster.”

Sally’s hands clenched. “What happened?”

Gladys shook her head. “I’ll tell you in a minute. But about Carmyle—if there’s *any* doubt—”

“There isn’t.”

“Then that’s that.” Gladys’s shoulders slumped. “Just… it’s a shame.”

Sally forced a laugh. “Why do you *care* so much?”

Gladys met her eyes.

“Because you’re about to lose everything.”

Bruce Carmyle was not a man accustomed to defeat. Whether in business or matters of the heart, he had the tenacity of a bulldog with a bone and the optimism of a lottery ticket enthusiast. So when Sally Nicholson had refused his proposal at Monk’s Crofton, he hadn’t so much accepted it as filed it under *"To Be Revisited."*

She haunted him. Not in a ghostly sense—no rattling chains or ominous whispers—just the relentless, cheerful flicker of her smile in his mind, like a neon sign he couldn’t unsee. He wanted her. Or at least, he was fairly certain he did. (There had been fleeting doubts, but rejection had a funny way of polishing those right out of existence.)

Now, Bruce was many things—stubborn, proud, occasionally imperious—but subtle was not one of them. His preparations for an impromptu voyage to America were about as discreet as a fireworks display in a library. And so it was no surprise when, on the eve of his departure, his doorbell rang to announce the arrival of the Family’s official representative: Uncle Donald.

Two hundred and forty pounds of familial disapproval waddled into the room and collapsed into the nearest chair, which let out a groan of protest. Uncle Donald was a man who had spent decades cultivating the physique of a well-fed walrus, aided by a strict regimen of avoiding exercise and embracing second helpings. His walrus mustache quivered with indignation as he caught his breath.

"You," he declared, pointing an accusatory finger, "are packing."

Bruce, who had been hoping for a modicum of privacy in his scheming, sighed. "Yes."

"Going somewhere?"

"America."

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"Why?"

This exchange, though brisk on paper, was stretched to agonizing lengths by Uncle Donald’s habit of pausing between sentences to wheeze like a deflating balloon. By the time they reached the final question, Bruce’s patience was thinner than the veneer on cheap furniture.

"You’re chasing that girl," Uncle Donald pronounced, as though announcing a criminal indictment.

Bruce’s jaw tightened. For the first time in his life, he understood why his cousin Ginger had once thrown a bread roll at this man.

"Whisky?" Bruce offered, hoping to derail the interrogation.

Uncle Donald’s eyes lit up. "Gimme."

The drink was consumed with the reverence of a man who considered alcohol a vital food group. Sadly, it did nothing to soften his resolve.

"Never took you for a fool," he grunted.

"I’m not a fool."

"You are. A *damn* fool."

Bruce bristled. "Need we discuss this?"

Uncle Donald’s mustache bristled right back. "*Need* we? I climbed these blasted stairs with my weak heart for *this*. Gimme another."

Another whisky was poured. Uncle Donald sipped, sighed, and launched his assault.

"Bad business. Shocking bad. If your father were alive, he’d—what *is* this whisky?"

"O’Rafferty Special."

"New to me. Not bad. Mellow. Where’d you get it?"

"Bilby’s in Oxford Street."

"Must order some. Now, as I was saying—your father would’ve had *words*. What’s the girl’s angle, eh? After your money, plain as day."

Bruce scoffed. "If she wanted money, why refuse me at Monk’s Crofton?"

"Luring you in! Classic tactic. Girl in ’81 tried the same on me. If not for my weak heart and impeccable judgment—"

"What do you even *know* about her?" Bruce snapped.

"Enough. Travelling alone? Unseemly. American or not, no decent girl—"

"She was introduced to me by Lancelot."

Uncle Donald recoiled as if struck. "*Lancelot?* That scoundrel? That’s all the proof I need. Unpack your bags, boy. Your aunt Mary’s found a nice Bassington-Bassington girl—"

Bruce’s teeth clenched. "I sail tomorrow."

Uncle Donald swelled like an offended toad. "You owe a duty to the *Family*!"

Something in Bruce snapped. "Oh, *damn* the Family!"

Silence. Heavy, oppressive silence.

Uncle Donald rose, his expression that of a man who had just witnessed sacrilege. "After that," he intoned, "I have nothing more to say."

"Good."

"Except this." A final glare. "If you come back married to that girl, I’ll cut you in Piccadilly. Swear I will."

Bruce said nothing. The moment stretched.

Uncle Donald’s hand was on the doorknob when he paused.

"...What was that whisky called again?"

"O’Rafferty Special."

"And where’d you say you got it?"

"Bilby’s. Oxford Street."

Uncle Donald nodded solemnly. "I’ll make a note of it."

And with that, he was gone.

“After *everything* I’ve done for her,” moaned Mr. Reginald Cracknell, his voice quivering with wounded pride and his eyes suspiciously damp—likely from both heartbreak and an overindulgence in his famously overpriced liquor. “She *throws* me down. Just like that.”

Sally didn’t answer. The Flower Garden’s orchestra was currently playing at a volume that discouraged conversation, and she was too busy trying to avoid Mr. Cracknell’s flailing feet to focus on his woes. They lurched past the table where Miss Mabel Hobson, the newest “hostess” of the establishment, sat surveying the room with the air of a queen tolerating a peasant uprising. Miss Hobson was resplendent in old gold and black, and Mr. Cracknell let out a tragic gulp as they stumbled beneath her disdainful gaze.

“If I told you,” he whispered wetly into Sally’s ear, “what I’ve spent on that woman—*oof*, sorry, was that your foot?—you wouldn’t believe me. And now she won’t even speak to me. All because I *dared* to say that hat didn’t suit her. And it *didn’t*. It was a *crime* against millinery. But that,” he concluded bitterly, “is women for you.”

Sally yelped as his heel found her toe. Mr. Cracknell, mistaking this for feminist protest, hastily backtracked.

“Not *you*, of course. You’re different. Sensible. Broad-minded. That’s why I’m confiding in you. You *understand*. I got her this job—paid a *fortune*, believe me. Starred her in a show. Those pearls she wears? *I* gave her those. And now—silence. Over a *hat*.” He blinked mournfully. “What would *you* do?”

“No idea,” said Sally honestly.

“Me neither. But I’ve got a plan tonight—*shhh*.” He leaned in conspiratorially. “A little present. Having it delivered. That’ll soften her, don’t you think?”

“A bigger one might work better.”

Mr. Cracknell tripped over his own feet in horror. “You really think so? Too late now, though. Unless—no, you’re probably right. Or are you?”

“Yes,” said Sally.

“Thought so,” he sighed, as the orchestra crashed to a halt, leaving him applauding weakly at nothing. Sally escaped back to her table while Mr. Cracknell wandered off, looking like a man who had misplaced his soul.

The room was a blur of noise and heat, the air thick with perfume and cigar smoke. Sally pressed her fingers to her temples. The headache she’d been nursing all evening pulsed in time with the music.

It had been a month since she’d returned to Mr. Abrahams’ employ, and the days had blurred into a dull, exhausting haze. The Flower Garden was always packed—it was one of those places that thrived on New York’s dance craze, “large and sumptuously appointed,” as its ads claimed, with an “incomparable dance-floor” (translation: polished enough to see your own exhaustion in).

Once, she’d found it thrilling. Now, it was just loud.

“Miss Nicholas.”

Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, was back with another partner—this one a large, enthusiastic gentleman from upstate who announced, mid-stumble, that he was *very* impressed with New York’s nightlife.

“You seen this year’s *Follies*?” he asked, plowing through the crowd like a steamship through pudding.

“No.”

“You *gotta* go. Swell show. Saw another one tonight—*The Wild Rose*—total dud. People were bolting for the exits by the second act.”

Sally stiffened. *Gerald’s play.* She’d seen the name in the papers—he was on tour with it.

“That bad?” she asked faintly.

“Worse,” her partner declared. “If the author showed his face, they’d tar and feather him.”

Somehow, she made it back to her table.

“Miss Nicholas.”

This time, it wasn’t Schoenstein. The voice was crisp, British, and jarringly familiar. She turned.

Bruce Carmyle sat beside her.

“I called at your apartment,” he said, lighting a cigarette with deliberate calm. “The porter said you were here, so I took the liberty.” His fingers trembled slightly, but his tone was steady. “Mind if I smoke?”

Sally stared at him. He looked the same as ever—dark, polished, faintly intimidating. But for the first time, his presence didn’t make her bristle. Maybe because, compared to the Flower Garden’s usual chaos, he seemed almost *restful*.

“You just landed?” she asked.

“Tonight.”

She blinked. “You came *straight* here?”

He exhaled smoke, watching her. “Yes.”

The unspoken challenge hung between them. Sally felt too tired to dodge it. The room was stifling, the music a relentless assault. A bird in one of the gilded cages overhead began to sing—a small, sweet sound fighting against the noise.

And suddenly, absurdly, she thought of Monk’s Crofton—cool green lawns, quiet, *peace*...

His hand closed over hers.

“Sally,” he murmured, low and urgent.

She didn’t pull away.

Bruce Carmyle was, for once, at a loss.

This was *not* how proposals were supposed to go. Where was the moonlight? The soft music? The poetic murmurs? Instead, they were drowning in a brass-heavy rendition of some Broadway rag, while two waiters behind them debated—*loudly*—what sounded like the entire menu in fractured Italian.

He cleared his throat. “Deuced noisy in here.”

“Yes.”

“Always like this?”

“Always.”

This was *not* the tender post-proposal dialogue he’d envisioned. Where were the whispered endearments? The lingering glances?

A waiter bellowed something about *ravioli hollandaise*. Bruce gritted his teeth.

“Do you come here often?” he tried.

“I work here.”

That stopped him cold.

“...Work here?”

“As a dancer.”

A *dancer*. The word hit him like a bucket of ice water. His mind raced—Uncle Donald’s disapproving ghost loomed large. A *professional* dancer. In a *Broadway club*.

But then Sally turned, and her eyes were clear and steady. No, he told himself fiercely. She wasn’t *that* kind of girl. She was—

A sudden yapping erupted from across the room. A redheaded man was presenting Mr. Cracknell with a basket, from which emerged an indignant Pekingese puppy. Cracknell, seizing his chance, hurried to Miss Hobson’s table. The puppy was accepted. Romance was rekindled.

Bruce Carmyle did not notice.

“A *dancer*?” he repeated, his voice strained.

Sally met his gaze. “Yes.”

For a long moment, he wavered. Then—slowly—he exhaled.

No. She wasn’t like the others. She was *Sally*.

And that, somehow, was enough.

Sally’s pulse, which had given a frantic leap at the sound of his voice, settled back into its usual rhythm. Oddly enough, she felt perfectly calm. She’d imagined this moment would leave her breathless, panicked—but now that it was here, it barely stirred her. The night’s events had drained her of any capacity for drama.

“Hullo, Sally!” slurred Gerald.

He swayed in the doorway, shirt rumpled, collar missing, wearing the unmistakable aura of a man who had spent the evening wrestling with bad decisions and losing spectacularly. His face was puffy, his smile weak and unfocused.

Sally said nothing. She just looked at him, cool and detached, as if he were a mildly interesting specimen under glass.

“Hullo!” he tried again.

“What do you want?”

“Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I’d come in.”

“What do you want?”

Gerald’s pathetic grin dissolved. A tear rolled down his cheek with the dramatic flair of a silent film star. “S-Sally… I’m *very* miserable.” He fumbled over the words like a man trying to navigate an icy sidewalk in dress shoes.

It struck Sally then—this was Reginald Cracknell all over again. A tragic figure, yes, but mostly just *tiresome*.

“You should go to bed, Gerald,” she said, her voice steady.

“What’s the use? Can’t sleep. No good. *Couldn’t* sleep.” He swayed, gripping the doorframe for balance. “Sally, you don’t know how worried I am. I see what a fool I’ve been.”

She braced herself for an apology—some maudlin lament about how he’d treated her. But Gerald, ever the egoist, had other regrets.

“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he moaned. “Got a winner first time, but can’t repeat. No good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Now I’ll have to go back to it. *Damn it.*” Another tear escaped. “Very miserable,” he concluded, as if summing up his life in two words.

Sally’s patience, already threadbare, snapped. “Oh, go to bed,” she said sharply.

Gerald blinked, wounded. “You’re very unsymp… unsympathetic.”

“I’m sorry,” said Sally, not sorry at all. She marched to the door and gave it a firm push. Gerald, still clinging to the handle like a shipwrecked sailor to driftwood, stumbled into the hallway. After a moment’s wobbling deliberation, he lurched toward his own door and vanished inside.

Sally retreated to her bedroom, fully intending to collapse into sleep—only to find herself too restless to close her eyes. She threw on a kimono and stalked into the kitchen, hunting for milk. (Regrettably, she’d given the last of it to a cat that morning—a cat who had, in hindsight, shown far more gratitude than certain humans.)

Then—*crash*.

A fusillade of shattering noises erupted from across the hall. Sally froze. A second barrage followed, louder this time, as if Gerald had declared war on his own furniture.

Panic flickered. Had he taken his maudlin dramatics to a dangerous extreme? She bolted for the door.

Gerald answered her frantic knocking with the same wobbly smile. “Hullo, Sally!”

Her alarm evaporated, replaced by irritation. He was *fine*. Just drunk and destructive.

“Noise?” He considered this with the grave intensity of a philosopher pondering the universe.

“Cleaning house,” he announced, as if this explained everything.

Sally pushed past him—and stopped dead.

The sitting room looked like the aftermath of a cyclone with a grudge. Every fragile ornament Elsa had ever collected lay in ruins. A niblick (why a niblick? Who kept a niblick in an apartment?) rested in the corner like the murder weapon in a detective novel.

Gerald, meanwhile, had collapsed onto a small table—which promptly gave way beneath him with a splintering *crack*.

And suddenly, absurdly, Sally *laughed*.

It was all so ridiculous—the wreckage, Gerald’s tragic face, the sheer *overkill* of it. The laughter bubbled up, unstoppable, as if years of tension were finally breaking free.

Gerald scowled. “No sympathy.”

“I can’t help it,” Sally gasped. “It’s *too* funny.”

“Not funny,” he corrected, swaying slightly.

She wiped her eyes and got to work. Half an hour later, the worst of the wreckage was swept into sad little piles. Exhausted, she slumped in a chair by the window—and was asleep before her head touched the cushion.

Dawn woke her. The room was bright, the city outside alive with noise. She stretched, stiff and groggy, then crossed the hall to her own apartment.

Gerald was there, sprawled in a chair, snoring.

She studied him—the slack mouth, the unshaven jaw—and felt nothing but weary detachment. Whatever spell he’d once cast over her was well and truly broken.

“Oh, *God*.”

“You’d better go wash up,” she suggested.

“So,” he said, the word dripping with frost.

“There’s no explanation necessary.”

“Good-bye.”

“Good-bye.”

Five minutes later, Ginger Kemp was singing in his shower, loud and off-key, and Sally was smiling for the first time in what felt like years.

The evening had the audacity to begin creeping in, as if apologizing for interrupting such a flawless summer day. A reluctant blush still lingered in the west, and a smug new moon hung over the barn like a freshly polished scythe.
