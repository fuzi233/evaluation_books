Twelve years had elapsed since I interred the remains of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, within the silent, sun-drenched mausoleum of the old Richmond cemetery. In those quiet intervals, I had frequently dissected the peculiar testamentary instructions he left behind regarding his final resting place: specifically, the mandate that he lie exposed in an open casket, and the intricate mechanical contrivance designed so that the massive bolts sealing the vault's colossal door could be manipulated solely from the interior—a paradoxical safeguard suggesting a consciousness that refused total oblivion.

Twelve years had also passed since I committed to paper the extraordinary manuscript of this enigmatic figure, a man whose biography defied linear chronology. He possessed no recollection of infancy, offering only a vague estimation of his age, yet he carried the vitality of youth while having once cradled my grandfather's great-grandfather upon his knee. This warrior had spent a decade traversing the alien landscapes of Mars, engaging in a dualistic struggle against both the verdant Martians and the crimson-skinned Tharks. Through these conflicts, he had secured the hand of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and for nearly a decade had reigned as a prince within the House of Tardos Mors, the Jeddak of Helium itself.

Now, twelve years after his body was discovered upon the bluff overlooking the Hudson, a shadow of uncertainty lingered in my mind. Had John Carter truly perished, or did he continue to wander the desiccated seabeds of that dying world? Had he returned to Barsoom only to discover that he arrived too late, finding the frowning portals of the atmospheric plant already sealed, condemning countless millions to suffocation on that catastrophic day that hurled him ruthlessly through forty-eight million miles of space back to Earth? Perhaps he sought his dark-haired Princess and the slender son he had dreamt awaited him in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors, only to find a living death upon a dead world. Was he truly gone forever, severed from both Mother Earth and his beloved Mars?

Thus lost in these futile speculations on a sultry August evening, old Ben, my body servant, approached with a telegram. My fingers trembled as I tore it open. The message was stark: *'Meet me tomorrow, Hotel Raleigh, Richmond.'* Signed simply, *JOHN CARTER*.

Early the following dawn, I boarded the first train for Richmond. Within two hours, I was ushered into the room occupied by the man I thought dead. As I crossed the threshold, he rose to greet me, his face illuminated by an old-time cordiality that seemed to banish the shadows of mortality. He appeared untouched by the passage of time, retaining the straight, clean-limbed physique of a fighter in his prime. His keen grey eyes burned with undimmed intensity; the only markings upon his visage were the etched lines of iron character and unyielding determination that had defined him since I first knew him nearly thirty-five years prior.

"Well, nephew," he greeted me, his voice resonating with familiar warmth, "do you feel as though you are witnessing a specter, or merely suffering the lingering effects of too many of Uncle Ben's juleps?"

"Juleps, I reckon," I replied, steadying my breathing, "for I certainly feel mighty good; but perhaps it is merely the sight of you again that stirs such emotion. Have you returned to Mars? Tell me. And Dejah Thoris? Did you find her well, awaiting your embrace?"

"Yes, I have returned to Barsoom again," he answered, a somber weight settling in his tone. "But it is a tale too vast to recount within the fleeting moments I possess before I must depart. I have uncovered the secret, nephew, and I may now traverse the trackless void at my will, moving freely between the countless planets as I choose. Yet, my heart remains anchored in Barsoom. While it resides there, in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt I shall ever again abandon the dying world that constitutes my very life."

"I have come now because my affection for you compelled me to see you once more before you pass forever into that other life which I shall never know," he continued, his gaze piercing. "And though I have died thrice and shall die again tonight—as you know death better than any mortal—I remain as unable to fathom its mechanics as you are. Even the wise and mysterious Therns of Barsoom, that ancient cult credited with guarding the secrets of life and death within their impregnable fastnesses upon the hither slopes of the Mountains of Otz, are as ignorant as we. I have proven this truth, though I nearly forfeited my life in the process. You shall read everything in the notes I have compiled during the last three months of my return to Earth."

He gestured to a swelling portfolio lying on the table beside him. "I know you are intrigued and convinced, and I know the world shares this interest, though they will not believe for many years—perhaps many ages, for they lack the capacity to comprehend. Men of Earth have not yet evolved to a point where they can grasp the truths written in those notes."

"Give them what you deem fit, what you believe will not harm them," he instructed gently. "But do not feel aggrieved if they mock you."

That night, I walked down to the cemetery with him. At the threshold of his vault, he paused and pressed my hand firmly. "Good-bye, nephew," he said softly. "I may never see you again, for I doubt I can bear to leave my wife and boy while they live. Furthermore, the span of life upon Barsoom often exceeds a thousand years."

He stepped inside. The great door swung slowly shut, the ponderous bolts grating into place with a sound like grinding bones, until the lock clicked with finality. I have never seen Captain John Carter of Virginia since.

Yet, here is the account of his return to Mars on that subsequent occasion, gleaned from the immense mass of notes he left upon the table of his room in the Hotel Raleigh. Much has been omitted; much I have not dared to reveal. However, you will find the story of his second quest for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, even more remarkable than the first manuscript I presented to an unbelieving world some time ago, through which we followed the fighting Virginian across the dead sea bottoms beneath the moons of Mars.

Upon the precipice of my exile, I stood alone against the chill of a March night in eighteen-hundred-and-eighty-six, the Hudson River sprawling below like a spectral corpse, grey and silent, severed from the pulse of life. Yet, even here, amidst the desolation of terrestrial winter, the magnetic pull of Mars—the Red God of War, my beloved sovereign—constricted my soul with a familiar, terrible intimacy. For ten long years, I had stretched my arms toward the void, imploring the crimson star to reclaim me, to drag me back to the woman whose loss had hollowed my existence.

It was a sensation identical to that other March night in eighteen-hundred-and-sixty-six, when I had knelt before the Arizona cave, my body wrapped in the shroud of simulated death, feeling the irresistible, gravitational tug of the deity of my profession. With arms flung wide toward the burning eye of the Great Star, I invoked the return of that supernatural potency which had twice before torn me through the infinite abyss of space. I prayed as I had prayed a thousand times during the decade of my waiting, my lips moving in a silent liturgy of desperation.

Then, a wave of nausea, visceral and crushing, seized me. My vision blurred into a kaleidoscope of gray, my knees buckled, and I pitched forward, crashing onto the very edge of the dizzying bluff.

Instantly, clarity returned, slicing through the fog like a laser. The horrific tableau of the Arizona cave flooded my memory with renewed, agonizing vividness. Just as on that distant night, my muscles betrayed my will, paralyzed by a reflexive terror. Even upon the banks of the placid Hudson, I could hear the phantom moans and the rustling of the fearsome entity that had lurked in the cave's dark recesses. Summoning a strength that defied mortal limits, I fought the bonds of this strange anesthesia. Again, the sharp *click* resonated—a sound like a taut wire snapping—and I stood naked and free, trembling beside the lifeless husk of the thing that had recently pulsed with the warm, red blood of John Carter.

With barely a glance backward, I turned my gaze to Mars, lifting my hands to catch his lurid rays, and waited.

I did not have to wait long. Scarcely had I turned when I was hurled with the velocity of thought into the awful void before me. There followed that same instant of unthinkable cold and absolute darkness I had endured twenty years prior, and then, abruptly, I opened my eyes in a new world, bathed in the searing heat of a sun that poured through a tiny aperture in the canopy of a mighty forest.

The scene that greeted me was so profoundly non-Martian that my heart hammered against my ribs, a sudden, primal fear seizing me that I had been aimlessly cast upon some alien sphere by a cruel fate. Why not? What compass had guided me through the trackless wastes of interplanetary space? Was there no guarantee that I might not have been hurled to some distant star of another solar system rather than to Mars?

I lay upon a carpet of close-cropped, red grass-like vegetation, surrounded by a grove of strange and breathtaking trees, draped in huge, gorgeous blossoms and teeming with brilliant, voiceless avians. I termed them birds because they possessed wings, though no mortal eye had ever rested upon such odd, unearthly forms. The flora bore a resemblance to the lawns of the great waterways of the red Martians, yet the trees and birds were unlike anything I had witnessed on Barsoom. Through the deeper layers of the woods, I glimpsed the most un-Martian sight of all: an open sea, its azure waters shimmering beneath the brazen glare of the sun.

As I attempted to rise to investigate, I suffered the same ridiculous catastrophe that had plagued my first attempt to walk under Martian conditions. The lesser gravitational attraction of this smaller planet, combined with the rarified density of its atmosphere, offered negligible resistance to my earthly musculature. The mere exertion of rising sent me soaring several feet into the air before I crashed face-first into the soft, brilliant grass of this strange world.

Yet, this stumble provided a sliver of reassurance. Perhaps I was indeed in an unknown corner of Mars, a possibility heightened by the fact that during my ten-year residency, I had explored but a fraction of the planet's vast expanse. Rising again, laughing at my own forgetfulness, I soon mastered the art of attuning my terrestrial sinews to these altered physical laws.

Walking slowly down the imperceptible slope toward the sea, I could not help but observe the park-like perfection of the landscape. The grass was clipped to a uniform, carpet-like precision reminiscent of an ancient English lawn, while the trees displayed evidence of meticulous pruning, capped at a consistent height of approximately fifteen feet. From a distance, the forest resembled a vast, high-ceilinged chamber, a testament to systematic cultivation. These signs convinced me that I had entered Mars through the domain of a civilized people, and that upon finding them, my rank as a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors would ensure me courtesy and protection.

My admiration for the forest deepened as I proceeded. Their colossal stems, some reaching a hundred feet in diameter, attested to prodigious heights I could only guess at, blocked from view by dense foliage that rose no higher than sixty or eighty feet. As far as my sight could penetrate, the trunks and branches were smooth and highly polished, akin to the finest American pianos. Some timber glowed black as ebony; others gleamed white as fine china; still others burned in hues of azure, scarlet, yellow, or deepest purple. The foliage matched this chromatic riot, and the blooms clustering thick upon the branches defied description in any earthly tongue, challenging the very lexicon of the gods.

As I neared the forest's confines, a broad expanse of meadowland revealed itself between the grove and the open sea. Emerging from the shadows, a sight banished all romantic reflection upon the landscape's beauty. To my left, the sea stretched to the horizon; ahead, a vague, dim line marked its further shore; and to my right, a mighty river, broad, placid, and majestic, flowed between scarlet banks to empty into the quiet sea. At a distance upriver, perpendicular bluffs rose from the very base of the water, appearing as if the river itself erupted from the cliff's foundation.

Yet, it was not these inspiring evidences of Nature's grandeur that captured my immediate attention. It was the sight of a dozen figures moving slowly along the meadow near the riverbank. Odd, grotesque shapes they were; unlike anything I had ever seen on Mars, yet, from afar, possessing a disturbingly human silhouette. The larger specimens stood ten or twelve feet tall, proportioned in torso and lower extremities precisely as earthly man. However, their arms were abominably short, resembling elephant trunks, moving in sinuous, snakelike undulations devoid of visible bone structure, suggesting a vertebral composition hidden beneath skin.

Watching from behind the stem of a giant tree, one of the creatures advanced, engaged in the principal occupation of its kind: running its oddly shaped hands over the surface of the sward, the purpose of which remained inscrutable. As it drew closer, I obtained a perfect view, and though I would later learn more of its kin, I can say that this single cursory examination of this awful travesty of Nature would have been sufficient to drive any sane being mad. The fastest flyer of the Heliumetic Navy could not have carried me far enough from this hideous monstrosity.

The blacks were massed at the entrance to a great chamber, attempting to block the further progress of a body of red men toward the inner sacred precincts of the temple.

Coming from within as I did, I found myself behind the blacks. Without waiting to even calculate their numbers or the foolhardiness of my venture, I charged swiftly across the chamber and fell upon them from the rear with my keen long-sword.

As I struck the first blow, I cried aloud, "For Helium!" And then I rained cut after cut upon the surprised warriors, while the reds without took heart at the sound of my voice. With shouts of "John Carter! John Carter!" they redoubled their efforts so effectively that before the blacks could recover from their temporary demoralization, their ranks were broken and the red men had burst into the chamber.

The fight within that room, had it had but a competent chronicler, would go down in the annals of Barsoom as a historic memorial to the grim ferocity of her warlike people. Five hundred men fought there that day, the black men against the red. No man asked quarter or gave it. As though by common assent, they fought, as though to determine once and for all their right to live, in accordance with the law of the survival of the fittest.

I think we all knew that upon the outcome of this battle would hinge forever the relative positions of these two races upon Barsoom. It was a battle between the old and the new, but not for once did I question the outcome of it. With Carthoris at my side, I fought for the red men of Barsoom and for their total emancipation from the throttling bondage of a hideous superstition.

Back and forth across the room we surged, until the floor was ankle-deep in blood, and dead men lay so thickly there that half the time we stood upon their bodies as we fought. As we swung toward the great windows which overlooked the gardens of Issus, a sight met my gaze which sent a wave of exultation over me.

"Look!" I cried. "Men of the First Born, look!"

For an instant the fighting ceased, and with one accord every eye turned in the direction I had indicated, and the sight they saw was one no man of the First Born had ever imagined could be.

Across the gardens, from side to side, stood a wavering line of black warriors, while beyond them and forcing them ever back was a great horde of green warriors astride their mighty thoats. And as we watched, one, fiercer and more grimly terrible than his fellows, rode forward from the rear, and as he came he shouted some fierce command to his terrible legion.

It was Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, and as he couched his great forty-foot metal-shod lance, we saw his warriors do likewise. Then it was that we interpreted his command. Twenty yards now separated the green men from the black line. Another word from the great Thark, and with a wild and terrifying battle-cry, the green warriors charged. For a moment the black line held, but only for a moment—then the fearsome beasts that bore equally terrible riders passed completely through it.

After them came utan upon utan of red men. The green horde broke to surround the temple. The red men charged for the interior, and then we turned to continue our interrupted battle; but our foes had vanished.

My first thought was of Dejah Thoris. Calling to Carthoris that I had found his mother, I started on a run toward the chamber where I had left her, with my boy close beside me. After us came those of our little force who had survived the bloody conflict.

The moment I entered the room, I saw that someone had been there since I had left. A silk lay upon the floor. It had not been there before. There were also a dagger and several metal ornaments strewn about as though torn from their wearer in a struggle. But worst of all, the door leading to the pits where I had hidden my Princess was ajar.

With a bound, I was before it, and, thrusting it open, rushed within. Dejah Thoris had vanished. I called her name aloud again and again, but there was no response. I think in that instant I hovered upon the verge of insanity. I do not recall what I said or did, but I know that for an instant I was seized with the rage of a maniac.

"Issus!" I cried. "Issus! Where is Issus? Search the temple for her, but let no man harm her but John Carter. Carthoris, where are the apartments of Issus?"
