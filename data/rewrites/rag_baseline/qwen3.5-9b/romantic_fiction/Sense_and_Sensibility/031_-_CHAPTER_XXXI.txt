The morning sun filtered through the curtains, yet Marianne awoke to a darkness that had settled deep within her soul. The night had offered her more rest than she dared expect, but the quality of that sleep was irrelevant; the consciousness of misery remained, sharp and unyielding, exactly as it had been when she closed her eyes.

Elinor, observing her sister's state, endeavored to draw her out, encouraging her to articulate the turmoil consuming her. Before breakfast was served, they had circled the tragedy of Willoughby's departure repeatedly. Elinor offered steady conviction and affectionate counsel, a constant anchor in the storm, while Marianne oscillated between impetuous despair and fleeting rationalizations. One moment, she could envision Willoughby as a victim of circumstance, as innocent and unfortunate as herself; the next, she found herself stripped of all consolation by the sheer impossibility of his innocence. Her emotional landscape shifted violently: sometimes she was utterly indifferent to the prying eyes of the world, at other times she sought total isolation, and occasionally she possessed the energy to resist scrutiny with fierce determination. Yet, in one crucial aspect, she remained consistent: whenever possible, she avoided the presence of Mrs. Jennings, and when avoidance was impossible, she maintained a rigid, defensive silence. Her heart had calcified against the possibility that Mrs. Jennings could comprehend her sorrow with anything resembling genuine compassion.

"No, no, no," Marianne cried, her voice trembling with suppressed rage. "She cannot feel. Her kindness is merely a performance; her good-nature is not tenderness. All she desires is gossip, and she clings to me solely because I provide her with a spectacle."

Elinor had long anticipated this distortion in her sister's judgment. She understood that Marianne, possessing excellent abilities and a noble disposition, was nonetheless plagued by an irritable refinement of mind and an inflated sense of the delicacy required by high sensibility. Like many of the clever and good, Marianne was neither reasonable nor candid. She projected her own internal compass onto others, expecting identical opinions and feelings, and she judged the motives of the world strictly by the immediate impact their actions had upon her own fragile psyche.

This psychological rigidity manifested clearly later that morning. While the sisters sat together in their private chamber after breakfast, a specific incident occurred that drove Marianne's contempt for Mrs. Jennings to new depths. Through her own weakness, Mrs. Jennings had inadvertently become a source of fresh anguish for Marianne, despite acting with the purest intentions.

Mrs. Jennings entered the room holding a letter, her countenance bright with the false assurance that she was delivering solace.

"Now, my dear," she announced, her smile radiant, "I bring you something I am certain will do you good."

Marianne heard the trap spring shut. In a single heartbeat, her imagination conjured a vision of redemption: a letter from Willoughby, brimming with tenderness and contrition, explaining every ambiguity, offering satisfaction and proof of his fidelity. Instantly following this epistolary salvation, she pictured Willoughby himself bursting into the room, eager to reinforce the written words with the eloquent pleading of his gaze. The entire edifice of her shattered hopes collapsed in the next moment. Instead of Willoughby, the handwriting of her mother appeared before her.

Until that instant, her mother's letters had been unwelcome intrusions; now, in the acuity of her disappointment, they became instruments of torture. The contrast between the ecstasy of her fabricated hope and the harsh reality of her mother's letter made her feel as though she had never truly suffered until this exact second.

The cruelty of Mrs. Jennings was such that no language Marianne could muster in her most eloquent moments could adequately express it. She could offer only a torrent of tears, streaming from her eyes with violent passion—a reproach so completely lost on its recipient that Mrs. Jennings, after expressing pity, withdrew, still urging Marianne to read the letter of comfort. When Marianne finally calmed enough to examine the document, it offered little solace. Every page was dominated by Willoughby's name. Her mother, still firmly convinced of their engagement and relying with undiminished warmth on his constancy, had only responded to Elinor's inquiries by imploring Marianne to be more open toward them both. Written with such apparent tenderness, such feigned affection for Willoughby, and such a naive conviction of their shared future happiness, the letter induced agony rather than peace.

An overwhelming urge to return home seized Marianne anew. Her mother, paradoxically, became dearer to her than ever before, precisely because of the excess of her misplaced confidence in Willoughby. Marianne was wild with urgency to depart. Elinor, equally torn between the merits of staying in London versus returning to Barton, offered no personal counsel save for patience until their mother's instructions arrived. Eventually, she secured her sister's agreement to await further news.

Mrs. Jennings departed earlier than usual, unable to find peace until the Middletons and Palmers could mourn as deeply as she did. Refusing Elinor's offer of company, she left alone to spend the remainder of the morning elsewhere. Elinor, burdened by a heavy heart and acutely aware of the pain she was about to inflict, sat down to write to her mother, detailing the events and begging for direction. Meanwhile, Marianne, having joined them in the drawing-room upon Mrs. Jennings' exit, remained fixed at the table where Elinor wrote. She watched the progress of her sister's pen, grieving for the hardship of the task, and grieving even more profoundly for the effect these words would inevitably have on their mother.

For approximately fifteen minutes, they existed in this suspended state of quiet tension, when a sudden rap at the door shattered the atmosphere. Marianne, whose nerves were currently too frayed to withstand abrupt noises, jumped.

"Who can this be?" Elinor exclaimed, startled. "So early! I thought we had finally found safety."

Marianne moved to the window, peering out with visible irritation.

"It is Colonel Brandon!" she declared, her voice laced with vexation. "We are never safe from *him*."

"He will not enter, as Mrs. Jennings is absent," Elinor reasoned.

"I will not trust in that assumption," Marianne retorted, retreating to her own room. "A man who possesses nothing to occupy his own time demonstrates no conscience regarding his intrusion upon the privacy of others."

Fate, however, proved her conjecture correct, albeit based on a profound misunderstanding. Colonel Brandon *did* enter. Elinor, convinced that his presence was driven by solicitude for Marianne—and seeing that genuine concern etched into his disturbed, melancholy features and his anxious, brief inquiry after her well-being—could not forgive her sister for dismissing him so lightly.

"I encountered Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street," Brandon began after the initial greetings, "and she encouraged me to come. I was readily persuaded, moreover, because I believed it probable that I might find you alone, which was my primary desire. My object—my wish, my sole wish in seeking this audience—I hope, I believe it is—to serve as a means of providing comfort. No, I must correct myself: not present comfort, but conviction, lasting conviction for your sister's mind. My regard for her, for yourself, and for your mother... will you permit me to demonstrate this by relating certain circumstances? Nothing but a very sincere regard, nothing but an earnest desire to be useful, I think justifies me. Though, considering the many hours I have spent convincing myself of my own correctness, is there not some reason to fear I may be wrong?" He paused, searching for the right footing.

"I understand," Elinor replied, her tone shifting from defensiveness to desperate curiosity. "You have something to tell me concerning Mr. Willoughby that will illuminate his character further. Your disclosure would constitute the greatest act of friendship one could show Marianne. My gratitude would be immediate upon hearing any information tending toward that end, and hers must eventually follow. Pray, pray, let me hear it."

"You shall; and, to be brief, when I left Barton last October..." He hesitated, realizing the complexity of the tale. "But this will give you no idea. I must go further back. You will find me a very awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood; I hardly know where to commence. A short account of myself, I believe, is necessary, and it *shall* be concise. On such a subject," he sighed heavily, "can I have little temptation to be diffuse."

He paused for a moment to gather his thoughts, then, with another sigh, resumed his narrative.

"You have undoubtedly entirely forgotten a conversation—"he glanced at her, noting the futility of expecting memory—"between us one evening at Barton Park. It was the evening of a dance, in which I alluded to a lady I had once known, remarking that she resembled, in some measure, your sister Marianne."

"Indeed," Elinor answered, surprised. "I have *not* forgotten it."
