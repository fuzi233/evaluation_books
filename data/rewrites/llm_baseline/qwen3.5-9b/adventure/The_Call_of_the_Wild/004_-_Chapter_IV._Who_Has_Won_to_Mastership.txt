"Eh? Wot I say? I spik true w'en I say dat Buck two devils."

It was François's voice the following morning, sharp with disbelief as he discovered Spitz vanished and Buck covered in fresh wounds. He dragged the massive dog to the warmth of the fire, pointing out the gashes by the flickering light. "Dat Spitz fight lak hell," Perrault observed, inspecting the deep tears and cuts. "An' dat Buck fight lak two hells," François replied grimly. "An' now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure."

While Perrault packed the gear and loaded the sled, François began harnessing the team. Buck trotted instinctively to the spot where Spitz had led, but François, unaware of the shift in dynamics, guided Sol-leks into the coveted lead position. In his expert judgment, Sol-leks was the finest remaining candidate. Instantly, Buck sprang upon Sol-leks in a frenzy, driving him backward and seizing the lead for himself.

"Eh? eh?" François cried, slapping his thighs in delight. "Look at dat Buck. Heem keel dat Spitz, heem t'ink to take de job."

"Go 'way, Chook!" François ordered, but Buck refused to move. Seizing the dog by the scruff of the neck, François dragged him aside and shoved Sol-leks back into place. The older dog hesitated, clearly intimidated by Buck's presence. François was stubborn, yet when he turned his back, Buck instantly reclaimed the lead, displacing Sol-leks, who was far too willing to yield. Angered, François returned with a heavy club.

"Now, by Gar, I feex you!" he roared.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater and retreated slowly. He did not charge when Sol-leks was restored, but he circled just beyond the range of the club, snarling with bitter rage. Having learned the danger of clubs, he watched François warily, ready to dodge any throw. The driver resumed his work, calling for Buck to return to his usual spot beside Dave. Buck stepped back twice; François advanced, and Buck retreated again. Finally, François tossed down the club, assuming the dog feared a beating.

But Buck was not in open revolt to avoid punishment; he demanded leadership. It was his by right, earned through victory, and he would accept nothing less. Perrault joined in, and together they herded Buck around the camp for nearly an hour, hurling clubs and unleashing curses upon him and his lineage. Buck dodged every blow, answering only with snarls, refusing to flee but also refusing to submit until his demand was acknowledged.

François scratched his head in frustration. Perrault checked his watch and swore; they were late. Seeing they were defeated, François approached Sol-leks, called to Buck, and laughed. Buck responded with a canine laugh, keeping his distance. François unfastened Sol-leks's traces and placed him back in the rear. The team stood harnessed, ready for the trail, with no room for Buck except at the front.

Once more François called, and once more Buck laughed triumphantly, stepping forward to claim his station. His traces were fastened, the sled broken loose, and with both men running, they dashed onto the river trail.

Highly as François had valued Buck with his "two devils," he soon realized he had undervalued him. At a single bound, Buck assumed the duties of leadership. Where judgment, quick thinking, and swift action were needed, he proved superior even to Spitz, whom François had never matched. Yet, it was in enforcing discipline that Buck truly excelled. Dave and Sol-leks accepted the change without complaint; their sole concern was the toil within the traces. As long as that duty was fulfilled, they cared little for who led. However, the rest of the team, having grown unruly during Spitz's final days, were quickly whipped into shape by Buck. Pike, who had previously pulled lazily, was shaken repeatedly for loafing and soon hauled harder than ever before. That first night, Joe, the sour dog, was punished severely—something Spitz had never achieved. Buck simply overwhelmed him with superior weight, silencing his snaps until he whined for mercy. Immediately, the team's spirit lifted, regaining its old solidarity, leaping as one unit in the traces.

At Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, were added to the team. The speed with which Buck broke them in left François breathless. "Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!" he exclaimed. "No, nevaire! Heem worth one t'ousan' dollair, by Gar! Eh? Wot you say, Perrault?" Perrault nodded. They were already breaking records, gaining ground daily.

The trail was in perfect condition—hard-packed and clear of new snow. Temperatures hovered at fifty degrees below zero throughout the journey. Men rode and ran in turns, keeping the dogs on the jump with minimal stops. Over the Thirty Mile River, partially frozen, they covered in one day what usually took ten. In a single run, they raced sixty miles from the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Flying across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett (seventy miles of lakes), they moved so fast that the runner had to tow behind the sled at the end of a rope. By the last night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended the sea slope, the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet visible beneath them. It was a record-breaking run.

For fourteen consecutive days, they averaged forty miles per day. Upon reaching Skaguay, Perrault and François paraded through the main streets, throwing chests up and down, deluged with invitations to drink while their team became the center of worshipful admiration from fellow mushers. Soon, three or four notorious Western desperados attempted to rob the town, only to be riddled like pepper boxes. Public attention shifted elsewhere, followed shortly by official dispatches.

François summoned Buck, threw his arms around him, and wept. That marked the end of François and Perrault's tenure with the team. Like other men, they faded from Buck's life forever. A Scotch half-breed took command, leading a dozen teams back over the weary trail to Dawson.

The pace changed. This was no longer a race; it was heavy toil with a heavy load, serving as the mail train carrying news from the civilized world to those seeking gold under the Arctic pole. Buck disliked the monotony but bore it well, taking pride in the work like Dave and Sol-leks, ensuring his mates contributed their fair share. Days blurred into identical routines: cooking fires lit, breakfast eaten, camps broken, dogs harnessed, and the team moving out before dawn. Nights were spent pitching tents, gathering wood, fetching water, and feeding the dogs. To them, the meal was the highlight of the day, followed by an hour of loafing among the fifty-seven other dogs. Among them were fierce fighters, but three battles with the strongest brought Buck to absolute mastery; when he bristled and bared his teeth, they wisely stayed out of his way.

Best of all, Buck loved lying near the fire, hind legs tucked, forelegs stretched, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller's sun-drenched home in Santa Clara Valley, the cement swimming pool, Ysabel the Mexican hairless, and Toots the Japanese pug. More often, he recalled the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the epic battle with Spitz, and the delicious food he craved. He was not homesick. The memory of the Sunland was dim and distant, powerless to stir him. Instead, potent memories of his heritage surfaced—the instincts of his ancestors, dormant for years, now waking anew.

As he crouched by the fire, watching the flames, it seemed he was sitting by a different fire entirely. Before him sat another man: shorter-legged, longer-armed, with stringy, knotted muscles instead of rounded ones. His hair was long and matted, his head slanting back under it. He made strange sounds, terrified of the encroaching darkness, clutching a stick with a heavy stone tied to the end. He was nearly naked, wearing only a ragged, fire-scalded hide, with thick fur matting his chest, shoulders, and limbs. He did not stand upright but leaned forward from the hips, moving with a peculiar, catlike springiness and an alertness born of perpetual fear.

At other times, this hairy man squatted by the fire, head between his knees, elbows resting on his knees, hands clasped above his head as if shedding rain. Beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck saw glowing coals appearing two by two, always paired, which he recognized as the eyes of great beasts of prey. He heard the crash of their bodies through the undergrowth and their nocturnal cries. Dreaming there by the Yukon bank, these visions of another world made the hair rise along his back and stand on end across his shoulders and neck until he whimpered low and suppressedly, or growled softly. The half-breed cook would shout, "Hey, you Buck, wake up!" and the other world would vanish, replaced by the reality of the present. He would yawn, stretch, and resume his vigil.
