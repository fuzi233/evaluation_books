Romola’s passage from dream to waking was like a slow ferrying—first down into a thick, unbroken sleep, then up again through busy, tangled visions, until she seemed to stretch her arms beneath the vault of the Bargello, where the taper-flames flared and flared, growing so fierce that darkness itself was burnt away. When her lids lifted, it was not fire that filled her eyes, but morning.

Her boat lay motionless in a small creek, as if the sea, too, had chosen to rest there. To her right spread the Mediterranean—sapphire, speckless, a sheet of blue so clean it might have been newly washed and laid out to dry beneath the sun. To her left, the land drew inward into one of those sheltered curves repeated all along that coast like a refrain: a bowl of green set in mountain folds, with slopes softened by tree-shadow, leaning up toward rock. Here and there a pale path winked between leaf and bough, climbing toward a huddle of buildings that seemed to have scrambled up the hillside simply to lift its belfry for the village below to look at, as one lifts a loaf high to see if it has browned evenly.

The sun had only begun its work. Its rays struck the westward horn of the crescent and gilded it, while the rest lay under a dew-cooled veil. The air held stillness as if it were a kept chamber. No oar dipped; no goat-bell answered; even the water had curved itself into quiet.

Warmth fell on Romola’s limbs with the gentleness of a hand laid on a fevered brow. She lay without moving, scarcely watching, only receiving—light through the lashes, salt-bright air through the nostrils, the faint rhythm of her own breathing in the hush. There are wakings in youth when bare existence is a draught of Lethe: sensation so subtle, so indefinite, that it is bliss without recollection and without craving. For a little while it seemed as if the agitated past had slid away as utterly as the Bargello’s darkness had been blotted out; as if the long afternoons of girlhood, when time itself had been wide and drowsy, had come back.

Then the thought returned, clear and edged: she was in the boat that had carried her through the night.

It had not borne her to death. It had rocked her, like a cradle, into another morning. And, despite the despair with which she had let herself drift, she felt the simple gladness of being alive under familiar sunlight instead of in an unknown dark.

Could she not remain? Here no sound from Florence would reach her. Yet even as she tried to hold fast the peace, shapes rose behind the golden haze of her mind—domes and towers, walls cut by a river, hills enclosing what she had fled. She sat up, willing herself to resist the rush of thought that began to count distances, to ask how far the current had carried her. Why should she mind? This nook looked sheltered; there would be villagers—plain people—who would not harm her. For a little while, at least, she might rest and resolve nothing. Bread and milk: the notion came like a domestic promise. She would buy it, and then lie down again in green quiet, letting life pause.

She turned her face back toward the crescent valley, as if by looking she could drink in that same first draught of stillness. She had scarcely held herself in that contemplative posture a few minutes when a sound cleft the calm.

A cry—thin at first, then steady, then piercing, drawn out without mercy. Not the shout of a man or the bark of a dog, but the continuous wail of a small child in distress, rising and rising until it seemed to press against Romola’s breast. It was all the more intolerable because it broke upon such peace; as if a knife had struck into fresh bread.

She started, setting a foot on the boat’s side, ready to spring to shore—then paused, listening, thinking, The mother must be near; it will cease. It did not. It went on, urgent and helpless, pulling her as a hand pulls at a sleeve. She leapt onto the beach and walked rapidly, not even certain at first which way she went, only following the line of the sound.

It seemed to come from a rough tangle of garden-growth to her right, where a half-ruined hovel sagged like a toothless mouth in the green. She climbed over a low broken stone fence and crossed patches of weedy crops and corn left standing—ripe, heavy-headed, neglected. The cry sharpened. Her pace quickened. Yet even in haste she felt the air change as she left the sea behind: the salt cleanliness thinned, and a heaviness met her, as if the green luxuriance hid a foulness underneath, the way sweet fruit can conceal rot.

The doorway yawned. The child’s cry darted through her like pain. She stepped within—and the dimness inside struck her with a shock of awe and horror.

On straw scattered over the floor lay three bodies: a tall man, a girl of about eight, and a young woman. The woman’s long black hair was being clutched and pulled by the living child who screamed beside her, as if the child thought to wake her by force.

Romola’s past among death and sickness made her swift. She lifted the little creature at once—olive-dark, light as hunger, hot with panic—and drew it to her bosom, rocking it, murmuring without knowing what words she used. Even while she soothed, her eyes went back to the bodies, searching for any sign of breath, of warmth that might answer her touch. The strongly marked type in their faces, the foreignness of their clothes, made her think they were Spanish or Portuguese Jews—one of those families driven out by the Inquisition, cast like refuse from port to port, preyed on by greed at sea and by superstition on land.

But the young woman’s body, though slack and still, had not the same wasted look as the man and the child. Doubt rose: could she be alive? Romola knelt and lifted the yellow woollen drapery that lay across the bosom to lay her hand to the heart—then stopped, chilled.

Purple spots.

The pestilence: the old, familiar mark she had learned to read as plainly as a cook reads scorch on the crust of a cake. It changed everything. If the villagers knew, they would not hurry to her aid; they might shrink from her as from fire. Yet the child needed milk; it needed a woman’s arms; it needed life.

She would find someone. She had money. She could buy goat’s milk, if nothing more. She rose with the child pressed to her, and began to make her way toward the village.

Across the cultivated ground she saw signs that were wrong in their quietness: patches of corn left too long, stalks over-heavy with grain no sickle had touched; golden apples and dark figs fallen and rotting where they lay, sweetening the air with the thick, sick scent of wasted harvest. There were grassy spaces, but no cow, no sheep, no goat moving through them. The silence began to feel less like peace and more like a held breath.

She hurried toward the densest cluster of houses, where there should be the clatter of morning—doors, voices, the scrape of pails, the thin music of daily labor. To quiet the child she stooped as she went, snatched up two figs, and bit off little pieces of the sweet, granular pulp, pressing them against the hungry mouth. The baby’s sobs took rhythm with the chewing; its cry fell to whimpers.

Between two lines of dwellings she entered the village street. It was already late enough for smoke to have risen, for voices to have crossed and called from one threshold to another. But there was no one. Doors stood open on emptiness, as if the houses had exhaled their inhabitants in the night and had not drawn them back.

The oppressive taint in the air thickened—heat mixed with sourness, with something like breath that had turned foul. An open doorway showed only a bare room. Another doorway: and there, in the dull light, a man lay dead with his garments on, his head fallen across a spade handle, an earthenware cruse still in his hand as if thirst had struck him down in the act of reaching. It was death caught mid-gesture, domestic and brutal.

Horror took possession of Romola. A village of unburied dead? She tried to listen for any faint stir, any cough or groan; but when she stopped feeding the child, it cried again, and the cry filled her ears, drowning any other sound.

At last she saw movement: a figure crawling slowly out of a house, then sinking down to sit against the wall. Romola hastened. The figure was a young woman, face flushed and drawn with fever, eyes bright with pain; she too clutched a pitcher.

As Romola approached, the woman did not start. The need in her was too consuming for surprise.

“Water—get me water!” she moaned.

Romola stooped, took the pitcher, and spoke close to her ear with the gentleness one uses to a child or a delirious mind. “You shall have water. Can you point me to the well?”

A trembling hand lifted toward the farther end of the street. Romola set off, as fast as she could manage with pitcher and child. The baby, lulled by fig pulp, quieted; its mouth worked, sticky with sweetness. With the cry stilled, Romola could hear again—the deadness of the village, the faint hiss of her own breath, the dull thud of her steps on packed earth.

The well lay beyond the street’s end. As she neared it, her gaze lifted instinctively to the opposite slope below the church, searching for anything living. High up, on a patch of grass between trees, she saw it—life in simple forms: a cow and two goats, small figures of sustenance against the green. She began tracing in her mind the path that would take her there once she had done her errand. Milk. She could already imagine the warm smell of it, the comfort of its heaviness on the tongue, the way it would quiet the hollow ache in her own belly and the sharper hunger in the child.

So intent was she on that line of ascent that she did not notice how close she had come to the well, nor that someone approaching from the other side had stopped short, eyes fixed on her.

At that moment, in that place, Romola’s figure might have seemed unearthly even to a less frightened mind: the long grey garment falling in straight lines that made her movement look like gliding; her hair rolling back, its left side caught by sun so that it shone; her face lifted, intent; and on her arm the little olive baby looking out with jet-black eyes, solemn as a tiny judge. A boy of fifteen, an acolyte accustomed to swinging a censer before painted Madonnas, stared as if a fresco had stepped out of plaster.

She has a pitcher—to fetch water for the sick. It is the Holy Mother, come for those with the pestilence.

Terror turned his limbs. He flung down his vessel and fled, black-and-white figure darting away up toward the slope Romola had been watching.

Romola saw him only in passing, and the sight might have puzzled her, but the parched sufferer behind her was urgent in her mind. She drew water quickly, half-filled the pitcher, and hastened back. In the sick woman’s house she found a small cup and noticed, with brief, practical surprise, salt meat and meal laid by—no sign here of famine, only of sudden plague.

She set the baby down on the floor for a moment. The child squatted unsteadily, eyes following her hands. Romola lifted water to the fevered woman, who drank greedily, throat working, then let her head fall back, eyes closing as if relief itself were a narcotic.

When she opened her eyes again, her gaze clung to Romola’s face. “Who are you?” she asked, voice thin.

“I came over the sea,” Romola said. “I came this morning. Are all the people dead in these houses?”

“I think they are all ill now—those that are not dead.” Her words dragged as if each one cost effort. “My father and my sister lie dead upstairs. There is no one to bury them. Soon I shall die.”

“Not so, I hope,” Romola said, though she did not give false comfort lightly. “I am come to take care of you. I am used to the pestilence; I am not afraid. But there must be some left who are not ill. I saw a youth running toward the mountain.”

“I cannot tell,” the woman whispered. “When the pestilence came, many went away—drove off the cows and goats. Give me more water.”

Romola gave it, and as the woman drank, Romola’s thoughts arranged themselves with the quick logic of necessity. If she followed the boy’s flight, she might find those who had escaped infection: the able-bodied, the frightened, the ones who still had strength to act. She must secure help for the child first—someone to feed it, to hold it when Romola went back among the sick and the dead. Then she could return, moving from house to house as she had moved in Florence, doing what hands and courage could do when medicine failed.

She promised the sick woman she would come back, lifted the dark bantling again, and set off toward the slope.

No burden of choice weighed her now. The longing for death, so heavy the night before, had fallen away as if it had been a cloak she had slipped off on waking. Her body was weary, hungry, thirsty, but her mind had a single direction: to find milk, then hands, then help.

The ascent wound. With the child on her arm, the path seemed longer than it looked from below. Heat pressed down; the earth smelled baked; the leaves held a faint bitterness where sun had warmed their oils. She began to feel faint, the hollow in her stomach sharpening. When the path made a double turning, she paused, thinking she might wait nearer the cow—someone would come to milk her—rather than climb all the way to the church before she had taken any rest.

She raised her eyes, measuring the steepness. Between boughs, no more than five yards off, she saw a broad, round face watching her—still as if carved—beneath it the black skirt of a priest’s garment, and a hand grasping a bucket.

Romola stood quietly, letting him see she did not rush. She had witnessed how dread mastered people in plague-time: it made them hide like animals and strike like animals. She lifted her voice with gentle pleading.

“I came over the sea. I am hungry, and so is the child. Will you not give us some milk?”

She had guessed part of the truth—this was the parish priest, this was the keeper of the cow—but she had not guessed what her words meant inside his frightened mind.

Only a short time before, the boy—Jacopo—had burst upon the priest with a tale: he had seen the Holy Mother with the Babe, fetching water for the sick. She was tall as cypresses. There was light about her head. She looked up toward the church. The old pievano had not wholly believed: he had lived more than fifty years without seeing the Madonna step down into his valley. He had suspected the boy’s eyes had turned some ordinary marvel into a miracle. Yet fear had pricked him. Before daring to come down and milk, he had mumbled many Aves, and his conscience—never entirely quiet—had tormented him. He trembled at the pestilence; he trembled too at the thought that Mercy itself might demand more from him than prayers.

So when he suddenly saw Romola at the parted way, her refined accent and her pleading tone did not reassure him. They sounded, in his panic, preternatural. He did not quite believe she was holy; but he feared she might be—and he feared equally that if there was a miracle, it would not be arranged for his comfort. He dared not run; he dared not advance.

“Come down,” Romola said after a pause, her voice steady, carrying authority without harshness. “Do not fear. Fear rather to deny food to the hungry when they ask you.”

A moment later the boughs parted and the priest stepped into view: thickset, broad-faced, harmless-looking, his black frock worn and soiled, bucket in hand. He kept a timid distance as he went in silence toward the tethered cow.

Romola followed, watching him. He seated himself and began to milk, hands nervous at first, the stream striking the pail with that soft, insistent sound which is itself a kind of comfort—like rain heard from inside. He poured milk into a brass cup he carried and handed it to her.

Romola lifted the cup to the child’s mouth. The baby drank eagerly, lips pursed, then loosened, sucking with a greed that was almost furious. Milk ran in a thin line at the corner; the child smacked, then sucked its own milky lips as if astonished by the richness. Romola drank too; the warm sweetness spread through her, easing the faintness and the dryness in her throat.

The priest watched from his low stool. He recognized, now that he was closer, that the baby was Hebrew—its dark skin and hair, the cast of its features. He recognized, too, that Romola was no vaporous apparition but a substantial woman. Yet the strangeness remained: why was she here, alone, with a plague-child in her arms? It felt, to his frightened mind, as if the world had shifted under his feet. He had a presentiment that his life—so long circled by small duties and large fears—was about to be altered.

Romola smiled down at the baby’s satisfied mouth and held out the cup again. “Give us more, good father.”

He obeyed more readily, though dread still lived in him like a lodged thorn.

When the second cup had been drained, Romola looked down at him and spoke with mild decision, as one speaks when bread must be kneaded and there is no time for hesitation.

“Now tell me, father, how this pestilence came—and why you let your people die without the sacraments, and lie unburied. For I have come over the sea to help those who are left alive. And you, too, will help them now.”

He told the tale, his words stumbling where shame and fear caught them. While he spoke, Jacopo—who had fled—crept back, peeping and edging forward behind bushes until he stood watching, wide-eyed.

Three families of Jews, twenty souls, had been put ashore weeks ago; some were already ill with the pestilence. The villagers, the priest said, had refused shelter to the miscreants, giving them only a distant hovel and heaps of straw. When the strangers died, some of the people had thrown the bodies into the sea. Then a great storm had come, and the sea—angry, implacable—had brought the bodies back again, casting them up as if refusing to be made an accomplice. Terror seized the village. A grave was dug and the bodies buried. But then the pestilence struck the Christians. Most fled over the mountain, driving away their few cattle and carrying provisions. The priest had not fled: he had stayed and prayed, and he had persuaded Jacopo to remain with him. Yet he confessed that mortal terror had mastered him; he had not dared to go down among the sick.

“You will fear no longer,” Romola said, her tone warming with encouraging command. “You will come down with me. We will see who is living. We will look for the dead and bury them. I have walked for months where the pestilence was, and see—I am strong.”

Then she turned her face toward the peeping boy and motioned. “Jacopo will come too.”

Jacopo came out slowly, as if something unseen drew him.

“Come, Jacopo,” Romola said again, smiling, letting kindness soften the order. “You will carry the child for me. See—your arms are strong, and I am tired.”

To Jacopo it was dreadful: to hold the plague-child, to go down into the valley of sickness. To the priest it was dreadful too. Yet both felt themselves under an influence stronger than dread—not the confusion of miracle now, but the sharper conviction that this woman was sent, and that to refuse her would be to refuse God’s command spoken in a human tongue.

“Now we will carry down the milk,” Romola said, “and see if any one wants it.”

So they went together down the slope—the priest with his bucket, the boy with the child held at arm’s length at first and then closer as the little one settled, Romola walking between them, her gaze already measuring doors, windows, thresholds—places where life might still be found. In the valley that morning, those who had given themselves up to despair saw help arrive, not with trumpets but with a cup of milk and hands willing to lift a head, wash a face, carry water, and speak without fear.

There were scarcely more than a score alive in all that nook between mountain and sea. But all were comforted; most were saved. The dead, who had lain with open doors around them as if the village had forgotten its own names, were gathered and buried. The air, which had seemed loaded with corruption, learned again the humbler smells of life: damp earth turned for graves, then for planting; smoke rising from cookfires; milk warming in pots; bread baking until its crust sent out that particular scent that makes even grief take one breath of solace.

Days lengthened into weeks; weeks into months. The men dug and sowed again. The women began once more to go to the well with their great vases on their heads, and their steps made the street audible in a way Romola came to love—the soft pad of sandals, the clink of pottery, the low calls from one doorway to another, as if sound itself were proof of survival. The valley’s acoustic emptiness, so terrible in those first hours, filled again with small human noises and the answering bleat of goats, the occasional lowing of cattle, the bell from the church up the slope cutting the air in measured strokes that marked time not as doom but as return.

The Hebrew baby grew sturdier. It ceased to be only a foundling in her arms and became a child with habits—wanting what it saw, crawling toward any basket that smelled of eggs or honey, smearing its hands in whatever sweetness it could reach. In time it was baptized in the mountain church, and named Benedetto, and the little dark creature—once nothing but hunger and a cry—became a tottering, tumbling Christian child, as much a part of the villagers’ future as any olive shoot newly planted.

But by then Romola herself began to pay the cost. The strain that purpose had kept at bay returned as fatigue and languor, settling into her bones. She took as a dwelling one of the abandoned houses standing a little apart from the village street. There, on a thick heap of clean straw—soft and fragrant, a bed for those who had never demanded down—she lay still through most of the daylight hours. A widow, made solitary by the pestilence, tended her and the little Benedetto, bringing water, washing cloths, setting by her hand whatever food the others had brought.

Each day the Padre, Jacopo, and the small flock of survivors came to the cottage to see the blessed Lady. They brought offerings of their best—honey that smelled of thyme and wild blossoms; fresh cakes still warm, their crusts browned and faintly crackling; eggs with shells cool as smooth stones; polenta, golden and steaming, cut with a string and laid on a board. The gifts were as much gratitude as sustenance. The sound of their steps outside, the murmured greetings, the rustle of baskets, became part of the cottage’s life.

It was a sight none of them forgot, a sight they repeated when they were old—how the sweet, sainted lady with the fair face and golden hair and brown eyes that seemed to carry blessing lay weary after labors that had saved them; how the queer little black Benedetto crawled on the straw by her side and wanted everything that was brought; how she always gave him a bit of what she took—letting him taste the honey, breaking off a corner of cake, wetting his lips with milk—and told them that if they loved her, they must be good to Benedetto.

In later years the valley told legends about the blessed Lady who had come over the sea. The tales grew as tales do: light gathered around her head; her step was said to have made the sick air sweeter; the sea itself was said to have carried her like a servant bearing a queen. Yet through all the embroidery, anyone who listened closely could hear the plain thread underneath: that once, in a time of terror, a woman had come and done beautiful, loving deeds there—feeding, tending, burying, commanding frightened men into courage—so that those ready to perish were rescued, and a child who had nothing but a cry was given a name that the village could speak.
