A stanza from William Fowler hung in the silence of the room, a melancholy testament to a heart both captive and defiant. "Your beauty was the first that won the place / And scal'd the walls of my undaunted heart..." The words seemed to echo the turmoil within Margaret Hale as she dragged herself upright the following morning. Though unrefreshed, she felt a strange, heavy restfulness settle over her. The household had passed through the storm with remarkable grace; her mother had awakened only once, and the air outside, though stiflingly hot, carried the faint promise of a breeze. Somewhere in the unseen copse or thick green wood, a murmur danced—a rushing, falling noise that promised a cool retreat, a distinct gladness that mirrored the peace she sought.

Seated at her work in Mrs. Hale's room, Margaret plotted a path to normalcy. Soon she would assist her mother with dressing, and later visit Bessy Higgins. Above all, she intended to banish the memory of the Thorntons, refusing to conjure images of them until they stood physically before her again. Yet, paradoxically, the sheer effort to suppress the thought served only to sharpen it. From time to time, a hot flush swept across her pale cheeks, rising like a sunbeam breaking through watery clouds, staining her complexion with a sudden, undeniable color.

Soft footsteps approached, and Dixon slid the door open, creeping silently across the floorboards. "Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He is in the drawing-room."

Margaret dropped her embroidery instantly, the needle clinking against the fabric. "Did he ask for me? Has Papa returned?"

"He asked for you, miss; Master is out."

"Very well, I will come," Margaret said, her voice steady, though her heart hammered a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She lingered by the shaded window, reluctant to leave the sanctuary of the room, but duty compelled her forward.

In the drawing-room, John Thornton stood near a window, ostensibly gazing out at the street, yet inwardly paralyzed by fear. His own heart beat a thunderous drumbeat at the prospect of her arrival. He could not shake the lingering sensation of her arms wrapped around his neck, an embrace he had initially resented as clumsy but which now haunted him with a terrifying tenderness. The memory of her clinging defense seemed to dissolve his iron resolve, melting his composure like wax before a flame. He dreaded the approach, terrified that if he moved to meet her, he would reach out with arms wide open, begging her to return to that safe haven she had created the previous day. A strong man, he trembled at the mere anticipation of the words he needed to speak, fearing how she might receive them. Perhaps she would wilt, flush, and flutter into his embrace, finding her natural home once more. One moment he burned with impatience at the possibility of this reunion; the next, a cold dread seized him—a vision of passionate rejection that withered his future with a blight so deadly he could scarcely bear to entertain it.

Startled by a shift in the air, he turned. She had entered so softly, her progress masked by the hum of the street, that he had heard nothing but her gentle tread. She stood by the table, refusing to sit. Her eyelids cast long shadows over her eyes, her lips slightly parted to reveal the pale line beneath. Her breathing was slow and deep, expanding her chin and the noble lines of her nose, the only movement on a face otherwise still. Her skin, usually warm and rosy, appeared wan today, the loss of health accentuated by the heavy curtain of dark hair framing her temples, hiding the bruise of the blow she had suffered. Even with her eyes lowered, her head was tilted back in its familiar, proud posture. She resembled a prisoner falsely accused of a crime she despised, too indignant to defend herself.

Thornton stepped forward hastily, then composed himself, walking with quiet firmness to the door—which she had left ajar—to close it. Returning to stand opposite her, he allowed himself a moment to take in the total impression of her presence before daring to disturb it, perhaps to repel it, with whatever words lay on his tongue.

"Miss Hale, I was very ungrateful yesterday—"

"You had nothing to be grateful for," she interrupted, raising her gaze to meet his directly. "You mean, I suppose, that you believe you ought to thank me for what I did." Despite her anger, a thick blush spread across her face, burning into her eyes, yet her gaze remained grave and steady. "It was only a natural instinct; any woman would have acted precisely the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when confronted with danger. I ought, rather," she hastened to add, "to apologize to you for those thoughtless words which sent you down into peril."

"It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, however pungently expressed," he countered, stalling just short of the edge where his hot passion threatened to carry him away. He weighed each word carefully, his will triumphing over impulse. "But you shall not drive me off upon that excuse and so escape the expression of my deep gratitude, my—" He paused, halting in his career.

"I do not attempt to escape from anything," she declared calmly. "I simply state that you owe me no gratitude; and I may add that any expression of it would be painful to me, for I do not feel I deserve it. Still, if it will relieve you from even a fancied obligation, speak on."

"I do not wish to be relieved from any obligation," he said, goaded by her serene demeanor. "Fancied or real, I question neither. I choose to believe that I owe my very life to you—ay, smile if you will, and call it an exaggeration. I believe it, because it adds a value to that existence to know—" He broke off, lowering his voice to a tender, intense whisper that made her shiver and tremble before him, "to think circumstances so wrought that whenever I exult in existence henceforward, I may say to myself, 'All this gladness in life, all honest pride in my labor, all this keen sense of being, I owe to her!' It doubles the joy, it makes the pride glow, it sharpens the sense of existence until I can barely distinguish whether it is pain or pleasure to realize I owe it to one person—nay, you must, you shall hear," he stepped forward with stern determination, "to one whom I love as I do not believe any man has ever loved a woman before." He gripped her hand tightly, panting as he awaited her response.

With sudden, icy fury, he drew his hand away. "How!" he exclaimed. "Offends you! I am indeed most unfortunate."

"Yes!" she replied, regaining her dignity. "I do feel offended; and, I think, justly. You seem to imagine that my conduct yesterday was a personal act between us, and that you may come to thank me for it. Instead, you fail to perceive, as a gentleman would—yes! a gentleman, as we discussed before—that any woman, worthy of the name, would step forward to shield, with her revered helplessness, a man from the violence of a mob."

"And the gentleman thus rescued is forbidden the relief of thanks!" he broke in contemptuously. "I am a man. I claim the right of expressing my feelings."

"And I yield to that right," she answered, her voice rising with indignation, "simply stating that you gave me pain by insisting upon it." Tears, held back for a long struggle, rose violently to her eyes, choking her voice. "But you seem to have imagined that I was not merely guided by womanly instinct, but"—she struggled for words—"but that I was prompted by some particular feeling for *you*—you! Why, there was not a man—not a single desperate soul in that entire crowd—for whom I did not feel greater sympathy, for whom I could not have done what little I could more heartily."

"You may speak on, Miss Hale. I am aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours. I now believe it was only your innate sense of oppression—that yes, I, though a master, may be oppressed—that made you act so nobly. I know you despise me; allow me to say, it is because you do not understand me."

"I do not care to understand," she replied, gripping the table to steady herself. To her mind, he was cruel, and she was weak with indignation.

"No, I see you do not. You are unfair and unjust."

Margaret compressed her lips. She would offer no answer to such accusations. Yet, despite his savage words, he could have thrown himself at her feet and kissed the hem of her garment. She remained silent and unmoving. Hot tears of wounded pride streamed down her face. He waited, longing for some taunt, some spark of rebuttal, but she remained a statue of silence. Finally, he took up his hat.

"One word more. You look as if you thought it tainted you to be loved by me. You cannot avoid it. Nay, I, if I would, cannot cleanse you from it. But I would not if I could. I have never loved any woman before: my life has been too busy, my thoughts too absorbed with other matters. Now I love, and will love. But do not be afraid of too much expression on my part."
