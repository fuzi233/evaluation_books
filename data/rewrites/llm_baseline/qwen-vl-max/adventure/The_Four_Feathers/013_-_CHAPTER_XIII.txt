Ethne stood at the open window of the drawing room in Hill Street, her gaze fixed on the quiet street below. Mrs. Adair sat across from her, tea untouched, both women waiting—listening—for a sound that would signal his arrival. The afternoon sun bathed London in golden light, June’s warmth filling the city with life. Trees swayed gently, flowers bloomed in windowsills across the way, and carriages rolled smoothly toward Hyde Park. Then came the jingle of bells—a cab approaching.

Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply. "That’s a cab," she said.

"Yes," Ethne replied, leaning forward slightly. But the cab passed by without stopping. The bells faded into silence.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock. "Colonel Durrance is late." She turned to Ethne, sensing something beneath her calm surface. There had been no eagerness in her voice, only tension—almost fear. And when the cab didn’t stop, Ethne seemed to exhale quietly, as though relieved.

"I wonder why you didn’t go to the station?" Mrs. Adair asked slowly.

"He might have thought I came because he needed help," Ethne answered quickly. "I don’t want him to think that. He has his servant."

She looked out again, hesitating, her lips parting once or twice as if to speak, then closing. Finally, she spoke plainly. "You remember the telegram I showed you?"

"The one from Lieutenant Calder? About Colonel Durrance going blind?"

"Yes. I want you to promise never to mention it. Not a word. I don’t want him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair frowned. She had seen the telegram, but not the letter Ethne had sent in reply—her pledge of love, made the moment she read the news. All Ethne had said was, “I am engaged to him.” Mrs. Adair assumed the engagement was long-standing. She never questioned it.

"You’ll promise?" Ethne insisted.

"Certainly, my dear," Mrs. Adair replied, shrugging. "But there must be a reason. Why such secrecy?"

"Two lives must not be ruined because of me."

The words settled between them. Mrs. Adair believed she meant Durrance and Harry Feversham—though she was mistaken. Ethne didn’t correct her. In fact, she was glad for the misunderstanding. It gave her space.

Ethne returned to the window, her mind already mapping the path ahead. Her future was a challenge—one she had chosen, yet feared. She could not afford a single slip, a careless word, an unguarded look. Forgetting him was impossible. So she would hide her feelings. Protect him. Protect herself.

And now, as she waited, fear crept in—not of him, but of failing. Yet the goal was worth every effort: a life lived with purpose, not pity.

Durrance had lost everything that defined him—the adventure, the open sky, the desert nights, the command of men, the joy of travel. Only one thing remained: Ethne. And only so long as she wanted him.

Then, without warning, the cab stopped at the door. The bell rang. Ethne started.

"He’s here!" she whispered.

Durrance entered, not as a man broken, but as one who had faced darkness and walked through it. His face was thinner, his hair greyer, but there was no sign of weakness. He moved with confidence, stepping into the room as if he saw clearly. He reached out once, gently, where a chair should be—just once. Then he smiled.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn’t true, then," she breathed. "You’ve recovered."

He shook his head. "No. I haven’t. But I heard you move at the window. The noise outside grew louder. I knew someone stepped aside. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange was strange—no greeting, no embrace—but it eased the silence. No need for false sympathy. They understood each other too well.

They shook hands. Then Ethne offered tea.

"We must talk," Durrance said seriously.

He chatted lightly about the journey home, even laughed at how helpless he’d felt at first—how he’d tripped over rugs, misjudged distances. Ethne’s anxiety began to fade.

But then he paused. "I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "Do you remember the letter?"

"Quite well," she replied.

"I had a friend hold the paper," he continued, smiling. "Calder—of the Sappers. You don’t know him."

He said the name fast, almost as a test. Ethne froze.

A trap. He was watching her. Waiting for a flinch, a pause, a breath too quick. Did he suspect? Did he know about the telegram? Did he think her letter was written out of mercy?

She studied his face—alert, still, unreadable. Once, she could have read his thoughts in his eyes. Now, she couldn’t see them at all.

"I read your letter easily," she said softly. "It was exactly what you would have written. But I had already written to you. Your news changed nothing. I take back no word."

Durrance leaned forward, hands on his knees. Still, Ethne couldn’t tell if he believed her.

"You know me," he said at last. "You wouldn’t take anything back. But let me speak my side."

He spoke slowly, deliberately, as if rehearsed. "Blindness changes men. It breeds selfishness, unless one fights it constantly. And can one fight it forever? It means isolation. For most men, yes. But for me—it means more. I am a soldier. Not a clever one. No desk work. No writing. No business. Just… nothing. Jealousy. Irritation. A constant need for attention. That’s my danger."

Ethne smiled faintly. "Perhaps we can keep those away."

"Maybe," he admitted. "But they must be considered. I’ve thought about this night after night since I got your letter. I wanted to say yes—so badly. With gratitude. But I couldn’t. Not until I knew—until I was sure."

He paused. "A great trouble clears the mind. At Cairo, I lay awake, sorting things out. The small things fell away. Only truths remained. I had to cling to them."

Ethne nodded. She understood. She had lived through the same kind of clarity. She felt a sudden kinship with him—this man who had stared into emptiness and found meaning.

"Well?" she asked.

"One truth stands out," he said. "Marriage between a man like me—crippled, finished—and a woman like you—full of life, young, strong—would be wrong unless each brings more than friendship. It would be wrong if it meant sacrifice for you."

"It means no sacrifice," she said firmly.

He nodded. It was the tone that mattered—the certainty in her voice. He listened not to the words, but to the conviction behind them.

"Then do you bring more than friendship?" he asked suddenly. "Be honest. Tell me."

Ethne hesitated. She must answer—clearly, honestly. "There is nothing," she said, just as firmly, "nothing in the world I wish for more than marrying you."

It was true. She had no idea of Harry Feversham’s return, of the Gordon letters found in the ruins of Khartoum, of the secret plans unfolding. To her, Harry was gone. Forever.

So her wish was sincere.

But it wasn’t the full truth. And she hoped—prayed—he would listen to the feeling, not the words.

He seemed satisfied. "Thank you, Ethne," he said, taking her hand. "I’m glad."

No more questions. No suspicion. Just peace.

Mrs. Adair entered then, tactful and warm. She greeted Durrance as if nothing had changed—as if he were still whole.

"I suppose Ethne has told you our plan?" she said, accepting her tea.

"No," Ethne said. "What plan?"

"We’ve arranged it," Mrs. Adair said. "You’ll want to go home to Guessens in Devonshire. I live nearby—just two fields away. So Ethne will stay with me until you’re married."

"Very kind," Durrance said. "Because, of course, there will be an interval."

"A short one," Mrs. Adair added.

Durrance nodded. "If there’s any chance I might regain sight, I should act at once. Time matters in these cases."

"Is there a chance?" Ethne asked.

"I’ll see a specialist tomorrow," he said. "And perhaps the oculist in Wiesbaden. But maybe I won’t need to go that far. I expect I can stay at Guessens and come up when necessary. Thank you, Mrs. Adair. It’s a good plan."

He added slowly, "From my point of view, it could not be better."

Later, as he left for his rooms in St. James’s Street, Ethne watched him go. She stood by the window, silent, thoughtful.

The street was now filled with carriages returning from the park. But inside, something had shifted.

She believed Durrance’s suspicions were quieted—for now. He didn’t know about the conversation with Calder. He didn’t know he was being lied to. He didn’t know the hope of recovery was a fiction.

Yet she felt uneasy.

Durrance was different now. Not just sharper in hearing, but in mind. His thoughts were deeper, clearer. He saw more—not with eyes, but with perception.

She realized, with a pang, that she was entering a battle—not of strength, but of wits.

"Two lives shall not be spoilt because of me," she whispered again.

But now it was less a resolution, more a prayer.

For one thing was certain: Durrance saw more clearly than ever—blind as he was.
