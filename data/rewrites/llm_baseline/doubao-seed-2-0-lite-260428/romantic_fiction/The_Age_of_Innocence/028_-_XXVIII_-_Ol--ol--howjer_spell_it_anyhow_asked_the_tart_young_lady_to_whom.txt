“Ol—ol—how d’you spell that ridiculous name, anyway?” the sharp, bright young telegraph clerk prodded, as Archer pushed May’s hastily scrawled telegram across the dented brass ledge of the Western Union counter.
“Olenska. O-l-e-n-s-k-a,” he repeated, plucking the slip back to print out the foreign syllables clear above his wife’s messy cursive, his chest tight at the shape of the word: the name he’d been chasing across half a dozen gilded drawing rooms, half a dozen quiet unspoken moments, for months now.
“Funny name for a New York telegraph office, wouldn’t you say? Leastways not this part of town,” an amused, familiar voice drawled at his elbow. Archer turned to find Lawrence Lefferts looming over him, stroking his impeccable waxed moustache, pretending he hadn’t been craning to peek at the name on the telegram slip. “Hey Newland, was just hoping I’d run into you here. Heard old Mrs. Mingott had a stroke, was heading over to her place when I saw you turn down this street and followed. Figured you’d come from there?”
Archer nodded, pushing the telegram under the lattice of the counter.
“Terrible bad, huh?” Lefferts went on, leaning in a little too close. “Wiring to the family, right? Guess it must be serious if you’re sending for Countess Olenska, of all people.”
Archer’s jaw locked so hard his teeth ached. He had a wild, feral urge to slam his fist right into that smug, vain, handsome face, to shut Lefferts’s gossiping mouth for good where Ellen’s name was concerned.
“Why do you say that?” he bit out.
Lefferts, the most famous coward in New York high society when it came to ungentle disagreements, raised his eyebrows in a pointed, ironic glance towards the telegraph clerk still hovering behind the counter. The look said plainly: Nothing is worse form than throwing a fit in public, Archer. You’ll look like a fool.
Archer couldn’t have cared less about form at that moment – but the thought of trading barbs about Ellen Olenska with Lawrence Lefferts, of letting that man drag her good name through public mud even for a second, made the whole fight feel unthinkable. He paid for his telegram without another word, and the two men stepped out onto the sun-dappled, gritty New York sidewalk together. By then he’d clamped his temper back down tight. “Mrs. Mingott’s already much better. Doctor says there’s no cause for concern at all.” Lefferts poured over him with flowery, relieved platitudes, then prattled on about the awful, ruinous rumours circling Beaufort.

By that afternoon, the news of Beaufort’s total financial collapse was splashed across every paper in the city. It overshadowed every line printed about Manson Mingott’s stroke, and only the tiny handful of people who knew Regina Beaufort had snuck over to beg the old matriarch for a secret loan the night before attributed her illness to anything other than old age and too many rich desserts.
All of New York was shrouded in the dark, giddy hum of the Beaufort scandal. As old Mr. Letterblair liked to grumble, no case half this bad had crossed his desk in his whole career, not even dating back to the first Letterblair who’d founded the firm. The bank had kept taking deposits for a full day after its total ruin was inevitable, and half its clients came from the oldest, most respected clans in the city – so Beaufort’s betrayal felt twice as sharp, twice as cynical. If Regina Beaufort hadn’t gone around telling everyone the collapse was “the true test of friendship,” public anger at her husband might have softened into pity for her. But between that and the news of her secret midnight visit to old Catherine, everyone agreed her cynicism was even worse than her husband’s – and no one could even make the old excuse that she was “a foreigner” to let her off easy. The only small comfort for people who hadn’t lost their own savings was reminding each other that Beaufort was a foreigner, but even that didn’t help much when a Dallas from South Carolina was running his mouth about how Beaufort would be right back on his feet soon. There was nothing for it but for society to agree to cut the Beauforts off entirely, shut their doors to them forever – except for all the poor hapless victims of the crash: Medora Manson, poor old Miss Lannings, and all those other well-born foolish ladies who everyone clucked their tongues at, saying if they’d only listened to Henry van der Luyden none of this would have happened.
“The best thing the Beauforts can possibly do,” Mrs. Archer pronounced over her tea that night, as if she were diagnosing a sick child and prescribing a tonic, “is move down to that little property of Regina’s in North Carolina. Beaufort’s always kept a racing stable, he’d do very well breeding trotting horses. He’s got every trait of a good successful horse dealer, after all.” Everyone nodded in agreement, but no one bothered to actually ask the couple what their real plans were.

The next day, old Mrs. Mingott was almost fully recovered. She got her voice back enough to order every last person in the house never to mention the Beauforts in front of her again, and when Dr. Bencomb showed up, she snapped at him to explain why the whole family was making such a ridiculous fuss over her health. “If people my age insist on eating chicken salad late at night, what do they expect?” she huffed. The doctor promptly adjusted her diet, and soon the whole “stroke” was rebranded as nothing more than a bad case of indigestion.
But even with her full health back, old Catherine wasn’t quite the same sharp, unfeeling matriarch she’d been before. Old age had blunted her very limited compassion for other people’s troubles, and she had no trouble at all erasing the whole Beaufort disaster from her mind. For the first time though, she was fixated on her own symptoms, and suddenly overflowing with soft sentiment for the members of her family she’d spent decades ignoring.
Mr. Welland, of all people, was now her new favourite. She’d brushed him off for years, laughing at his wife’s claims that he was a secret man of great force and intellect if he’d ever just chosen to exercise it. Now his reputation as a dedicated, meticulous valetudinarian made him endlessly fascinating to her, and she sent an imperial summons that he come over the second his temperature was normal so they could compare their restricted diets. No one, she announced, could ever be too careful about checking their temperature.

Twenty four hours after the telegram was sent to Ellen Olenska in Washington, a reply came back saying she’d arrive the next evening. The Newland Archers happened to be lunching at the Wellands’ that day, so the whole family immediately fell to debating who would meet her at the Jersey City train station. The Welland household operated like a ramshackle frontier outpost, perpetually short on resources, so every little obstacle felt enormous. They agreed Mrs. Welland couldn’t possibly make the trip, because she was accompanying her husband over to old Catherine’s that afternoon, and the family brougham couldn’t be spared – if Mr. Welland got “upset” seeing his mother-in-law for the first time after her attack, they might need to rush him home at a moment’s notice. The Welland sons were all downtown at work, Lovell Mingott would be rushing back from a shooting trip right that same evening and his carriage was reserved to pick him up, and no one could possibly ask May to go alone across the Hudson ferry to Jersey City, not even in her own carriage, on a cold dark winter evening.
But if no one met Ellen at the station, they’d be horribly inhospitable, going directly against old Catherine’s explicit orders to welcome her home. It was just like Ellen, Mrs. Welland’s tired sigh implied, to dump the whole family in this mess. “It’s one crisis right after another,” she grieved, in one of her rare little bouts of rebellion against fate. “The only reason I think Mamma might be even sicker than Dr. Bencomb is letting on, is this sudden mad urge to have Ellen come all the way here immediately, no matter how much trouble it causes everyone.”
The words were out before she could think, and Mr. Welland pounced on them immediately, turning pale and laying down his fork. “Augusta,” he said, “do you have any actual reason to think Dr. Bencomb is less reliable than he used to be? Have you noticed him slacking off on my treatments, or your mother’s?”
Mrs. Welland went white as a sheet as she realized what a mess she’d made, what endless cascades of anxious trouble her careless comment would cause. She laughed, took a second helping of scalloped oysters, and climbed right back into her usual cheerful armour. “Darling, what on earth made you think that? I only meant, after the very firm stand Mamma took that Ellen’s duty was to go back to her husband in Europe, it’s so strange she’s suddenly got this whim to see her now, when there are half a dozen other grandchildren she could have summoned instead. We can’t forget that wonderful as her vitality is, Mamma is a very old woman.”
Mr. Welland’s brow stayed furrowed, his anxious mind already latching on to that last line. “Yes, your mother is very old. For all we know, Bencomb might not be nearly as good at treating elderly patients. You’re right dear, it’s always one thing after another. In ten or fifteen years I suppose I’ll have the pleasant job of hunting down a new doctor. Much better to make the change before you’re absolutely forced to.” With that very Spartan, sensible decision made, he picked his fork back up.
“All the same,” Mrs. Welland said, pushing back from the lunch table and leading everyone into the dark back drawing room, all draped in purple satin and cluttered with malachite knickknacks, “I still don’t see how we’re going to get Ellen here tomorrow evening. I do like to have every single thing settled at least twenty four hours ahead of time.”

Archer tore his eyes away from a tiny, gaudy painting of two cardinals drinking wine, set in an octagonal ebony frame studded with onyx medallions. “I’ll go pick her up,” he offered. His heart was thudding so hard against his ribs he could feel it in his ears. “I can slip away from the office early enough to drive May’s brougham to the ferry, and meet her train.”
Mrs. Welland sighed in unbridled relief, and May, who’d drifted over to the window, turned back to smile at him, bright and full of wifely approval. “See, Mamma? Everything is all sorted out, more than twenty four hours ahead of time,” she said, leaning down to kiss her mother’s worried forehead.
May’s brougham was waiting out front to drive Archer to Union Square, where he could catch a Broadway streetcar down to his office. As she settled into her corner of the carriage, she spoke softly. “I didn’t want to upset Mamma with more new problems, but how on earth are you going to meet Ellen tomorrow, when you’re supposed to leave for Washington tomorrow? You’re off to argue that big patent case before the Supreme Court, remember?”
“Oh, I’m not going,” Archer said.
“Not going? What on earth happened?” Her voice was clear as a bell, brimming with perfectly calibrated wifely concern.
“The case got called off, postponed.”
“Postponed? How odd. I got a note from Mr. Letterblair to Mamma this morning, saying he was heading off to Washington today to argue that very patent case. You said it was the patent case you were going for, didn’t you?”
Archer felt heat creep up his face. She was pressing him so insistently, so unlike the soft, compliant May he knew, that he felt himself blushing for her, for breaking all of society’s unspoken rules of delicate politeness. “No, the case isn’t postponed. My trip is,” he said, cursing all the extra little details he’d spilled when he first told everyone he was going to Washington, remembering some old line he’d read somewhere that mediocre liars pile on details, but the very cleverest ones don’t bother. It hurt him far less to lie to May than it did to watch her pretend she hadn’t already caught him in the lie. “I’m going later, that’s all. Lucky for your family’s convenience, I guess,” he added, letting a little sharp sarcasm creep into his voice.
He could feel her staring at him, so he turned his head to meet her eyes, determined not to look like he was hiding something. Their gazes locked for a long, heavy second, and in that look they each read far more of what the other was hiding than either of them ever wanted to admit out loud.
“Yes,” May agreed, her tone bright and perfectly even, “it’s such a happy coincidence that you’re free to meet Ellen after all. You saw how grateful Mamma was that you offered.”
“Oh, I’m very happy to do it,” Archer said.
The carriage rolled to a stop, and as he jumped down to the sidewalk, May leaned over to rest her hand on his. “Goodbye, dearest,” she said. Her eyes were so bright, so vivid a blue, that he wondered later if they’d been glistening with unshed tears.
He hurried across Union Square, muttering the same line to himself under his breath, over and over, like a half-holy, half-wicked prayer: It’s two whole hours from the Jersey City station to old Catherine’s house. Two whole hours. Maybe even more.
