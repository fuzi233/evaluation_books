The intelligence Major Pendennis had gathered touching the Claverings—partly from Strong’s blundering honesty, partly from his own seasoned habit of listening with one ear and laying traps with the other—was enough to make even that old calculator pause over any scheme in which his nephew might be asked to stake his comfort.

A wife, to be sure, was an excellent thing in the abstract, like a well-roasted capon or a well-ripened Stilton; but the Major had never yet met with an abstract wife, and he knew that the bird came with its stuffing. In Miss Amory’s case, the stuffing was two fathers-in-law: one a bankrupt baronet with a talent for signing his own ruin; the other an invisible gentleman who, if he ever became visible within reach of British law, would have the kindness to save everybody trouble by swinging at the end of a rope. A guardian may have nerves; he may have digestion; he may have a tender regard for the family name and the family liver. Why should he season a young man’s marriage-broth with such ingredients?

Nor, even if the Begum (that magnificent Eastern saucepan of gold) were to pour money into Clavering’s empty pot, would the dish ever come wholesome. The Baronet would ladle it out to every rogue in London who brought a stamped paper and an oily grin; the treasury would be scraped to the bottom; and the young lady, instead of bringing income and influence, would bring—what? Her beauty, her gentle breeding, and a pedigree which at its last male name carried, as it were, a black little note in the margin—like the smell of something burnt that no amount of rose-water quite gets rid of.

The Major, revolving these matters, felt that another line of manoeuvre remained. He did not like to show his cards; he liked still less to lose the game. But before we arrive at that, the reader must submit to a small domestic circumstance of London life: how a gentleman under money troubles becomes as hard to catch as a trout, and much more slippery to hold when caught.

When a man in embarrassments drops out of his usual flock—ceases to wheel round Pall Mall and perch at Clubs—it is astonishing in what queer back-yards he will reappear, chirping, after dusk. He has a house, but never inhabits it; an address, but only the innocent ever go there. His friends are those who know where he takes breath—over bad brandy, worse tobacco, and the consolations of other people’s cash.

So Sir Francis Clavering, after his squabbles with his wife and after Strong had been moralising at him till morals became unbearable, was not to be found “at home” for love or money—especially money, which has so strange an attraction for a man who is running away from it. The tradesmen might call; the lawyers might write; the family friends might knock; the Baronet was always “out,” and his very knocker looked as if it had been bribed to lie.

Major Pendennis, having an object that required speech—not a note, not a message, not a third person’s version—set himself to trap this shy bird. It cost him time, patience, and (what was harder) meals.

One day he wrote with honeyed innocence and invited Sir Francis to Greenwich—whitebait, a few friends, pleasant air, the river making pretty noises against the boat, and no business on earth. Sir Francis accepted, suspected, and stayed away; leaving the Major, who had meant to be “the few friends” in his own person, to eat the whitebait alone, with the sound of other parties laughing round him like knives on plates. Another day the Major begged ten minutes’ talk. Sir Francis replied instantly, named four o’clock next day at “Bays’s precisely,” and underlined “precisely” as though punctuality were a virtue which could be manufactured by ink. Four o’clock came—faithful as a boiling kettle—and no Baronet appeared. To look for Sir Francis in daylight, with conscience and accounts in the air, was like looking for a mouse on a drum.

Yet that same day—while Strong’s patron had been blessing him to his face and cursing him behind his back, in the wholesome old fashion of the world—Sir Francis, who had sworn on a Bible (and very likely kissed it) that he would draw or accept no more bills, had actually set his respectable name to a piece of stamped paper. His friend Mr. Moss Abrams carried it off, promising to have it “done” by some accommodating personage. And Strong heard of this performance from the best possible witness: the landlord of the little back-parlour where it had been cooked.

It was at Mr. Santiago’s cigar-shop—an establishment where the air was always thick with Havana, and conversation always had something smouldering in it. Strong, who haunted the place in the evenings, received the news as a man receives a whiff of scorching when his roast has caught.

“He is at his old work again,” said Santiago, with the calm of one who has seen many reputations burnt to ashes and swept up next morning. “He and Moss Abrams were in my parlour. Moss sent out my boy for a stamp. It must have been a bill for fifty pound. I heard the Baronet tell Moss to date it two months back. He will pretend it is an old bill, and that he forgot it when he settled with my Lady. They will squeeze a little more out of her now he is clear.”

To have the habit of signing promises to pay is at least to have the satisfaction of knowing that one’s signature is passed round London like a questionable sausage at a cheap ordinary—everybody handles it, nobody knows what is in it, and the smell follows the owner.

Mr. Santiago’s shop stood near St. James’s Street, close to Major Pendennis’s lodgings in Bury Street. The Major, stepping delicately along as if his boots were afraid to make a sound on the pavement, met Strong striding toward him with wrath in his eye and Havana on his breath.

“Confound these young men,” thought the Major, sniffing as though smoke were an indecency like garlic at a duchess’s luncheon. “How they poison everything. Mustachios and cigars—low fellows. Oh! it’s Mr. Strong.”

He bowed with dignity, produced his polished key, and was about to slip into his own door and away from trouble, when Strong stopped him and held out a frank hand. The Major offered two reluctant fingers, as though charity were contagious.

“What is your good news?” the Major asked, in that tone by which he could make the commonest remark sound like a favour. “Still in town, Mr. Strong? I hope I see you well.”

“My news is bad news, sir,” Strong said, blunt as a cleaver. “It concerns our friends at Tunbridge Wells, and I should like to talk to you about it. Clavering is at his old tricks again, Major Pendennis.”

“Indeed! Pray come into my lodging,” cried the Major, suddenly hospitable; for nothing opens a prudent man’s door like a secret that may be useful.

Within the drawing-room, Strong poured out his indignation as one might empty a saucepan that has boiled over. He spoke of the Baronet’s recklessness and treachery, of oaths made on bended knees and broken standing up, of the Bible fetched to sanctify a lie, and the bell rung for Heaven’s witness to a fraud.

“No promises will bind him, sir,” Strong said. “You remember how he wanted to swear perdition on his soul if he ever gave another bill. He has been signing one this very day, sir. He will sign as many more as you please for ready money. He will deceive anybody—his wife, his child, his old friend who has backed him a hundred times. Why, there’s a bill of his and mine due next week.”

“I thought we had paid all,” the Major said, with a slight tightening of the mouth.

“Not that one,” Strong answered, colouring. “He asked me not to mention it, and—and—I had half the money for that, Major. They will be down on me. But I don’t care; I’m used to it. It’s Lady Clavering that riles me. It’s a shame that that good-natured woman—who has paid him out of gaol a score of times—should be ruined by his heartlessness. A parcel of bill-stealers, boxers—any rascals—get his money; and he don’t scruple to throw an honest fellow over. Would you believe it, sir—he took money of Altamont—you know whom I mean.”

“Indeed? of that singular man,” said Major Pendennis, whose face could have been carved in mahogany, “who I think came tipsy once to Sir Francis’s house? Who is Altamont, Mr. Strong?”

Strong stared, half astonished, half suspicious, as if the Major had asked what salt was. “I am sure I don’t know, if you don’t know,” he said.

“To tell you frankly,” the Major went on smoothly, “I have my suspicions—I suppose—mind, I only suppose—that in our friend Clavering’s life (who, between you and me, Captain Strong, is about as loose a fish as any in my acquaintance) there are some queer secrets and stories which he would not like to have known. None of us would. And very likely this fellow Altamont knows something against him, and has some hold, and gets money out of him. I know men of the best families in England paying through the nose in that way. But their private affairs are no business of mine, Mr. Strong. I do not pry. I am interested for Lady Clavering’s sake, and her daughter’s. When her ladyship asked me, I looked into her affairs, and tried to set them straight. I shall do so again if called upon—if called upon. And by the way—this Mr. Altamont—how is he? I believe you are acquainted with him. Is he in town?”

“I don’t know that I am called upon to know where he is, Major Pendennis,” Strong said, rising and taking up his hat with the offended stiffness of a man whose honesty has been patted on the head like a lap-dog.

The Major’s tone changed at once; his hauteur melted into a knowing good-humour, as a lump of sugar disappears in hot tea.

“Ah, Captain Strong, you are cautious too,” he said, smiling. “Quite right, my good sir—quite right. We don’t know what ears walls may have. There is no use throwing away your fire. You may have your ideas, I may put two and two together and have mine. But there are things that many a man had better not know until he has reason for knowing them—and that I believe is your maxim too. With regard to our friend the Baronet, I agree with you: it would be advisable he should be checked. I reprehend any man’s departure from his word, or any conduct giving pain to his family. That is my full and frank opinion, and I am sure it is yours.”

“Certainly,” said Strong, drily, as one says “certainly” when the weather is obviously wet.

“I am delighted to hear it,” the Major replied, as though unanimity were an elegant sauce. “Delighted. And I am exceedingly glad of this lucky meeting. Good evening. Thank you. Morgan, show the door to Captain Strong.”

Thus dismissed, Strong went out more puzzled than appeased, and Morgan, who had floated about the room as silently as smoke itself, was puzzled too—for he had caught fragments, names, hints, and the sharp clink of money behind them, as a man hears knives at table without seeing the dish.

“Who is that Altamont? Know anything about him and Strong?” Morgan asked Mr. Lightfoot at the Club, upon the first occasion when a valet could talk like a gentleman behind gentlemen’s backs.

“Strong’s his man of business,” Lightfoot answered. “Draws the Governor’s bills, indorses ’em, does his odd jobs. I suppose Altamont’s in it too. That kite-flying always takes two or three to set the paper going. Altamont put the pot on at the Derby and won a good bit. I wish the Governor could get some somewhere, and I could get my book paid up.”

“Do you think my Lady would pay his debts again?” Morgan asked, with a softness that was all arithmetic. “Find out that for me, Lightfoot, and I’ll make it worth your while.”

Major Pendennis had often said laughingly that Morgan was richer than himself. The joke had a bone in it. Morgan, with that valet’s genius for knowing everything without seeming to listen, had been gathering money as squirrels gather nuts—quietly, persistently, and always in other people’s woods. He had made acquaintance with valets of distinction, had learned their masters’ weaknesses as a cook learns where the joint is tender, and had turned knowledge into profit without ever appearing to have an opinion.

When Arthur came into his little property, Morgan had approached him with an air of meek domesticity and offered, with the simplicity of a man offering salt at dinner, to lend him money if ever he wanted it—a little sum, fifty or a hundred pounds, which Morgan wished to “lay out.” Would Mr. Arthur, who lived among lawyers and papers in the Temple, kindly advise an investment?

Arthur laughed, declined, and told his uncle; and the Major, amused and a little nettled, observed that Morgan had been getting devilish rich for a devilish long time—had even bought the house in Bury Street where his master lodged—and had done it, in part, by buying up Clavering paper on the sly, because he knew the Begum would pay.

On the day of Strong’s visit, that recollection came back to the Major like the aftertaste of some pungent relish. He bethought him: if anyone could find Sir Francis, it would be a man who tracked money by smell. So he sent for Morgan and rallied him with that free insolence which a certain class of gentleman practises as naturally as breathing.

“I hear you have got some money to invest, Morgan,” said the Major.

“It’s Mr. Arthur has been telling, hang him,” thought Morgan, keeping his face demure.

“I’m glad my place is such a good one.”

“Thank you, sir. I’ve no reason to complain of my place, nor of my master,” said Morgan.

“You’re a good fellow. I believe you are attached to me. I hope you’ll be prudent and not be taking a public-house or that kind of thing.”

A public-house! thought Morgan, with inward scorn. Me in a public-house! The old fool! If I was ten years younger, dammy, I’d sit in Parliament before I died. Aloud he said, “No, thank you kindly, sir. I don’t think of the public line, sir. And I’ve got my little savings pretty well put out, sir.”

“You do a little in the discounting way, eh, Morgan?” the Major asked, as if speaking of a harmless hobby like fishing.

“Yes, sir, a very little. I—I beg your pardon, sir—might I be so free as to ask a question—”

“Speak on, my good fellow,” said the Major, gracious as a man granting alms.

“About Sir Francis Clavering’s paper, sir. Do you think he’s any longer any good, sir? Will my Lady pay on ’em any more, sir?”

“What, you’ve done something in that business already?” the Major cried.

“Yes, sir, a little,” replied Morgan, lowering his eyes like a saint confessing to a sin that has paid interest. “And I don’t mind owning, sir—and I hope I may take the liberty of saying, sir—that a little more would make me very comfortable, if it turned out as well as the last.”

“Why, how much have you netted by him, in Gad’s name?” demanded the Major, half shocked, half impressed—as one might be on discovering that the quiet footman makes better sauces than the chef.

“I’ve done a good bit, sir, at it: that I own, sir. Having some information, and made acquaintance with the fam’ly through your kindness, I put on the pot, sir.”

“You did what?”

“I laid my money on, sir—I got all I could, and borrowed, and bought Sir Francis’s bills. Many of ’em had his name, and the gentleman’s as is just gone out, Edward Strong, Esquire, sir. And of course I know of the blow-hup and shindy as took place in Grosvenor Place, sir. And as I may as well make my money as another, I’d be very much obleeged to you if you’d tell me whether my Lady will come down any more.”

Major Pendennis was as astonished as if Morgan had suddenly produced a coronet from his pocket. To be sure, he was indignant too—indignant that a fellow in livery should grow rich under his nose, should handle sums and secrets, should have a private fortune like a gentleman and yet continue to answer a bell like a shadow. But the Major, who admired money as sincerely as he despised smoke, felt an involuntary respect creep into him; and, what was worse, a slight fear—such as men feel when they discover that the person they have been patronising has teeth.

“Well, Morgan,” he said at last, “I mustn’t ask how rich you are; and the richer the better for your sake, I’m sure. If I could give you information to serve you, I would. But frankly—if Lady Clavering asks me whether she shall pay any more of Sir Francis’s debts, I shall advise— and I hope she won’t—though I fear she will. That is all I know. And so you are aware Sir Francis is beginning again in his—eh—reckless course?”

“At his old games, sir—can’t prevent that gentleman. He will do it,” Morgan replied, with the calm of a man discussing the weather.

“Mr. Strong was saying that a Mr. Moss Abrams holds one of Sir Francis Clavering’s notes. Do you know anything of this Mr. Abrams—or the amount of the bill?”

“Don’t know the bill, know Abrams quite well, sir.”

“I wish you would find out about it for me,” the Major said; and then, lowering his voice with the relish of a man ordering a delicate dish, “I wish you would find out where I can see Sir Francis Clavering, Morgan.”

“Thank you, sir—yes, sir—I will, sir,” said Morgan, and glided away as noiselessly as he had entered, leaving the Major to sit and digest the new fact that his servant had been frying, turning, and serving a whole little financial stew under his very eyes.

Next morning Morgan returned with the promptness of a well-trained kitchen-boy bringing the dish when it must be hot. He had seen Mr. Abrams; he knew the amount of the bill that gentleman wished to “negotiate;” and he knew where Sir Francis would be found that very day at one o’clock.

“In the back-parlour of the Wheel of Fortune Tavern, sir,” Morgan said.

At one o’clock, sure enough, Sir Francis Clavering sat in that parlour—a descendant of an ancient line set down among spittoons and Windsor chairs, cheerful prints of boxers, trotting horses and pedestrians, and the stale tobacco-fumes of last night hanging about the room like a dirty curtain. Before him lay an old copy of Bell’s Life in London, blotched with beer, as if even the news had been drinking.

Into this temple of aristocratic retirement walked Major Pendennis, neat as a pin, scented as propriety, and looking as much out of place as a silver fork in a greasy chop-house.

“So it’s you, old boy?” cried Sir Francis, brightening for a moment, taking him—naturally—for money in person, and thinking Mr. Moss Abrams had arrived in human shape with the cash.

“How do you do, Sir Francis Clavering? I wanted to see you, and followed you here,” said the Major.

At the sight of him, the Baronet’s face fell—like a soufflé that hears footsteps.

Now that he had his man, the Major meant to strike quickly and not allow time for slinking out of the back door. He went into action with a sharpness that would have done credit to a cook catching a thief with his hand in the larder.

“I know,” he said, “who is the exceedingly disreputable person for whom you took me, Clavering; and the errand which brought you here.”

“It ain’t your business, is it?” Sir Francis answered, sulky and frightened at once, like a boy caught with jam on his fingers. “Why are you following me about and taking the command, and meddling in my affairs, Major Pendennis? I’ve never done you any harm, have I? I’ve never had your money. And I don’t choose to be dodged about in this way, and domineered over. I don’t choose it, and I won’t have it. If Lady Clavering has any proposal to make to me, let it be done in the regular way, and through the lawyers. I’d rather not have you.”
