The public acclaim for Edward's conduct was loud and enthusiastic, yet only Elinor and Marianne possessed the clarity to discern its true weight. They alone understood the narrow margin from which he had saved his honor, and the hollow consolation that remained to him—a mere internal acknowledgment of righteousness amidst the crushing loss of fortune and family. Elinor took pride in his unyielding integrity; Marianne, overwhelmed by pity for his suffering, granted him absolution for every transgression. Though the breach of trust between sisters was healed by this revelation, neither sought to dwell upon it in private. Elinor avoided the subject by principle, fearing that Marianne's fervent reassurances of Edward's enduring love would only anchor those feelings more deeply in her own mind, contrary to her desire to sever them. Conversely, Marianne's resolve crumbled under the weight of the comparison; conversing on the topic only intensified her self-loathing, highlighting the chasm between her own vacillating actions and Elinor's steadfast fortitude.

Marianne felt the sting of this disparity acutely, but not as a catalyst for redemption. Instead, it fueled a relentless tide of self-reproach, a bitter regret that she had never summoned the strength to act sooner. This realization offered only the agony of penitence, devoid of any hope for future correction. Her mental faculties were so eroded by despair that she deemed further effort impossible, a paralysis that only deepened her despondency.

For several days thereafter, no fresh news emerged regarding the turmoil in Harley Street or Bartlett's Buildings. Yet, Mrs. Jennings, armed with sufficient gossip to satisfy her curiosity without further investigation, had resolved from the outset to visit her cousins with inquiries of comfort. Only the unexpected influx of other guests delayed her departure until the third day following the initial disclosure.

This day proved to be a resplendent Sunday, the beauty of the spring sun drawing crowds to Kensington Gardens despite the calendar marking only the second week of March. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor ventured forth, but Marianne, aware that the Willoughbys had returned to town and haunted by the dread of encountering them, elected to remain indoors rather than risk exposure in such a public arena.

Shortly after entering the gardens, an old acquaintance of Mrs. Jennings joined them. Elinor welcomed the distraction, grateful that the woman engaged Mrs. Jennings in continuous chatter, leaving Elinor free for quiet contemplation. For a time, she observed nothing of the Willoughbys, nothing of Edward, and no one capable of stirring her emotions, whether through gravity or levity. Eventually, however, she was surprised to find herself accosted by Miss Steele. Though appearing somewhat bashful, Miss Steele radiated immense delight at the reunion. Encouraged by Mrs. Jennings' evident kindness toward her, Miss Steele temporarily abandoned her own entourage to merge with theirs.

Mrs. Jennings leaned close to whisper urgently, "Extract every detail from her, my dear. She will confess anything if you simply ask. I cannot leave Mrs. Clarke."

Fortune favored Mrs. Jennings' insatiable curiosity and Elinor's equally strong desire for truth, for Miss Steele possessed the peculiar trait of volunteering information without prompting. Without her, nothing would have been revealed.

"I am so delighted to see you," Miss Steele exclaimed, seizing Elinor's arm with familiar familiarity. "I needed to locate you above all others in the world." Lowering her voice conspiratorially, she added, "I presume Mrs. Jennings has heard the whole saga. Is she furious?"

"Not at all, I believe, with you."

"That is a blessing. And Lady Middleton—is *she* enraged?"

"It seems improbable that she could be."

"I am monstrously relieved. Good heavens! I have endured such torment! I have never witnessed Lucy in such a fury. She initially swore she would never trim a single bonnet or perform any service for me again, so long as she drew breath. But now she has completely recovered, and we are as cordial as ever. Observe, she fashioned this bow for my hat and inserted the feather last night. There now, *you* are about to mock me. But why shouldn't I wear pink ribbons? I don't care if it happens to be the Doctor's favorite hue. I am certain, for my part, I would never have deduced his preference if he hadn't explicitly stated it. My cousins have been relentlessly pestering me! I honestly struggle to determine which direction to gaze before them."

She drifted into a tangent where Elinor had no contribution to offer, prompting her to steer the conversation back to its original course.

"Very well, Miss Dashwood," she resumed triumphantly. "People may whisper whatever they please about Mr. Ferrars declaring he would not wed Lucy, but it is absolute nonsense, I assure you. It is a disgrace for such malicious rumors to circulate. Regardless of Lucy's personal sentiments, it was none of the public's business to assert certainty."

"I have never heard such suggestions implied," Elinor replied.

"Oh, truly? Well, they *were* spoken, clearly, by more than one individual. Miss Godby informed Miss Sparks that no rational person could expect Mr. Ferrars to abandon a woman like Miss Morton, possessing a fortune of thirty thousand pounds, for Lucy Steele, who owns nothing. I received this confirmation directly from Miss Sparks. Furthermore, my cousin Richard admitted that when the moment arrived, he feared Mr. Ferrars would retreat. When Edward vanished from our sight for three consecutive days, I was utterly bewildered. In my heart, I believed Lucy had resigned herself to defeat. We departed your brother's residence on Wednesday, and saw nothing of him throughout Thursday, Friday, and Saturday, unsure of his whereabouts. Lucy once attempted to write to him, but her spirits rebelled against the idea. However, this morning he appeared precisely as we returned from church, and the entire truth unfolded. He had been summoned to Harley Street on Wednesday, subjected to counsel from his mother and others, and had publicly declared before them all that he loved no one but Lucy, and that no one but Lucy would he consider marrying. Distressed by these proceedings, he mounted his horse immediately upon leaving his mother's house and rode into the countryside, somewhere or other. He remained at various inns throughout Thursday and Friday, deliberately attempting to regain his composure. After exhaustive deliberation, he concluded that, stripped of his fortune and possessing nothing, it would be cruel to prolong the engagement, as it would inevitably result in her ruin. With only two thousand pounds and no prospect of advancement—and considering his potential entry into the clergy, which would yield merely a curacy—they could not subsist. He could not bear the thought of her settling for less, so he implored her, if she shared his sentiment, to terminate the matter immediately and allow him to fend for himself. I heard him articulate this plainly. It was entirely for *her* sake, based solely on *her* circumstances, that he proposed separation, not out of any waning affection. I swear on my life he uttered not a syllable regarding weariness of her, nor any desire to marry Miss Morton or similar prospects. Naturally, Lucy refused to entertain such reasoning. She responded instantly—with considerable rhetoric concerning sweetness and love—you know, and all that sort of thing—Oh, la! one cannot recount such matters—that she harbored no intention whatsoever of withdrawing, for she could endure life with him on a trifling sum. Whatever meager amount he possessed, she would gladly accept it all, or words to that effect. Consequently, he was ecstatic, discussed their future plans at length, and they agreed he would enter holy orders immediately, postponing their marriage until he secured a benefice. At that juncture, I could hear no further, as my cousin summoned me from below to inform me that Mrs. Richardson had arrived in her carriage to transport one of us to Kensington Gardens. I was compelled to ascend and interrupt them to inquire if Lucy desired to accompany them, but she showed no wish to depart Edward's side. Thus, I hurried upstairs, donned a pair of silk stockings, and descended with the Richardsons."

"I fail to comprehend what you imply by 'interrupting them,'" Elinor remarked. "Were you not all situated in the same room?"

"No, indeed, not us. La! Miss Dashwood, do you genuinely believe people engage in courtship rituals when others are present? Oh, for shame!—You surely possess superior judgment. (Laughing artificially.) No, no; they were secluded in the drawing-room. All I perceived was by eavesdropping at the door."

"How!" Elinor cried. "Have you been recounting to me information acquired solely through spying? I regret knowing this beforehand; I certainly would not have permitted you to relay particulars of a conversation you ought not to have witnessed. How could you behave so unjustly toward your sister?"

"Oh, la! There is nothing improper in *that*. I merely stood at the threshold and heard what reached my ears. I am certain Lucy would have treated me identically; a year or two ago, during the confidences shared between Martha Sharpe and me, she never hesitated to conceal herself in a closet or behind a chimney-piece specifically to overhear our discourse."

Elinor attempted to pivot the discussion elsewhere, but Miss Steele proved incapable of suppressing the topics dominating her mind for more than a few moments.

"Edward speaks of proceeding to Oxford shortly," she noted. "Currently, he lodges at No.—, Pall Mall. What a vindictive woman his mother is, isn't she?
