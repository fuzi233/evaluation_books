At last the lean years were well and truly behind me, and I no longer needed to battle hand to hand with grinding poverty. The death of my little kinsman, the heir to the Warrington title and estate, turned all my worldly prospects on their head at a stroke. I was now next in line to a fine, thriving property; my uncle and his wife could not possibly have another child. “That woman would commit any crime to cross you,” Sampson swore roundly, but the truth was Lady Warrington harboured no such treachery in her grief. Crushed to the dust by the loss of her only son, she turned to the pious sect she had long half-inclined to, and her spiritual advisors taught her to see the tragedy as a just chastisement from Heaven, a call to submit to the divine will. “While your son lived, your heart was turned away from the better world,” her clergyman told her, “and your ladyship set far too much store by this earthly life. You desired rank and title for him, and you might well have won an earthly coronet. Of what use is it now, to one who has but a few short years left on this earth? What weight has it compared to the heavenly crown that is assuredly reserved for you?”

The accident caused no small stir across the county, and in the chapels of her new chosen faith, a dozen sermons were preached that alluded pointedly to the sad event. Far be it from me to question the path the bereaved mother took, or look on anything but with respect and sympathy for a soul in grief seeking the only refuge the world offers from sin, disappointment, and unendurable sorrow. A year after her loss, while she was staying in London, Lady Warrington sent word that she would receive me, and I waited on her at her lodgings. She delivered me a long, didactic homily on her own position and mine, marvelling aloud at the decree of Heaven that had punished her poor child’s disobedience (it seemed the little boy had snuck out hunting with his gun without his mother’s permission) by taking his life, and leaving the inheritance to benefit me. She prayed that if I ever did come into the property—though the Warringtons were, thank Heaven, a famously long-lived family, save for my own father whose life was cut short by the excesses of his dissolute youth—my present careless way of life might mend. She begged me to cast off my unworthy companions, sever all ties with the licentious theatre and its disreputable frequenters, and turn at last to the path that led to true peace, and the sacred duties she feared, very much feared, I had long neglected. She larded her speech with chapter and verse of Scripture, which I shall not trouble to repeat here. When I took my leave, she pressed a packet of printed sermons into my hands for my wife Theo, and a slim little hymn book written by Miss Dora, who would soon become a celebrated preacher in that circle, and three years after the dowager’s death in Bath, would marry the well-known young divine Mr. Juffles.

The poor lady had forgiven me, but she could not bear the sight of our first surviving son, Miles. When we lost our second child in infancy, however, my aunt and her daughter rushed to Theo’s side, full of gentle care, and even extended an invitation for us to stay at their country home. My uncle soon began to spend nearly every day at our small lodgings, sitting for hours staring at our baby boy, bringing him endless piles of toys and sweetmeats, and begging the child to call him “Godpapa.” Long before Theo knew the sting of losing a child of her own, her soft heart had already learned to pity the sorrowing old man. Mine, far more selfish, only learned that bitter sympathy through experience, and I was reconciled to my uncle at last over the tiny coffin of our lost little one.

The poor man sent his own private coach to follow our humble, unassuming funeral procession, and afterwards took young Miles out for a long airing. The boy chattered nonstop all the way, delighted with his smart new black mourning clothes and the novelty of the ride, quite forgetting any momentary grief he might have felt. That innocent prattle of his pierced Theo’s heart to the quick, though she would never wish that wound to heal. Even now, twenty-five years later, I know her face so well I can tell the exact moment she slips into thought of that little lost soul: it is not a sharp, raw grief for a parting decades past, but a quiet, soft communion with the child who waits for her in Heaven.

Soon afterwards we returned to our bright, sunlit lodgings in Bloomsbury. Young Miles, my once wild “bear cub” who would no longer answer to my leading, had struck up a fierce friendship with Charley Foker, and was sent off to Chartreux School, where Charley made sure he received no more beatings than were good for him, and where (thanks no doubt to his excellent private tutor, not to mention his own natural quickness) he soon took and kept a high place in his class. He loved the school so well that he swears to this day he will send his own son there, when the time comes.

I had no more time to act as my bear cub’s keeper, for other work claimed my attention. My uncle, who was such an obsequious supporter of the government that the Ministers never even thought to doubt his vote and loyalty, had long used all his influence on my behalf, and I was at last gazetted as one of His Majesty’s Commissioners for the Licensing of Hackney-Coaches, a post I filled with honour for many years, until a falling out with the Minister, which I shall recount in its proper place, saw me removed from the role. I took my formal law degree at the Temple too, and walked the halls of Westminster Hall in full barrister’s wig and gown. That same year, my good friend Mr. Foker had business to attend to in Paris, and I had the great pleasure of accompanying him. There I was welcomed with open arms by my dear old American preserver Monsieur de Florac, who introduced me to his noble family and a far wider circle of polite Parisian society than I ever had leisure to properly attend to; for I would not desert my good patron Foker, whose own acquaintance lay almost entirely among the city’s bourgeoisie, most notably Monsieur Santerre, a great Paris brewer who would later distinguish himself far more in bloodshed than in beer. Foker needed me as an interpreter, and I was only too glad to serve him, and pay back some of the great kindness he had shown me in my days of deepest want. Our ladies, meanwhile, stayed at Mr. Foker’s new villa at Wimbledon, and wrote to say they were vastly amused by the regular “Parisian letters” I sent them home, sent through the good offices of our distinguished friend Mr. David Hume, then at the British Embassy, and which would later be published in a neat bound volume.

While I went quietly about my small official duties in London, the first sparks of the great conflict that would end in the permanent separation of the American colonies from the mother country were beginning to catch fire. When Mr. Grenville first proposed the Stamp Act, I told Theo that the bill would stir up great discontent at home—for we English had always been determined to take as much as we could from the colonies, and pay back as little as possible—but I never anticipated the sheer white-hot anger the scheme would stoke across the Atlantic. It is as it is with all families and personal quarrels: the given pretext for the fight is trivial, but the real cause lies in years of petty bickering and long-simmering resentment. A hundred foolish exactions, tiny acts of petty tyranny, the habitual insolence of Englishmen towards every foreigner, every colonist, every soul who dared to think their own rivers and lands were as good as our own Abana and Pharpar: all of it stirred the natural spirit of men who had grown weary of our imperious bullying, and set Britain and her colonies on the path to war. The astonishing blunders of England’s governing class pushed that quarrel all the way to its final break, and for my part I have never once regretted it. Had I been in Virginia instead of London, it is more than likely I would have taken the colonial side, if for no other reason than plain opposition to that iron-willed mistress of Castlewood, Madam Esmond, who would have driven me to revolt just as surely as Parliament drove the Americans. Was the Stamp Act the true cause of the revolution? It was a tax no greater than the same sum paid cheerfully by every man in England. Ten years earlier, when the French were deep in our territory and we were begging for reinforcements and supplies from home, not a single colonist would have dreamed of protesting the tax. Do not all men see the tax gatherer as their natural enemy? Thousands of high-spirited, brave men took the rebel side in America, but thousands more joined the fight for profit, or for their own private reasons. I protest even now I can not say if my own sympathies were selfish or patriotic, or which side was truly in the right, or if both sides were not equally at fault. I know only that once the fight was joined, we English had no choice but to see it through, and after the first natural sting of losing the game, I for one bore no lasting rancour.

What was it that made my brother Harry write such fiery, flaming patriotic letters home from Virginia, a place he seemed loath to ever quit? My kind, dear, best of brothers had always been led by whoever stood nearest him: when we were together he followed my lead, for he had such an absurdly high opinion of my wit and wisdom that if I said the weather was fine, he would ponder the observation as if it was one of the sayings of the Seven Sages, and when I was gone he would follow the lead of some other local wiseacre. Who had inspired this rabid, noisy patriotism he was sending us in London? “He’s rebelling against Madam Esmond,” I told Theo. “He’s got some colonial influence leading him—perhaps some lady,” my wife hinted. Harry had never told us who that lady might be, and indeed begged me repeatedly in letters not to ever allude to the delicate subject in any open correspondence I sent him, “for Madam reads every scrap of post that comes to the house, and I don’t want to say a word about you-know-what until the proper moment comes,” he wrote. No brotherly affection could have been deeper than that his letters showed. When he heard, through the same source who had revealed the truth to him, that in the depths of my own poverty I had kept no more than a hundred pounds out of his aunt’s legacy, he was ready to mortgage the new estate he had just bought to make the whole sum good to me, and quarrelled openly with his mother more than once on my behalf, speaking his mind with a frankness I should never have dared to muster. Not until Madam Esmond turned on him in angry recriminations, complaining how she had toiled and saved for him all these years only to be repaid with ingratitude, did the poor fellow even learn that our mother had cut off my supplies entirely to advance his interests; and by the time that news reached him, the deeds on his new property were already signed, and I was thankfully long out of want.

Our thrifty parent at Castlewood taped, docketed, and stored away every single scrap of paper any of us ever wrote. We boys were far more careless about our own correspondence: I especially, who rather affected to look down on my younger brother’s scribbling attempts. But Theo is far less supercilious, and has kept no small number of Harry’s letters, along with those written by the angelic being we would soon come to welcome as our sister-in-law.

“To think of who he has chosen, when he might have had Hetty!” Theo cried, all but stamping her foot, when we received that famous letter in which Harry first told us the full name of his charmer.
“She was a pretty little maid when I left home, she may well be a perfect beauty now,” I remarked, reading over the longest letter Harry had ever written on private family affairs.
“But is she to compare to my Hetty?” my wife protested.
“We agreed long ago that Hetty and Harry would never have made a happy pair, my love,” I reminded her.
Theo gave me a soft kiss. “My dear, I still wish they had tried,” she sighed. “I was afraid Hetty would have led him entirely, you see—though she does have the far sharper head. But from what I read here, it seems this new lady has taken full command of poor Harry already.” She handed me the letter, written in Harry’s scrawling, bold hand:
> My dearest George, you have had hints in my previous letters to understand how a certain lady has conquered my heart, which I have given away entirely in exchange for something infinitely more valuable: her own. She is sitting right beside me as I write this, and if there is none of the bad spelling you used to laugh at so often, it’s because I have my pretty little dictionary right to hand, who never makes a mistake even in the longest word, nor in anything else I know of: I do declare she is perfect.
> Seeing as Madam Esmond reads every letter you send, I told you not to drop the smallest hint of this delicate matter before, but now it is no secret at all, all the county knows it. You are not the only one in our family who made a secret marriage and got scolded for it by our mother. As a dutiful younger brother, I followed your example, and now I can tell you how this great event came to pass.
> I had not been home long before I knew my fate was sealed. I will not tell you how beautiful Miss Fanny Mountain had grown since I left home for Europe. She says I never really thought that, and I am glad, for she is the only thing in this whole world I would grudge even to my dearest brother.
> It is a miracle neither Madam Esmond nor her own mother Mrs. Mountain saw our attachment growing—you can only account for it by saying love makes everyone else blind. My love for my Fanny only grew stronger when I saw how harshly Madam treated her, for Madam was very rough and haughty with the girl, and my Fanny bore it all with a sweetness that is nothing short of angelic (though she will scold me for writing such “nonsense” when she reads this). She was scarcely treated better than a servant in the house—indeed, our enslaved negroes can speak far more freely before Madam Esmond than Fanny ever dared to.
> And yet my Fanny says she does not regret Madam’s unkindness at all, for without it, I might never have woken up to what she was to me. Oh, dear brother! When I remember all your great goodness to me: how when I was in the depths of want you paid my debts and dragged me out of prison, how you lived through all that poverty that never would have happened if not for my own folly, how you could easily have paid yourself back every penny I owed you and chose not to, putting my advantage ahead of your own comfort— I am lost in wonder at your kindness, and ought I not to be thankful to Heaven for giving me both such a wife and such a brother?
> When I wrote asking you to send me my aunt’s legacy money, for which I had the most profitable, urgent use possible, I never had the slightest idea you were suffering poverty yourself. That you, the head of our family, would stoop to be tutor to a brewer’s son! That you would write work for booksellers, unless your own genius prompted you to it! That never once crossed my mind, until Mr. Foker’s letter came out to us, a letter Madam had kept secret and never shown me, until we fell out over this whole business.
> Poor Tom Diggle’s plantation and all his negroes were up for sale, after he lost everything gambling, just like his father before him. Madam Esmond saw at once what a great chance this was to make a proper provision for me: for six thousand pounds, to cover the farm, the land, and all the stock, I would own a property as fine as most younger sons in this whole colony could ever hope for. It lies just a few miles outside Richmond, between Kent and Hanover Court House. The house itself is nothing grand compared to our old Castlewood, but the soil is rich, and the air is so healthy no one ever falls ill there.
> Madam said it was a chance that would never come again. By selling my army commission, plus her own life’s savings, we could pay more than half the price, and borrow the rest on mortgage—though money is so scarce out here it would have been hard to come by, and cost us a fortune in interest. Just at that very moment, with that new relation of ours Mr. Van den Bosch bidding against us, his agent fuming that we outbid him, your aunt’s legacy turned up most opportunely. Now I am the owner of a fine house and my own negroes here in my native Virginia, I will no doubt be elected to our House of Burgesses soon, and I hope to welcome my dearest brother and all his family under my own roof one day. To sit by my own fireside, ride my own horses after my own hounds, is far better than soldiering, now the war is over and there are no more Frenchmen left to fight. Indeed, Madam Esmond made leaving the army and settling at home one of her conditions, when she handed over her life’s savings, all 1750 pounds of them. She said she had already lost one son who chose to write plays and live in England—she wanted the other one to stay at home with her.
> But no sooner had we finished the purchase of the estate, and sent the papers off to sell my commission, than my mother tried to force me to marry a lady of her own choosing, who was not at all to my taste. You remember Miss Betsy Pitts from Williamsburgh? She did not improve at all after her face was dreadfully scarred by smallpox, and though Madam swears the young lady has every virtue under the sun, I must say her virtues never suited me. Her eyes don’t look straight, and one of her legs is shorter than the other—and oh, brother! Did you never notice Fanny’s ankles when we were boys? I never saw neater ones even at the Opera in London.
> When we had agreed I was leaving the army, that certain dear girl (can you guess her name?) one day, when we were all alone, burst into tears of pure joy that I was so touched by it I hardly knew what to do.
