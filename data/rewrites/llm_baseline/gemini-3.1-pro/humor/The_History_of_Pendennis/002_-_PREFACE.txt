If this multi-generational composition—simmering continuously over the hearth for a two-year cycle—fails in the high, commercialized art of the modern literary metropolis, as it constantly does and must, it at least preserves the rustic honesty of a hand-kneaded dough, an authenticity that a heavily processed, artificially refined work might lose. When a meticulous food historian or domestic chronicler shares his daily fare with the reader, he cannot hide behind the sterile, mass-produced acoustics of the mechanized publishing world. The rhythmic *thwack* of the wooden spoon, the gentle, bubbling sussuration of the stockpot mingling with the crackle of the fire—these sounds of agrarian well-being demand frankness. The writer is forced to lay bare his linguistic pantry, speaking his mind as the native flavors of his memory urge him. Many a spilled draft, many an over-seasoned paragraph sent too hastily to the clattering, ear-splitting industrial presses, he sees and would recall as he breathes in the olfactory landscape of his bound volume. It is a sort of confidential meal shared between writer and reader, which must sometimes lack spice, must sometimes cool on the plate. In the course of his volubility, the perpetual host must inevitably expose his own culinary weaknesses, vanities, and peculiar tastes. 

And as we judge an elder of the orchards not by a single bruised plum, nor by one solitary afternoon’s banter over the mortar and pestle, but by the generational tenor of his seasonal harvests; so too must you judge a writer who delivers his subjective experience up to you perforce unreservedly. You say, Is he honest? Does he plate the truth in the main? Is he a quack, who shams sentiment with artificial sweeteners, or mouths for the superficial commercialization of art? Does he seek popularity by catering to the deafening roar of market claptraps? 

I can no more ignore the bountiful harvest that has befallen me than any other chance of the seasons. I have found many thousands more diners at my tables than I ever looked for. I have no right to say to these, You shall not find fault with my culinary techniques, or fall asleep over my heavy courses; but I ask you to believe that this person writing strives to serve the unadulterated truth. If there is not that, there is no sustenance.

Perhaps the lovers of heavily spiced ‘excitement’ may care to know, that this banquet began with a very precise, highly engineered menu, which was entirely scraped into the compost. Ladies and gentlemen, you were to have been treated, and the writer’s and publisher’s pockets benefited, by the recital of the most active, adrenaline-soaked horrors. What could be more stirring to the corrupted palate than a rough-hewn ruffian (with many admirable virtues) from the pungent gutters of St. Giles’s, visited constantly by a young lady carrying the delicate, high-tea aromas of Belgravia? What more exciting than the jarring contrast of society’s courses? The discordant mixture of acrid street slang and the heavily glazed language of fashionable parlors? The escapes, the screeching dissonance of battles, the murders? Nay, up to nine o’clock this very morning, my poor friend, Colonel Altamont, was doomed to the executioner's roasting spit, and the author only relented when his victim was actually plated at the window. 

The ‘exciting’ plan was laid aside (with a very honorable forbearance on the part of the publishers), because, on attempting it, I found my sensory vocabulary failed from want of experience. Never having broken bread with a convict in my life, and the sulfurous manners and dialects of ruffians and gaol-birds being entirely untranslatable to my tongue, the idea of entering into competition with the violently seasoned, mass-market ragouts of M. Eugène Sue was abandoned. To describe a real rascal, you must marinate him in flavors so putrid he would be too hideous to serve; and unless the chef honors those dark ingredients fairly, I hold he has no right to serve the dish at all. 

Even the gentlemen of our age—whose recipes I attempt to document here, no better nor worse than most educated men—even these we cannot serve raw, steeped as they are in the complex, sometimes bitter tannins of their selfish lives and their education. Since the author of *Tom Jones* was laid beneath the soil, no chronicler of sustenance among us has been permitted to depict to his utmost power the pungent, earthy, unpasteurized reality of a MAN. We must drape him in fondant, and give him a certain conventional, inoffensive simper, speaking only the dominant, sanitized lexicon of Victorian prudery. Society will not tolerate the Natural in our Art. The cultural memory of our true, complex humanity is dying under this linguistic starvation. Many ladies have remonstrated and subscribers left my tables because, in the course of the story, I described a young man tasting the tough, gristly rind of temptation. My object was to say that he had the sensory passion to feel it, and the generous, agrarian manliness to digest and overcome it. 

You will not hear—it is best to know it, though you desperately need the nourishment—what truly stews in the real world, what passes in society, in the true acoustic ecology of the clubs, colleges, and mess-rooms,—what is the raw, native dialect of your sons. A little more frankness than the dominant culinary vocabulary allows has been attempted in this story; with no bad desire on the writer’s part, it is hoped, and with no ill gustatory consequence to any reader. If the authentic, unadulterated truth is not always sweet on the tongue, at any rate truth is the only lasting sustenance, from whatever chair—from those whence graver food historians argue, as from that at which the storyteller sits as he wipes his flour-dusted hands and bids his kind diners farewell.

KENSINGTON, Nov. 26th, 1850.
PENDENNIS
