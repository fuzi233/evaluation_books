The cold March wind of 1886 bit through my tunic as I stood on the edge of the Hudson valley bluff, and below me the great river coiled like the silver, silent ghost of a long-dead waterway. For ten long, lonely years I had stood here, arms outstretched to the glinting red pinprick of Mars hanging high in the night sky, begging my old patron, the god of war, to drag me back across the void to the lost Dejah Thoris I had left behind on that dying, red world. Not since that other March night twenty years prior, outside a hidden Arizona cave where my own earthly body lay locked in a death-like trance, had I felt that same unshakable, thrumming pull that once wrenched me across interplanetary space.
I stood bareheaded, straining toward Mars’ lurid glow, when a wave of nausea crashed over me. My senses swam, my knees buckled, and I pitched forward so close to the bluff edge I could feel the cold air of the hundred-foot drop on my cheeks.
The paralysis hit instantly, exactly as it had in that Arizona cave. My muscles locked tight, useless to my commands, and for a split second I swore I could hear the scrape and low, hungry moans of the unnameable thing that had lurked in that cave’s dark recesses so long ago. I poured every last ounce of my superhuman will into breaking the invisible bonds holding me, and then—*click*—the sharp, clear snap of a taut wire parting, and I was standing naked and free beside my own still, lifeless earthly corpse.
I did not waste a glance on the body I was leaving behind. I lifted my hands to Mars, and waited.
The void swallowed me faster than thought. For a single, unbearable instant I was adrift in utter, freezing darkness, and then I blinked awake beneath a burning bronze sun, lying on a carpet of close-cropped, blood-red grass beneath the dome of a forest unlike any I had ever seen on my previous trips to Barsoom.
My throat tightened in sudden, cold fear: I had missed Mars, I thought, been thrown blind across the trackless wastes of space to some alien, uncharted world. The trees around me bore blooms so huge and vivid no earthly word could describe their color, winged silent shapes darted through the branches that bore no resemblance to any bird or beast I knew, and past the treeline glinted the impossible blue of an open sea—something no Barsoom explorer had ever documented on its parched, dying sea bottoms.
I tried to push myself up, and forgot. The low gravity of the planet sent me flying three feet into the air, and I face-planted straight back into the soft red grass. The silly, familiar mishap made me grin. This *was* Mars, or an unknown corner of it. After ten years of wandering its vast deserts I had only explored a tiny fraction of its surface, after all. I got to my feet again, quickly recalibrating my Earth-born, gravity-strengthened muscles to the light, thin Barsoomian air, and started down the gentle slope toward the sea.
The whole grove was tended like a king’s private park: the grass was clipped as tight as an English nobleman’s lawn, the trees pruned smooth to a uniform fifteen feet off the ground, so the forest stretched around me like a vast cathedral with a ceiling woven of leaves and blooms. Their hundred-foot-wide trunks were polished as smooth as a new piano, in hues of polished ebony, bone white porcelain, electric azure, scarlet, and deep violet. I assumed I had landed in the territory of a peaceful, civilized red Martian settlement, and that soon enough I would be welcomed with all the honor due my rank as Prince of the House of Tardos Mors.
I was almost at the treeline edge, where the meadow opened out to the sea, when the romantic glow of the beautiful landscape vanished entirely.
A score of grotesque, man-sized figures were grazing in the meadow, near the banks of a wide, slow-moving river that poured out of the foot of a sheer line of towering cliffs. The tallest stood twelve feet high, their torsos and legs shaped exactly like a human’s, but their arms were boneless, sinuous, coiling like an elephant’s trunk as they moved their strange hands over the red grass.
I ducked behind a massive polished scarlet trunk to watch, and one of the creatures wandered close enough to get a full look at. Its hairless skin was a ghoulish, sickly blue, ringed with a band of white around its single bulging eye—a dead, milky orb with no pupil, no iris, all of it pale, blind white. Its face had no mouth, only a raw, ragged circular hole of a nose that looked exactly like a fresh, unbled bullet wound. Its “hair” was a tangled mass of eight-inch-long jet-black worms, wriggling and crawling across its scalp as if each one had a mind of its own. Its feet were broad, three feet long, flat as paddles, and a six-foot blade-tipped tail trailed behind it, held at a right angle to the ground. It was not grazing with its face, I realized: the palms of its hands had mouths, lined with razor talons that snipped the grass, sucking the vegetation up through the hollow, throats that ran the length of its boneless arms. Dangling from its armpits on thin little living stems were two tiny, perfectly formed replicas of itself, no more than six inches long.
I was half-moved to step out of hiding and approach the herd, curious to test their temper, when a high, shrill shriek cut through the quiet, coming from the direction of the cliffs. Every single wriggling “hair” on the plant men’s heads snapped upright, stiff as wires. Those weren’t hair, I realized. They were ears— a thousand tiny, living ears, the last leftover trait of that strange, ancient Barsoomian race that had sprung first from the planet’s original Tree of Life.
The largest of the creatures, their leader, purred a low resonant hum from the mouth in his palm, and the whole herd bounded off toward the cliffs in 20 and 30 foot leaps, moving faster than any kangaroo on Earth. Curiosity burning, I gave chase, my own Earth muscles sending me bounding even farther across the meadow than they could go.
I ducked behind a giant weathered boulder at the base of the cliffs, and the full scene broke over my view. The plant men had surrounded a tiny party of six green Martians—two men, four women, adorned in the war ornaments of different, mutually hostile hordes. For them to stand back to back fighting a common enemy was unheard of, save for that one historic battle when Tars Tarkas of Thark had united a hundred and fifty thousand green warriors to storm Zodanga and rescue Dejah Thoris.
They were all armed with longswords and daggers, but no firearms. Before they could make a coordinated move, the plant man leader bounded ten feet over their heads, sweeping his blade tail down so hard he crushed one green warrior’s skull like an eggshell. The rest of the herd circled at bewildering speed, screeching their purring cries to confuse their prey, and two more green Martians died to the tails of leaping attackers.
Only one green warrior and two women were left, and they would have been dead in seconds if the warrior had not learned fast. As two more plant men charged, he swung his longsword in a clean, perfect arc that sliced one creature open from chin to groin. The other’s tail caved in the skulls of the two remaining women, and the lone green warrior cut a swath straight through the herd, running for the shelter of the distant forest.
Acting on the same old battle impulse that had served me well through a hundred Barsoomian fights, I sprinted from my hiding spot, snatched a longsword from the hand of a dead green warrior, and gave chase, the old battle-lust burning hot in my chest.
I caught up to the herd before they even noticed I was there. Four of the plant men dropped dead under my blade before they realized a new foe had joined the fight. The lone green warrior saw his opening, bounded to my side, and fought back to back with me, his longsword swinging in endless, looping figure-eight strokes that cut through plant man flesh, bone, and the thin metal ornaments on their bodies as if they were thin air.
The plant men’s thick, sticky sap oozed over us, green and thick as the juice from a crushed caterpillar, splattering our skin and faces. One of the monsters leapt on my back, its talons sinking deep into my shoulders, the moist sucking mouths on its palms latching to my wounds to drink my blood. The green warrior tore it off my back with a single, lightning fast sweep of his blade.
We held our ground, backs pressed to the boulder to keep the plant men from leaping over our heads to strike with their tails, and we were cutting through the last of the nearby herd when that shrill shriek blared out again. I looked up.
On a narrow natural balcony carved into the cliff face, a lone human figure was standing, shrieking the call over and over, waving one hand toward the mouth of the distant river to summon backup. I followed his gaze. Hundreds of the bounding, hideous plant men were swarming toward us from every direction, pouring out of the forest, across the meadow, and over the far banks of the river. We were outnumbered a hundred to one.
“It will be a great death,” I said to my companion. I nodded toward the horde rushing us.
He smiled, his tusks glinting in the sun. “We may at least die fighting as great warriors should, John Carter.”
I froze. No one on Barsoom who I had not known intimately would call me by my Earth name, not here, not ten years after I had vanished from the red planet. I stared at the green warrior beside me, at his familiar, broad-shouldered frame, at the proud, steady light in his lateral eyes, at the great battle scars I recognized across his chest.
Standing there covered in sticky plant man sap, his longsword still dripping with the gore of the monsters we had slain, was the greatest of all Barsoom’s green men. My oldest, truest friend. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.
