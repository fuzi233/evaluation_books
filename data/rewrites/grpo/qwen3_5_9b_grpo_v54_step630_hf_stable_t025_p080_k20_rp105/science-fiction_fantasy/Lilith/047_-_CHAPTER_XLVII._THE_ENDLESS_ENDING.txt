The search for Lona remains fruitless, yet Mara's presence anchors me. She has imparted wisdom, and her lessons deepen with every passing hour. A shadow of doubt lingers: was that final awakening merely another layer of the dream? Am I still trapped within the chamber of death, suspended in slumber because I am not yet ready to rise? Perhaps I did not surrender fully to sleep, waking prematurely before the true dawn. If that awakening was itself a fabrication, then it must be a prelude to a far superior reality, a false vision guarding a lovelier truth beneath its surface.

In these moments of uncertainty, I cry out, "Could God Himself create such exquisite things as I dreamed?"
"Whence then came thy dream?" asks Hope.
"From the depths of my darkness, rising into the light of my consciousness."
"But whence first into thy dark self?" Hope presses.
"My brain was its mother, and the fever coursing through my blood its father."
"Speak rather," Hope suggests gently, "that your brain was the violin from which the music issued, and the fever the bow that drew the strings. But who crafted the violin? Who guided the bow across those strings? Consider again: who placed the songbirds upon the branches of the Tree of Life, startling them into song in their proper order? Where did the fantasia originate? And where did the life that danced to it come from? Did you, in the abyss of your own unconscious, speak the command, 'Let beauty be; let truth seem!' and instantly beauty existed, while truth remained only a semblance?"

Man dreams and desires; God broods, wills, and brings life into being.
When a man dreams his own dream, he is merely the plaything of his imagination; but when Another grants him the dream, that Other holds the power to fulfill it.

I have never again sought the reflection in the mirror. The hand that sent me back has barred the door; I will not step through that threshold again. "All the days of my appointed time," I resolve, "I shall wait until my change comes."

Occasionally, glancing at my books, they seem to tremble as if a wind ripples through their solid forms, hinting that another world is poised to burst forth. Sometimes, when I walk among the living, the heavens and the earth, the trees and the grass, appear to shake as though about to dissolve; yet lo, they settle once more into their familiar faces. At times, I hear whispers surrounding me, as if beloved ones speak of me, but when I strive to catch the words, they vanish, leaving profound silence. I cannot discern if these sensations arise within my mind or enter from without. I do not chase them; they arrive, and I let them pass.

Strange, dim memories, refusing to be pinned down, gaze upon me through misty windows of the past even in broad daylight. Yet, I no longer dream. It may be that when I am most awake, I am merely dreaming more deeply! But when I finally awaken into that life which, like a mother carrying her child, holds this existence within its bosom, I shall know with absolute certainty that I have woken, and doubt shall cease forever.

I wait. Whether asleep or awake, I wait.
As Novalis wrote, "Our life is no dream, but it should and will perhaps become one."
