"I suppose you'll be packing up shortly now that Full Term is over, Professor," remarked a stranger seated beside the Professor of Ontography at a banquet in the hospitable hall of St James's College.

The Professor was young, immaculate, and spoke with surgical precision.

"Yes," he replied, "my associates have pressured me into taking up golf this term. I intend to travel to the East Coast—specifically Burnstow—for a week or ten days to refine my technique. I aim to depart tomorrow."

"Oh, Parkins," interjected his neighbor on the other side, "if you're heading to Burnstow, I'd appreciate it if you inspected the site of the Templars' preceptory. Let me know if you deem it viable for excavation this summer."

It was, naturally, a man of antiquarian bent speaking, yet since he appeared only in this prologue, detailing his credentials serves no purpose.

"Certainly," said Parkins, the Professor. "If you outline the location, I'll provide a preliminary assessment of the terrain upon my return. Alternatively, I could correspond with you if you specify your probable whereabouts."

"Don't bother with correspondence, thanks. I'm planning to bring my family in that direction during the Long Vacation. Given that few English preceptories have ever been properly documented, I figured I might find an opportunity to contribute something useful on my spare days."

The Professor scoffed slightly at the notion that mapping a preceptory constituted utility. His neighbor persisted.

"The site—doubtless nothing remains above ground—must lie quite close to the beach now. The sea has advanced significantly along that stretch of coastline. Based on the maps, I estimate it's roughly three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn at the town's northern extremity. Where do you intend to lodge?"

"Well, *at* the Globe Inn, as a matter of fact," Parkins admitted. "I've secured a room there. Most boardinghouses are shuttered for the winter, and apparently, the only accommodation available is a double-bedded suite. They claim they lack space to store the second bed properly. However, I require a spacious chamber to transport my library and conduct research. While I wouldn't relish sharing a study with an empty bed—and certainly not two beds—I suppose I can endure the inconvenience for this brief excursion."

"Do you consider accommodating an extra guest 'roughing it,' Parkins?" asked a boisterous figure opposite. "Let me assure you, I shall descend and occupy it temporarily. It will serve as excellent companionship."

The Professor stiffened but maintained a veneer of courtesy.

"By all means, Rogers; I would welcome the company. However, I fear you would find it monotonous. Do you play golf?"

"No, thank Heaven!" retorted the rude Mr. Rogers.

"Well, when I am not composing, I shall likely be traversing the links, which, as I stated, might prove tedious for someone without a club."

"Oh, I don't know! There's surely someone I recognize in the place. But, of course, if you prefer solitude, speak the word, Parkins; I shall not be offended. Truth, as you invariably remind us, is never offensive."

Parkins was, indeed, scrupulously polite and rigorously truthful. It is regrettable that Mr. Rogers occasionally exploited this trait. Within Parkins's breast, a conflict raged, momentarily silencing his response. When the pause ended, he declared:

"Well, if you demand absolute candor, Rogers, I was weighing whether the aforementioned room offers sufficient space for two adults comfortably. Furthermore, and please note I would not have voiced this had you not pressed me, I question whether your presence would not constitute a hindrance to my scholarly endeavors."

Rogers erupted in laughter.

"Bravo, Parkins!" he exclaimed. "All is settled. I pledge not to interrupt your work; do not fret. No, I shall not come if you decline; but I reasoned it would be delightful to keep the specters at bay." He winked and nudged his adjacent neighbor. Parkins flushed visibly. "Forgive me, Parkins," Rogers continued, "I erred. I forgot you disdain levity regarding such matters."

"Well," Parkins conceded, "since you broached the subject, I freely admit I harbor no fondness for careless discourse concerning what you term ghosts. A man in my position cannot afford to appear complicit in prevailing superstitions. As you know, Rogers, or should by now understand, given I have never obscured my stance—"

"No, you certainly have not, old man," Rogers whispered sotto voce.

"—I maintain that any semblance, any appearance of concession to the belief that such entities exist, equates to a renunciation of all principles I hold sacred. Yet, I fear I have failed to capture your attention."

"Your *undivided* attention, was what Dr. Blimber actually *said*," Rogers interrupted, feigning earnest precision. "[4]"

[4] Mr. Rogers was incorrect, *vide Dombey and Son*, Chapter XII.

"Not at all," Parkins dismissed. "I recall no Blimber; perhaps he predates my tenure. But I need not elaborate. I trust you grasp my meaning."

"Yes, yes," Rogers hastened, "precisely so. We shall dissect this fully at Burnstow, or wherever fate decrees."

In recounting this dialogue, I endeavored to convey the impression Parkins made upon me: a figure reminiscent of an elderly hen, utterly devoid of humor, yet dauntless and sincere in his convictions, meriting profound respect. Whether the reader discerned this essence is irrelevant; this was the character of Parkins.

The following day, Parkins successfully departed the college and arrived in Burnstow. He was welcomed at the Globe Inn and installed in the large double-bedded room previously described. Before retiring, he meticulously arranged his materials in perfect order upon a commodious table occupying the outer end of the chamber, surrounded on three sides by windows facing the sea. The central window gazed directly outward to the ocean, while those to the left and right commanded views along the shoreline to the north and south respectively. To the south lay the village of Burnstow. To the north, no structures were visible, only the beach and the low cliffs backing it. Immediately foregrounded was a narrow strip of rough grass, scattered with ancient anchors and capstans, followed by a broad path and finally the beach. Regardless of the original distance between the Globe Inn and the shore, no more than sixty yards now separated them.

The remainder of the inn's clientele consisted primarily of golfers, comprising few individuals requiring special description. The most prominent figure was undoubtedly an *ancien militaire*, secretary of a London club, possessing a voice of formidable volume and holding distinctly Protestant views. These opinions often found expression following his attendance at services led by the Vicar, an estimable man inclined toward picturesque ritual, whom he gallantly suppressed as much as possible out of deference to East Anglian tradition.

Professor Parkins, characterized principally by his pluck, spent the majority of the day after arrival attempting to improve his game alongside this Colonel Wilson. During the afternoon—whether attributable to the exertion of practice or otherwise, I remain uncertain—the Colonel's demeanor adopted such a lurid hue that even Parkins recoiled at the prospect of returning home with him from the links. Determined, after a fleeting glance at the bristling moustache and inflamed features, that it would be wiser to allow the soothing effects of tea and tobacco to temper the Colonel before dinner rendered a confrontation inevitable, Parkins resolved to avoid him.

"I might walk home tonight along the beach," he reflected. "Yes, and inspect the ruins Disney mentioned. I do not precisely recall their location, but I expect I shall stumble upon them inevitably."

This endeavor proved literally accurate. Picking his way from the links to the shingle beach, his foot caught on a gorse root and a substantial stone, sending him tumbling. Rising to survey his surroundings, he found himself in a patch of uneven ground marked by small depressions and mounds. Upon closer examination, these proved to be masses of flints embedded in mortar and cloaked in turf. He correctly deduced he stood upon the site of the preceptory he had promised to investigate. It seemed promising for the explorer's spade; sufficient foundation remained at shallow depths to illuminate the general layout. Recalling vaguely that the Templars, owners of this site, customarily constructed buildings in circular formations, he noted that a specific series of mounds nearby appeared arranged accordingly. Few can resist the temptation to engage in amateur research outside their expertise, driven solely by the satisfaction of proving hypothetical competence. Our Professor, if he harbored such a petty desire, was equally eager to oblige Mr. Disney. Thus, he carefully paced the circular area, recording its rough dimensions in his pocket-book. He then examined an oblong eminence lying east of the circle's center, which he surmised served as the base of a platform or altar. At its northern end, a patch of turf had been removed by some creature of *ferae naturæ*. Deeming it prudent to probe the soil for evidence of masonry, he produced his knife and began scraping away the earth.

Another discovery ensued: as he scraped, a portion of soil collapsed inward, revealing a small cavity. He lit match after match to illuminate the nature of the hole, but the wind extinguished them all.
