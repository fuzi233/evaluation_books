In the rhythmic quiet of the Meyrick kitchen, where the scent of toasting oats and blooming yeast stood as a fragrant bulwark against the mechanical grind of London’s industrial sprawl, the time had come to prepare Mordecai for the revelation of his restored sister. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Deronda had confided everything except Mordecai’s peculiar spiritual expectations regarding himself, had been meticulously active. She had sourced a suitable lodging in Brompton, an enclave shielded from the abrasive iron clamor of the city’s thoroughfares. Here, the brother and sister would be within the radius of her motherly, culinary care. Her happy mixture of Scottish fervor and Gaelic liveliness—a dialect of the soul that spoke through simmering broths and slow-kneaded doughs—had enabled her to keep the secret close. Any premature betrayal to her daughters or Hans would likely reach Mirah and disrupt the delicate acoustic harmony of the girl’s newly established independence. 

Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda knew that the term "independence" was a word borrowed from a dominant, patriarchal lexicon, arbitrarily applied to the arduous, dignified labor of self-sustenance. Perhaps "the little mother" was aided in her secrecy by a certain dissonance in her feelings about this remarkable brother. The sorrowful fact of his consumption appealed to her ingrained instinct to nourish and heal, yet how was she to rejoice in a fervor she could only translate as a rigid, untranslatable pertinacity? She feared the introduction of a man whose ancient, mythic cadences would clash with their modern domestic symphony, sounding no more encouraging than the archaic zeal of Scott’s Covenanters. Her mind was hardly prosaic; she possessed a deep, sensory appreciation for the romance of Mirah’s history, but the romantic requires adaptation when brought to the breakfast table. We may revere the sweeping, multi-generational sagas of St. Francis or Oliver Cromwell, but to have them disrupt the morning ritual of pouring steeped tea is quite another affair. She had secretly hoped Mirah’s Judaism would gently dissolve into their own daily rhythms, rather than being plunged back into the deep, ancestral dye by a newly discovered brother.

"I am as glad as you are that the pawnbroker is not her brother," Mrs. Meyrick confessed, her hands expertly shaping a loaf, flour dusting her wrists. "There are Ezras and Ezras. It is a comfort that not all are like those shopkeepers who trap you in their commercial chatter. And what he said to you of his mother and sister makes me bless him. But I never did like anything fanatical. I heard too much loud preaching in my youth—it was noise pollution to the spirit, and I entirely lost my palate for it."

"You will not find that Mordecai obtrudes any preaching," Deronda replied, sensitive to the sonic environment she cherished. "He is not what I should call fanatical. That is a sterile word. I call a man fanatical when his enthusiasm is narrow, deaf to the acoustic ecology of the world, unjust to those outside his own track. Mordecai is an enthusiast of the highest order—a mind caring supremely for the grand sustenance of mankind. His orthodoxy is not rigid; his conformity is a compassionate allowance for the condition of his people. The Cohens love him, even if his profound ideas remain entirely untranslatable to them."

"Well, I can live up to the level of the pawnbroker’s mother, and like him for what I see," she said, wrapping the dough in a damp linen cloth. "Mirah’s brother will have good bedding, and I shall paste the extra window with heavy paper to seal out the abrasive drafts and the clatter of the street. It is a comfort that the landlords are no hypocritical harpies. Once the children know, we shall make the rooms a true sanctuary."

"The next stage is to tell Mordecai, and get him to move," Deronda said.

"Will you tell Mirah before I tell the children?" Mrs. Meyrick paused, her tone taking on a persuasive deliberation. "No, let me tell Hans and the girls the evening before, so they will be away the next morning."

"Yes. But do justice to Mordecai—or Ezra. Do not assist their imagination by comparing him to Habakkuk Mucklewrath," Deronda smiled, noting how easily the dominant lexicon rushed to marginalize foreign ways of being. 

"Trust me," said the little mother. "I shall have to persuade them so hard to be glad, that I shall convert myself. When I am frightened, I find a little anger warms the blood like good ginger."

Deronda might have argued further had he not been so anxiously preoccupied with the delicate task ahead. He had been meeting Mordecai at the *Hand and Banner*, but the public house's din was ill-suited for profound revelations. He had written to Mordecai, requesting an hour in his workroom—a space where the mythic could safely intersect with the domestic—hoping to create a quiet, preparatory expectation.

When Deronda arrived at the Cohen shop, he left the harsh, industrial scrape of the London pavement for an interior rich with olfactory history. Beneath the superficial scent of brass polish and old pawned coats lay the deep, sustaining aroma of Mrs. Cohen's kitchen—schmaltz, slow-braising onions, and toasted cumin—a culinary tether to an agrarian past long geographically lost. He was received with the usual friendliness and a silent, wondering respect from the elders. 

Jacob, however, broke the quiet. Scurrying up to Deronda’s knee, he demanded, "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?"

"Something very interesting to him," Deronda said, pinching the lad’s ear, "but that you can't understand."

"Can you say this?" Jacob challenged. He immediately launched into a string of rote-learned Hebrew verses. The boy delivered the ancient syllables with a mixture of throaty friction and nasal projection. To Deronda, it was a poignant display of the erosion of cultural memory—the profound, mythic weight of a dying language reduced to a parlor trick in a commercialized world, yet still possessing a raw, acoustic power that altered the air in the room.

"No, really," Deronda said, his face grave in the presence of this linguistic ghost. "I can't say anything like it."

"I thought not," Jacob said, dancing a triumph on small scarlet legs, pulling various trinkets from his knickerbockers to assert his commercial dominance, before running to the workroom door. "Mordecai, here’s the young swell!" he shouted, thoughtlessly adopting his father’s superficial slang to cap the recitation of sacred Hebrew.

Hushed by his mother and grandmother, Jacob retreated. Deronda entered the workroom and closed the door, instantly sealing out the shop’s mercantile chatter. The acoustic shift was profound. Here, a bit of carpet had been laid down, a chair placed, and the fire tended—a ritualistic preparation of space. Mordecai rose, his face pale with solemn expectation, as if Deronda’s letter had heralded a miraculous, cross-generational alignment. 

Deronda drew the chair to the hearth. Neither spoke at first. The silence between them was not a void, but a rich, resonant medium of shared understanding. Finally, Mordecai spoke in a tone of fervid certainty.

"You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for."

"I have something very weighty to tell you—something I trust you will rejoice in," Deronda said gently, guarding against the heartbreaking untranslatability of Mordecai’s deepest hopes.

"It is all revealed," Mordecai leaned forward, hands clasped. "You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us."

"I have learned nothing new about myself," Deronda said. The disappointment was an inevitable dissonance; it was better to sever the false chord immediately. 

Mordecai sank back, the sudden silence in the room turning sickeningly hollow. The entire day his mind had been tuned to one mythic frequency. He closed his eyes.

"Except," Deronda continued softly, his voice a soothing balm, "except that I had really some time ago come into another sort of hidden connection with you."

Mordecai's eyelids fluttered. 

"I had made the acquaintance of one in whom you are interested. One who is closely related to your departed mother," Deronda said, navigating the delicate power dynamics of the revelation. Noticing Mordecai shrink back, he added, "Whom she and you held dear above all others."

Mordecai started, laying a spasmodic grasp on Deronda’s wrist. A great terror vibrated within him. Deronda’s clear tones held a perceptible tremor. 

"What was prayed for has come to pass: Mirah has been delivered from evil."

Mordecai’s grasp relaxed. He panted with a tearless sob. 

"Your sister is worthy of the mother you honored," Deronda finished. 

Throwing himself backward, Mordecai closed his eyes and began to whisper almost inaudibly in Hebrew. It was a private, subjective experience of overwhelming grace, inherently untranslatable into English. Deronda watched the new suffused sweetness on the man’s face, a serene echo of the beautiful dead. In that moment of ancestral connection, Deronda saw, for the first time, a clear, physical resemblance to Mirah. 

When Mordecai was ready, Deronda narrated the rest, carefully filtering the harshness of the father’s conduct and omitting Mirah’s attempt to drown herself. He focused instead on the sensory and emotional reality of her yearning for her family, and the safe, nurturing hearth she had found with the Meyricks. 

"It was in search of them," Deronda smiled, "that I turned into this house. I cared very much what I should find Mirah’s friends to be. I found a brother worthy of her when I knew Ezra was disguised under the name Mordecai."

"Mordecai is really my name—Ezra Mordecai Cohen."

"Is there any kinship between this family and yours?" Deronda asked.

"Only the kinship of Israel," Mordecai replied. "My soul clings to these people, who have given me succor out of the affection that abides in Jewish hearts, as sweet odor in things long crushed and hidden from the outer air. It is good for me to bear with their ignorance, to keep in mind the spiritual poverty of the Jewish million, and not put impatient knowledge in the stead of loving wisdom."

"But you do not feel bound to continue with them now?" Deronda urged. "You should live with your sister. I have prepared a home, insulated and quiet, where she may join you. You shall have books, and I shall take you to see the river and the trees, to hear the natural world. This is the claim I make on you."

Deronda pleaded with the affectionate earnestness of an elder brother. Mordecai looked at him with listening contemplation, absorbing the timbre of Deronda's voice as much as the words. Then, with almost reproachful emphasis, he said:

"And you would have me hold it doubtful whether you were born a Jew! Have we not from the first touched each other with invisible fibres—have we not quivered together like the leaves from a common stem? I know what I am outwardly—stricken, dying. But our souls know each other. All their speech is understanding. The life of Israel is in your veins."

Deronda sat perfectly still, his face tingling. He could neither deny nor assent to this profound assertion of shared blood and language. He waited. 

Finally, Mordecai said firmly, "What you wish of me I will do. I will accept what your loving kindness has prepared. But I shall grieve to part from these parents and the little ones. You must tell them, for my heart would fail me."

"Shall we go now at once?" Deronda asked, relieved. 

"Yes. But do not dwell on my sister more than is needful."

They entered the parlor. The firelight cast a rich, amber glow over the room, illuminating the dark gems of eyes that looked kindly upon one another. The scene was a portrait of domestic sustenance: on one side, the baby lay under a scarlet quilt, rocked by the young mother; Adelaide Rebekah sat on the grandmother’s knee; and Jacob stood between his father’s legs. 

"Ask your father to come, and tell Sarah to mind the shop," Mordecai said to Jacob. "My friend has something to say."

When Mr. Cohen entered, pen behind his ear, the harsh commercial world intruded upon the sanctuary. "Well, sir! Glad you’re doing us the honor to join our family party," he said, rubbing his hands. 

"I have just been telling Mordecai of an event that makes a great change in his life," Deronda began. "A very precious relation wishes to be reunited to him—a good and lovely young sister. A home is already provided for him."

"Relations with money, sir?" Cohen burst in, his dominant lexicon of profit overriding the delicate emotional frequency of the room. 

"No," Deronda smiled. "But with talents which will secure her a maintenance."

A profound silence fell, heavy with impending loss, before the grandmother wailed in a tone of ancient, untranslatable grief, "Well, well! And so you’re going away from us, Mordecai."

"And where there’s no children as there is here," the mother caught the wail, mourning the sudden rupture of their domestic ecosystem. 

"Ay, ay, Jacob’s learning ’ill all wear out of him," Cohen said with grim decision. "He must go to school. It’ll be hard times for Jacob."

To Jacob, the phrase "hard times" sounded like a doom. The wordless, acoustic reality of Mordecai’s absence crashed over him, and he broke into loud lamentation. Adelaide Rebekah immediately howled in sympathy, and the awakened baby contributed angry screams. The room’s serene ecology dissolved into a chaotic symphony of grief. Mordecai, pierced by the cries, put out his arms. Jacob ran to him, burying his black head against Mordecai’s chest.

Cohen, feeling the patriarchal need to impose order over this emotional spillage, squared his elbows. "It’s not as we’re the people to grudge anybody’s good luck, sir," he addressed Deronda loudly. "If I’m offal, let a wise man tell me. In point of business, I’m not a class of goods to be in danger. You’re taking some of our good works from us, which is property bearing interest, but we can afford that. A Jew must not be like a servant who works for reward—though I see nothing against a reward if I can get it. But the truth is, the women and children are fond of Mordecai. A child-bearing, tender-hearted thing is the woman of our people. So you must excuse present company for not being glad all at once."

Before Deronda could navigate this strange collision of mercantile defensiveness and genuine sorrow, Mordecai exclaimed, "Friends, friends! For food and raiment and shelter I would not have sought better. You have sweetened the morsel with love. It has been taught us that the reward of one duty is the power to fulfill another. You have made your duty to one of the poor a joy; your reward shall be the joy of like deeds in the time to come. And may not Jacob come and visit me?"

"Surely," Deronda said. "It is no further than Brompton."

The word "visit" functioned as a magical invocation for Jacob, translating into a vision of cakes and leisurely afternoons. He danced away, his sorrow instantly evaporating.

The grandmother, however, possessed the meticulous foresight of a true steward of ancestral sustenance. She sighed, a sound laden with generations of cultural preservation. "I hope there’ll be nothing in the way of your getting *kosher* meat, Mordecai. For you’ll have to trust to those you live with." It was not merely a question of diet, but a profound anxiety over the erosion of his identity, an acknowledgment that the most sacred components of their worldview were maintained daily at the butcher's block and the hearth.

"That’s all right, mother," Cohen waved off the inquiry, uneasy with testing Deronda's standing on such intimate, rigorous matters. "So, sir," he added to Deronda with an amused look of commercial enlightenment, "it was better than learning you had to talk to Mordecai about! I thought somehow there was a something."

"Mordecai will perhaps explain to you how it was that I was seeking him," said Deronda, feeling the deep acoustic resonance of the evening settling into its final chord. He rose to take his leave, carrying with him the fragile, interwoven threads of their shared history.
