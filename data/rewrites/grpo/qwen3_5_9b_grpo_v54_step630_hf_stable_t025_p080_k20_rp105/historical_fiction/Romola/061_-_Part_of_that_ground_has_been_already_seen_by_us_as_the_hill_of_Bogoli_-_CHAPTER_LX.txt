The moon, rising just above the rooftops of the Piazza della Signora past the stroke of midnight, failed to cast its usual patterns of light and shadow upon the cobblestones. There was no pavement visible, no empty space for the moon to illuminate; there was only the sea of heads, a churning multitude pressing forward. The silence that usually accompanied the nocturnal wanderers of Florence—the soft patter of feet, the hum of conversation, the strumming of lutes—had been swallowed by a deafening roar. Shouts, curses, the trampling of boots, and the accidental clash of weapons formed a chaotic backdrop against which only a piercing shriek or the heavy, rhythmic tolling of a bell could be distinguished.

Every citizen of Florence who could stir himself was awake, either trapped within the square or fighting desperately to reach it. Inside the Old Palace, the Council Chamber remained a theater of fierce contention. The chief magistrates, the eighty senators, and the select citizens had debated through the daylight and torchlight whether to grant an appeal or execute the prisoners immediately to prevent the dangers of delay. The debate had degenerated into a quarrel so violent that its echoes reached the crowd outside. Only in the final hour had a decision been reached. The Signoria had been split, with four members stubbornly voting for an appeal despite the threat that their homes would be sacked if they refused. It was Francesco Valori who broke the deadlock. In a brief, furious speech, he declared that if the Signoria would not defend the liberties of the Florentine people by executing the five conspirators, others would seize the cause and endanger everyone who opposed them. The Florentine Cato had triumphed. When the votes were recounted, the four obstinate white beans vanished; the entire nine cast fatal black ballots, sentencing the five prisoners to immediate death—a verdict that also, though tacitly and with greater delay, sentenced Francesco Valori himself.

As the judicial Eight departed for the Bargello to prepare the scaffold, the five condemned men were led barefoot and in irons through the center of the council chamber. This humiliation had been orchestrated by their friends: would the Florentines not be moved by the sight of such cruel ignominy visited upon two venerable elders, Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi, whose loyalty predated the current political shift? Would they not mourn the brilliant young men, Tornabuoni and Pucci, whose absence would leave a haunting void in every gathering of the elite? It was futile. Any pity stirred now was of a hopeless variety, leading not to rescue but to the slower, darker action of revenge.

While this scene unfolded above, Romola stood below, leaning against a massive pillar in the palace courtyard. She waited for the moment her godfather would appear on his way to execution. Through powerful intervention, she had secured permission to visit him that evening and remain until the council's decision was final. Now, she waited with his confessor to follow the guard leading him to the Bargello. Her heart was resolved to cling to the presence of this childless old man until the very last moment, just as her father would have done; she had silenced all remonstrances. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a disciple of Savonarola who went in bitterness to witness the death of his elder brother Niccolò, had promised to guard her and now stood beside her.

Tito was also in the palace, though Romola had not seen him. Since the evening of the seventeenth, they had avoided one another. Tito knew only by inference from the friar's neutral report that Romola's pleas had failed. Surrounded by official and foreign dignitaries awaiting the council's conclusion, he maintained a subdued air and grave silence, caught in a painful strife between public duty and private feeling. When allusions were made to his wife regarding these events, he implied that her violent mental excitement caused her to view his continued office under a government condemning her godfather with diseased hostility. For her sake, he felt it best not to approach her.

"Ah, the old Bardi blood!" Cennini remarked with a shrug. "I shall not be surprised if this business shakes her loose from the Frate, as well as some others I could name."

"It is excusable in a woman, who is doubtless beautiful, since she is the wife of Messer Tito," a young French envoy smiled, bowing to Tito. "To think that her affections must overrule the good of the State, and that nobody is to be beheaded who is anybody's cousin; but such a view is not to be encouraged in the male population. It seems to me your Florentine polity is much weakened by it."

"That is true," Niccolò Machiavelli replied. "But where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they raise must be taken into account. Many of these half-measures are merely hot-headed blunders. The only safe blows to be inflicted on men and parties are those too heavy to be avenged."

"Niccolò," Cennini said, "there is a clever wickedness in thy talk sometimes that makes me mistrust thy pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan."

"Not at all, my good Domenico," Machiavelli smiled, laying his hand on the elder's shoulder. "Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of novelty, who made a stupendous failure. If he had succeeded, we should all be worshipping him, and his portrait would be far more flattering."

"Well, well," Cennini conceded. "I say not thy doctrine is not too clever for Satan: I only say it is wicked enough for him."

"I tell you," Machiavelli insisted, "my doctrine is the doctrine of all men who seek an end a little farther off than their own noses. Ask our Frate, our prophet, how his universal renovation is to be brought about: he will tell you, first, by securing a free and pure government; and since this cannot be achieved by making all Florentines love each other, it must be done by cutting off every head that happens to be obstinately in the way. Only if a man incurs odium by sanctioning a severity that is not thorough enough to be final, he commits a blunder. And something like that blunder, I suspect, the Frate has committed. It was an occasion on which he might have won lustre by exerting himself to maintain the appeal; instead, he has lost lustre and gained no strength."

Before anyone else could speak, the announcement came that the prisoners were leaving the council chamber. The majority of those present hurried toward the door, intent on securing the clearest path to the Bargello behind the prisoners' guard, for the scene of the execution drew alike those moved by deep passion and those driven by cold curiosity.

Tito remained behind. He possessed a native repugnance to sights of death and pain. Five days prior, whenever he had considered this execution a possibility, he had hoped it would not happen, that the utmost sentence would be exile; his own safety demanded no more. But now, he felt that the death of Bernardo del Nero would be a welcome guarantee of his security. The new knowledge and new attitude revealed to him by Romola on the day of his return had instilled in him a fresh dread of the power she possessed to make his position insecure. If any act of hers succeeded in making him an object of suspicion and odium, he foresaw not only frustration but frustration under unpleasant circumstances. Her belief in Baldassarre had clearly determined her wavering feelings against further submission, and if her godfather lived, she would win him to share her belief without much trouble. Romola seemed more than ever an unmanageable fact in his destiny. But if Bernardo del Nero were dead, the difficulties that would beset her in placing herself in opposition to her husband would probably be insurmountable to her shrinking pride. Therefore, Tito felt easier knowing that the Eight had gone to the Bargello to order the instant erection of the scaffold. Four other men—his intimates and confederates—were to die besides Bernardo del Nero. Yet a man's own safety is a god that sometimes makes very grim demands. Tito felt them to be grim: even in the pursuit of what was agreeable, this paradoxical life forced upon him the desire for what was disagreeable. As he heard through the open doorway the shuffle of many feet and the clanking of metal on the stairs, he answered the questions of the young French envoy without showing signs of any feeling other than sad resignation to state necessities.

Those sounds fell upon Romola as if her power of hearing had been exalted along with every other sensibility of her nature. She needed no arm to support her; she shed no tears. She felt that intensity of life which seems to transcend both grief and joy—in which the mind seems to itself akin to elder forces that wrought out existence before the birth of pleasure and pain. Since her godfather's fate had been decided, the previous struggle of feeling within her had given way to an identification of herself with him in these supreme moments. She was inwardly asserting for him that, if he suffered the punishment of treason, he did not deserve the name of traitor; he was the victim of a collision between two kinds of faithfulness. It was not given him to die for the noblest cause, and yet he died because of his nobleness. He might have been a meaner man and found it easier not to incur this guilt. Romola was feeling the full force of that sympathy with the individual lot that is continually opposing itself to the formulae by which actions and parties are judged. She was treading the way with her second father to the scaffold, nerving herself to defy ignominy by the consciousness that it was not deserved.

The path was fenced in by three hundred armed men, placed as a guard by the orders of Francesco Valori. Among the apparent contradictions belonging to this event, not the least striking was the alleged alarm on one hand at the popular rage against the conspirators, and the alleged alarm on the other lest there should be an attempt to rescue them in the midst of a hostile crowd. When they arrived within the court of the Bargello, Romola was allowed to approach Bernardo with his confessor for a moment of farewell. Many eyes were bent on them even in that struggle of an agitated throng as the aged man, forgetting that his hands were bound with irons, lifted them toward the golden head bent toward him, and then, checking that movement, leaned to kiss her. She seized the fettered hands that hung down again and kissed them as if they had been sacred things.

"My poor Romola," Bernardo said in a low voice, "I have only to die, but thou hast to live—and I shall not be there to help thee."

"Yes," Romola said hurriedly, "you *will* help me—always—because I shall remember you."

She was taken away and conducted up the flight of steps leading to the loggia surrounding the grand old court. She took her place there, determined to watch until the moment when her godfather laid his head on the block. Now, while the prisoners were allowed a brief interval with their confessor, the spectators pressed into the court until the crowd became dense around the black scaffold. Torches fixed in iron rings against the pillars threw a varying, startling light at one moment on passionless stone carvings, at another on pale faces agitated with suppressed rage or suppressed grief—the faces of many near relatives of the condemned, who were presently to receive their dead and carry them home.

Romola's face looked like a marble image against the dark arch as she stood watching for the moment when her godfather would appear at the foot of the scaffold. He was to suffer first, and Battista Ridolfi, who stood by her side, had promised to take her away through a door behind them once she had seen the last look of the man who alone in all the world had shared her pitying love for her father. And still, in the background of her thought, there was the possibility striving to become a hope, that some rescue might yet come, something that would keep that scaffold unstained by blood.

For a long while there was constant movement, lights flickering, heads swaying to and fro, confused voices within the court, rushing waves of sound through the entrance from without. It seemed to Romola as if she were in the midst of a storm-troubled sea, caring nothing about the storm, caring only to hold out a signal till the eyes that looked for it could seek it no more.

Suddenly there was stillness, and the very tapers seemed to tremble into quiet. The executioner was ready on the scaffold, and Bernardo del Nero was seen ascending it with a slow, firm step. Romola made no visible movement, uttered not even a suppressed sound; she stood more firmly, caring for *his* firmness. She saw him pause, saw the white head kept erect while he said, in a voice distinctly audible:

"It is but a short space of life that my fellow-citizens have taken from me."

She perceived that he was gazing slowly around him as he spoke. She felt that his eyes were resting on her, and that she was stretching out her arms toward him. Then she saw no more till—a long while after, as it seemed—a voice said, "My daughter, all is peace now. I can conduct you to your house."

She uncovered her head and saw her godfather's confessor standing by her, in a room where there were other grave men talking in subdued tones.

"I am ready," she said, starting up. "Let us lose no time."

She thought all clinging was at an end for her: all her strength now should be given to escape from a grasp under which she shuddered.
