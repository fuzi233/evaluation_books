It was a peculiar irony of Arthur Pendennis's nature that, while he poured out his soul to Warrington regarding the discovery of his old tutor Smirke, the eccentricities of his wife, and his grand departure from Clapham for Rome, he remained mysteriously silent about Blanche. When pressed, Arthur would offer vague, diplomatic responses. "She is a good-natured, clever little thing," he'd say, adding that with proper guidance she might not turn out a disaster, though for the moment, romance was dead and buried. He claimed contentment with his current lot, effectively locking the door on any further discussion of the girl who had captured his imagination.

Yet, the silence was broken by the arrival of satin envelopes at Lamb Court. Superscribed in a neat, looping hand and sealed with a cipher that would have baffled even the sharpest eye, these letters revealed a correspondence between Arthur and a young lady whose initials were B.A. Arthur's replies were a masterclass in gallantry—filled with witty observations, town gossip, and, most dangerously, poetry. Indeed, those verses that later graced the pages of *Roseleaves*, the annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas, were originally penned for Blanche, serving as pre-publication cornets to Pinkney's pictorial garland.

"Verses are all very well," the elder Pendennis remarked one evening at the Club, watching his nephew scratch out another artless effusion. "And letter-writing, if Mamma permits it, is fine between old country friends. But mind, Pen, don't commit yourself. Who knows what the doose may happen? The safest path is to keep your letters safe. I've never written a letter in my life that would bind me, and *demmy*, sir, I have had some experience of women." The Major, growing garrulous with age, launched into a lecture on the evils of indiscretion, recounting how young Spoony had been forced into a marriage with a widow old enough to be his mother after sending too ardent a note, and how Sir John Bird had been duped into a union with Miss Stickney after receiving letters from Miss Salter.

Pen laughed, blushing slightly at his uncle's warnings. "Don't you remember the lesson I learned in Lady Mirabel's? Miss Fotheringay's affair? I am not to be caught again, Uncle." He spoke with mock humility, perhaps because he already was cautious. Instinctively, or perhaps out of a strange honesty, he had refrained in his letters to Miss Blanche from any avowals that might compromise him.

Old Pendennis congratulated himself on his nephew's prudence, unaware that Warrington would have judged the situation differently. Warrington would have argued that the boy's foolish letters were superior to the man's slippery gallantries, that to truly win a woman, one must advance without subterfuge, with no retreat secured. But Pen knew his friend was guilty of the very faults he preached against, so he said nothing.

Meanwhile, the winds of fate were turning violently against Captain Strong. After refusing a loan from Colonel Altamont—a sacrifice to his own conscience that now gnawed at him—Strong found himself besieged. The tradesmen, once confident in his jolly face, now poured in their bills with cowardly unanimity. Tailors, bootmakers, and pastrycooks held levees on his stairs, while young clerks lurked nearby with writs demanding his appearance before the Queen.

Poor Strong, having no guinea in his pocket, retreated into the "Englishman's castle," barring the doors against the creditors. From behind a curtain over his letter-box, the Chevalier peeped out to watch the furious faces of the duns dash against the wood. But he was not entirely alone. His allies, Huxter and Miss Fanny Bolton, maintained a secret supply line. When hostile visitors prowled, Fanny's little sisters would innocently whoop a specific jodel in the court; upon hearing this signal, Fanny and Huxter would arrive, singing the same note, allowing the honest garrison to emerge, provisions and porter in hand, to pass a comfortable night.

Beyond his allies, Strong had secured a vital retreat. It had been noted previously that the Costigan and Bows household lived next door, with a leaden water-pipe connecting their window to Strong's kitchen. Laughingly demonstrated to Altamont, this escape route was now his lifeline. One afternoon, Strong burst into the Costigans' apartment, explaining that the enemy was waiting on his staircase, and he had taken this means to give them the slip. Thus, while the sheriff's aides-de-camp waited in the passage of No. 3, Strong walked down the steps of No. 4, dined at the Albion, attended the play, and returned home at midnight, baffling Mrs. Bolton and Fanny, who could not conceive how he had passed the line of sentries.

For weeks, Strong bore this siege with the spirit of a veteran soldier, though his patience wore thin. What truly riled him was the infernal indifference of Sir Francis Clavering, to whom Strong wrote letter after letter, receiving not a single acknowledgment or remittance. A five-pound note, Strong lamented, would have been a fortune at that time.

Then, salvation arrived. "Yes, if it hadn't been for this good fellow here," Strong declared, his spirits lifted. "For a good fellow you are, Altamont, my boy... I think, Pendennis, it would have been all up with Ned Strong. I was in the fifth week of my imprisonment... my spirit was quite broken... when who should drop down from heaven but Altamont!"

"Heaven ain't exactly the place, Ned," Altamont corrected cheerfully. "I came from Baden-Baden. I'd had a deuced lucky month there."

True to his word, Altamont took up Marks's bill and paid the others. The meeting, held at Richmond in the coffee-room, was a raucous affair of wine and hilarity. Strong, an admirable storyteller, recounted his siege and escapes with great liveliness, describing the talk of the officers at his door and the pretty signals of Fanny. Altamont, flushed with victory and claret, proposed a toast to "every good fellow like Strong finding another good fellow to stand by him," though he admitted he disliked the name Pendennis.

"Why?" Arthur asked.

Strong pressed the Colonel's foot under the table. Altamont, rather excited, filled another bumper, nodded to Pen, and declared, "He was a gentleman, and that was sufficient, and they were all gentlemen."

Altamont then unveiled his grand plan. Having won a good sum at Baden-Baden, he intended to return to the country to fetch his money and Captain Strong. He possessed an "infallible system" for gambling, a method he claimed would make everyone's fortune. "I'll put in for that little Fanny," he announced enthusiastically, noting that she had lent two pounds to Ned Strong. "Let's drink her health."

Arthur pledged the toast with cordiality. As Altamont emptied his bottle, he detailed his plans to Pen: he would form a bank with partners, put every shilling he had into it, and trust Strong to play for him, believing Strong's temper superior to his own or that of Bloundell-Bloundell.

Later, Pen recounted the story to his uncle. "I met that queer fellow Altamont the other day," he said.

"Altamont? What Altamont? There's Lord Westport's son," the Major replied.

"No, no; the fellow who came tipsy into Clavering's dining-room... he said he did not like the name of Pendennis."

"I don't know any man of the name of Altamont," the impenetrable Major insisted. "And as for your acquaintance, I think the less you have to do with him the better, Arthur."

Arthur laughed. "He is going to quit the country and make his fortune by a gambling system. He and my amiable college acquaintance, Bloundell, are partners, and the Colonel takes out Strong with him as aide-de-camp. What is it that binds the Chevalier and Clavering, I wonder?"

"My uncle has talked him over," the Major answered. "Clavering thinks he ain't fit for Parliament. No more he is. What's to prevent him from putting you or anybody else into his place if he likes? Do you think that Government or the Opposition would make any bones about accepting the seat if he offered it to them!"

The Major's logic was sound, though Pen accepted it more because he wished to believe it than because he fully understood the mechanics of political maneuvering.

At his next visit to Tunbridge, Pen amused Miss Blanche with the history of the Chevalier's imprisonment and Altamont's rescue, praising little Fanny's generosity. Blanche, however, was somewhat jealous and piqued by the attention given to Fanny. During their drives and evening walks, Arthur described the drama of the "poor little Ariadne of Shepherd's Inn" with becoming modesty. His moral, drawn in his usual satirical mood, was that women recovered from first loves as easily as men did, transferring their affections to a second object without difficulty. Poor Fanny was offered up as a sacrifice to prove this theory.

"You wicked odious creature," Miss Blanche said, her voice trembling. "I believe that you are enraged with Fanny for being so impudent as to forget you, and that you are actually jealous of Mr. Huxter."

Perhaps she was right. The blush that tingled upon Pendennis's cheek proved he was angry to think he had been superseded by such a rival—a fellow without good looks, good manners, or wit, possessing only ardour. "If Nature had not made that provision for each sex in the credulity of the other," he mused internally, "there would not have been near so much marrying and giving in marriage."

"Jealous or not," Pen said aloud, "I should have liked Fanny to have come to a better end than that. I don't like histories that end in that cynical way... Is a life a compromise, my lady fair, and the end of the battle of love an ignoble surrender? Is the search for the Cupid... to result in Huxter smelling of tobacco and gallypots?"

"And don't you intend to be good and happy, pray, Monsieur le Misanthrope?" Blanche asked, her tone sharp. "Are you very discontented with your lot—and will your marriage be a compromise?"

"Again, I don't say no," Pen replied, looking gloomily at her. "I don't think I have much of what people call heart; but I don't profess it. I made my venture when I was eighteen, and lighted my lamp and went in search of Cupid. And what was my discovery of love?—a vulgar dancing-woman! I failed, as everybody does... only it is luckier to fail before marriage than after."

"Merci du choix, Monsieur," said the Sylphide, making a curtsey.

"Look, my little Blanche," Pen said, taking her hand, "at least I stoop to no flatteries. I tell you no foolish lies, as vulgar men do. Why should you and I, with our experience, ape romance and dissemble passion? I do not believe Miss Blanche Amory to be peerless among the beautiful... But if I don't set you up as a heroine, neither do I offer you your very humble servant as a hero. But I think you are—well, there, I think you are very sufficiently good-looking."

"Merci," Miss Blanche said, with another curtsey.

"I think you sing charmingly. I'm sure you're clever. I hope and believe that you are good-natured, and that you will be companionable."

"And so, provided I bring you a certain sum of money and a seat in Parliament, you condescend to fling to me your royal pocket-handkerchief," Blanche retorted, playing the role of the ambitious suitor. "Que d'honneur! We used to call your Highness the Prince of Fairoaks. What an honour to think that I am to be elevated to the throne, and to bring the seat in Parliament as backsheesh to the sultan!"

"And if thieves are about the house," Pen said grimly, pursuing the simile, "forty besetting thieves in the shape of lurking cares and enemies in ambush and passions in arms, my Morgiana will dance round me with a tambourine, and kill all my rogues and thieves with a smile. Won't she?" But Pen looked as if he did not believe that she would.

"Ah, Blanche," he continued after a pause, "don't be angry; don't be hurt at my truth-telling. Don't you see that I always take you at your word? You say you will be a slave and dance—I say, dance. You say, 'I take you with what you bring:' I say, 'I take you with what you bring.' To the necessary deceits and hypocrisies of our life, why add any that are useless and unnecessary? If I offer myself to you because I think we have a fair chance of being happy together, and because by your help I may get for both of us a good place and a not undistinguished name, why ask me to feign raptures and counterfeit romance, in which neither of us believe?"

"Do you want me to come wooing in a Prince Prettyman's dress from the masquerade warehouse, and to pay you compliments like Sir Charles Grandison? Do you want me to make you verses as in the days when we were—when we were children? I will if you like, and sell them to Bacon and Bungay afterwards. Shall I feed my pretty princess with bonbons?"

"Mais j'adore les bonbons, moi," said the little Sylphide, with a queer, piteous look.
