When ex-master thief turned private detective Flambeau booked his month’s holiday away from his Westminster office, he didn’t opt for a fancy coastal resort or a crowded continental spa. He bought a tiny sailboat that spent half its time being rowed, and pointed its bow straight for the tangled, overgrown little rivers of the Norfolk Broads. The waterways were so narrow, hemmed in by meadows and waist-high corn, that the craft looked less like a boat and more like something from a fairy tale, gliding over land through walls of wild grass. It was only big enough for two, so Flambeau packed nothing but his four non-negotiables: tins of salmon if they got hungry, a pair of loaded revolvers if trouble found them, a bottle of brandy in case one of them fainted, and Father Brown, in case one of them died.

No plans, no deadlines: they drifted slow down the reed-channels, lingering to fish in shaded back pools, staring at the mirrored facades of riverside manor houses half hidden by overhanging rose gardens, hugging the shore as much as they dared. Flambeau had no strict agenda for the trip, but he carried a half-joking, half-serious errand he’d been nursing for years. Back in his wild youth, when he was the undisputed king of Paris’s thief underworld, he’d gotten stacks of odd mail: fan notes, death threats, even love letters. One of them had stuck with him for decades, a plain visiting card postmarked London, scrawled on the back in bright green French ink: If you ever retire and go respectable, come find me. I’ve met every other great rogue of my era, and your little trick of getting one detective to arrest the other was the finest scene in all of modern French history. The front of the card read, in neat formal engraving: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk*.

Back then he’d only looked up the prince out of idle curiosity: a glamorous, notorious socialite who’d once eloped with a high-ranking married woman in southern Italy. The scandal would have blown over fast in their jet-set circle, except for the brutal tragedy that followed: the cuckolded husband had supposedly flung himself off a sheer Sicilian cliff, and the story had clung to Saradine ever after. He’d drifted through Vienna for a while, then vanished into decades of restless, nonstop travel across every continent. Now that Flambeau himself had laid down his burglar’s tools and settled in England, it felt like as good a time as any to drop in unannounced on the old rogue.

They moored for one night under a bank thick with tall grasses and pollarded willows, and fell asleep hard after hours of heavy sculling. They woke long before dawn, the sky a vivid deep violet, a fat lemon-yellow moon hanging low just above the grass tops. For a second both men felt like small children again, lost in a nursery fairy tale: the daisies around them loomed as big as dinner plates, dandelions towered over the boat like giant sunflowers, and the river sat so low below the root systems of the shrubs that they might as well have been gazing up at a forest of wildflowers. “By Jove,” Flambeau laughed. “This is straight out of fairyland.”

Father Brown sat bolt upright in the small boat and crossed himself so sharply his friend blinked.
“The people who wrote the old medieval ballads knew more about fairyland than you do,” the priest said quietly. “Nice things aren’t the only ones that happen there.”
“Oh, bosh,” Flambeau scoffed, already hauling up the anchor. “Nothing bad could happen under a moon that soft. I’m pushing on now. We might live to a hundred and never see a night half this magical.”
“Fair enough,” Father Brown said, scrambling to steady his hat as the boat lurched forward. “I never said you couldn’t go into fairyland. I only said it’s always dangerous.”

They drifted slow up the brightening river, the violet sky fading to pale pearlescent grey before the first streaks of pink and gold split the horizon. A small riverside hamlet hove into view, its low, sloped thatch roofs hunkered so close to the water they looked like huge red and grey cattle leaning down to drink. Full daylight was breaking before they spotted a single soul on the wharves: a plump, placid man in his shirtsleeves, face as round as the just-set moon, fringed with fuzzy red whiskers, leaning on a post staring out over the tide. On impulse Flambeau stood up in the swaying boat and yelled to ask for Reed Island or Reed House. The man smiled, said not a word, and just pointed upriver toward the next sharp bend.

Ten minutes later they rounded that bend, and the whole river opened into a quiet wide pool, ringed edge to edge with rustling rushes. Dead center lay a low, long islet, strung along its length with a bungalow built entirely of pale yellow bamboo, its sloped roof made of darker red-brown cane. The morning breeze sighed through the ribbed walls, and the whole house hummed like a giant pan-pipe.
“Here we are!” Flambeau whooped. “That fat whiskered bloke was a fairy, I swear it.”
“Maybe,” Father Brown said evenly. “If he was, he was a bad one.”

Flambeau had already run the boat aground in the rattling reeds, and they stepped ashore onto the soft grass of the island path. The house’s back faced the landing stage, so they circled three sides of the low eaves to reach the front entrance, peeking in three different side windows along the way at the same long, sunlit room, panelled in light pale wood and lined with mirrors from end to end, laid out for an elegant lunch. The front door was flanked by two bright turquoise blue flower pots, and opened by a gaunt, listless old butler with parchment-grey skin, who mumbled that Prince Saradine was out but expected any minute. The second he spotted the green ink scrawl on Flambeau’s old visiting card, a flicker of life crossed his dead face, and he invited them in with shaky, apologetic courtesy. “His Highness would be distraught to miss you,” he said. “We keep cold lunch ready for him and his guests at all times.”

They ate, served the butler whose name was Paul, sipping thick coffee and sweet liqueurs after, wandering the small garden and tiny library before being introduced to the housekeeper: a tall, dark, stately Italian woman named Mrs. Anthony, who looked like a somber Madonna statue who had witnessed unforgivable sins. It turned out she and Paul were the only remaining members of Saradine’s old household staff, all the other servants hired locally in Norfolk. The whole house carried a strange, luminous, heavy sadness. Hours dragged by like whole days, the bright daylight spilling through the windows feeling flat and dead, and under every sound of clinking glasses or distant footstep, they could hear the low melancholy lap of the river against the islet banks.

Paul was sullenly loyal to his master, and only ever spat venom about Saradine’s younger brother, Captain Stephen. The man, he grumbled, was a ne’er-do-well who had bled the prince dry for thousands of pounds, forcing him to flee polite society and hide out here in the broads. Mrs. Anthony, when they stopped to stare at a red chalk sketch of the two young Saradine boys hanging on the wall, slipped up beside Father Brown while Flambeau wandered off to the garden. Her dark eyes were tragic, sharp with a painful secret.
“Your friend said you could never tell which of the two boys was the good one and which the bad,” she hissed, leaning in like she was sharing a confession. “He’s right. It would be very, very hard to pick out the good one. There isn’t one. The captain’s no blackmailer, not the way you think. The prince paid him all that money not out of kindness. He was buying his silence.”
Father Brown’s mouth formed the word *blackmail* silently, but before he could say a thing, Mrs. Anthony blanched and stumbled back. Paul was standing in the doorway, and thanks to all the mirrors lining the walls, it looked like five identical Pauls had slipped into the room at once.
“His Highness,” he said, “has just arrived.”

A tall, elegant man with bright white curled hair and pale ivory-yellow skin strode past the windows, his eagle profile reflected in every mirror frame as he walked: Prince Saradine, wearing a crisp white top hat, a yellow waistcoat, bright yellow flapping gloves, and an orchid pinned to his lapel. He strolled in laughing, five copies of him walking through five mirrored doorways at once, shook Flambeau’s hand warmly, and said he’d been waiting years to meet the famous rogue. He was charming, endlessly well-traveled, rattling off stories of opium dens in Shanghai, Australian bushranger camps, and Italian bandit hideouts, and within twenty minutes he was guiding Flambeau down the river in his canoe to show him the best fishing spot, leaving Father Brown alone in the library before long.

Evening was falling, the sky turning soft amber over the water, a bittern booming far out in the reeds like an elf beating a tiny drum. Father Brown frowned, glancing out at the rushes. He wished Flambeau would come back.
“Do you believe in doom?” Saradine asked him suddenly, turning from the window, his face half in shadow.
“No,” Father Brown said. “I believe in Doomsday. That’s the one where no one gets tricked, and the punishment never lands on the wrong person.”
Saradine let out a strange, thin, animal huff of a laugh, repeating the words *the wrong person* over and over, more times than made sense, his eyes glittering wild in the sunset dark.

Then Paul appeared again, his five mirrored copies lining the doorway.
“I thought I should inform you immediately,” he said stiffly. “A rowboat with six men aboard has just pulled up to the landing stage. There’s a gentleman sitting in the stern.”

Before anyone could speak, a new face passed the windows in profile: a young man with a stern, clean-shaven face that looked exactly like portraits of young Napoleon, wearing a shabby old-fashioned black top hat, a faded blue frock coat, and a bright red military waistcoat that had gone out of style sixty years prior. He stepped onto the lawn, his six swarthy, earring-wearing boatmen standing behind him holding their oars upright like spears.
“Your name is Saradine?” the young man said, his brown eyes dull and stubborn as a loyal dog.
Saradine nodded, half amused.
“My name is Antonelli,” the newcomer said.
He politely tipped his old top hat with his left hand. With his right, he slapped Saradine so hard across the face that the white top hat rolled all the steps down to the lawn, and one of the turquoise flower pots wobbled violently on its stone pedestal.

Saradine lunged for his throat, no trace of cowardice in him, but Antonelli pulled back with odd, flustered politeness, panting.
“That is the required insult,” he said in halting English. “I will give you satisfaction. Marco! Open the duel case.”
