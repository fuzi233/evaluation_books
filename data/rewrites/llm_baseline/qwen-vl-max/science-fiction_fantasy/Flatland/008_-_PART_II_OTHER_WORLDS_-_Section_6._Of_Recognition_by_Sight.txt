I am about to appear inconsistent, and I must confess it at once. Earlier in this narrative, I stated that all figures in Flatland appear as straight lines to the eye—and from that observation, I concluded that visual distinction between individuals was impossible. Yet now I shall explain how, in fact, we do recognize one another through sight. The resolution lies not in contradiction, but in nuance.

Let me clarify: while it is true that every figure presents itself as a line when viewed directly, this does not mean that all lines are indistinguishable. That principle holds only among the lower classes—those who lack both education and opportunity. Among the higher ranks, and particularly in our temperate zones, recognition by sight is not only possible but highly refined. And the key to this ability? Fog.

To you, inhabitants of Spaceland, fog may seem a nuisance—a shroud that blurs landscapes, dulls spirits, and weakens health. But to us, it is something far greater: a gift almost equal to air itself. It is the nurse of science, the mother of geometry, the foundation upon which our most advanced knowledge rests. Without fog, all lines would appear equally clear, sharp, and identical—rendering sight useless for discrimination. But because fog exists—because it thickens with distance—it creates gradations of brightness and dimness that reveal shape, angle, and rank.

Imagine two figures approaching you: one an Equilateral Triangle (a Merchant), the other a Pentagon (a Physician). At first glance, both appear as straight lines. But if you position your eye so that your line of vision bisects the angle of the approaching figure, you will perceive more than just a line—you will see a gradient.

With the Triangle, the center point will glow brightly, being closest to your eye. From there, the line fades rapidly into dimness on either side, because the sides recede sharply into the fog. The ends—what appear as the figure’s extremities—will be nearly invisible. This rapid fading signals a sharp angle: a Triangle.

Now observe the Pentagon. Its central point will also shine, but the transition from bright to dim is much slower. The sides of the Pentagon slope outward less steeply, so they fade gradually into the mist. The extremities remain comparatively brighter. This softer gradient reveals a broader form—a Polygon of higher rank.

This method, though simple in theory, requires years of training. A child might grasp the principle quickly, but mastering it demands constant practice. In real life, the challenges multiply. If my Father, a Triangle, turns sideways instead of facing me with his angle, he may appear momentarily as a straight line—indistinguishable from a Woman. Only after rotation or careful movement can his true nature be confirmed.

And consider a Hexagon—my own grandson. When viewed head-on along one of its sides, I see a long, uniformly bright line flanked by two shorter, dimmer lines that fade toward their ends. These represent the adjacent sides, receding into the fog. The pattern is unmistakable—but only to one trained in the art.

In crowded settings—such as a ballroom or social gathering—the complexity grows exponentially. Figures move, rotate, advance, retreat. Some face you; others turn away. The air hums with shifting gradients, flickering brightnesses, and fleeting shadows. To navigate such scenes without error requires not merely knowledge, but instinct—developed through years of disciplined study.

At the University of Wentbridge, this art is taught rigorously. Students learn both static and dynamic recognition—how to interpret shapes at rest and in motion. The curriculum is demanding, and only the elite afford it. Even I, a mathematician of some standing and grandfather to two fine hexagonal heirs, find myself occasionally bewildered in a throng of high-ranking Polygons.

For the common Tradesman or Serf, such refinement is beyond reach. Their lives depend on immediate practicality, not abstract thought. They rely on touch—on feeling—to determine rank. And while this grants them early agility and alertness, it also limits their development. Like the deaf who never learn to read lips, those who grow up relying on "Feeling" never fully master the subtler, more powerful sense of "Seeing."

Thus, among the upper classes, Feeling is discouraged—even forbidden. Children are sent not to public schools, where Feeling is taught, but to exclusive seminaries where Sight Recognition is cultivated from infancy. At the university, to "feel" is considered a grave offense—punishable by rustication for the first offense, expulsion for the second.

The result is a stark divide. The lower classes, untrained in visual discernment, remain locked in a world of crude approximation. The upper classes, though slow to develop initially, surpass them in maturity, precision, and intellectual depth once properly educated.

Yet not all rise to the occasion. A small number fail the Final Examination at the University. These unfortunate souls are rejected by the higher orders and scorned by the lower. They possess neither the cultured insight of the educated nor the natural dexterity of the working class. They are cast adrift—without profession, without status, without hope.

History shows that many of the great rebellions of our past were led by such men—disgraced nobles, failed scholars, misfits of society. The harm they cause is profound, and increasingly, voices call for reform. Some argue that mercy demands their elimination—either through lifelong imprisonment or painless termination—lest they breed further disorder.

But I digress. This matter of Irregularity, though vital, deserves its own section. For now, let it stand: sight recognition is not mere perception—it is an art, a science, a mark of civilization. And like all such arts, it is born not in talent alone, but in discipline, environment, and the courage to see beyond the surface.
