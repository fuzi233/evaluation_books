Let us kindly skip ahead a few months in the chronicle of Mr. Arthur Pendennis's life. During this interval, countless events surely transpired that thrilled him deeply yet would bore the average reader of these memoirs to tears. Last time we saw him, he had officially embarked on his career as a professional writer—or, as his pal Mr. Warrington prefers to call it, "literary hack." Now, anyone who knows the grind of a hack, whether legal, literary, clerical, military, or commercial, understands that existence is defined by a monotonous routine and a tedious lack of drama. One day's labor mirrors the next with uncanny precision. A writer often toils for his crust of bread against the clock, his own will, his failing health, his sheer laziness, or his revulsion toward the subject matter, much like any other daily drudge. When you need to monetize Pegasus (assuming you possess no other marketable assets), forget poetry and soaring flights. Modern Pegasus ascends only like Mr. Green's balloon: at pre-advertised intervals, after the spectators have parted with their cash. He trots in harness across cobblestones, pulling a cart or cab, often panting with trembling knees and frequently receiving a sharp cut from his whip-wielding driver.

However, let us not be overly lavish with our pity for Pegasus. There is no justification for exempting this creature from labor, sickness, or decay any more than any other creation of God's earth. If he receives a lash, Pegasus often deserves it. I, for one, stand ready to defend my friend George Warrington against the sentimental notion held by poetic sympathizers: that letters and genius are immune from the prosaic duties of daily life, tax-paying, and earning a living alongside one's neighbors.

Thus, with the *Pall Mall Gazette* firmly established and Arthur Pendennis recognized as a flippant, witty, and amusing critic, he threw himself into his work every week. He prepared reviews of whatever fell into his department, writing with assured flippancy, yet with honesty and to the best of his ability. Occasionally, a historian of seventy years, who had spent twenty-five years crafting a tome that our young gentleman devoured in two days at the British Museum, felt unfairly treated by such a facile reviewer. Or perhaps a poet, laboring over sublime sonnets deemed fit for immortality, was annoyed by two dozen snappy lines in Mr. Pen's critique, wherein the critic acted as a stern judge and the author a trembling defendant. The theater actors complained bitterly, too, and likely deserved it. Yet, little harm was ultimately done. Times have changed since then; but in Pen's era, there were so few truly great historians, poets, or actors that almost none appeared before his critical desk. Those who received a slight beating generally found it beneficial; not that the judge was inherently wiser than those he sentenced, nor did he ever imagine himself superior. Pen possessed a robust sense of humor and justice, lacking the arrogance to overvalue his own opinions. Furthermore, he had Warrington at his side—a terrifying critic if Pen grew conceited, and far more savage toward Pen than toward anyone else he tried at his literary assizes.

Through these critical labors and occasional contributions to leading articles—where, without offending his paper, this eminent publicist could speak his conscience—Mr. Arthur Pendennis earned four pounds and four shillings weekly, accompanied by significant effort. Additionally, he supplied magazines and reviews with original articles and is rumored to have served as the London correspondent for the *Chatteris Champion*, which at the time featured brilliant metropolitan correspondence. These endeavors enabled the fortunate youth to generate an income nearing four hundred pounds annually. On the second Christmas following his arrival in London, he actually delivered a hundred pounds to his mother as repayment on the debt he owed to Laura. Mrs. Pendennis read every word of her son's writings, deeming him the profoundest thinker and most elegant writer of the day. She viewed his return of the hundred pounds as an act of angelic virtue, feared he was sacrificing his health, and rejoiced whenever he mentioned the society he encountered or the great figures of letters and fashion he met. Any reader acquainted with maternal son-worship can imagine the scene: the simple, charming love with which country women track their darlings' careers in London. Whether John secured a brief, Tom attended a specific ball, or George dined with a celebrity, it brings immense joy to mothers and sisters in Somersetshire. Young Hopeful's letters become sacred texts, fueling village gossip and friendly congratulations. In the second winter, Pen visited briefly, cheering the widow's heart and brightening the lonely house at Fairoaks. Helen had her son entirely to herself; Laura was visiting old Lady Rockminster; the guests of Clavering Park were away; and the few remaining old friends, led by Dr. Portman, treated Mr. Pen with marked respect. Between mother and son, there was pure fondness, confidence, and affection. It was the happiest fortnight of the widow's life, perhaps of both theirs. The holiday vanished too swiftly, and Pen returned to the bustling world, leaving the gentle widow alone once more. She forwarded Arthur's money to Laura; I cannot determine why this young lady chose to leave home precisely when Pen arrived, nor whether he felt more piqued or relieved by her absence.

By this time, through his own merits and his uncle's introductions, Arthur was well-integrated into London, known in both literary and polite circles. His fashionable reputation proved invaluable; he was regarded as a gentleman of decent current means and promising future prospects, a trait considered the ultimate recommendation for a young literary aspirant. Bacon, Bungay & Co. proudly accepted his articles; Mr. Wenham invited him to dinner; Mr. Wagg regarded him favorably. They recounted how they met him at fashionable houses, where he was warmly welcomed regardless of his financial standing, thanks to his impeccable appearance, address, and reputation as a clever fellow. Eventually, he was invited to one house simply because he had been spotted at another. Thus, the young man experienced a vast array of London life, becoming familiar with everyone from Paternoster Row to Pimlico, feeling equally at home at Mayfair dining tables as at the tavern boards where his scribbling companions gathered.

Brimming with high spirits and curiosity, easily adapting to everyone he met, the young fellow delighted in this strange jumble of humanity, making himself welcome or at least comfortable wherever he went. For instance, he might breakfast at Mr. Plover's in the morning alongside a Peer, a Bishop, a parliamentary orator, two fashionable ladies, a popular preacher, the author of the latest novel, and the newest celebrity imported from Egypt or America. Then, he would depart this distinguished assembly for the back room of the newspaper office, where pens, ink, and wet proofs awaited. Here sat Finucane, the sub-editor, with fresh news from the Row; soon Shandon would arrive, nodding to Pen before beginning his leading article at the other end of the table, flanked by a pint of sherry that the serving boy would silently deliver upon spotting the Captain. Or Mr. Bludyer's roaring voice would echo from the front room, where that truculent critic would seize books from the counter despite the timid protests of Mr. Midge, the publisher. After inspecting the volumes, he would sell them at his stall, consume the proceeds in a tavern box, and then demand ink and paper to "smash" the author of his dinner and the novel. As evening approached, Mr. Pen would stroll toward his club to pick up Warrington for a constitutional walk. This exercise cleared the lungs and stimulated an appetite for dinner, after which Pen enjoyed the privilege of paying calls at various pleasant houses or exploring the town for amusement. There was the Opera, the Eagle Tavern, a ball in Mayfair, a quiet night with a cigar and a book and a long chat with Warrington, or a new sensation at the Back Kitchen. At this stage of his life, Mr. Pen witnessed all manner of places and men, likely unaware of the depth of his enjoyment until much later, when balls ceased to please him, farces failed to make him laugh, tavern jokes produced no excitement, and the loveliest dancer never stirred him from his chair after dinner. At his present mature age, all these pleasures are past; the times have moved on. It has been only a very short while since Bludyer bullied authors or cheated landlords, since Shandon slept his final sleep, and since honest Doolan was buried. Never again will he cringe or flatter, nor pull a long bow or drain a whisky-noggin.

The London season was now in full bloom, with fashionable newspapers teeming with details of grand banquets, routs, and balls enlivening the polite world. Our gracious Sovereign held levees and drawing rooms at St. James's; the bow-windows of the clubs were packed with the heads of respectable, red-faced, newspaper-reading gentlemen; thousands of carriages trailed along the Serpentine; squadrons of dandy horsemen trampled Rotten Row. Everyone was in town, and naturally, Major Arthur Pendennis, who was someone, was not absent.

On a certain morning, with his head wrapped in a smart bandana handkerchief and his meager frame enveloped in a brilliant Turkish dressing gown, the worthy gentleman sat by his fireside, letting his feet gently simmer in a bath while sipping his early tea and perusing the *Morning Post*.
