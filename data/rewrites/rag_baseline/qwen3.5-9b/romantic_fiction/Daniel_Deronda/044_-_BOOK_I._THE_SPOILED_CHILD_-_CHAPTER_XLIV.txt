The air in the garden hummed with a latent vitality, as if the earth itself were tuning a string instrument for unseen listeners. Fairy folk, invisible to the eye but keen of ear, listened intently as seeds broke their winter slumber beneath the soil. For their music, they needed no instruments; the hedgerows woke from a trance, sending sap trembling upward into budding green, creating little rhythmic floods of sound that filled the ears of spirits clad in light. Beauty, in this realm, was born of sound, vibrating into existence for senses refined beyond the mundane.

But Gwendolen was not listening to the symphony of the natural world. Her mind was a closed circuit, looping obsessively on Daniel Deronda. She pondered his thoughts with a intensity that bordered on feverish, wondering what constituted his "ideas about things" and how his days were truly spent. Yet, to frame the motives and adventures of her own life—or indeed, of anyone else's—required a complexity she lacked. It was as futile as expecting a lapdog to comprehend the grand strategy of canine existence. Just as impossible was it for her to conceive that Deronda's life was governed by the sweeping, historical destiny of the Jewish people; nor could she imagine him rising atop a brazen horse to vanish from her horizon as a twinkling star.

Despite the crushing weight of inferiority forced upon her by her marriage, Gwendolen inevitably imagined a far larger space for herself in Deronda's affections than she actually occupied. One assumes that older, wiser souls are immune to seeing their own anxieties or elations reflected in the minds of others, but Gwendolen, trapped in her youthful solitude, could not resist dwelling on the faintest signs of special interest he showed her. She mistook the color and proportion of those signals, projecting onto him a depth of regard that was not there.

So, what counsel would he offer? *He* had told her she must cultivate interest in others, acquire knowledge, and care for the "best things"—but how was she to begin? She wandered mentally through his potential recommendations, recalling famous writers she had either avoided or found impenetrable, harboring a half-smiling wish to mischief ask if they were simply "medicine for the mind." Then, guilt pricked her conscience. When safely away from observation, she ascended to her room carrying a miscellaneous selection: Descartes, Bacon, Locke, Butler, Burke, Guizot. As a clever young lady of education, she knew these authors were ornaments of mankind, assuming Deronda had devoured them. Hoping that by dipping into them sequentially, relying on her rapid intellect, she might bridge the chasm between their worlds, she sought a viewpoint closer to his level.

Yet, time for these vast mental excursions was a scarce commodity. Constantly, she had to appear on the scene as Mrs. Grandcourt, acutely aware of being watched by the exacting eyes of a husband who had found a potent motive to exercise his tenacity: ensuring his marriage answered every end he chose, and achieving that goal with greater completeness the more he discerned any opposing will in her. Whatever rebellions churned within her, Gwendolen could not reconcile herself to failure in her performance. No feeling had yet softened her toward any act, word, or look that might serve as a confession to the world. What she most dreaded was a violent impulse that would force an involuntary admission. Consequently, the will to remain silent in every other direction had thrown a frantic impetuosity into her confidences toward Deronda, to whom her thoughts continually turned as a lifeline against herself. Her riding, hunting, and socializing were performed in a spirit of grim achievement, devoid of zest or young gladness. Around Diplow, during those weeks of the new year, Mrs. Grandcourt was regarded as wearing her honors with triumphant detachment.

"She disguises it under an air of taking everything as a matter of course," observed Mrs. Arrowpoint. "A stranger might suppose she had condescended rather than risen. I always noticed that doubleness in her."

To her mother, above all, Gwendolen was bent on projecting complete satisfaction. Poor Mrs. Davilow was so effectively deceived that she interpreted the unexpected emotional distance maintained by her daughter—as enforced by Grandcourt's handsome provision for her—as a mere comparative indifference, now that marriage had supposedly created new interests. Being fetched for lunch and then dinner alongside the Gascoignes, driven back shortly after breakfast the following morning, and receiving brief calls where her husband waited outside on horseback or in the carriage, constituted the entirety of her permitted intercourse.

The truth was stark. The second time Gwendolen proposed inviting her mother with Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, Grandcourt had initially remained silent before drawling, "We can't be having *those people* always. Gascoigne talks too much. Country clergy are always bores—with their confounded fuss about everything."

That speech brimmed with foreboding for Gwendolen. To have her mother categorized under "those people" confirmed her previous dread of drawing her too near. Still, she could not articulate the true reasons; she could not tell her mother, "Mr. Grandcourt wishes to recognize you as little as possible; moreover, it is better you see little of my married life lest you discover I am miserable." So, she waved aside every allusion to the subject with practiced lightness. When Mrs. Davilow again hinted at securing a house near Ryelands, Gwendolen replied, "It would not be so nice for you as being near the rectory here, mamma. We shall perhaps be very little at Ryelands. You would miss my aunt and uncle."

All the while, this contemptuous veto by her husband on any intimacy with her family, driving her to proudly shrink from presenting them as troublesome pensioners, was stirring a deeper, inward inclination toward them. She had never felt so kindly toward her uncle, so disposed to recall his cheerful, complacent activity and spirit of kind management—even when mistaken—as a genuine comfort compared to the neutral, chilling loftiness that permeated her daily existence. Here, perhaps, she was unconsciously finding fragments of that mental enlargement that proved elusive from her occasional dashes into difficult authors, who demanded dismissal rather than blending with her agitations.

One day, a delightful surprise awaited the household at Offendene. Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne were present when Gwendolen rode up without her husband, accompanied only by the groom. All, including the four girls and Miss Merry, seated in the dining-room at lunch, witnessed the welcome approach. Even the elder sisters were not without a trace of Isabel's romantic sensibility, viewing the beautiful sister astride the splendid chestnut—which held its head as if proud to bear her—as a reincarnation of Harriet Byron or Miss Wardour emerging from her "happiness ever after."

Her uncle stepped to the door to offer his hand, and she sprang from her horse with an air of alacrity that encouraged that notion of guaranteed bliss. Gwendolen was particularly bent today on setting her mother's heart at rest; her unusual sense of freedom in making this solitary visit enabled her to endure the pressure of painful facts that were urging themselves anew. The seven family kisses were not so tiresome as they had been.

"Mr. Grandcourt is gone out, so I determined to fill up the time by coming to you, mamma," Gwendolen said, laying down her hat and seating herself beside her mother. Then, fixing her with a playfully monitory gaze, she added, "That is a punishment to you for not wearing better lace on your head. You didn't think I should come and detect you—you dreadfully careless-about-yourself, mamma!" She gave a caressing touch to the dear, worn head.

"Scold me, dear," said Mrs. Davilow, her delicate face flushing with delight. "But I wish there was something you could eat after your ride—instead of these scraps. Let Jocosa make you a cup of chocolate in your old way. You used to like that."

Miss Merry immediately rose to leave, though Gwendolen interjected, "Oh, no, a piece of bread, or one of those hard biscuits. I can't think about eating. I am come to say good-bye."

"What! Going to Ryelands again?" asked Mr. Gascoigne.

"No, we are going to town," Gwendolen said, beginning to break a piece of bread but putting no morsel into her mouth.

"It is rather early to go to town," remarked Mrs. Gascoigne, "and Mr. Grandcourt not in Parliament."

"Oh, there is only one more day's hunting to be had, and Henleigh has some business in town with lawyers, I think," Gwendolen replied. "I am very glad. I shall like to go to town."

"You will see your house in Grosvenor Square," said Mrs. Davilow. She and the girls were devouring every movement of their goddess, soon to vanish.

"Yes," Gwendolen said, her tone assenting to the interest of that expectation. "And there is so much to be seen and done in town."

"I wish, my dear Gwendolen," Mr. Gascoigne said, offering a kind of cordial advice, "that you would use your influence with Mr. Grandcourt to induce him to enter Parliament. A man of his position should make his weight felt in politics. The best judges are confident that the ministry will have to appeal to the country on this question of further Reform, and Mr. Grandcourt should be ready for the opportunity.
