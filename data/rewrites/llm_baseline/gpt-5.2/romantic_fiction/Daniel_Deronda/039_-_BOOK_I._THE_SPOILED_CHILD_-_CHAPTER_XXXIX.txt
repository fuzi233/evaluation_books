Two days after Daniel Deronda had sat in this same small Chelsea house and helped decide the name Mirah should carry into the world, the consequence that mattered most to the Meyricks arrived not as a thought but as a sound.

It was near four o’clock when wheels drew up outside—hansom wheels, quick and light, their iron rims brushing the cobbles with a brief, crisp hiss that died under the larger hush of the street. Then came a knock, not the friendly rap of a neighbor but a measured, authority-laden summons, followed by a ring that trembled a moment in the bell-wire like a bright note held too long. In a neighborhood where the most habitual music was the muffin-man’s cry and the clink of milk-pails, that ring enlarged the house itself—made the little front room feel, for an instant, as if it stood inside some vast social instrument waiting to be played.

All the girls were at home. The two rooms had been thrown into one by pushing back the door and shifting chairs, so that Kate could spread out her drawing things and the long embroidery might lie like a pale river across the space. It was their daily pageant: Mrs. Meyrick bending near one corner, needle dipping and rising with practiced economy; Mab in the middle, quick hands bright as sparrows; Amy at the far end, steadying the work so it would not drag. The satin cushions were gone—no frivolous softness today—this embroidery was their chief dish, their sustaining course, taken up whenever any clever fingers were free.

Mirah, who could hem and mend only with the makeshift neatness of a bird stitching leaves together, had been placed on a camp-stool and given the reader’s office—her voice the warm kettle-singing that kept their labor companionable. She also served Kate as a model for a title-page vignette: a fair public, as Kate had teased, absorbed in the successive volumes that came by way of the family tea-table. Mirah was reading Lamb—“The Praise of Chimney-Sweeps”—with a charming distinctness, and their smiles rose and fell with his “innocent blackness,” when the knock and ring lifted every head as one, like a sudden change in wind over a field.

“Dear me,” said Mrs. Meyrick, pausing with her needle in the air. “Can it be Lady Mallinger? Is there a grand carriage, Amy?”

“No—only a hansom cab,” Amy answered, peering. “It must be a gentleman.”

“The Prime Minister, I should think,” Kate said, dryly, charcoal poised like a weapon. “Hans says the greatest man in London may get into a hansom cab.”

“Oh, oh, oh!” Mab cried. “Suppose it should be Lord Russell!”

The old maid-servant came in with a card and that distracted air which always accompanied the appearance of rank; she even left the parlor-door open, and in the gap there was seen bowing toward Mrs. Meyrick a figure quite unlike any Premier: tall and physically imposing even in kid and kerseymere, massive of face, flamboyant of hair, gold spectacles catching what little light the afternoon offered and throwing it back as if he were already on a stage.

Mrs. Meyrick’s eyes ran to the card: Julius Klesmer.

If embarrassment could have made the little mother awkward, she would have been saved by the speed of her perceptions. Her heart gave a practical leap of satisfaction. He had come here, into their smallness, instead of requiring Mirah to climb into his largeness. That, to her, was the sign of active interest, of kindness with power behind it.

But as Klesmer entered, the rooms seemed to shrink into closets. The cottage piano, which had so often been a hearth of sound, looked to Mab suddenly like a toy set up for dolls. Their whole domestic arrangement—the embroidery, the scattered drawing materials, the Holbein faces pinned on the walls—had the minuteness and privacy of a mouse-establishment beneath palace staircases. Klesmer’s very way of glancing round him suggested multitudinous audiences, vast areas, orchestras warming up beyond the door. It was as if, with him, the air carried the remembered rustle of concert-halls: not the wholesome little ecology of their street—the distant hawker, the sparrows, the occasional laughter of children—but that other louder human weather, where applause comes down like rain and vanity may sprout like weeds.

And yet, in the same glance that enlarged the world, there was a keen, thoroughly kind sensibility taking in their details. He looked at the embroidery as one might look at a dish prepared with care in a small kitchen: not grand, perhaps, but honest and sustaining. He had known a home no larger on the outskirts of Bohemia; he had lived among the romances of small incomes where art and hunger share the same plate. He addressed Mrs. Meyrick with deference.

“I hope I have not taken too great a freedom. Being in the neighborhood, I ventured to save time by calling. Our friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned to me an understanding that I was to have the honor of becoming acquainted with a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth.”

Though he had discerned Mirah the instant he crossed the threshold, his politeness made him look bowingly at the three sisters as if uncertain which was the young lady in question.

“Those are my daughters: this is Miss Lapidoth,” Mrs. Meyrick said, turning her hand toward Mirah.

“Ah,” Klesmer answered, with a tone of gratified expectation, and he turned on Mirah a radiant smile and deep bow.

Mirah was not startled. There was calm pleasure in her face. She liked the look of him, feeling sure he would scold her as great musicians scold—severely, and for her good. His presence had its own acoustics: even his breathing seemed measured, as if his lungs kept time.

“You will not object to beginning our acquaintance by singing to me,” he said, stripping away ceremony as one peels fruit to reach what matters. “I shall be very glad,” Mirah replied, moving to the piano. “It is good of you to be willing to listen to me. Shall I accompany myself?”

“By all means,” Klesmer said, seating himself where, at Mrs. Meyrick’s invitation, he could see her clearly.

The little mother would not confess it as a weakness, but she said inwardly, with the quick practical instinct of women who have managed households on narrow means: He will like her singing better if he sees her.

All the feminine hearts except Mirah’s beat fast with anxiety. Klesmer sat with a listening frown that made him seem terrific; his spectacles flashed when he shifted his head, and the girls dared only furtive looks, as if the very act of watching might provoke condemnation. If he spoke severely, it would bruise them all.

But Mirah chose her song and began as if she had entered a familiar kitchen and set her hands to a task she must do well: not for show, but because it was necessary. Her voice rose in recitative—Leopardi’s Ode to Italy set to music—words like stone and fire: “O patria mia, vedo le mura e gli archi…” Her tone had the clean edge of something honed, but not metallic; it carried warmth as bread carries warmth from an oven. Then came the mournful melody—“Ma la gloria—non vedo”—a rhythmic plaint, as if grief itself were being kneaded into shape, pressed down and lifted, pressed down again, until it could be borne.

And then, from that sorrow, a devout triumph: an Andante where the subdued adoration seemed to move like a careful hand stirring a pot so it would not scorch—“Beatissimi voi…”—and after it the joyous outburst of an exultant Allegro, “Oh viva, oh viva,” her voice opening like a window flung wide on a clear day, letting light and air rush in.

When she ended, Klesmer held silence a moment, as if tasting what lingered.

“That is old Leo’s music,” he said at last.

“Yes. He was my last master—at Vienna: so fierce and so good,” Mirah answered, with a melancholy smile. “He prophesied that my voice would not do for the stage. And he was right.”

“Con-tinue, if you please,” Klesmer said, putting out his lips and shaking his long fingers; and while he made smothered sounds—words bitten in two and swallowed—none of the listeners could understand. The three girls detested him unanimously for not saying one word of praise. Mrs. Meyrick felt a little alarmed. Mirah, intent on doing what he desired, went on without pause, thinking now he would want German, and she sang Prince Radzivill’s settings for Gretchen in Faust, one after another, not daring to ask a question with her eyes even.

When she finished, Klesmer rose. He paced to the extremity of the small space as if it were a length of stage marked for him; then he walked back to the piano. Mirah had risen and stood looking toward him with her hands crossed before her, meekly awaiting judgment.

Suddenly his brow unknit. His eyes beamed. He stretched out his hand and said abruptly, as if the sentence had been waiting behind his teeth and could no longer be held in: “Let us shake hands: you are a musician.”

Mab felt herself beginning to cry. In the same breath, all three girls held Klesmer adorable. Mrs. Meyrick drew a long, relieved breath, as if the house itself had been granted a better air.

But the frown returned as quickly as if it belonged to his craft. He stretched out his hand again, back uppermost, touching Mirah’s with the finger-tip in a different meaning, and with protruded lip he said, “Not for great tasks. No high roofs. We are no skylarks. We must be modest.”

Mab’s adoration cooled at once. As if Mirah had shown the least conceit.

Mirah stayed silent, understanding that more was coming—something specific. And presently Klesmer went on, measured and practical.

“I would not advise—I would not further your singing in any larger space than a private drawing-room. But you will do there. And here in London that is one of the best careers open. Lessons will follow. Will you come and sing at a private concert at my house on Wednesday?”

“Oh, I shall be grateful,” Mirah said, and her hands came together as if in prayer. “I would rather get my bread in that way than by anything more public. I will try to improve. What should I work at most?”

Klesmer made preliminary answers in noises that were hardly words—his fingers shaking as if he were seasoning a dish by instinct before he gave the recipe aloud—then said clearly, “I shall introduce you to Astorga: he is the foster-father of good singing and will give you advice.”

Then he addressed Mrs. Meyrick. “Mrs. Klesmer will call before Wednesday, with your permission.”

“We shall feel that to be a great kindness,” Mrs. Meyrick said.

“You will sing to her,” Klesmer added, turning again to Mirah. “She is a thorough musician, and has a soul with more ears to it than you will often get in a musician. Your singing will satisfy her: ‘Vor den Wissenden sich stellen;’ you know the rest?”

“Sicher ist’s in allen Fällen,” Mirah replied promptly.

“Schön!” Klesmer said, and put out his hand again in farewell.

He had chosen the most delicate way of praising Mirah, and the Meyrick girls now gave him their entire esteem. But Mab’s amazement was renewed when Klesmer suddenly fixed his eyes on her and said decisively, “That young lady is musical, I see!”

Mab became nothing but a blush and a scorching sense of being exposed.

“Yes,” Mirah said for her, quick as kindness. “And she has a touch.”

“Oh, please, Mirah—a scramble, not a touch,” Mab said, in anguish, with the horrible fear that this dreadful divining personage—Satan in gray trousers—might order her to the piano on the spot. Her heart went soft and hot like wax.

But then her amazement turned to joy when Klesmer said benignantly, turning to Mrs. Meyrick, “Will she like to accompany Miss Lapidoth and hear the music on Wednesday?”

“There could hardly be a greater pleasure for her,” Mrs. Meyrick answered. “She will be most glad and grateful.”

Klesmer bowed round to the three sisters more grandly than they had ever been bowed to. It made an amusing picture: the little room with so much of its diagonal taken up by that magnificent bend, the small feminine figures like images a little less than life-size, and the grave Holbein faces on the walls looking hard at this stranger whose face seemed a dignified contemporary of their own, while his garments seemed, to Kate’s fastidious eye, a lamentable mockery of the human form.

Mrs. Meyrick could not help going out with him and closing the door behind her. The parlor’s hum—thread drawn through cloth, breath, the faint settling creak of the floor—was shut away. In the passage, Klesmer understood her at once, and with a frowning nod said, “She will do: if she doesn’t attempt too much and her voice holds out, she can make an income. I know that is the great point: Deronda told me. You are taking care of her. She looks like a good girl.”

“She is an angel,” Mrs. Meyrick said, warmth breaking through her prudence.

“No,” Klesmer answered, with a playful nod. “She is a pretty Jewess: the angels must not get the credit of her. But I think she has found a guardian angel,” he added, and bowed himself out.

Inside, the four young creatures stared at one another mutely until the front door banged and Mrs. Meyrick re-entered. Then came the explosion—sound returning like water released.

Mab clapped her hands and danced everywhere inconveniently; Mrs. Meyrick kissed Mirah and blessed her; Amy said, with the emphasis of one thinking already of pins and pennies, “We can never get her a new dress before Wednesday!” and Kate exclaimed, “Thank heaven my table is not knocked over!”

Mirah had reseated herself on the music-stool without speaking. Tears rolled down her cheeks as she looked at her friends, as if happiness had overflowed and found no other outlet.

“Now, now, Mab!” Mrs. Meyrick said. “Come and sit down reasonably and let us talk.”

“Yes, let us talk,” Mab cried, returning to her low seat and hugging her knees. “I am beginning to feel large again. Hans said he was coming this afternoon. I wish he had been here—only there would have been no room for him. Mirah, what are you looking sad for?”

“I am too happy,” Mirah said. “I feel so full of gratitude to you all; and he was so very kind.”

“Yes, at last,” Mab said sharply. “But he might have said something encouraging sooner. I thought him dreadfully ugly when he sat frowning and only said, ‘Con-tinue.’ I hated him all the long way from the top of his hair to the toe of his polished boot.”

“Nonsense, Mab; he has a splendid profile,” Kate said.

“Now, but not then. I cannot bear people to keep their minds bottled up for the sake of letting them off with a pop. They seem to grudge making you happy unless they can make you miserable beforehand. However, I forgive him everything,” Mab added with a magnanimous lift of chin, “but he has invited me. I wonder why he fixed on me as the musical one? Was it because I have a bulging forehead, ma, and peep from under it like a newt from under a stone?”

“It was your way of listening to the singing, child,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “He has magic spectacles and sees everything through them, depend upon it. But what was that German quotation you were so ready with, Mirah—you learned puss?”

“Oh, that was not learning,” Mirah said, her tearful face breaking into an amused smile. “I said it so many times for a lesson. It means that it is safer to do anything—singing or anything else—before those who know and understand all about it.”

“That was why you were not one bit frightened, I suppose,” Amy said. “But now, what we have to talk about is a dress for you on Wednesday.”

“I don’t want anything better than this black merino,” Mirah said, rising to show it—simple, serviceable, the cloth already acquainted with their little household smells: tea-steam, warmed wool, the faint iron scent of needles. “Some white gloves and some new bottines.” She put out her little foot, still in the famous felt slipper that had padded so quietly through their days.

“There comes Hans,” Mrs. Meyrick said, as footsteps sounded—boyish, quick, with the particular thud and scrape of someone who brought the outside world in on his boots. “Stand still, and let us hear what he says about the dress. Artists are the best people to consult about such things.”

“You don’t consult me, ma,” Kate said, lifting an eyebrow in playful complaint. “I notice mothers are like the people I deal with—the girls’ doings are always priced low.”

“My dear child,” Mrs. Meyrick replied, “the boys are such a trouble—we could never put up with them, if we didn’t make believe they were worth more,” just as her son entered.

Hans came in with his habitual air of being too large for the room in a different way from Klesmer—youthful largeness, as if he carried future canvases inside him. Mrs. Meyrick wasted no time.

“Hans, we want your opinion about Mirah’s dress. A great event has happened. Klesmer has been here, and she is going to sing at his house on Wednesday among grand people. She thinks this dress will do.”

“Let me see,” Hans said.

Mirah, in her childlike docility, turned toward him to be looked at; and he, stepping back, knelt with one knee on a hassock to survey her as if she were already a figure arranged for a picture.

“This would be thought a very good stage-dress for me,” she said pleadingly, “in a part where I was to come on as a poor Jewess and sing to fashionable Christians.”

“It would be effective,” Hans admitted, with a considering air; “it would stand out well among the fashionable chiffons.”

“But you ought not to claim all the poverty on your side, Mirah,” Amy put in. “There are plenty of poor Christians and dreadfully rich Jews and fashionable Jewesses.”

“I didn’t mean any harm,” Mirah said quickly. “Only I have been used to thinking about my dress for parts in plays. And I almost always had a part with a plain dress.”

“That makes me think it questionable,” Hans said, and in that moment he became as fastidious and conventional as he had thought Deronda had been about the Berenice pictures. “It looks a little too theatrical. We must not make you a rôle of the poor Jewess—or of being a Jewess at all.”

Underneath his words lay something he did not quite know how to confess: a desire to neutralize the Jewess in private life, to stop that name from being a label others might use like a blunt instrument.

But Mirah’s answer came steady, like a truth she had carried across seas and hungers.

“But it is what I am really. I am not pretending anything. I shall never be anything else,” she said. “I always feel myself a Jewess.”

“But we can’t feel that about you,” Hans said, and there was a devout look in him, as if he were trying to lift her out of any harshness the world might throw at her. “What does it signify whether a perfect woman is a Jewess or not?”

“That is your kind way of praising me; I never was praised so before,” Mirah said, smiling—smiling in a way that was rather maddening to Hans because it made his own generosity feel cosmopolitan and incomplete, as if it spoke a language that missed certain native sounds.

“People don’t think of me as a British Christian,” he said, his face creasing merrily. “They think of me as an imperfectly handsome young man and an unpromising painter.”

“But you are wandering from the dress,” Amy insisted. “If that will not do, how are we to get another before Wednesday? and to-morrow Sunday?”

“Indeed this will do,” Mirah said entreatingly; and then, looking at Hans with that misplaced trust which always made his conscience wince, she added, “It is all real, you know—even if it seemed theatrical. Poor Berenice sitting on the ruins—any one might say that was theatrical, but I know that this is just what she would do.”

Hans flinched as if she had put her finger on a bruise.

“I am a scoundrel,” he burst out. “That is my invention. Nobody knows that she did that. Shall you forgive me for not saying so before?”

“Oh, yes,” Mirah said, after a small pause of surprise. “You knew it was what she would be sure to do—a Jewess who had not been faithful—who had done what she did and was penitent. She could have no joy but to afflict herself; and where else would she go? I think it is very beautiful that you should enter so into what a Jewess would feel.”

“The Jewesses of that time sat on ruins,” Hans said, starting up with the sense of being checkmated by her earnestness. “That makes them convenient for pictures.”

“But the dress—the dress,” Amy repeated, not to be drawn into moral histories when there was cloth to be bought. “Is it settled?”

“Yes; is it not?” Mirah asked, looking doubtfully at Mrs. Meyrick.

Mrs. Meyrick looked to her son. “What do you think, Hans?”

“That dress will not do,” Hans said decisively. “She is not going to sit on ruins. You must jump into a cab with her, little mother, and go to Regent Street. It’s plenty of time to get anything you like—a black silk dress such as ladies wear. She must not be taken for an object of charity. She has talents to make people indebted to her.”

“I think it is what Mr. Deronda would like—for her to have a handsome dress,” Mrs. Meyrick said, deliberating. In her mind was Daniel’s quiet care for Mirah’s dignity—care that never rang loud, never made a spectacle of itself.

“Of course it is,” Hans said, with some sharpness. “You may take my word for what a gentleman would feel.”

“I wish to do what Mr. Deronda would like me to do,” Mirah said gravely; and Hans, turning on his heel as if his own feelings needed to be masked by activity, went to Kate’s table and took up one of her drawings, pretending sudden interest.

“Shouldn’t you like to make a study of Klesmer’s head, Hans?” Kate said. “I suppose you have often seen him?”

“Seen him!” Hans exclaimed, and immediately threw back his head and mane, seated himself at the piano, and looked round as if he were surveying an amphitheatre. He held his fingers down perpendicularly toward the keys, his face a swift caricature of Klesmer’s grandeur. The room filled with their brief laughter—the kind of laughter that is a balm after strain, restoring the small domestic soundscape to its usual cheerful measures.

But in another instant he wheeled round on the stool, looked at Mirah, and said half timidly, “Perhaps you don’t like this mimicry; you must always stop my nonsense when you don’t like it.”

Mirah had been smiling at the quickly made image; she smiled still, but now with something gentler and deeper than amusement.

“Thank you. But you have never done anything I did not like,” she said. “I hardly think he could, belonging to you,” she added, looking at Mrs. Meyrick.

Hans took that as food for hope. How could the rose help it when, one after another, several bees came and mistook its sweetness for a message meant only for them?
