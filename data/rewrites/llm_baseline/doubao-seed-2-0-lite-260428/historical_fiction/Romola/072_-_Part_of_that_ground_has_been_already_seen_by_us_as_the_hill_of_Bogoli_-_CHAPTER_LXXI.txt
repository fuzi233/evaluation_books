When Romola brought Tessa and the two children home to her villa in the last fading days of April, the banks of the Arno were already losing their pale spring blossom, and a second, heavier anxiety hung over her alongside the quiet work of sheltering the little family. That anxiety had swelled to a fever pitch with the official print publication of Fra Girolamo Savonarola’s purported trial record: the set of confessions the sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned to interrogate him had drawn from his broken frame.

The document, issued under the Signoria’s direct order, sparked such immediate, furious public suspicion and protest that the ruling council rushed out decrees to seize and burn every circulating copy. But inevitably, some volumes were mislaid or hidden away, and an unauthorized, pirated second edition was soon being passed between eager hands in apothecary shops, monastic cells, and back street taverns, every Florentine literate enough to read hungrily devouring its pages. Romola, who had long since despaired of ever being granted a chance to speak to the imprisoned Fra Girolamo, read and re-read the printed text, desperate to judge its truth for herself, rather than being tossed between the contradictory, loud assertions of his fierce partisans and his bitter enemies.

The most devout of Savonarola’s followers were struck dumb by his public disavowal of his prophetic claims, his apparent admission that he had lied of being sent by God to reform Florence and the corrupt Church. But that initial shock soon hardened into certainty: every line that ran counter to their faith in him was a forgery, penned by Ser Ceccone, the disreputable, famously hostile notary who had compiled the interrogation digest. It was impossible, they argued, to trust the document at all. Half of the sixteen appointed examiners were his most vicious foes. The brutal gang leader Dolfo Spini, head of the anti-Savonarola Compagnacci, had been prematurely seated on the new governing Eight specifically to stack the deck against a man whose ruin the ruling faction had already preordained. This was no trial: it was a slow, formally staged murder being played out inside the walls of the Palazzo Vecchio. The Signoria had struck a bargain to hand the Frate over to the Pope and the Duke of Milan in exchange for the right to tax Church lands, rid themselves of the man who hounded corrupt local citizens, greedy foreign tyrants, and debased clergy alike. The only remaining point of negotiation was whether Florence would deliver him alive to Rome, or claim the small, vainglorious privilege of hanging and burning their own prophet in their own main piazza. Who under such circumstances would believe a word of this so-called confession? The denial of his prophetic gift, they insisted, had been wrenched from his frail, sensitive body by the agony of the rope and pulley torture—far more rounds than the examiners’ lie of three sessions in a single day, torture enough to break any man’s mind to raving.

More moderate supporters of Savonarola, less ardent in their partisan faith, conceded that the core of the confession was almost certainly genuine, even if Ceccone had twisted phrasing, added malicious flourishes, and reordered lines to paint him in the worst possible light. But they raged that no content in the record justified a death sentence, or even heavy punishment. No charge of political treason had ever been proven against him. No stain of personal vice had ever been found on his character: even his brother monks, including a former secretary of several years who had long criticized Savonarola’s strict rule as Prior, testified even after the shock of his retraction that his life was marked by unimpeachable purity and consistent devotion, which had commanded their unforced veneration. Even the Pope himself had never managed to raise a formal heresy charge against the Frate, only citing him for disobeying a summons to Rome and ignoring the sentence of excommunication. Grave, respected Florentine men felt a deep moral rebellion against the corrupt Roman Curia, softening the scandal of Savonarola’s disobedience: they saw clearly that the triumph of his enemies was nothing less than the triumph of unbridled, gross licence. Sharp, pragmatic statesmen like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini smiled bitterly at the farce of the trial, noting acidly that if the Frate had faked his prophecies to serve powerful men rather than oppose them, no one would have called it fraud—it would have been praised as clever lubrication for the wheels of Church and state. Even these shrewd men, however, were forced to concede that Pope Alexander VI had little choice but to try to eliminate Savonarola: the Frate had openly called for a General Council of the Church to depose the Borgia pontiff, a direct, dangerous act of rebellion that threatened the very foundation of Rome’s power. Savonarola had told Florence openly, and everyone understood his meaning, that he would never obey the devil. The fight between the Frate and the Pope was inevitably a life-and-death struggle—but Florence never had to volunteer to be the Pope’s executioner.

Romola’s ears hummed with these two competing narratives: the ardent, wounded faith of the piagnoni, and the cold, worldly pragmatism of men who judged all actions by the moderate standard of common human frailty. She could not rest satisfied with either. As she pored over the printed pages, she called up half-conscious memories of small, unremarked inconsistencies she had noted through the years of her discipleship, whispering a quiet premonition that Savonarola’s retraction of his prophetic claims was not merely a spasmodic, desperate effort to escape torture. But her soul cried out for an explanation of his lapses that would let her still believe the core of his life’s striving was pure and grand. She remembered the bitter, blighting selfish despair that had overwhelmed her when she first lost her faith in the man who had once been the very embodiment of the highest moral purpose to her, and a new, stubborn faith rose in her out of the ruins of that despair. It could not be true, she told herself, that the disbelieving thoughts that had turned her soul arid and empty were rooted in reality. The spirit that had breathed through the Frate’s sermons, that had lit a new life in her, had been a living thing, not a hollow pretence. Whatever falsity lay in his claims, it had been a fall, not a premeditated plan: a gradual tangle he had wandered into and struggled to escape, not a fraud he had designed for profit from the start.

Many lines on the printed page bore the obvious stamp of clumsy, alien fabrication: overblown, repetitious self-accusation of a kind no man but the crudest hypocrite would ever utter to his fellows. That these forgeries stood out so sharply against the rest of the text only strengthened Romola’s impression that the bulk of the record was true to words the Frate had actually spoken. Not a single line dishonoured him save for the section disavowing his prophetic claims. He never wavered in stating that the ends he had pursued had always been the moral good of Florence, the reform of the Church, and the betterment of all humanity. Even through the notary’s malicious edits, Fra Girolamo shone through as a man who had indeed lusted for glory, but for glory earned by labouring for the highest possible end: to turn abstract moral belief into tangible action that shaped every detail of ordinary people’s lives. One famous, memorable passage, even through its possible erasures and interpolations, echoed all too clearly in Romola’s memory:
“Everything that I have done, I did with the design of being forever famous in this age and ages to come; that I might win credit in Florence, so that no great matter of import should be done in the city without my sanction. Once I had secured that standing, I meant to do great things across Italy and beyond its borders, through the great rulers I had befriended and consulted on high matters, including the summoning of that General Council. As my first efforts bore fruit, I would have moved to further, greater works. Above all, once the General Council was assembled, I intended to rouse the princes of Christendom, and especially those beyond the Alps, to subdue the infidels. It was never much in my thoughts to make myself a Cardinal or Pope, for when I had achieved the work I set out to do, I would have been the first man in the world in authority and reverence, without ever taking the papal crown. If the office had been offered to me I would not have refused it, but it seemed to me that leading that great work was a far greater thing than being Pope—for any man, even a vicious man, may be made Pope, but such a work as I contemplated demands a man of excellent, unyielding virtue.”

That blending of burning ambition and absolute certainty that he was the man chosen to fulfil God’s grand design was no new revelation to Romola. It was the habitual tone of Savonarola’s mind, the voice she had heard ring through the rafters of the Duomo a hundred times. In moments of private ecstatic contemplation, his sense of self melted away into awe of the Unspeakable, and in that space lay the roots of genuine, unfeigned humility. But standing before the crowds who looked to him for leadership, pre-eminence felt to him like a necessary condition of his life’s purpose. It was no wonder, Romola thought, that under the terrible pressure of his new circumstances, his old certainty could shatter. When instead of adoring faces and kneeling crowds, instead of a great work rising to success that stamped him as a chosen instrument of God, he was met with jeers, spittle, curses, the hard faces of his enemies sitting as his judges, then the agony of torture pulling his limbs apart until the irrepressible cry burst out of him: “It is true, whatever you say. Let me go. Do not torture me again. Yes, yes, I am guilty. O God, thy stroke has reached me.”

The thought of that agony, and the deeper, sharper sorrow that must have followed in the silence of his cell after the confession was wrung from him, pressed on Romola’s own heart until slow, bitter tears tracked down her cheeks. Every shallow, self-satisfied fool in Florence was glibly passing judgement on the man’s supposed sins, while he endured a depth of sorrow only known to souls that have strived for the most perfect good, and then seen themselves fallen.

As the days passed, no new transcripts of further interrogations circulated; no changes appeared in his recorded confessions. At last, Savonarola was left alone in his prison cell, and granted pen and ink, to write with his bruised, overstrained right arm if he wished. What he wrote was no defence of his innocence, no protest against the injustice of his trial. It was a long, tender colloquy with the divine purity he longed to be reunited with, an outpouring of unreserved self-abasement, one long, desperate cry for inner renewal. None of his old fiery self-assertion remained, no thunderous proclamations that his enemies were the children of the devil. The soft voice of his own sadness told him: “God placed thee in the midst of his people as one of his chosen excellent servants. In this way thou hast taught others, and failed to learn the lesson thyself. Thou hast cured the wounds of others, and thou thyself art still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up at the beauty of thy own good deeds, and through that thou hast lost thy wisdom, and become, and shalt be to all eternity, nothing. After so many benefits with which God has honoured thee, thou art fallen into the depths of the sea; and after so many gifts bestowed on thee, thou, by thy pride and vainglory, hast scandalised all the world.” And when Hope spoke to him, it no longer argued that he would finish his great work, that he would triumph over his enemies. It said only: “Thou art not forsaken, else why is thy heart bowed in penitence? That penitence itself is a gift from God.”

There is no jot of worthy evidence that from the time of his imprisonment to his final, supreme moment, Savonarola ever thought or spoke of himself as a martyr. The idea of martyrdom had once been a grand passion for him, sharing equal space in his dreams with the triumph of seeing his great reform work completed. Now both were gone, replaced by a quiet, unglorified resignation that he never described in grand, heroic terms.

And that is why he may be called a martyr by all subsequent ages, all the more truly. The power that rose up to destroy him did not strike him for his sins, but for his greatness—not because he sought to deceive the world, but because he sought to make it noble. Through that very greatness he endured a double agony: not just the reviling, the torture, the final throes of death, but the deeper, far more searing agony of sinking down from the bright vision of glorious achievement into that deep shadow where he could only whisper: “I count as nothing now. Darkness encompasses me. Yet the light I saw once was the true light.”
