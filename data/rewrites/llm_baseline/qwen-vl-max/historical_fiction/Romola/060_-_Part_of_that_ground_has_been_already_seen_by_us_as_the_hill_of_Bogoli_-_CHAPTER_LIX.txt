The morning light was still fresh and clear when Romola set out once more for San Marco, her heart heavy with purpose. The previous evening, through the intercession of Fra Salvestro, she had secured an audience with Fra Girolamo in the chapter-house of the convent. Such meetings were rare—Savonarola guarded his privacy with rigid care, fearing even the faintest shadow of scandal. When granted, they were conducted with deliberate openness, never shrouded in secrecy. That was why this meeting was scheduled at a time when others might be present in the outer cloisters—so that no suspicion could cling to their conversation.

Romola chose the main route through Florence, not merely for convenience, but to gauge the mood of the city. Every loggia, every corner of the piazza, every shop that served as a gathering place for gossipers, buzzed with talk. Trade had slowed, and in its absence, politics had taken over. The people were divided—some demanding swift justice for the conspirators, others insisting on the right of appeal under Florentine law. Three days remained before the sentence was to be carried out, and both sides were using the interval to sway public opinion.

Handbills had already begun to circulate. Some bore bold declarations: “Justice or Ruin!” Others proclaimed, “Observe the Law! Uphold the Appeal!” Around these stark headlines, smaller print swam with arguments—arguments that few would read, for it was believed, in those early days of printing, that large type alone could move the masses. Yet Romola cared less for the slogans than for the reasoning beneath them. She hurried forward, glancing anxiously about, determined not to miss a single copy.

For much of the way, she saw only handbills already clutched by eager readers or nailed to walls—only to be torn down by sbirri, the city’s enforcers, who seemed to take pleasure in erasing dissent. But then, near San Giovanni, she spotted Bratti, a familiar figure from the lower ranks of Florentine life. He stood hunched over a bundle of wet sheets, exchanging them for small coins.

“Bratti,” she called, stepping closer. “Do you have two kinds? Give me one of each.”

“Two kinds?” he replied, slowly separating the papers, his movements deliberate enough to make Romola impatient. “One says ‘Law.’ One says ‘Justice.’”

“And which sells better?”

“‘Justice’—it flies off fastest. So I raised the price to two danari. Then I thought—why should ‘Law’ not be worth as much? People don’t value things if they’re cheap. If I sold it for one danaro, I’d be doing it a disservice. I’m fair, I am. ‘Law’ or ‘Justice,’ they’re both good wares. I got them free, and I sell them fairly. You want more than one?”

“No,” said Romola, slipping a white quattrino into his hand. “Just one of each.” She folded the papers quickly and moved on.

She soon reached the outer cloisters of San Marco. Fra Salvestro waited beneath the arches, chatting with several lay visitors. Under the new government’s favor, the fear that had once kept people away after Savonarola’s excommunication had faded. Among the group, Romola recognized Andrea Cambini—a known follower of Francesco Valori—gesturing emphatically as he spoke. Fra Salvestro listened with mild interest, the kind of attention given to news without regard for its substance.

Romola felt a surge of irritation. She knew from the fragments of speech drifting toward her that Cambini was discussing the fate of the conspirators. She did not approach. Instead, she walked directly to the door of the chapter-house, and as Fra Salvestro noticed her, he gave a nod and stepped aside into the inner cloister.

A lay brother opened the door for her and closed it behind her. Inside, the air was still, the walls lined with frescoes of mournful saints and prophets. Romola glanced up—those painted figures seemed to watch her with quiet sorrow, just as they had when Dino died. For a moment, memories stirred—the grief, the silence, the desperate hope. But now, her mind was too full of the present to dwell on the past. It was not remembrance so much as a haunting echo, mingling with her fears like a ghostly prelude to what might come.

She pulled out the handbills and began to read. By the time she finished the one advocating the rule of law, the door creaked open. She folded the papers and stood waiting.

Fra Girolamo entered. Romola knelt, as custom dictated, but rose immediately after his blessing. It had been many months since she had seen him—since he had been cut off from public life—and the change in him was striking. His face bore deeper lines, etched by long hours of study, fasting, and sleepless nights. Yet it was not just age or exhaustion that disturbed her. There was something else—something harder, more distant. Was it the absence of the serene compassion she once saw in his eyes? Or was it the growing chasm between her own feelings and his? Perhaps both.

He looked at her with a glance that held a hint of self-assurance, almost defensiveness. He knew why she had come. He knew she would speak of the condemned men—especially Bernardo del Nero, her godfather. And he knew that such a plea would challenge not only his judgment, but his authority.

“It is a matter of great weight,” he began, his voice calm but controlled. “I know you do not come lightly.”

“Father,” Romola said, her voice rising with emotion, “you know what I need. It is my godfather—Bernardo del Nero. He has lived in truth and honor all his life. Since I can remember, he has been as a father to me. That is why I come—not because I expect you to save him, but because I must try. Perhaps you have already decided to use your influence to uphold the law. Perhaps you have already chosen to let justice be denied to those who most deserve it.”

“I do not meddle in state affairs,” Savonarola replied firmly. “I preached for good governance, yes—for a government that serves the Christian life. But I do not interfere in matters best left to experienced citizens.”

“Then how can you say that?” Romola interrupted, her voice trembling. “This is not a minor issue. This is about the right to appeal—about mercy. You helped win that right for Florentines. Does it not follow that you must defend it now, especially when it concerns men like Bernardo?”

She paused, then added, “If you believe in the power of mercy, then why deny it to those who are innocent of treason, yet accused because of their name? Why allow hatred to triumph over law?”

There was a flicker in Savonarola’s lips—a brief contraction, like pain. But he did not yield.

“My daughter,” he said, “I speak only as I am guided. I cannot claim divine insight in every matter. In this case, I have no vision beyond human wisdom. The law of appeal was passed to protect Florentines from tyranny. But these five men conspired to destroy the Republic. They refused to bring their case before the Great Council. They have forfeited their right to appeal.”

“How can they have forfeited it?” Romola demanded. “They have not been condemned yet. And if it is private hatred that denies them the appeal—if it is fear that drives the council apart—then why not stand against it? You yourself taught that hatred must not find its way into public acts. Do you not see that this is exactly what is happening? That very hatred you warned against is being used to justify murder.”

Savonarola’s voice grew heavier. “There is mercy, and there is weakness. At times, mercy becomes treason. Florence needs strength now. Not only to punish past crimes, but to prevent future ones. The tyrant still gathers forces in Romagna. The enemies of Florence wait in the courts of Italy. To spare these men would be to endanger the whole city.”

“What plot?” Romola asked, her voice sharp with alarm. “You speak of a second conspiracy. What proof do you have?”

“You carry papers,” Savonarola said, pointing to the handbills. “One may tell you.”

Romola unfolded the unread sheet. It confirmed her worst fears: the government had uncovered a new plot, set to unfold in August. Her breath caught. Tito had lied. He had claimed to want Savonarola’s help—when in truth, he had only wanted to escape. The betrayal was complete.

She crumpled the paper and turned back to Savonarola.

“Father,” she said, her voice breaking, “how can Florence be safe when the worst men always escape? And you—you encouraged this deception by favoring Lorenzo Tornabuoni, who wore two faces, while my godfather has always been honest. Ask Florence who among the five has the truest heart. I dare say Bernardo del Nero will be named by most. You once intervened for another prisoner. You were not neutral then. Why are you silent now?”

“I do not wish Bernardo dead,” Savonarola said, his face darkening. “Only banished.”

“Then why not speak for him?” Romola cried. “Why let an old man of seventy-five die in shame, when you could give him the chance the law allows? Is it because he does not flatter you? Because he is not loyal to San Marco? Is it because he is not one of yours?”

She paused, her cheeks flushed, her lips trembling. Silence stretched between them.

Then, suddenly, she stepped forward. “Forgive me, father. I know I have spoken harshly. But I cannot stop. I was weak, I was blind—but I believed in you. I trusted your light. Now… I cannot see it. You say the soul must listen to its own voice. Then I must speak.”

Savonarola stood motionless. He was not angry—though he could have been. But he was burdened. He saw the depth of her pain, the sincerity of her love. And he saw, too, the difficulty of his own position. He was not free to act as conscience alone commanded. He was bound by duty, by politics, by the weight of expectation.

“You have my pardon,” he said quietly. “You speak from love. But love must yield to the greater good. The leaders of Florence believe the safety of the Republic demands this punishment. I cannot override their judgment.”

“Then you desire their death,” Romola said, her voice low but fierce. “You do not protest. You do not urge the law. You let them die.”

“I do not desire their death,” he repeated.

“Then why do nothing?” she shouted. “You could have spoken. You could have reminded them of the law you helped pass. You could have said—this is not justice. But you are silent. You are afraid. Or worse—you are glad.”

Her words hung in the air. Savonarola’s face hardened.

“Be thankful,” he said, “that your soul has not faced such trials. You see only one side. I see many. I must choose the path that furthers the work God has given me. The death of five men—were they less guilty—is a small price to pay for stopping tyranny, for advancing God’s kingdom upon earth. That is my purpose. That is why I live. That is why I am ready to die.”

Romola stared at him. Once, she would have bowed her head in reverence. Now, she saw only contradiction. His words rang hollow. He spoke of God’s kingdom, yet defended the bloodshed of innocents. He claimed purity, yet stood by while hatred ruled.

“You speak of God’s kingdom,” she said, her voice shaking. “But what if it is not God’s kingdom you seek? What if it is only your own power? The French king brought reform? No. He brought conquest. Take care, father—lest your enemies be right. Lest your visions be only dreams of dominance.”

Savonarola’s eyes flashed. “The cause of my party *is* the cause of God’s kingdom.”

“I do not believe it,” Romola whispered, her body trembling. “God’s kingdom is wider. Wider than hatred. Wider than vengeance. If it is not, then let me stand outside it—with the ones I love.”

They stood facing each other—two souls, two convictions, two truths. No words remained.

Romola turned, covered her head, and walked out in silence.
