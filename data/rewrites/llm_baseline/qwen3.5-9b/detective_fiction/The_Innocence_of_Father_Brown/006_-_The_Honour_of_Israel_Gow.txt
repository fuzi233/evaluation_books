A storm of olive and silver hues closed in upon Father Brown as he traversed the grey valley, emerging at the terminus of a peculiar glen to behold the strange castle of Glengyle. It stood like a blind alley at the end of the world, its steep roofs and spires of sea-green slate rising in the fashion of old French-Scottish châteaux. To an Englishman, the structure resembled the sinister witch-steeplehats of fairy tales, framed by pine woods that rocked around the green turrets, appearing as black as countless flocks of ravens. This atmosphere of dreamy, almost sleepy devilry was not merely scenic; it rested upon the place like a cloud of pride, madness, and mysterious sorrow, heavier on Scottish noble houses than on any other lineage. Scotland possesses a double dose of the poison known as heredity: the aristocrat's obsession with bloodline and the Calvinist's sense of inevitable doom.

The priest had seized a day from his duties in Glasgow to join his friend Flambeau, the amateur detective, who was stationed at Glengyle alongside a formal Scotland Yard officer. They were investigating the life and sudden disappearance of the late Earl of Glengyle. This enigmatic figure was the last survivor of a race whose valor, insanity, and violent cunning had rendered them terrifying even among the sinister nobility of the sixteenth century. Few delved deeper into the labyrinthine ambitions surrounding Mary Queen of Scots than the Ogilvies of Glengyle.

Local rhymes attested to their motives and results:

For centuries, no "decent" lord had ever resided in Glengyle Castle. By Victorian standards, one might assume all eccentricities were exhausted, yet the last Earl satisfied his tribal tradition by doing the only thing left for him: disappearing. He did not leave the country; by all accounts, he remained within the castle walls, or wherever he was. Though his name appeared in church registers and the Peerage, no one ever saw him under the sun.

Only a solitary manservant claimed to have seen him. Described variously as deaf, dumb, or half-witted, this gaunt, red-haired laborer with a dogged jaw and blank blue eyes was named Israel Gow. He was the sole silent servant on the deserted estate. Yet, his vigorous digging of potatoes and his punctual disappearances into the kitchen led observers to believe he was provisioning a hidden superior. When pressed, Gow persistently insisted the master was not at home. One morning, the provost and the minister were summoned to the castle, only to discover that the gardener, groom, and cook had added the profession of undertaker to their roles, nailing up their noble master in a coffin. Whether this oddity warranted further inquiry remained unclear until Flambeau arrived a few days prior. By then, the body—or what was believed to be the body—of Lord Glengyle had lain for some time in the local churchyard.

As Father Brown passed through the dim garden beneath the looming chateau, the clouds thickened, the air grew damp and thundery, and the last streaks of sunset faded. Against the gloom, he spotted a black silhouette: a man in a chimney-pot hat carrying a large spade. The image was strangely reminiscent of a sexton, but recalling the deaf servant who tended the potatoes, Father Brown deemed it natural. He understood the Scotch peasant's pragmatism—their respectability often necessitating formal dress for inquiries, and their economy refusing to waste an hour of digging. Even the man's startled, suspicious glare as the priest passed aligned perfectly with the vigilance of such a character.

The great door was opened by Flambeau himself, accompanied by a lean man with iron-grey hair holding papers: Inspector Craven of Scotland Yard. The entrance hall was largely stripped and empty, though the pale, sneering visages of the wicked Ogilvies seemed to watch from black periwigs and aging canvases above.

Moving into an inner room, Father Brown found the investigators seated at a long oak table. Papers covered one end, flanked by whisky and cigars. The remainder of the table held detached objects arranged at intervals, items as inexplicable as any could be. One resembled a small heap of glittering broken glass; another, a high pile of brown dust; a third, a plain stick of wood.

"You seem to have established a sort of geological museum here," Father Brown remarked, sitting down and nodding toward the dust and crystals.

"Not a geological museum," Flambeau replied. "Say, a psychological museum."

"Oh, for the Lord's sake!" Craven laughed. "Let's avoid such long words."

"Do you not know what psychology means?" Flambeau asked with friendly surprise. "It simply means being off one's chump."

"I still fail to follow," Craven admitted.

"Well," Flambeau decided, "it means we have discovered one thing about Lord Glengyle: he was a maniac."

At that moment, the black silhouette of Gow, with his top hat and spade, passed the window, dimly outlined against the darkening sky. Father Brown stared passively and responded, "I can understand there must have been something odd about the man, or he wouldn't have buried himself alive—or been in such a hurry to bury himself dead. But what leads you to conclude it was lunacy?"

"Listen to the list of items Inspector Craven has found in the house," Flambeau said.

"We require a candle," Craven interjected suddenly. "A storm is brewing, and it is too dark to read."

"Have you found any candles among your oddities?" Brown smiled.

Flambeau raised a grave face, fixing his dark eyes on his friend. "That is curious, indeed. Twenty-five candles, and not a single candlestick."

In the rapidly darkening room, with the wind rising, Father Brown moved along the table to where a bundle of wax candles lay among the other exhibits. As he bent over the heap of red-brown dust, a sharp sneeze cracked the silence.

"Hullo!" he exclaimed. "Snuff!"

Taking a candle, he lit it carefully and stuck it into the neck of a whisky bottle. The restless night air blew through the broken window, waving the long flame like a banner. Outside, the miles of black pine wood seemed to seethe like a black sea around a rock.

"I shall read the inventory," Craven began gravely, picking up a paper. "This lists items found loose and unexplained in the castle. Generally, the place was dismantled and neglected, but certain rooms had clearly been inhabited in a simple, non-squalid style by someone—not the servant Gow. The list follows:

'First: A considerable hoard of precious stones, nearly all diamonds, entirely loose and unset. Naturally, the Ogilvies possess family jewels, yet these are typically set in ornaments. These appear to have been kept loose in pockets, like coppers.

'Second: Heaps of loose snuff, not contained in horns or pouches, but scattered on mantelpieces, sideboards, and pianos. It suggests the occupant lacked the inclination to check pockets or lift lids.

'Third: Scattered throughout the house are tiny heaps of minute metal pieces—some resembling steel springs, others microscopic wheels. As if someone had gutted a mechanical toy.

'Fourth: The wax candles, which must be inserted into bottle necks due to the absence of holders.'

Craven paused. 'Note how much stranger this is than we anticipated. We are prepared for the central riddle regarding the Earl's fate—whether he lived, died, or was murdered by Gow. But consider the most lurid solutions: the servant killing the master, the master feigning death, or the servant being buried for the master. Invent a Wilkie Collins tragedy, and you still cannot explain a candle without a holder, nor why a gentleman of good breeding would habitually spill snuff on a piano. The core narrative we can imagine; the fringes remain mysterious. No amount of imagination connects snuff, diamonds, wax, and loose clockwork.'

'I perceive the connection,' the priest said. 'Glengyle was mad against the French Revolution. An enthusiast for the *ancien régime*, attempting to reenact the domestic life of the last Bourbons. He used snuff as an eighteenth-century luxury; wax candles for eighteenth-century lighting; the metal scraps represent the locksmith hobbies of Louis XVI; the diamonds stand for the Diamond Necklace of Marie Antoinette.'

Both men stared at him with wide eyes. 'What an extraordinary notion!' Flambeau cried. 'Do you truly believe this?'

'I am perfectly sure it is incorrect,' Father Brown answered. 'Yet you stated that no one could connect snuff, diamonds, clockwork, and candles. I offer that connection immediately. The true reality lies far deeper.'

He paused, listening to the wind wailing in the turrets. 'The late Earl of Glengyle was a thief. He lived a second, darker life as a desperate housebreaker. He possessed no candlesticks because he used these candles cut short in the lantern he carried. He employed snuff as the fiercest French criminals use pepper—to throw it densely in the face of a captor. But the final proof lies in the coincidence of the diamonds and the small steel wheels. Surely that clarifies everything? Diamonds and small steel wheels are the only two instruments capable of cutting a pane of glass.'

A branch of a broken pine tree lashed heavily against the windowpane, mimicking a burglar, yet the men did not turn.
