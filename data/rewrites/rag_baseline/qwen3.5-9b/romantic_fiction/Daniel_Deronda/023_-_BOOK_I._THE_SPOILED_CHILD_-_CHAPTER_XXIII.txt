The inheritance of art, much like the apportionment of the Promised Land, demands a war of attrition from every claimant; the divine dispensation offers only a title, a prophetic promise devoid of actual possession. To clutch a map of an estate one does not yet hold is to possess a flimsy copyhold tenure, and to imagine casting one's shoe over Eden provides no guarantee that one will ever plant a boot upon a single acre of that soil.

The most stubborn delusions mortals harbor regarding themselves stem from a lack of evidence beyond a perpetual, spontaneous throbbing of self-satisfaction—a latent seed of madness, a delirious certainty that they can shift the globe without understanding their leverage or their footing.

"Please go to church, Mamma," Gwendolen declared the following morning. "I prefer seeing Herr Klesmer alone." (He had replied to her note stating he would meet her at eleven.)

"That seems hardly proper, I believe," Mrs. Davilow interjected, her anxiety palpable.

"Our concerns are too weighty for us to adhere to such nonsensical regulations," Gwendolen retorted, her voice dripping with disdain. "They are insulting as well as absurd."

"You wouldn't mind if Isabel sat with you? She could read in a corner."

"No; she cannot. She would gnaw her nails and stare. It would be intolerably irritating. Trust my judgment, Mamma; I must be alone. Send them all to church."

As was inevitable, Gwendolen prevailed. Only Miss Merry and two of the girls remained behind, staying inside to lend the house the appearance of habitation by lingering at the dining-room windows.

It was a resplendent Sunday morning. The melancholy, fading sunlight of autumn lay heavy upon the half-mown grass, filtering gently through the panes in slanted ribbons of brilliance. These beams illuminated the antique furniture, the glass panels reflecting the room's interior, the tapestried chairs adorned with their faded floral wreaths, the dark, enigmatic paintings, and the superannuated organ at which Gwendolen had delighted in playing Saint Cecilia upon her first triumphant arrival. Through the open doors of the antechamber, a crowd of pallid, dusty trinkets caught the eye—the very place where she had previously enacted the role of Hermione in her Greek dress. This final memory was vividly active in her mind; surely Herr Klesmer had been struck by the admiration of her posture and expression then? Regardless of what he had actually said, or what she imagined he had thought, the possibility was now scrutinized with the sharpest intensity. Perhaps she had never in her life felt so inwardly dependent, so acutely aware of her need for another's validation. A fresh fluttering animated her spirit, introducing a new element of deliberation into her self-assessment, a quality that had previously been a blissful, intuitive gift. Yet, the recurring burden of her internal monologue remained: Klesmer had seen very little of her, and any unfavorable conclusion he reached would rest on a foundation too narrow to support it. She truly believed herself capable of anything.

To pass the time, she gathered her volumes and musical scores, arranging them atop the piano, intent on classifying them. Then, catching the reflection of her movements in the glass panel, she was drawn into contemplating her own image and walked toward it. Clad in black, devoid of ornament, with the warm whiteness of her skin framed between her light-brown crown of hair and her square-cut bodice, she might have tempted an artist to attempt the Roman trick of a statue carved from black, white, and tawny marble. Watching her reflection advance slowly, she thought, *I am beautiful*—not with exultation, but with grave resolve. Beauty, after all, was the condition upon which she most desperately required external testimony. Should anyone object to the turn of her nose or the contour of her neck and chin, she lacked the sense that she could immediately demonstrate her power of attainment in these branches of feminine perfection.

There was little time to waste in such contemplation before the clatter of wheels, the loud ringing of bells, and the swinging of doors confirmed she was not, by some accident, to be disappointed. This slight intensification of her inner flutter was accompanied by a dread, despite her self-confidence. She feared Klesmer as part of that unmanageable world existing independently of her wishes—a corrosive force that would continue to burn regardless of whether she smiled or frowned at it. Poor creature! She stood at a higher crisis of her woman's fate than in her last encounter with Grandcourt. Previously, the question had been whether she should accept a specific man as a husband. Now, the deepest fold of her inquiry was whether she needed a husband at all—whether she could not achieve substantial independence and know gratified ambition without bondage.

Herr Klesmer executed his most deferential bow in the wide doorway of the antechamber, displaying simultaneously the respect due to the finest gray kerseymere trousers and immaculate gloves (for the 'masters of those who know' are, happily, altogether human). Gwendolen received him with unusual gravity, extending her hand. "It is most kind of you to come, Herr Klesmer. I hope you have not thought me presumptuous."

"I took your wish as a command that did me honor," Klesmer replied with matching seriousness. He was indeed setting aside his own affairs to devote his utmost attention to whatever Gwendolen might have to say; yet his temperament remained in a state of excitation from the events of the previous day, likely lending his expressions a sharper, more biting edge than usual.

For once, Gwendolen was under such a strain of emotion that she forgot formalities. Standing near the piano, she waited, and Klesmer positioned himself near the opposite end, his back to the light, his terribly omniscient eyes fixed upon her. No affectation could avail her, and she began without delay.

"I wish to consult you, Herr Klesmer. We have lost all our fortune; we have nothing. I must earn my own bread, and I desire to provide for my mother, saving her from any hardship. The only path I can conceive—and I should prefer it above anything else—is to become an actress, to go on the stage. But, naturally, I aspire to a high position, and I thought—if you believe I could"—here Gwendolen grew slightly nervous—"it would be preferable for me to be a singer, to study vocal arts as well."

Klesmer placed his hat upon the piano and folded his arms as if concentrating his entire being.

"I know," Gwendolen resumed, shifting from pale to pink and back again—"I know that my method of singing is extremely defective; I have been ill-taught. I could be better taught; I could study. And you will understand my wish: to sing and act, like Grisi, represents a much higher station. Naturally, I should wish to attain the highest rank possible. I can rely on your judgment. I am certain you will tell me the truth."

Gwendolen possessed a conviction that now, making this serious appeal, the truth would prove favorable.

Yet Klesmer remained silent. He swiftly removed his gloves, tossed them into his hat, rested his hands on his hips, and walked to the far end of the room. He was filled with compassion for this girl; he sought to place a guard around his speech. When he turned again, he regarded her with a mild frown of inquiry. "You have never seen anything, I think, of artists and their lives?—I mean of musicians, actors, artists of that kind?"

"Oh, no," Gwendolen replied, unperturbed by a reference to this obvious fact in the biography of a young lady hitherto well-provided-for.

"You are—pardon me," Klesmer said again, pausing near the piano—"coming to a conclusion on a matter of such magnitude; everything must be taken into consideration. Are you perhaps twenty?"

"I am twenty-one," Gwendolen said, a faint fear rising within her. "Do you think I am too old?"

Klesmer pursed his lower lip and shook his long fingers upward in a manner utterly enigmatic.

"Many persons begin later than others," Gwendolen remarked, betrayed by her habitual consciousness of possessing valuable information to dispense.

Klesmer paid her no heed, instead speaking with even greater studied gentleness. "You have probably not considered an artistic career until now: you did not entertain the notion, the longing—what shall I say?—you did not wish to be an actress, or anything of that sort, until this present trouble?"

"Not exactly: but I was fond of acting. I have acted; you saw me, if you remember—you saw me here in charades, and as Hermione," Gwendolen said, genuinely fearing Klesmer had forgotten.

"Yes, yes," he answered quickly, "I remember—I remember perfectly," and once more walked to the other end of the room. It proved difficult for him to restrain this peculiar movement whenever engaged in any argument, audible or silent.

Gwendolen felt herself being weighed. The delay was unpleasant. However, she did not yet conceive that the scales could tip to the wrong side, and it seemed to her only gracious to remark, "I shall be very much obliged to you for taking the trouble to give me your advice, whatever it may be."
