"A lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe," muttered a coarse voice, perhaps Sybrandt's. "With steel, the wound bleeds, and the blood tells the tale. But a lie? It spreads like ivy, choking roots unseen, growing far beyond the reach of any sword or shield."

Indeed, truth is bound by physics, but falsehood knows no limits. In the rough trade of the streets, a single untruth has toppled kings and buried innocents beneath piles of stone. Here, within the walls of this cloister, one cruel fabrication had already begun to unravel lives with terrifying speed.

Yet, strangely, the architects of the deceit reaped little reward. The brothers, who concocted the story solely to diminish their share of their father's estate, found themselves displaced in their mother's affections by the ghost of little Gerard. Worse still, Eli, emboldened by the fiction, declared that since Gerard was lost, perhaps dead, he had already secured provisions by will for both the orphan and his widowed mother.

Between the conspirators, a warning glance passed—a silent pact of silence. Cornelis, standing on the brink of speaking the truth—that Gerard might yet live—caught his mother's gaze and swallowed his words. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other partner in this treachery, felt the weight of his crimes pressing down. He was a man failing in body, aware that soon his gold would leave him, leaving only his sins to haunt his soul.

Though his conscience gnawed at him, Ghysbrecht had long ago bargained with himself. "I cannot surrender my lands and coin," his greed had whispered, while a smaller voice replied, "I will cause her no further pain." Thus, having tampered with the seal of Gerard's letter, discovering it contained no clause regarding the deed, Ghysbrecht delivered it to Margaret himself. He rationalized this betrayal as a necessary compromise, a self-righteous balance sheet where he was both the sinner and the judge.

But time, that relentless healer, had brought a shock to his system: the birth of Margaret's child. Suddenly, the implications of his forgery loomed large. If his forged letter were to take effect, it would strip Margaret of her inheritance, branding her a thief, and disgrace the lineage of friends he had once sworn to protect—the granddaughter of his friend, the grandchild of another. As his strength faded, memories of old loyalties resurfaced, filling him with gloomy terrors. Yet, confession was impossible; his doctor was a man of honor who would demand restitution, and Ghysbrecht's penance was but a fleeting sentiment, easily drowned by ingrained habits of avarice.

One afternoon, returning from the town hall, Ghysbrecht found a figure waiting in his vestibule. A woman, her face hidden beneath a veil, cradled a child. Assuming shame, he greeted her with stiff, magisterial formality. But as he spoke, she lowered the veil, revealing a countenance etched with sorrow and resolve.

It was Margaret Brandt.

Her sudden presence jarred him, exposing a flash of confusion he could not hide.

"Where is my Gerard?" she cried, her chest heaving with emotion. "Is he alive?"

"For all I know," Ghysbrecht stammered, hoping to deflect, "I pray for your sake that he is. Pray, step inside. Let the servants attend!"

"Not a step," Margaret commanded, seizing his shoulder with the fierce energy of a desperate woman. "You hold the key to what breaks my heart. Why does Gerard not come? Why no word for these many months? Speak, or I will tear this house apart, let alone the ears of your servants. My misery is too vast to be treated as a game."

Ghysbrecht insisted he knew nothing of Gerard. But Margaret's gaze burned with certainty. "Those who sent you to me told a different story," she pressed. "You know why he neither returns nor writes."

Ghysbrecht paled, his dignity crumbling. "Would you believe the twain knaves against a man of worship?" he gasped.

"Which two knaves?" she demanded sharply.

He faltered, his voice trembling. "Did I not say...? I am a broken old man, my mind fogged. You confuse me, I know not what words escape me."

"Yes, sir, your mind is shaken, or you would not stand as my enemy," Margaret countered, her tone cutting. "My father rescued you from the plague when others fled; your friend Floris aided you in poverty, loved you then and now. Three generations of friendship have you known. Here stands the fourth. Look down, look at this innocent face. Think of them!"

"Woman, you torment me," Ghysbrecht sighed, collapsing onto a nearby bench.

Seeing her opportunity, Margaret knelt, placing the boy upon his knees. "This fatherless babe belongs to poor Margaret Brandt, who has never harmed you, coming from a lineage that loved you. Look at his face! It will melt you more than any plea. What crime could a desolate girl and her infant commit to erase the memory of your own youth, when you were as blameless as he now gazes up at you, begging you to restore his father?"

Holding the child aloft with her arms, smiling faintly beneath the old man's horrified stare, she intensified the vision. Ghysbrecht gazed wildly at the child, then at the kneeling mother, and sprang up, screaming, "Away with you, you pair of adders!"

The sting of her words sparked a final burst of vitality in his aged frame. He stumbled forward, wringing his hands, clutching at his white hair. "Forget those days? I forget everything! Oh, woman, whether I sleep or wake, I see only the faces of the dead, hear only their voices. Soon I shall join them! Here, here, what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell!"

His unnatural exertion led swiftly to collapse. He staggered, and without Margaret's support, he would have fallen. She caught him with one arm, crying aloud for help. Servants rushed in, carrying him away in a state bordering on syncope. The last sight Margaret had of him was his ancient, furrowed face, pale and helpless, his white hair hanging limp over a servant's elbow.

"Heaven forgive me," she whispered, tears streaming down. "I fear I may have killed the poor old man."

Yet, this desperate attempt to pierce the mystery only deepened the shadows. Her suspicions hardened: Ghysbrecht knew something about Gerard. "And who were the two knaves he claimed did a good deed and lied to me? Oh, my Gerard, my abandoned child, we are wading in deep waters."

The journey to Tergou drained her resources, and a customer absconded with her funds. Forced once again to reveal Catherine's angelic token, she descended the stairs only to find loose silver coins scattered on the table, accompanied by a scrawled note: *For Gerard, his wife.*

She collapsed over the paper, a cry of surprise rising instantly into a shout of joy.

"He is alive! Some kind hand delivers this!"

She kissed the note repeatedly, clutching it to her breast. Time passed, bringing no further news of Gerard. Yet, roughly every two months, small sums of silver appeared in the household. Sometimes they lay on the table; once, a purse was thrown through the bedroom window; another time, it was discovered at the bottom of Luke's basket. The giver remained elusive, never caught. Catherine disavowed knowledge of it; Margaret Van Eyck denied involvement; even Eli refused to acknowledge a role. Whenever the money arrived, Margaret would whisper to little Gerard, "Oh, my poor abandoned child, you and I are wading in deep waters."

She devoted at least half of this meager supply to dressing the boy beyond his station, insisting, "If it truly comes from Gerard, he shall see his son neat." Neighboring mothers began to sneer, particularly those with children ragged at the elbows.

Months bled into years, and a deadly sickness of the heart replaced the sharper torments of uncertainty. Margaret returned to her initial conviction: Gerard must be dead. She believed she would never see her husband again, nor secure her marriage lines. This grief, previously soothed by Eli and Catherine's recognition of her betrothal, flared anew. Others viewed her story with suspicion, and she often mourned over her son's illegitimacy.

"Is suffering enough for us, bereaved as we are? Must we bear dishonor as well? Oh, that we had never been born!"

A shift occurred in Peter Brandt. After nearly two years of mental clouding, his mind seemed to clear, granting moments of lucidity. During these times, he and Margaret spoke of Gerard until his condition darkened again. One day, returning after hours away, Margaret found him conversing with Catherine in a manner unseen since his paralytic stroke.

"Eh, girl, why must you be out?" he asked.

"But indeed, I have told him all," Catherine replied softly. "We have been weeping together over your troubles."

Margaret stood silent, her eyes lighting with joy as she looked from one to the other. Peter smiled, reaching out. "Come, let me bless thee."
