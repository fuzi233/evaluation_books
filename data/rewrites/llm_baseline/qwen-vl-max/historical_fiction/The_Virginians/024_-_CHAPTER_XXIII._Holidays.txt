Mrs. Lambert’s little dream had come to an end. Miss Theo and her mother, though they said little aloud, both knew in their hearts that the young man they had imagined as a hero was merely a plain, ordinary fellow. Yet no words were needed between them—love speaks louder than speech—and the mother, with gentle touches and tender glances, tried to comfort her daughter for the quiet disappointment.

“Never mind, my dear,” she whispered, pressing a soft kiss to Theo’s cheek, “our hero has turned out to be only a mortal man—and none such is good enough for my Theo. You shall have a real husband soon, if there be one left in England. Why, I was barely fifteen when your father first saw me at the Bury Assembly, and while still at school, I vowed I’d never marry any other man. If Heaven gave me such a husband—the best man in all the land—surely it will bless my child too, who deserves a king, should she fancy him!”

And perhaps Mrs. Lambert, who well knew the age and reputation of the Prince of Wales, secretly hoped he might ride past her gate one day, fall from his horse, be brought into the house, cured by her care, and cause his royal grandfather to grant Martin Lambert a regiment—while falling madly in love with Theo, of course.

Colonel Lambert and his younger daughter, Miss Hetty, stood firmly on the side of laughter and disbelief. Their mother was always dreaming up matches. Indeed, Mrs. Lambert had a deep fondness for novels, reading them with fervent emotion and often weeping over their tragedies. Every carriage that passed the gate stirred her hopes: surely, she thought, a noble suitor would step down and ring the bell.

Miss Hetty, meanwhile, took delight in teasing. Her tongue wagged with sharp wit, and she peppered their conversations with sly references to Prussia and Persia. She asked whether the King of Prussia was called “Shaw” or “Sophy,” and how far it was from Ispahan to Saxony—since His Majesty was currently invading the region, a matter her father studied intently in maps and newspapers. She quietly placed *The Persian Tales* upon the parlour table, where the family sat together. She declared she wouldn’t marry a Persian prince—she preferred a gentleman who kept only one wife. She began calling Harry Warrington “Theo’s prince,” “Theo’s gentleman,” and once even asked her mother if she wished her, Hetty, to take the “black prince” for herself.

She mocked her sister and mother endlessly about their romantic notions, driving them to anger before bursting into tears and rushing to embrace them, kissing each fiercely until they relented and laughed again.

Harry Warrington, meanwhile, remained utterly unaware of all this drama. A hundred jokes flew his way—about Prussians, Sophies, Shaws—but he didn’t grasp a single one. He likely thought “Sophy” was a woman’s name, and found it absurd that a man with a great black beard could bear such a title. Like a stone dropped into a still pond, he entered their lives quietly, unaware of the ripples he caused—disturbing the fish, startling the ducks, agitating the water's surface.

How could Harry know what effect his arrival had? He thought well enough of himself in many ways, but was still shy around women—just at the age when young men need encouragement and guidance. Raised in a modest household, he had grown up with limited contact with the opposite sex. So Miss Hetty’s jests floated past him like summer gnats—annoying, perhaps, but harmless.

It wasn’t that he was dull, as Hetty believed; rather, he was simple, absorbed in his own honest thoughts and concerns. And so, without knowing it, he became the subject of countless stories, secrets, and dreams—yet remained blissfully ignorant of it all.

One evening, as the sisters combed their long curls by candlelight or chatted in the large bed where, by custom, they slept together, Harry had no idea how deeply he occupied their minds.

Three days after his arrival, the family walked with him through Lord Wrotham’s beautiful park, where they were free to wander. Near a small lake, they came upon a group of swans, which the girls often fed with bread. As the birds approached, Hetty glanced sideways at her mother and sister, then at her father, standing nearby in his cheerful red waistcoat.

“Mamma’s swans are something like these, papa,” she said with a mischievous grin.

“What swans, my dear?” her mother asked.

“Something like, but not quite. They’ve shorter necks than these, and there are scores of them on our common,” Hetty continued. “I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We’ll have it for dinner—with apple-sauce and…”

“Don’t be a little goose!” Theo interrupted.

“And sage and onions,” Hetty persisted. “Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?”

“I shot three last winter on our river,” Harry replied simply. “Ours aren’t as white as these—but they eat very well.”

He had no idea that at that very moment, he was being used as a metaphor—Hetty was telling a fable about him, comparing him to the swan, noble yet doomed.

In some ancient Latin text I once read, it was said that long before Virginia was discovered, people were just as slow to understand meaning. So perhaps Theo’s fluttering heart—her tender, sudden stirrings when she first saw the pale, handsome Virginian—was premature. It was not true love, not yet. Like a bird that sings at false dawn, it had awakened too early.

Return to your perch, little creature, and cover your head with your wing. It is not yet light. Rest is better than song. Soon enough, the sky will glow crimson, and you will rise into it, singing your full song to the sun.

Earlier in this chapter, I mentioned: “Three days after his arrival, Harry was walking with…” But if he could walk—indeed, he walked perfectly—he had no reason to remain at Oakhurst. His shoulder was healed, his health restored. He hadn’t even changed his clothes, relying entirely on the Colonel’s generosity for attire. Surely a young man in such condition had no excuse to linger? Duty, love, family ties, and even the washerwoman’s expectations demanded his return to Tunbridge Wells.

Why did he stay?

Could it be he didn’t want to go?

Have you noticed the mysterious S at the beginning of the previous chapter—the faint sketch of Sinbad the Sailor, crowned by that dreadful old sea-man? What if Harry Warrington is that sailor, and his fate the crushing, inevitable old man? What if, for two days now, he has felt those knees tightening around his neck? What if his aunt’s cruel purpose has succeeded, and his former love lies dead—killed not by sword or illness, but by poison from her lips, just as fair Rosamond fell to her royal rival?

Is Hero lighting the lamp and preparing supper, while Leander sits comfortably elsewhere, never thinking of swimming across the dark waters?

Since that cowardly blow struck Lady Maria Esmond’s back by her own kin, can we not feel compassion for her? Yes, she has flaws—perhaps even wears false hair and false jewels. But is she not a woman in distress? Should we mock her because of her years? Between her cruel aunt and her shattered illusions, Lady Maria must endure terrible loneliness at Tunbridge Wells.

No one protects her. Madam Beatrix holds her captive. Lady Maria is poor, hoping for money from her aunt. She carries secrets—secrets the old woman knows and uses like weapons. For myself, I cannot help but feel pity. Imagine her alone, chained like Andromeda to a rock on Mount Ephraim, offered to the dragon of a Baroness!

Succour, Perseus! Come swiftly with your winged feet and shining sword!

But Perseus does not hurry. The dragon feasts on Andromeda day after day.

Harry Warrington, who would not let a dislocated shoulder stop him from hunting, stayed contentedly at Oakhurst, finding each passing day more pleasant than the last. Perhaps he had never, since his grandfather’s death, been in such good company. At home, he lived among Virginian squires—men of fox hunts, horses, punch bowls, and broad shoulders. The ladies he knew were well-bred, pious, and proper, but narrow in spirit. His world was small—filled with pompous customs, petty rules, flattery, and gossip. Until he left, he did not realize how confined his life had been.

Here, at Oakhurst, everything was different. He was among people who had seen the world, though they lived apart from it. Both the Colonel and his wife had known good books—and better, living ones—good company that taught humility and wisdom. Society, when truly good, teaches us our insignificance and introduces us to our betters.

Young reader, if you are reading this, remember: nothing is healthier than acknowledging and learning from those above you. I would not wish my son Thomas to be the top scholar, the best oarsman, the pride of the school. Better for him to have a fair place, decent rivals, and the occasional thrashing—followed by a firm handshake. Who among us, if we could choose, would want to be a prince, surrounded only by servants and fools, while the world remains silent unless we speak?

One of the greatest gifts Harry received at Oakhurst was the realization that he was profoundly ignorant—and that there were many people far wiser than he. Arrogant with some, he became humbly attentive in the presence of those superior to him. He had admired his brother and his friend, the gallant Colonel of Mount Vernon, and considered himself equal to the gentlemen of Castlewood. But here, he met a man who had read more books than he could imagine, who had faced danger and war and emerged unscathed, whose face bore honesty and kindness—qualities Harry instinctively respected.

As for the women—kind, merry, and delightful—they were unlike anyone he had known. More charming than Parson Broadbent’s loud-laughing daughter, equally refined as the Castlewood ladies (except for Madam Beatrix, who sometimes seemed as grand as an empress). Yet after talking with Madam Beatrix, Harry would leave feeling sour, as if the world were full of deceit. But the others—no, they were not prudish. They laughed over Fielding, wept over Richardson, discussed scandals with amusement, not malice. Their neighbors greeted them with warmth, their hospitality was generous. Truly, it is good to be among good people.

Harry may not have known it then, nor fully grasped it until later, but this was a peaceful interlude—a calm, golden hour in a life that would soon be storm-tossed. Nothing dramatic happened. Only sweet sleep, gentle waking, warm welcomes, and quiet joy. The gates of Oakhurst seemed to shut out the harsh world, and within, the people were kinder, purer, more genuine.

He was not in love—not with saucy Hetty, nor with gentle Theo. But when the time came to leave, he clutched both their hands, feeling a deep affection for them. He wished to meet their brothers, sure they must be fine fellows. And Mrs. Lambert—well, she was as sentimental at his departure as if he were the final volume of *Clarissa Harlowe*.

“He is very kind and honest,” Theo said gravely, watching from the terrace as Harry rode away with her father and the servants toward Westerham.

“I don’t think he’s stupid at all now,” Hetty said. “And, mamma, I think he really is like a swan.”

“It felt just like one of the boys going off to school,” the mother murmured.

“Just like it,” Theo echoed, sadly.

“I’m glad he has Papa to ride with him to Westerham,” Hetty added, “and that he bought Farmer Briggs’s horse. I don’t like him going to those Castlewood people. I’m sure Madame Bernstein is a wicked old woman. I expected to see her ride away on her crooked stick.”

“Hush, Hetty!”

“Do you think she’d float if they tried her in the pond, like poor old Mother Hely did at Elmhurst? The other old woman seemed fond of him—I mean the one with the fair hair. She looked very sad when she left. But Madame Bernstein whisked her off with her crutch, and she had to go. I don’t care, Theo. I know she’s a wicked woman. You think everyone’s good because you never do anything wrong yourself.”

“My Theo is a good girl,” the mother said, gazing lovingly at both daughters.

“Then why do we call her a miserable sinner?”

“We are all so, my love,” said the mother gently.

“What, Papa too? You know you don’t think so,” cried Miss Hester.

And that was almost too much for Mrs. Lambert to bear.

“What did you tell John to give to Mr. Warrington’s black man?”

With a touch of shame, she admitted: “A bottle of my cordial water and a cake I had Betty make.”

“I feel quite like a mother to him,” she confessed, “and you know both our boys still carry one of our cakes to school or college.”
