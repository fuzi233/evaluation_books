Fashion has long abandoned the verdant, picturesque Temple Garden. Where Shakespeare once placed York and Lancaster to pluck the innocent white and red roses that would later stain the fields of battle, today the air is thick with smoke, and the hardiest blooms refuse to unfurl their petals. Most of the current residents of the surrounding buildings neither notice nor care if a rose exists there, unless they are rushing past the old gate en route to a lawyer's office. Attorneys' clerks do not carry bouquets in their satchets or posies tucked under their arms as they dash to counsel chambers. Even the few lawyers who still take constitutional walks have lost interest in the rivalry of York and Lancaster, particularly since the railway boom has taken center stage.

Only antiquarians and literary enthusiasts remain to gaze upon the gardens with genuine affection, conjuring up visions of Sir Roger de Coverley and the sharp-faced Mr. Spectator pacing the gravel paths. Or perhaps they imagine Oliver Goldsmith resting in the summerhouse, pondering his next *Citizen of the World*, worrying about the new suit Mr. Filby is tailoring for him, or fretting over a dunning letter from Mr. Newbery. One might even spot the Great Doctor approaching Goldsmith, clad in a snuff-colored suit and a wig desperately needing a barber's touch, accompanied by his Scotch lackey trailing behind like a loyal dog slightly tipsy from port wine at the Mitre. Mr. Johnson invites the poet home for tea with Miss Williams. Oh, the enduring power of Fancy! These fictional figures feel as tangible as the historical characters we've just described.

Since Arthur Pendennis possessed a distinctly romantic and literary soul, far removed from the dry legalities dominating the neighborhood, it is safe to assume he was indulging in similar poetic reveries. This occurred on the evening following the events of the previous chapter. Having chosen the Temple Gardens for exercise and contemplation, he found the atmosphere uniquely suited to his mood. Sundays usually bring a profound calm to the Temple. The chambers lie mostly empty; the senior lawyers are hosting grand dinners in the Belgravia or Tyburn districts, while the promising young barristers are off paying homage to Mr. Kewsy's fine claret or Mr. Justice Ermine's accomplished daughters. Those left behind at the Club are enjoying an economical joint and a modest pint of wine, regaling one another with circuit jokes and points of law.

The only souls inhabiting their offices are the unfortunate Mr. Cockle, bedridden and subsisting on gruel prepared by his laundress; Mr. Toodle, a flute enthusiast whose solo practice echoes from his second-floor chamber; and young Tiger, the student, whose open window belches clouds of cigar smoke while piles of dirty dishes from "Dicks" or "The Cock" accumulate outside. But wait! It is vacation time. With the exception of Pendennis, the chambers are entirely deserted. Perhaps it was this solitude that drew him to the garden. Though he had previously passed the gate with casual indifference, noting the neat flower beds and the leisurely citizens strolling the lawn, tonight something compelled him. After dining alone at a nearby tavern, he decided to return home via the gardens, seeking the fresh evening air and the gleaming waters of the Thames.

After a brief stroll, admiring the peaceful groups scattered about, fatigue set in. He retreated to one of the summerhouses flanking the main path and sat down modestly. What filled his mind? The evening was brilliantly clear and tranquil. The sky held not a cloud; the chimneys across the river remained silent; the wharves and warehouses glowed rosy in the sunlight, appearing freshly scrubbed for the holiday. Steamers raced up and down the stream, packed with holidaymakers. From the multitude of city churches, the evening bells rang out for prayer—peaceful Sabbath sounds that reminded Pen of his childhood, when he had walked with his arm around his mother's waist on the terrace at home. The sun was casting a golden light on the little village of Brawl as well as the broad Thames, dipping majestically behind the Clavering elms and the tower of the familiar village church. Were these memories, or simply the beauty of the sunset, causing the flush on the young man's cheeks?

He tapped his foot to the rhythm of the distant bells, flicked dust from his polished boots with his handkerchief, then sprang up, stamped his foot, and declared, "No, by Jove, I'll go home." This resolve suggested an internal struggle regarding propriety—he debated whether to linger or depart—and he stepped out of the summerhouse. Almost immediately, he collided with two small children, who barely reached his knees. They were trotting along the gravel path, their long blue shadows stretching toward the east. One cried out, "Oh!" while the other burst into laughter, delivering a knowing, infantile chuckle: "Missa Pendennis!"

Arthur looked down and recognized his two little friends from the day before, Mesdemoiselles Ameliar-Ann and Betsy-Jane. A deeper blush covered his face. Seizing the child he had nearly bumped into, he lifted her into the air and kissed her. To this sudden assault, Ameliar-Ann responded with a scream of terror. Her cry summoned two ladies dressed impeccably in clean collars, new ribbons, and grand shawls: Mrs. Bolton, draped in a rich scarlet Caledonian Cashmere and black silk, and Miss F. Bolton, wearing a yellow scarf, sprigged muslin, and carrying a parasol—truly the epitome of ladyhood.

Fanny said not a word. Yet, her eyes flashed a warm welcome, shining brighter than the most dazzling windows in Paper Buildings. Mrs. Bolton, however, after scolding Betsy-Jane, exclaimed, "Lor, sir—how very odd that we should meet you year! I ope you ave your ealth well, sir. Ain't it odd, Fanny, that we should meet Mr. Pendennis?"

*What do you mean by sniggering, Mesdames?* Arthur thought internally. *When young Croesus stays at a country house, haven't you, by some singular coincidence, been walking with your Fanny in the shrubberies? Haven't you and your Frances happened to listen to the band of the Heavies at Brighton when young De Boots and Captain Padmore arrived? Haven't you chanced upon old Widow Wheezy's cottage when the young curate dropped in with a tract for rheumatism?* He reasoned that if coincidences occurred at the Hall, they surely happened at the Lodge. It was merely fate.

Earlier, during conversation, Mr. Pendennis had innocently remarked that while some courts were gloomy, parts of the Temple were cheerful, especially the riverside chambers and the gardens, which made for delightful Sunday walks crowded with people. Thus, by sheer chance, everyone gathered together, much like the genteel crowds of polite society. How artless, good-natured, and natural it all seemed!

Pen appeared grave, pompous, and dandified. His attire was strikingly brilliant: white duck trousers, a white hat, a multi-colored neckcloth, a light waistcoat, gold chains, and shirt studs gave him the air of a prince of the blood. How splendidly the outfit suited his figure! Someone whispered, "Was anybody ever like him?" Another added, "How his blushes become him!"

The children, having been captivated by him the previous day, had played at being him afterward. Ameliar-Ann, mimicking Pen's habit of sticking her fingers into the armholes of her pinafore, had declared, "Now, Bessy-Jane, I'll be Missa Pendennis." Fanny had laughed until she cried, burying her sister in kisses for the imitation. How joyful she was to see Arthur embrace the child! While Arthur's face burned red, Fanny appeared remarkably pale and weary.

Noticing her condition, Arthur asked kindly why she looked so fatigued.
"I was awake all night," Fanny replied, blushing slightly.
"You were reading!" Pen observed, amused. "And what was it that interested you so?"
"Oh, it's so beautiful!" Fanny sighed. "'Walter Lorraine.' How I do hate that Neaera—I don't know the pronunciation. And I love Leonora, and Walter, oh, how dear he is!"

How had Fanny discovered the novel *Walter Lorraine* and realized Pen was its author? This little person remembered every word Mr. Pendennis had spoken the night before, recalling how he wrote in books and newspapers. Eager to learn more, she almost considered being civil to old Bows, who had been under her displeasure since yesterday, but decided instead to approach Captain Costigan.

She began by coaxing the Captain, smiling in her most winning manner as she arranged his dinner and tidied his humble room. She noted his linen needed mending (indeed, his closet held curious specimens of flawed fabric) and offered to mend all his shirts. "What horrid holes—what funny holes!" she giggled, poking her face through one, laughing at the old warrior in the most charming way possible. She then cleared his dinner tray, danced lightly to his cupboard, retrieved his whisky bottle, mixed a tumbler, and insisted on tasting a tiny drop. In exchange, the Captain sang one of his dear songs, teaching it to her. As he performed an Irish melody in his rich, quavering voice, pretending to fascinate the little siren, she finally asked about Arthur Pendennis and his novel.
