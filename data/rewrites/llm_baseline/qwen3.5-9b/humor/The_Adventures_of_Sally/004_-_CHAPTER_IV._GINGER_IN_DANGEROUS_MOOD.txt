A few days later, circumstances conspired to bring Bruce Carmyle face-to-face with his cousin, Lancelot "Ginger" Farnaby, in the bustling heart of Piccadilly. Both had returned from Roville via different routes, and Ginger, secretly relieved by the distance, had been hurrying along with a polite nod intended to signal his wish for continued separation. However, Carmyle halted him abruptly.

"Just the man I wanted to see," Carmyle announced cheerfully.

"Oh, hello!" Ginger replied, devoid of genuine warmth.

"I was planning to drop by your club anyway," Carmyle pressed.

"Yes?"

"Yes. Cigarette?"

Ginger stared at the offered cigarette case with the wary skepticism of someone who has been tricked onto a train platform only to find themselves holding a playing card from a stage magician. He felt utterly bewildered. In all the years they had known one another, he couldn't recall a single instance where his cousin displayed such overt geniality. He was genuinely surprised that Mr. Carmyle was addressing him at all; the *Scrymgeour* affair remained a festering, unhealed wound, and the entire family was currently in high council regarding the matter.

"Been back in London long?" Carmyle asked.

"A day or two."

"I heard quite by accident that you'd returned and that you're staying at the club. By the way, thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas."

Ginger jumped as if shot. "What?"

"I was in that compartment at Roville Station, you know. You threw her right on top of me. We decided that counted as an introduction. She's quite attractive."

Bruce Carmyle hadn't fully settled his feelings regarding Sally, but on one crucial point he was certain: she should not, if he could help it, disappear from his life. Her sudden departure had left him with a baffled, unsatisfied itch—a sensation distinct from love at first sight, yet producing identical effects. She had clearly won the upper hand in their brief encounter, and he found himself driven by a desire to meet her again and prove that there was far more substance to him than she apparently suspected. In short, Bruce Carmyle was piqued. Whether he liked or disliked Sally was still undecided, but he was convinced that a future without her would lack a certain necessary spice.

"She was very attractive," Ginger admitted, though his tone dripped with envy. "We had a very pleasant conversation."

"I bet you did," Ginger muttered resentfully.

"By the way, did she happen to give you her address?"

"Why?" Ginger asked, his guard instantly rising. His attitude toward Sally's address mirrored that of a connoisseur who has just acquired a rare masterpiece; he wanted to keep it secret and savor it alone.

"Well, I—er—I promised to send her some books she was eager to read..."

"I wouldn't imagine she has much spare time for reading."

"Books that aren't published in America."

"Oh, pretty much everything is published in America nowadays, isn't it? Bound to be, I mean."

"Well, these specific titles aren't," Mr. Carmyle stated curtly. Finding Ginger's reserve increasingly irritating, he wished he had been more creative in his approach.

"Give them to me, and I'll mail them to her," Ginger offered.

"Good heavens, man!" snapped Carmyle. "I'm perfectly capable of sending a few books to America. Where does she live?"

Reluctantly, Ginger surrendered the sacred number of the holy street that served as Sally's headquarters. With a persistent devil like his cousin, escape seemed impossible, so he complied with a heavy heart.

"Thanks."

Carmyle jotted down the details with a gold pencil in a neat, Morocco-bound notebook. He was the type of man who always carried a pen and never relied on the backs of envelopes. After a brief silence, Carmyle cleared his throat.

"I saw Uncle Donald this morning," he said. His earlier friendliness evaporated instantly. There was no need for pretense anymore, and he was a man who despised wasted effort. He spoke coldly, his voice carrying that familiar, subtle sting of reproach.

"Yes?" Ginger responded moodily.

This was the uncle in whose office Ginger had begun his career as a hasher: a respectable figure at the National Liberal Club, but never a favorite of Ginger's. While the family tree included various minor uncles and subsidiary aunts, Uncle Donald was undoubtedly the managing director of the whole operation. In Ginger's considered opinion, in this role, he approximated a human blister.

"He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke's."

Ginger's spirits plummeted further. A dinner with Uncle Donald would scarcely be a cheerful affair, even surrounded by the exotic banquets of the Arabian Nights. There was something about Uncle Donald's personality that would dampen the wildest orgies of Emperor Tiberius at Capri. To dine with him at a place as grim as Bleke's Coffee House—a relic of Old London catering almost exclusively to regulars who hadn't missed a single evening in fifty years—was to tread dangerously close to bedrock. Ginger doubted that mere flesh and blood could withstand it.

"Tonight?" he asked weakly.

"Oh, you mean tonight? Well..."

"Don't be a fool. You know as well as I do that you have to go." Uncle Donald's invitations were royal decrees within the family hierarchy. "If you have another engagement, you must cancel it."

"Oh, all right."

"Seven-thirty sharp."

"All right," Ginger groaned.

The two cousins parted ways, Carmyle heading eastward to attend to clients in his Temple chambers, while Ginger trudged west toward Hyde Park Corner. Despite the lack of sympathy between them, their thoughts strangely converged on the same subject. As Carmyle navigated the throngs of Piccadilly Circus, he thought of Sally. So too did Ginger, as he loitered aimlessly near the corner, bumping through pedestrians in a sort of mental fog.

Since returning to London, Ginger had been in a terrible state. He drifted through the daylight hours and slept fitfully at night. If there is one thing more rotting than another in a pretty blighted world—one thing that gives a fellow the shivers and reduces him to a weeping onion—it is hopeless love. Hopeless love had thoroughly agitated Ginger. Previously, his soul had been placid. Even the financial crash that had reshaped his existence had barely bruised him; his temperament allowed him to endure life's slings and arrows with a philosophical "Right ho!" But now, everything had changed. Trivialities irritated him acutely, things he once accepted as inevitable—such as Uncle Donald's moustache and his habit of using it during meals as a makeshift barrier against the assault of soup.

"By gad!" Ginger thought, stopping abruptly opposite Devonshire House. "If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup strainer tonight, I'll slosh him with a fork!"

Hard thoughts... hard thoughts! And growing harder by the second, for nothing spreads faster than a mood of rebellion. Rebellion is a forest fire that leaps across the soul. The spark had been lit in Ginger, and long before he reached Hyde Park Corner, he was ablaze and crackling. By the time he returned to his club, he was practically a menace to society—at least to the faction comprising Uncle Donald, his minor uncles George and William, and his aunts Mary, Geraldine, and Louise. The foul mood persisted even as he dressed for the dismal festivities at Bleke's. He scowled as he wrestled morosely with an obstinate tie. One cannot hide the truth: Ginger was warming up.

And it was precisely at this critical moment that Fate, as if waiting for the perfect psychological instant, delivered the final blow.

There was a knock at the door, and a waiter entered bearing a telegram. Ginger glanced at the envelope. It had been readdressed and forwarded from the Hotel Normandie. It was a wireless message, handed in aboard the White Star liner *Olympic*, and it read simply:

Ginger sank heavily onto the bed.

At twenty-five minutes past seven, the driver of the taxi cab pulling up to the dingy door of Bleke's Coffee House in the Strand took notice of his passenger's demeanor and appearance. He formed a definite verdict: a determined-looking sort of young bloke.
