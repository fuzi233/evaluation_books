Approximately three months prior—that is, toward the close of May in the year 1900—the author whose name graces the title page received a correspondence of such extraordinary nature, accompanied by a packet of documents, as had rarely fallen into his lap. These items arrived from a close confidant, a gentleman whose identity I shall not now obscure: Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P. For two years, I had been residing primarily in France, while Dr. Browne maintained a medical practice in Norfolk, meaning our paths had not crossed during my occasional returns to London. Despite the intimacy of our bond, we were notoriously inept at penmanship; consequently, only two brief notes exchanged hands between us throughout those years.

Then, last May, the letter—and the packet—arrived. The package contained four notebooks, densely filled with the dizzying forms of Pitman's shorthand, an *ensemble* that so closely mimics startled swarms hovering in erratic flight. They were scrawled in pencil, lacking the distinction of thick and thin strokes, and devoid of vowels; thus, their slow decryption has proven a laborious endeavor, far from a leisurely holiday. The accompanying letter was likewise penned in shorthand. I now publish both this epistle and the second notebook I have successfully decoded (marked 'III.'), though I must confess that in approximately five instances, sentences required reconstruction via my own conjecture, and in two cases, the characters were so mystically indecipherable that I abandoned the passages in a state of headache. However, these minor obstructions prove immaterial to the overarching narrative.

First, Dr. Browne's letter:

'DEAR OLD SHIEL,—I have just been reclining, lost in thought of you, wishing you were here to grant me a final clasp of the hand before I—"go": for, by every outward sign, "going" I am. Four days hence, I began to feel a gnawing soreness in my throat. Passing by Old Johnson's surgery at Selbridge, I entered and requested an examination. He muttered something regarding membranous laryngitis, a diagnosis that initially amused me, but by the time I returned home, my voice was lost, and the smile vanished. Before nightfall, I suffered from dyspnea and laryngeal stridor. I immediately telegraphed London for Morgan, and between him and Johnson, they have been performing tracheotomies, cauterizing my interior with chromic acid and galvanic instruments. While the immediate difficulty of breathing has subsided, and it is miraculous how little pain I endure, I am far too seasoned a practitioner to ignore the obvious: the bronchi are implicated—_too deeply_ implicated. In absolute truth, there is no hope. Morgan still, I suspect, dwells fondly upon the possibility of adding me to his statistics of successful tracheotomies, but prognosis has always been my forte, and I say No. The sole solace of my impending demise will be the crushing of a specialist in his own field. So, we shall see.

'I have been organizing my affairs this morning and recalled these notebooks. I intended to entrust them to you months ago, but my habit of procrastination, combined with the fact that the lady from whom I transcribed these words was still living, delayed me. Now she is dead, and as a student of life and literature, you ought to be intrigued, provided you possess the stamina to read them. You may even find them invaluable.

'I am currently under a slight influence of morphia, propped up in a pleasant state of languor. Since I can write without significant exertion, I shall tell you, in the old Pitman's script, about her. Her name was Miss Mary Wilson; she was approximately thirty when I first encountered her and forty-five when she passed, and I knew her intimately for those fifteen intervening years. Are you acquainted with the philosophy of the hypnotic trance? Well, that defined our relationship—I was the hypnotist, she the subject. She had been under the influence of another master prior to my arrival, but no one ever wielded such dominion over her as I. She suffered from _tic douloureux_ of the fifth nerve. Most of her teeth had been extracted before I saw her, and attempts were made to sever the nerve on the left side via external incision. To no avail: all the clocks of Hell ticked relentlessly within that poor woman's jaw. It was indeed the mercy of Providence that she ever stumbled upon _me_. My organizational faculties possessed almost complete, and remarkably effortless, control over hers. With a few passes, I could expel her legion of tormentors.

'Now, you have never beheld anyone so singular in personal appearance as my friend, Miss Wilson. As a physician, I could scarcely gaze upon her without a jolt of shock: she evoked so inevitably what we term "the _Other_ World," carrying about her some odor of the worm, giving the distinct impression that here was rather a ghost than a woman. Yet, I struggle to convey the essence of this, save through dry particulars of her anatomy: the contours of her lofty brow, meager lips, pointed chin, and ashen cheeks. She stood tall and was deplorably emaciated, her entire skeleton, barring the thigh bones, laid bare to view. Her eyes held a bluish hue reminiscent of cigarette smoke, possessing a strangest, feeble, unearthly gaze; meanwhile, at thirty-five, her scant tuft of hair was already quite white.

'She was affluent and resided alone in Old Wooding Manor-House, situated five miles from Ash Thomas. As you know, I was establishing my practice in these regions at the time and soon took up residence at the manor. She insisted that I dedicate myself solely to her, a single patient constituting the most lucrative practice I have ever enjoyed.

'Well, I quickly discerned that, in the state of trance, Miss Wilson possessed very remarkable powers: remarkable, I mean, not because peculiar in _kind_, but because they were so constant, reliable, exact, and far-reaching in degree. The most novice of psychical scientists will now sit and discourse finely to you about the reporting capacities of the mind in its trance state—as though it were a novel discovery! This simple fact, I assure you, which the Psychical Research Society, only after endless investigation, admits as scientific, has been perfectly well known to every old crone since the Middle Ages, and, I assume, long prior thereto. What an unnecessary air of discovery! The certainty that someone in trance in Manchester could inform you of events unfolding in London, or in Pekin, was never left to the acumen of an office in Fleet Street; and the Society, in establishing this fact beyond doubt for the general public, has not advanced one step toward explanation. They have, in fact, revealed nothing that many of us did not, with absolute assurance, know beforehand.

'But speaking of poor Miss Wilson, I say that her powers were _remarkable_ because, though not exceptional in _genre_, they were so special in quantity—so "constant," and "far-reaching." I believe it to be a fact that, _in general_, the powers of trance manifest themselves more particularly concerning space, as distinct from time: the spirit roams in the present—it traverses a plain—it does not _usually_ attract the attention of observers by great ascents, or by great descents. I fancy that is so. But Miss Wilson's gift was unique in this respect: she traveled in every direction, and easily in all but one, north and south, up and down, through the past, the present, and the future.

'This I discovered, not at once, but gradually. She would emit a stream of sounds in the trance state—I can hardly call it _speech_, so murmurous, yet guttural, was the utterance, mixed with puffy breath-sounds at her languid lips. This state was accompanied by an intense contraction of the pupils, absence of the knee-jerk, considerable rigor, and a rapt, arrant expression. I fell into the habit of sitting for long hours at her bedside, utterly fascinated, attempting to grasp the import of that opiate and visionary language which drifted and fluttered in a deliberate monotone from her lips. Gradually, over the course of months, my ear learned to detect the words; "the veil was rent" for me as well; and I became able to follow, to some extent, the course of her musing and wandering spirit.

'At the conclusion of six months, I heard her one day repeat certain words familiar to me. They were these: "Such were the arts by which the Romans extended their conquests, and attained the palm of victory; and the concurring testimony of different authors enables us to describe them with precision..." I was startled: these are excerpts from Gibbon's "Decline and Fall," which I easily surmised she had never read.

'I said, in a stern voice: "Where are you?"

'She replied, "Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above. A man is writing. Us are reading.""
