When Romola seated herself upon the rough stone beneath the cypress, a profound and terrifying silence seemed to envelop the landscape. The familiar walls of Florence receded behind her, replaced by the vast, unbounded sky and the sombre, brooding mountains that loomed in the distance. Between her and those dark peaks lay a deep depression in the road, a physical gulf mirroring the chasm opening within her soul. Her husband, Tito, rode away toward Siena, leaving her alone in this desolate stretch, moving further from him with every passing hour. For the first time, the air felt heavy not with the oppression of the city, but with a raw, elemental solitude. There was no human law here, no eye watching, no hand to guide her. She felt entirely exposed to the earth and the heavens, stripped of her social armor.

Then, a voice cut through the stillness, close and intimate, startling her like a sudden gust of wind.

"You are Romola de' Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema."

She knew that voice instantly. It had resonated through her spirit on occasions before, vibrating with an authority that bypassed reason. Because she recognized it, she did not turn to see the speaker, nor did she lift her eyes. Instead, she sat frozen, a tremor running through her limbs—a mixture of superstitious awe and a fierce, internal rebellion. A monk, clad in his black habit, dared to intrude upon her flight, to interrupt her private agony. Yet, as he spoke, she felt as though some ancient, crowned god had descended, seizing her with tangible, flesh-and-blood fingers.

"You are fleeing from Florence in disguise," the voice continued, unwavering. "I hold a mandate from Heaven to halt you. You are not permitted to escape."

A surge of indignation flamed within Romola. How dare this man presume to dictate her course? With deliberate slowness, she refused to turn, resenting the scrutiny of his gaze.

"What authority do you possess to address me, or to obstruct my journey?" she demanded, her voice steady despite the turmoil within.

"The authority of a messenger," the monk replied firmly. "You have donned religious attire not out of piety, but as a mask. You sought to pass unseen, but Providence ensured you were seen. It was revealed to me who you are: a woman attempting to flee the station God has assigned you. You desire to bury your true name and your true calling, replacing them with a fabrication of your own choosing, bound only by your own caprice. I have been commanded to summon you back. Return to your appointed place, my daughter."

With every word, Romola's resolve hardened. She feared that if she showed even a flicker of weakness, she would succumb to paralysis. Irritated, she spoke again.

"I shall not return. I recognize no jurisdiction in priests or monks over my actions. You wield no power over me."

"I understand," the monk said softly, "that you have been raised in contempt for obedience. Yet, it is not I, a poor friar, who seeks to bind you. It is Truth itself that commands you. And Truth cannot be evaded. You stand at a fork: either submit to it, and it shall become your guide; or resist it, and it shall weigh upon you like a millstone for eternity. You will submit, my daughter. My faithful servant returns with the mules shortly, and you shall accompany them back to Florence."

Suddenly, Romola sprang to her feet, her eyes blazing with defiance. She faced the speaker directly. It was Fra Girolamo; she knew him well. They were nearly equal in height, their faces level. She had opened her mouth to hurl a torrent of accusations, but the words died in her throat. Fra Girolamo regarded her with a calm, piercing gaze. It was a look so unfamiliar, so devoid of judgment yet brimming with a strange, intense attention, that her anger evaporated, replaced by a shameful humility.

There was no mystic radiance about Savonarola's face, nor any conventional beauty. It was a visage carved by stern mental habits and rigorous bodily discipline. Yet, the effect of his glance upon Romola was profound. It conveyed a sense of genuine interest and care, detached from any personal affection. It was the first time she had encountered a look where simple human fellowship manifested as an unbreakable bond. Half the vocation of a spiritual leader lay in such a gaze, and Romola found herself unable to challenge his authority any longer. She stood silent, staring at him.

He spoke again, his tone gentle yet cutting.

"You boast loudly of your liberty, my daughter. Yet, who is more base than a debtor who believes himself free?"

His words stung, and a pallor washed over Romola's features.

"And you are fleeing from your debts," he continued, his voice rising slightly. "The debt of a Florentine woman; the debt of a wife. You are turning your back upon the lot ordained for you, seeking to carve out a new destiny. Can a man or a woman truly choose their duties? No more than they can select their birthplace or their parents. My daughter, you are running from the presence of God into the wilderness."

As the heat of anger drained from her, a new sensation took its place: a vague, terrifying intuition of the power inherent in submission, *if* this man indeed possessed a valid law. But it was absurd—he could not possibly know the depths of her inner torment. Yet, she could not simply refuse guidance. Constrained by a desperate need, she pleaded, trying to maintain a veneer of reverence while fighting back.

"My father..." she whispered, the title slipping from her lips unbidden. "You cannot comprehend the reasons driving me away. Only I can know them. Only I can judge. Great sorrow has consumed me, and I am resolved to depart."

"I perceive enough, my daughter," Savonarola replied, his expression softening. "My understanding of you is clear. You are unhappy in your marriage. However, I am not a confessor, and I seek no secrets sealed by the sacrament. I act upon a divine warrant, independent of such knowledge. You were warned by a message from Heaven, delivered in my very presence, prior to your wedding. You had the lawful opportunity to remain free, yet you chose the bond. By deliberately breaking it—and speaking to you as a pagan, for if the sanctity of matrimony holds no sway over you, I treat you as one outside the covenant—you are violating a sacred pledge. Why will you complain of others' wrongs when you yourselves commit the gravest sin a woman and citizen can perpetrate? Secretly withdrawing from a promise made before God and your fellow men. Breaking the most fundamental law binding humanity together: faithfulness to the spoken word. Is this the wisdom you have acquired by scoffing at the Church? Not to discern the bare duty of integrity, where the Church would teach you to see not merely integrity, but religion?"

Blood surged to Romola's cheeks, and she shrank back as if struck by a blow. "I would not have assumed a disguise," she began, but her voice faltered. She could not finish; the implication in the friar's words—that her conduct mirrored Tito's—shook her foundations.

"To sever that pledge, you flee Florence," he pressed gently. "Florence, the only place where men and women exist to whom you owe the debt of a fellow citizen."

"I never would have left Florence," Romola murmured, her voice trembling, "so long as there remained any hope of fulfilling a duty to my father there."

"Do you acknowledge no tie save that of a child to a father of the flesh?" Savonarola asked sharply. "Your life has been lived in blindness, my daughter. You have dwelt with those perched high on hills, gazing down upon the lives of their neighbors with disdain. I hear their hollow discourses, filled with fancied wisdom of the past, while they scorn God's work in the present. Perhaps you were taught of pagan women who lived for the Republic, yet you have never felt that you, a Florentine woman, ought to live for Florence. If your own kin wear a yoke, will you slip away rather than struggle to lighten it? There is hunger and misery in our streets, and yet you declare, 'I care not; I have my own sorrows.' The servants of God strive for justice, peace, and charity so that the hundred thousand citizens among whom you were born may be ruled rightly. Yet you regard this as trivial, like a bird spreading its wings to seek food wherever it pleases. You have scorned the teaching of the Church, my daughter. As if you, a wilful wanderer chasing your blind desires, were not inferior to the humblest Florentine woman who stretches out her hands with her people, craving a blessing for them; who feels a sisterhood with the neighbor kneeling beside her, stranger though she be; who contemplates the mighty purpose God has for Florence and waits, enduring, because the work is grand and she feels herself small."

"I was not departing for ease or indulgence!" Romola cried, lifting her head, compelled to defend herself. "I went away to hardship. I expect no joy; it has departed from my life."

"You seek your own will, my daughter," Savonarola countered, his voice firm. "You seek some good other than the law you are bound to keep. But how shall you find good?
