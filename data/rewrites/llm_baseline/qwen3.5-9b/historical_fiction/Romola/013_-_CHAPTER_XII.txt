Tito walked with a light, buoyant step. The paralyzing fear that had gripped him earlier had dissolved, allowing his natural disposition to reclaim its cheerful dominance. His destination was clear: he was going to see Romola. Yet, as he approached, the image of her life struck him—a portrait of loving, pitying devotion and patient endurance of tedious duties, qualities from which he had previously shrank and excused himself. However, Tito was not devoid of love for goodness, nor was he prepared to plunge fully into vice. He was still in the flush of youth, his pulses soft to all charm and loveliness, retaining a healthy appetite for ordinary human joys. The poison worked slowly, by degrees. Though he had sold himself to evil, life currently felt so unchanged to him that he remained unconscious of the bonds he had forged. He intended for everything to proceed exactly as before, both within and without him. He planned to win golden reputations through meritorious exertion, ingenious learning, and amiable compliance. He had no intention of doing anything that would disrupt his harmony with the beings he cherished. Above all, he cared for Romola; he desired to have her as his beautiful, loving wife. While a wealthier alliance might theoretically await someone of his eventual success, there was no woman in all Florence comparable to her. When she stood near him, regarding him with her sincere hazel eyes, he was subdued by a delicious influence as powerful and inevitable as the rhythmic vibrations of music that seize the soul, demanding to begin anew the moment they cease.

Even as he climbed the stone stairs, still outside the door with only Maso beside him, this influence seemed to activate merely through the anticipation of her presence.

"Welcome, *Tito mio*," the old man's voice greeted him before Tito could speak. There was a renewed vigor in the voice, a fresh cheerfulness in the blind face, since their first meeting more than two months prior. "You have brought fresh manuscripts, doubtless; but since we spoke last night, I have conceived new ideas. We must broaden our scope—we must return upon our footsteps."

Tito paid his customary homage to Romola as he advanced, then went straight to Bardo's chair, placing his hand in the palm extended to receive it. He settled onto the cross-legged leather seat with scrolled ends, positioning himself close to Bardo's elbow.

"Yes," Tito replied gently. "I have brought the new manuscript, but that can wait until your pleasure dictates. I have young limbs, you know, and can walk back up the hill without any difficulty."

He did not look at Romola while speaking, yet he knew perfectly well that her eyes were fixed upon him with delight.

"That is well said, my son," Bardo remarked, having recently adopted this form of address. "I perceive with gladness that you do not shrink from labor, without which, as the poet wisely noted, life gives nothing to mortals. Too often, young ambition seeks the *palma sine pulvere*, the prize of glory without the dust of the race. But what does the Greek say? 'In the morning of life, work; in the mid-day, give counsel; in the evening, pray.' It is true, I might be thought to have reached that helpless evening; but not so, while I possess counsel within me that remains unspoken. For my mind, as I have often stated, was shut up as by a dam; the plentiful waters lay dark and motionless. But you, my Tito, have opened a duct for them, and they rush forward with a force that surprises even myself. Now, what I require is that we review our preliminary ground again, adopting a wider scheme of commentary and illustration. Otherwise, I may lose opportunities which I now see retrospectively, chances that may never recur. Do you mark what I am saying, Tito?"

Just then, Bardo stooped to retrieve his manuscript, which had rolled down, and his jealous ear caught the slightest movement. Tito might have been excused for shrugging at the prospect ahead, but he was not naturally impatient. Moreover, he had been bred in that laborious erudition, both minute and copious, which defined the chief intellectual pursuit of the age. With Romola nearby, he was floated along by waves of agreeable sensation that made every task appear effortless.

"Assuredly," Tito agreed. "You wish to enlarge your comments on certain passages we have cited."

"Not only so," Bardo insisted. "I wish to introduce occasional *excursuses* where we have studied authors to whom I have given special attention, for I may die too soon to complete a separate work. Furthermore, this is not a time for scholarly integrity and well-sifted learning to remain idle. We must fear not only rash ignorance, but men like Calderino, who, as Poliziano has demonstrated, resort to impudent falsifications of citation to serve their vanity and secure triumphs for their own errors. Therefore, my Tito, I deem it ill-advised to let slip the opportunity beneath our hands. Let us turn back to the point where we cited the passage from Thucydides. First, I wish you to accompany me through all my notes on the Latin translation by Lorenzo Valla. The incomparable Pope Nicholas the Fifth—who honored me with his personal notice when I was young, and when he was still Thomas of Sarzana—paid him the sum of five hundred gold scudi (I say not unduly). Yet, since Valla, though otherwise of dubious fame, is held in high honor for his severe scholarship, whence the epigrammatist jocosely declared that since he descended to the shades, Pluto himself dares not speak in the ancient languages, it is all the more necessary that his name should not serve as a stamp warranting false goods. Thus, I propose introducing an *excursus* on Thucydides, wherein my castigation of Valla's text may find a fitting place. My Romola, you will fetch the needed volumes—you know them—from the fifth shelf of the cabinet."

Tito rose simultaneously with Romola, declaring, "I will fetch them, if you will point them out," and hurriedly followed her into the adjoining small room, where the walls were lined with ranges of books arranged in perfect order.

"There they are," Romola said, pointing upward. "Every book rests exactly where it was when my father ceased to see them."

Tito stood beside her, delaying his approach to the books. They had never shared this room together before.

"I hope," she continued, turning her gaze fully upon Tito with a look of grave confidence, "that he will not weary you; this work brings him such happiness."

"And me too, Romola—if you will only allow me to say I love you—if you will only consider me worthy of a little love."

His words were the softest murmur. The dark, beautiful face, drawn closer to hers than ever before, regarded her with beseeching tenderness.

"I do love you," Romola whispered. She looked at him with the same simple majesty as always, yet her voice had never before sunk to such a murmur. To both of them, it seemed they stared into each other's eyes for a long duration before her lips moved again; yet it was but a moment until she said, "I know *now* what it is to be happy."

Their faces met, and the dark curls mingled for an instant with the rippling gold. Swift as lightning thereafter, Tito stepped onto a projecting ledge of the bookshelves and retrieved the required volumes. Both were content to remain silent and separate, for that first blissful experience of mutual consciousness was all the more exquisite for being unperturbed by immediate physical sensation.

Everything had proceeded as rapidly as the irreversible mingling of waters; even the eager and jealous Bardo had not grown impatient.

"You have the volumes, my Romola?" the old man asked as they returned near him. "Now you must prepare your pen. As Tito marks off the scholia we decide to extract, it is well for you to copy them without delay—mind numbering them carefully to correspond with the numbers in the text which he will write."

Romola always possessed some task that granted her a share in this joint endeavor. Tito took his stand at the *leggio*, where he both wrote and read, while she positioned herself at a table directly in front of him, ready to hand anything he might request to her father's hands or relieve him of a volume he had finished. They had always occupied these positions since the work began, yet today it felt new. It was so different now for them to be opposite one another; so different for Tito to take a book from her as she lifted it from her father's knee. Yet, there was no finesse employed to secure an additional glance or touch. Each woman creates in her own likeness the tokens of love offered to her; and Romola's deep, calm happiness encompassed Tito like the rich, quiet light of an evening that dissipates all unrest.

Two hours had passed in their work, and they were pausing only because of the fading light, when the door opened and a figure entered, strangely incongruous with the current of their thoughts and the suggestions of every object around them. It was the figure of a short, stout, black-eyed woman, about fifty years old, wearing a black velvet *berretta* or close cap embroidered with pearls. Underneath, surprisingly massive black braids surmounted a little bulging forehead and fell in rich, plaited curves over her ears, contrasting with a startling carmine tint on the upper region of her fat cheeks, which stood out against the surrounding sallowness.
