Mrs. Dashwood listened to the defense of her former favorite with a heart deeply stirred, yet her joy was tempered by sorrow. To see Willoughby absolved of some charges was a relief, but it could not undo the past. The image of Marianne's suffering, born of his deceit, burned too brightly to be extinguished by a man's words alone. No amount of vindication could restore the unblemished faith Marianne once held, nor erase the stain of his betrayal regarding Eliza. Consequently, whatever peace Willoughby sought, it could not bring back to Marianne's love, nor harm Colonel Brandon's standing in her daughter's eyes.

If Mrs. Dashwood, like her daughter, had heard Willoughby's tale directly from his lips—if she had seen the raw distress etched upon his face and been swayed by the melancholy cadence of his voice—perhaps her pity would have overwhelmed her judgment. But Elinor had not yielded to such impulses. Having heard the story secondhand, reflection had cooled her emotions and sharpened her discernment. She resolved to speak only the bare truth, laying out the facts of his character without the softening filter of tenderness that might mislead the imagination.

That evening, when the three sat together, Marianne, after a struggle, turned the conversation back to him. There was an evident effort behind her words; her cheeks flushed with agitation, and her voice trembled slightly as she spoke. "I wish to assure you both," she declared, "that I see everything as you desire me to."

Mrs. Dashwood, moved by maternal instinct, would have interjected with gentle comfort, but Elinor, wishing to hear her sister's unbiased thoughts, signaled for silence. Marianne continued slowly. "It is a great relief to me. What Elinor told me this morning... I have now heard exactly what I wished to hear." For a moment, her voice failed, but regaining composure, she spoke with a newfound calm. "I am perfectly satisfied. I wish for no change. I never could have been happy with him, for I must eventually have known the truth. After learning it, I would have possessed no confidence, no esteem for him. Nothing could ever alter those feelings."

"I know it, I know it!" cried her mother. "To be happy with a man of libertine practices! With one who shattered the peace of our dearest friend and injured the best of men! No, my Marianne, you have not a heart capable of finding joy in such a man. Your conscience, so finely tuned, would have felt every wrong he committed as a husband ought to feel."

Marianne sighed, repeating, "I wish for no change."

"You view the matter," said Elinor, "exactly as sound judgment dictates. You surely perceive, as I do, reasons enough to conclude that your marriage would have entangled you in endless troubles and disappointments, unsupported by any genuine affection on his part. Had you married, poverty would have been your constant companion. His extravagance is admitted even by himself; his entire conduct shows he understands little of self-denial. On a meager income, his demands coupled with your inexperience would inevitably have brought distress far more grievous than anything you have faced thus far, precisely because you had never anticipated it. Your sense of honor and honesty would have driven you to extreme economies, retrenching first on your own comforts, and perhaps, if allowed, on necessities. Yet, how little could your solitary management halt the ruin already begun? If you had dared to curtail *his* enjoyments, would you not fear that, given his selfish nature, he would resent it, thereby weakening your influence over his heart and making him regret a union that plunged him into such difficulty?"

Marianne's lips quivered as she echoed the word "Selfish?" in a tone that questioned whether her sister truly believed it.

"The entirety of his behavior," Elinor replied firmly, "from the beginning to the end of this affair, was rooted in selfishness. It was selfishness that first toyed with your affections, then delayed the necessary confession when his own heart was claimed, and finally drove him from Barton. In every instance, his own pleasure or ease was his sole guiding principle."

"It is very true," Marianne whispered. "My happiness was never his objective."

"At present," Elinor continued, "he regrets his actions. Why does he regret them? Because they have not served him. They have not made him happy. His circumstances are now secure; he suffers no financial ills, and he merely believes he wedded a woman of inferior temperament to yourself. Does it follow, however, that had he married you, he would have been content? The inconveniences would have differed. He would have endured the pecuniary straits he currently ignores, for they are now removed. He would have had a wife of admirable temper, yet he would have been perpetually needy, perpetually poor. Soon, likely, he would have ranked the comforts of a clear estate and a steady income above all else, even above the gentle spirit of a partner."

"I have no doubt of it," Marianne said. "And I have nothing to regret save my own folly."

"Rather blame your mother's imprudence, my child," Mrs. Dashwood gently suggested. "_She_ must bear the responsibility."

Marianne cut her mother short. Elinor, sensing that both sisters had recognized their errors, wished to avoid dwelling on the past lest it weaken Marianne's spirits. Turning to the immediate topic, she observed, "One conclusion may be fairly drawn from the whole story: every difficulty Willoughby faces stems from his first offense against virtue, his treatment of Eliza Williams. That initial crime is the source of every subsequent failure and all his current discontent."

Marianne assented with deep feeling. This remark prompted her mother to enumerate Colonel Brandon's virtues and the injustices he suffered, speaking with warmth and conviction. Yet, Marianne appeared barely to listen.

Over the next few days, Elinor noted that Marianne did not recover her strength as quickly as hoped. While her resolve remained unshaken and she strove to maintain a cheerful demeanor, Elinor trusted that time itself would heal her sister's health. Soon, Margaret returned, and the family resettled quietly at the cottage. Though their usual studies lacked the vigor of their early days at Barton, they planned to resume them with renewed force.

Yet, Elinor grew increasingly impatient for news of Edward. Since leaving London, she had heard nothing of his plans or whereabouts. Letters exchanged during Marianne's illness contained a chilling sentence from John: "We know nothing of our unfortunate Edward... we conclude him to be still at Oxford." Edward's name vanished from all subsequent correspondence, leaving Elinor in a state of anxious uncertainty.

Then, one morning, the footman who had gone to Exeter on business returned. As he waited at table, answering his mistress's inquiries about his journey, he volunteered a startling piece of news. "I suppose you know, ma'am, that Mr. Ferrars is married."

Marianne jumped violently, her gaze locking onto Elinor, who turned deathly pale. Marianne sank back into her chair, overcome with hysteria. Mrs. Dashwood, having followed Elinor's terrified glance, was horrified to see the depth of her daughter's suffering. A moment later, equally distressed by Marianne's collapse, she struggled to decide where to direct her primary care.

Seeing only that Miss Marianne was ill, the sensible footman summoned a maid. Together, they supported the fainting girl into another room. By the time Marianne had steadied somewhat, her mother left her to Margaret and the maid, returning to Elinor. Though shaken, Elinor had regained her voice and reason, beginning to ask Thomas for the source of his intelligence. Mrs. Dashwood immediately took charge, gathering the details so Elinor need not exert herself.

"Who told you that Mr. Ferrars was married, Thomas?"

"I saw Mr. Ferrars myself, ma'am, this morning in Exeter, along with his lady, Miss Steele as she was. They were stopped in a chaise at the door of the New London Inn when I delivered a message from Sally at the Park to her brother, a post-boy. As I passed, I glanced up and saw directly it was the youngest Miss Steele. I took off my hat, and she recognized me, calling out to inquire after you, ma'am, and the young ladies, especially Miss Marianne. She offered her and Mr. Ferrars's best compliments, expressing how sorry they were for not having time to visit, noting they were in a great hurry to travel further west for a brief stay, promising to return and call soon."

"But did she tell you she was married?"

"Yes, ma'am. She smiled and said how she had changed her name since visiting these parts. She is always a very affable and free-spoken young lady, well-behaved. So, I freely wished her joy."

"Was Mr. Ferrars in the carriage with her?"

"Yes, ma'am. I saw him leaning back, but he did not look up; he has never been a gentleman fond of conversation."

"Elinor's heart readily accounted for his silence. Mrs. Dashwood likely shared the same understanding."

"Was anyone else in the carriage?"

"No, ma'am, only the two of them."

"Do you know where they came from?"

"They came straight from town, as Miss Lucy—now Mrs. Ferrars—informed me."
