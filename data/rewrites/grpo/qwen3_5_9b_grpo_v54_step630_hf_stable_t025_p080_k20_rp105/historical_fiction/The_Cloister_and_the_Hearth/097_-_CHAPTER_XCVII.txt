In the silent, hallowed chamber of death, a priest finds his truest vocation. Gerard performed the final rites for the departed with the mechanical precision he would have applied to his humblest parishioner, his mind still fogged by the shock of loss. Only after the rituals were concluded did the full weight of the blow strike him, leaving him stupefied. Then, in a sudden surge of religious fervor, he knelt to thank Heaven for her Christian end.

His singular thought became to remove her from the gaze of strangers and lay her to rest within his own churchyard. That very evening, a covered cart drawn by a single horse rolled toward Gouda. Inside lay a coffin, and beside it, a broken-hearted man slumped with his arms and chin resting upon the cold wood.

The mourner's brief reserve of strength had vanished in the necessities of preparation. Now, he lay crushed, clinging desperately to the lead coffin that held her. The driver, a silent figure who walked by the horse's head, offered no words of comfort. Even when the horse needed rest, the driver spoke only in whispers, respecting the mute agony of his passenger. When they halted for the night, the innkeeper and his guests, well-meaning in their concern, urged the mourner to seek rest and food. Gerard repulsed them, snarling like a faithful dog denied its master, refusing to let go of the lead all through the long darkness. Finally, they draped a heavy cloak over him and left him in peace.

At noon, the sorrowful cart arrived at the manse. A dozen parishioners had gathered, their small troubles forgotten in anticipation of the parson. Seeing him so utterly stricken, they raised a loud wail of grief that pierced the air. The sound roused Gerard from his lethargy of woe; he saw their faces, tried to speak—"Oh, my children! my children!"—but choked back the words in his anguish.

Yet, the next day, despite all remonstrances, he buried her himself. He read the service with a voice that trembled only occasionally, and many tears fell upon the fresh earth. When the service ended, he stood like a statue in the churchyard, unmoving, while the people departed out of respect.

He remained in a trance-like state until the sexton, a man of simple wit, began to fill the grave without warning. At the sound of earth falling upon her, Gerard uttered a piercing scream. The sexton paused. Gerard staggered, clutching his breast, and the sexton supported him, calling for aid. Jorian Ketel, who had lingered nearby to mourn his benefactress, ran into the churchyard. Together, the two men supported Gerard back to the manse.

"Ah, Jorian! good Jorian!" he cried, his voice breaking. "Something snapped within me; I felt it, and I heard it. Here, Jorian, here," he gasped, pressing his hand firmly against his chest.
