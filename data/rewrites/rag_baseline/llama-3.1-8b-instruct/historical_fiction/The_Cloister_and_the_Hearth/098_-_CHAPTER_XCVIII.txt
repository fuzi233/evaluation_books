In the stillness of a Goudan dawn, a gaunt figure emerged from the mist-shrouded streets, his footsteps echoing off the stone façades of the Dominican convent. A fortnight had passed since the prior's departure, and the novice master, Brother Ambrose, governed the community with a gentle hand. The newcomer, Gerard, had come to seek solace within these hallowed walls, his frame pale and bent beneath the weight of memories.

As he pushed open the heavy door, a warm light spilled out, beckoning him towards the soft murmur of chanting monks. Gerard's eyes, red-rimmed from lack of sleep, adjusted slowly to the radiance, and he spotted Ambrose, his friend and confidant, waiting for him with an expectant smile. Their paths had crossed many times, in the bustling town of Gouda, where Gerard had served as a vicar, and later, in the quiet moments of contemplation, when the weight of his responsibilities threatened to consume him.

The memory of those moments lingered, a bittersweet ache that Gerard carried with him, like the scent of old incense wafting through the convent's corridors. He had come here to lay down the burden, to surrender to the gentle guidance of Ambrose, and the comforting routine of monastic life. The thought of his manse, once a symbol of security and love, now felt oppressive, a constant reminder of the woman who had filled its rooms with laughter and light.

As Gerard approached, Ambrose rose from his seat, his eyes shining with a warm welcome. "Gerard, my friend," he said, his voice low and soothing, "it's good to see you. Come, rest with us, and let us share a meal together." The monks, sensing the tension in Gerard's shoulders, moved to make room for him, their faces a blur of concern and compassion.

But their kindness was short-lived. A harsh voice, like the crack of a whip, cut through the tranquility, sending a shiver down Gerard's spine. The new prior, a man named Jerome, had arrived, his presence as unforgiving as the stone walls that enclosed them. "Let him first give up his vicarage," he growled, his eyes narrowing as he surveyed Gerard, "or let him go. I'll have no fat parsons in my house."
