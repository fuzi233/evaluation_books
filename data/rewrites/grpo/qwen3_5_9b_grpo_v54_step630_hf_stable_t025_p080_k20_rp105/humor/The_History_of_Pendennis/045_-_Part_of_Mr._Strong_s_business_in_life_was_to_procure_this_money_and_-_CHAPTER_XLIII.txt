If you've ever wondered where the real magic of London hides, look no further than Shepherd's Inn. Nestled right next to Lincoln's-Inn Fields and the Temple, tucked behind the grimy gables and soot-blackened chimneys of Wych Street and Holywell Street, lies a quadrangle that seems to have forgotten how to let the sun in. You find it through winding, smoky alleyways where the light barely dares to tread. Here, the air is thick with the scent of slop-sellers, brandy-ball vendors, and peddlers of cheap theatrical prints for the young. The shops sell dingy furniture that promises comfort but delivers only dreams of sleeplessness.

The doors are ringed with bells, and the steps are crowded with dirty children forming endless, noisy groups. Around the shellfish dealers' trays, the damp pavement echoes with the clatter of pattens, stained perpetually by mud. Ballad singers drone out satirical songs against the Whigs, the bishops, and even the German relatives of royalty, while Punch sets up his tiny theatre, confident of an audience and a halfpenny. Women scream after loitering kids or, worse, at husbands stumbling out of gin-shops. It is a ceaseless, chaotic din—a perfect contrast to the quiet, old-fashioned peace of the Inn's central courtyard. In the middle of a patchy grass plot stands the statue of Shepherd, guarded by iron rails from the inevitable assault of boys. The Inn's hall, painted with the founder's arms, lines one side, while tall, ancient chambers wrap around the others, opening onto Oldcastle Street and the great London thoroughfare beyond.

Long ago, this place belonged to lawyers, but the law has largely moved on. Today, the ground floor houses the offices of the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines and the Registry of Patent Inventions. The only gentleman of note listed in the "Law List" is Mr. Campion, a man with mustachios who arrives in his cab two or three times a week. In his West End offices on Curzon Street, he is Mr. Somerset Campion, lending money to nobility; here, in Shepherd's Inn, he is simply "Campion and Co." The same tuft of hair that decorates his chin sprouts from the lower lip of his entire firm. It is a spectacle to see his cab-horse's harness blazing with heraldic bearings as the vehicle stops; the horse froths at the mouth, chafing under the shiny bit, while the groom's white breeches and reins gleam like a sudden burst of sunshine in the gloom.

Our old friend, Captain Costigan, spends his afternoons inspecting Campion's cab and horse. Dressed in carpet slippers and a dressing-gown, with his old hat cocked over one eye, he trails about the court like a ghost of a bygone era. He suns himself there after breakfast, visiting the porter's lodge to pat the children's heads and chat with Mrs. Bolton about the theatres and her daughter, Leedy Mirabel. Mrs. Bolton, once a dancer at the Wells as the thirteenth of Mr. Serle's pupils, knows a thing or two about the stage.

Costigan lives on the third floor of No. 4, in the rooms formerly belonging to Mr. Podmore—whose name still hangs above the door, though almost every door in Shepherd's Inn bears someone else's name. After the charming tenor singer Charley Podmore married and moved to Lambeth, he handed over his chambers to Mr. Bows and Captain Costigan, who share them. You can often hear Mr. Bows's piano tinkling when the windows are open, either for amusement or to teach Fanny Bolton, the porteress's daughter. Fanny, with her good voice and pretty figure, dreams of emulating her mother's glory. In exchange for cleaning and cooking for the two gentlemen, Mr. Bows teaches her music. She thinks the Captain is the finest gentleman in the world, ignoring his unfortunate tendency toward liquor, believing all fashionable men indulge in excess. She loves Mr. Bows too, and this shy, eccentric old man returns the affection with a fatherly warmth, for his heart is full of kindness and he cannot be at ease unless he loves someone.

Captain Costigan has certainly hosted distinguished visitors before, and in the mornings, he speaks with such grandeur that you'd imagine Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel drop by daily to visit "the honest old half-pay Captain, poor old Jack." But the truth is far less romantic. Lady Mirabel left her husband's card in the little mirror over the mantelpiece months ago and hasn't visited her father in person recently. Upon marrying Sir Charles, she settled a small pension on him, allowing him occasional access to their table. At first, Cos's behavior in high society was merely absurd; he dressed in his best and spoke in the longest, richest words he could muster. "Was your Leedyship in the Pork to dee?" he would ask. "I looked for your equipage in veen!" He would claim to have attended dozens of Levees at Dublin Castle and threaten to leave his yard upon Apsley House. He was magnificent in his courtesy, addressing not just the masters and guests but the servants, who struggled to maintain their gravity while serving Captain Costigan.

However, after a few visits, Cos's caution vanished. He would maintain sobriety until he reached the Back Kitchen, where he would brag about his son-in-law's wines until his speech failed him over his sixth tumbler of whisky-punch. Familiarity bred disaster; he prematurely inebriated himself at Sir Charles's table, leading to a carriage being called and the hospitable door shutting in his face. He spoke sadly to his friends at the Kitchen about being a "thankless child" and a "pore worn-out lonely old man," drowning his sorrows in punch.

When his credit ran dry, he began begging money from his daughter, sometimes making statements that weren't entirely truthful. Once, a bailiff was about to take him to prison; he wrote a desperate letter asking for three pounds and five shillings to "liberate a poor man's grey hairs from gaol." Lady Mirabel sent the money with a stern warning to be more economical. Another time, he broke a plate-glass window in the Strand. Again, the money came, delivered by her servant to the Captain's "aide-de-camp"—a poorer Irish gentleman sent to run errands. If the servant had followed, he would have seen the Captain at the Roscius Head, Harlequin Yard, Drury Lane, in pawn for rum-and-water, surrounded by his staff. On a third occasion, claiming illness, he asked for funds for a physician. Alarmed, Lady Mirabel drove to Shepherd's Inn. The porteress pointed to No. 4, third floor, "name of Podmore over the door." Inside, Cos opened the door in his shirt-sleeves, ready with a gridiron to cook mutton-chops Mrs. Bolton had bought.

It was also unpleasant for Sir Charles Mirabel to receive constant letters stating the Captain was waiting in the hall, or to rush out of his brougham at the Travellers' Club to avoid his father-in-law seizing him. Sir Charles, a weak, elderly man with many infirmities, cried to his wife about the ordeal, declaring he must move abroad or to the country, fearing death or another fit if he saw that man again. Only after a second stern visit, where Lady Mirabel threatened to withdraw his allowance entirely if he continued to plague her husband with letters or street encounters, did she manage to keep her papa in order. She also rebuked Mr. Bows for failing to watch the Captain, forbidding him to drink in taverns and telling the owners to refuse him credit. "Papa's conduct is bringing me to the grave," she declared, though she looked perfectly healthy. "And you, as an old man... ought to be ashamed of abetting him." Honest Bows received these thanks for his lifelong devotion, though one wonders if the old philosopher was much better off than many others.

Next door, at No. 3, live two other acquaintances: Colonel Altamont, agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow, and Captain Chevalier Edward Strong. Their door bears no name. The Captain prefers anonymity, listing a Jermyn Street hotel on his cards, while the Colonel claims to be on a confidential mission independent of the East India Company. "In fact," Strong explains, "Colonel Altamont's object is financial; he wishes to sell some of the principal diamonds and rubies of the Lucknow crown to private capitalists, avoiding the India House."

Since the arrival of Sir Francis Clavering, Strong's chambers have been comfortably furnished. The Chevalier boasts that few retired captains are more snugly quartered than he. Below are three rooms: an office where Strong conducts his mysterious business, a bedroom, and a sitting-room. A private stair leads to two upper apartments—one for the Colonel, the other a kitchen and bedroom for Mr. Grady, the attendant. These rooms are on the same level as Bows and Costigan's at No. 4, connected by leads over which Grady can reach the mignonette box blooming in Bows's window.

From Grady's kitchen often wafted fragrant odors. The three old soldiers guarding No. 3 were culinary experts. Grady mastered Irish stew; the Colonel was famous for pilau and curry; and Strong could cook anything. French dishes, Spanish stews, fricassees, and omelettes came from his hands to perfection. When his purse was full or his credit good, he was the most hospitable man in England, offering friends a good dinner, a good glass of wine, and a good song. Poor Cos often heard with envy the roar of Strong's choruses and the musical clinking of glasses from his distant room, separated yet near to those festivities. It wasn't expedient to invite Costigan always; his drunkenness was lamentable, and he bored Strong's guests with stories when sober and maudlin tears when drunk.

They were a strange, motley set, these friends of the Chevalier. Though Major Pendennis wouldn't have relished their company, Arthur and Warrington enjoyed it, and Pen found it as amusing as the society of the finest gentlemen in the finest houses. Every man had a history, a tide of luck and bad fortune. Jack Holt, once in Don Carlos's army, was now organizing a tobacco smuggling scheme promising thirty thousand a year for a fifteen hundred-pound bribe. Tom Diver, from the Mexican navy, knew of a sunken specie-ship carrying three hundred and eighty thousand dollars and gold bars, offering eighteen hundred pounds to dive for it and return to Parliament. Keightley, manager of the underwater copper mines, had a Smyrna Sponge Company and a quicksilver operation in view. Filby had been everything: a dragoon, field-preacher, missionary, and Greenwich fair actor, inheriting a property from his deceased father whose location remained a mystery. Adding to this mix was Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., who liked their society despite lacking their convivial powers. Made much of for his wealth and position, he told little stories and sang a few songs with great affability, having seen the inside of more prisons than one and signed many stamped papers in his past.

When Altamont first returned from Paris, having communicated with Sir Francis Clavering from a hotel in a denuded state despite his entrusted wealth, Strong was sent by the Baronet to pay his bills and invited to stay at the chambers. To negotiate with Altamont was one thing, but having such a person settled in his rooms and burdened with his society did not suit the Chevalier's taste. He grumbled to his principal.

"I wish you would put this bear into somebody else's cage," he told Clavering. "The fellow's no gentleman. I don't like walking with him. He dresses himself like a nigger on a holiday. I took him to the play the other night; and, by Jove, sir, he abused the actor playing the villain and swore so that the people in the boxes wanted to turn him out. The after-piece was 'The Brigand,' where Wallack comes in wounded and dies. When he died, Altamont began to cry like a child, saying it was a d——d shame, crying and swearing so that there was another row and everybody laughing. Then I had to take him away because he wanted to take his coat off to one fellow who laughed at him and bellowed to him to stand up like a man. Who is he? Where the deuce does he come from? You had best tell me the whole story, Frank. You and he have robbed a church together, that's my belief. You had better get it off your mind at once, Clavering, and tell me what this Altamont is, and what hold he has over you."

"Hang him! I wish he was dead!" was the Baronet's only reply. His countenance grew so gloomy that Strong dared not question him further, though he resolved to discover for himself the secret tie between Altamont and Clavering if need be.
