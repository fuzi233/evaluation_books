My hand fell from the curtain, but do not imagine—oh, do not imagine—that my immediate concern was the awkwardness of my position! So deeply did I care for Mr. Godfrey that I barely paused to wonder why he had not gone to the concert. No—my thoughts were consumed by the words he had just spoken: *“I’ll do it today.”* He had said it with a tone so firm, so final, that it chilled me to the bone. What could he possibly mean? Something worse than anything he had already done? Would he abandon his faith? Would he turn his back on all the charitable work he had championed? Had we seen the last of his radiant smile in the committee rooms? Had we heard the last of his stirring speeches at Exeter Hall?

I was so overwhelmed by the thought of such a fall from grace—so utterly shaken—that I nearly leapt from my hiding place, ready to beg him, in the name of every Ladies’ Committee in London, to explain himself. But then—another voice cut through the silence.

It came through the curtains, clear and bold, devoid of any softness or gentleness. It was Rachel Verinder’s voice.

“Why have you come up here, Godfrey?” she asked. “Why didn’t you go into the library?”

He laughed softly. “Miss Clack is in the library.”

“Clack in the library!” she exclaimed, instantly taking a seat on the ottoman in the back drawing-room. “You’re quite right, Godfrey. We’d better stay here.”

A moment ago, I had been burning with anxiety, uncertain what to do. Now, I was suddenly icy cold, and my decision was clear. To reveal myself after hearing what I had—impossible. To retreat—except into the fireplace—equally impossible. A kind of martyrdom awaited me. In fairness to myself, I quietly adjusted the curtains so I could both see and hear. Then, like a faithful disciple of old, I faced my ordeal with quiet resolve.

“Don’t sit on the ottoman,” Rachel said. “Bring a chair, Godfrey. I like people to be opposite me when I talk.”

He took the nearest seat—a low one, ill-suited to his height. His legs stretched out awkwardly. I had never seen them look so ungainly before.

“Well?” she pressed.

“What did you say to them?”

“Just what you told me, dear Rachel,” he replied.

“That mamma wasn’t well today? And that you didn’t quite like leaving her to go to the concert?”

“Exactly those words. They were disappointed to miss you, but they understood. Everyone sent their love—and expressed hope that Lady Verinder would recover soon.”

“You don’t think it’s serious, do you, Godfrey?”

“Not at all. I’m certain she’ll be better in a few days.”

“I think so too. I was frightened at first—but I believe it now. It was very kind of you to make my excuses to people who are almost strangers to you. But why not go with them to the concert? It seems unfair that you should miss the music too.”

“Don’t say that, Rachel! If only you knew how much happier I am—here, with you!”

He clasped his hands and looked at her. As he did so, he turned slightly toward me. And then—how can I describe it?—I saw the same tender, pleading expression on his face that once moved thousands at Exeter Hall when he spoke for the destitute. The same expression that had stirred my own heart.

“It’s hard to break old habits, Godfrey,” she said. “But try—please, for my sake—to stop paying compliments.”

“I’ve never paid *you* a compliment, Rachel,” he said gently. “Successful love may use flattery. But hopeless love—dearest—always speaks the truth.”

He drew his chair closer and took her hand as he said “hopeless love.”

There was a pause. The man who could electrify crowds had, without doubt, stirred her.

Now I understood his earlier words: *“I’ll do it today.”* Alas—the strictest sense of propriety could scarcely have prevented anyone from seeing that he was doing it now.

“Have you forgotten what we agreed upon in the country?” Rachel asked. “We said we’d be cousins—nothing more.”

“I break that agreement every time I see you,” he murmured.

“Then don’t see me.”

“Impossible. I break it every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel—how kindly you told me the other day that my place in your regard had never been higher! Am I mad to build hopes on those words? Am I mad to dream of a future when your heart might soften? Don’t tell me so—if I am! Let me keep this delusion, dearest. I must have it—to cherish, to comfort me, if nothing else.”

His voice trembled. He raised his white handkerchief to his eyes.

Exeter Hall again—only missing the audience, the cheers, the glass of water.

Even Rachel, so stiff and reserved, seemed touched. I saw her lean slightly closer. Her next words carried a new warmth.

“Are you really sure, Godfrey, that you feel this way?”

“Sure,” he said. “You know what I was, Rachel. Let me tell you what I am. I’ve lost every interest in life—except in you. A transformation has come over me that I cannot explain. Would you believe it? My charitable work now feels like an unbearable burden. When I see a Ladies’ Committee, I wish I were at the ends of the earth!”

If there is any record in history of such a dramatic renunciation, I confess I’ve never encountered it. I thought of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes Society. The Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision. All the others—too many to list—built upon his strength, his presence, his devotion. And now, this man—this same Mr. Godfrey—had just called our noble cause a *nuisance*, and wished he could escape us entirely!

My young female readers may take courage from knowing that even I, trained in self-control, struggled to suppress my outrage. Yet I managed—not a word was missed.

Rachel spoke again. “You’ve made your confession,” she said. “I wonder—if I made mine—would it cure you of your attachment to me?”

He started. I started too. We both thought she was about to speak of the Moonstone.

“Would you believe,” she went on, “that I am the most wretched girl alive? It’s true, Godfrey. What greater misery is there than living degraded in your own eyes? That is my life now.”

“My dear Rachel! How could you possibly think such a thing?”

“How do you know I have no reason?”

“Because I know you. Your silence has never lowered you in the eyes of your true friends. The disappearance of your birthday gift—your unexplained involvement in that event—may seem strange…”

“Are you speaking of the Moonstone?”

“I thought you referred to it—”

“I referred to nothing of the sort. I can hear of the Moonstone’s loss, whoever speaks of it, without feeling ashamed. If the truth ever comes out, it will be known that I accepted a terrible responsibility—that I kept a painful secret—but also that I did nothing dishonorable. You’ve misunderstood me, Godfrey. It’s my fault for not being clearer. Listen—I’ll be plain. Suppose you weren’t in love with me. Suppose you loved another woman?”

“Yes?”

“Suppose you discovered her to be unworthy of you—so unworthy that thinking of marrying her makes your face burn with shame?”

“Yes?”

“And yet—despite all that—you couldn’t tear her from your heart? Suppose the feeling she awakened in you—when you believed in her—was something you cannot hide? Suppose the love this woman inspired… oh, how can I express it? How can I make a man understand that a feeling which horrifies me, can also fascinate me? It’s the breath of my life—and the poison that kills me—both at once! Go away! I must be mad to speak like this. No—you mustn’t leave. You mustn’t carry a false impression. I must defend myself. Remember this: *He* doesn’t know—never will know—what I’ve told you. I will never see him again—no matter what happens. Never, never, never! Don’t ask his name. Don’t ask anything more. Change the subject. Are you a doctor, Godfrey? Can you tell me why I feel like I’m suffocating? Is there a form of hysteria that turns into words instead of tears? Probably. What does it matter? You’ll get over whatever trouble I’ve caused you. I’ve fallen to my rightful place in your estimation, haven’t I? Don’t notice me. Don’t pity me. For God’s sake, go!”

She turned abruptly, striking the ottoman wildly with her hands. Her head sank onto the cushions. She broke down, sobbing.

Before I could react, I was stunned by Mr. Godfrey’s next move.

Was it possible? He dropped to his knees—*both* knees, I swear it! And then—did he truly wrap his arms around her? And did he, with a voice charged with passion, utter only two words?

“Noble creature!”

That was all. But delivered with the same fervor that had won him fame on the platform.

She sat motionless—either stunned or enchanted—I couldn’t tell. She made no effort to push him away.

As for me—my sense of decorum was shattered. I couldn’t decide whether to close my eyes or cover my ears. I did neither. I held the curtain in place, clinging to it as though it were my only anchor. I attribute my steadiness entirely to suppressed hysterics. Doctors admit that during such states, one must hold onto something.

“Yes,” he said, his voice full of its familiar evangelical power, “you are a noble creature. A woman who speaks the truth for truth’s sake—someone who sacrifices pride rather than betray an honest man who loves her—is the rarest treasure. When such a woman marries, and her husband wins only her respect and regard—then he wins enough to ennoble his entire life. You’ve spoken of your place in my estimation. Judge it now—when I kneel before you, begging you to let me heal your wounded heart. Rachel! Will you honor me? Will you bless me? By becoming my wife?”

By now, I would have covered my ears—had Rachel not given me reason to keep listening. She answered in the clearest, most sensible words I had ever heard from her.

“Godfrey,” she said, “you must be mad.”

“I’ve never spoken more reasonably—in your interests as well as mine. Look to the future. Is your happiness to be sacrificed to a man who knows nothing of your feelings—and whom you vow never to see again? Is it not your duty to forget this doomed attachment? And can you find peace in the life you lead now? You’ve tried it—and you’re already weary. Surround yourself with nobler pursuits than the petty concerns of the world. A home where love and honor await you—where daily duties grow gently into peace—try that consolation, Rachel. I don’t ask for your love. I’ll be content with your affection and regard. Leave the rest to my devotion—and to Time, which heals even wounds as deep as yours.”

She was beginning to yield. Oh, what a upbringing she must have had! How differently I would have acted in her place!

“Don’t tempt me, Godfrey,” she whispered. “I’m wretched enough, reckless enough already. Don’t make me worse.”

“One question, Rachel. Do you have any personal objection to me?”

“I? I always liked you. After what you’ve just said, I’d be blind not to respect and admire you too.”

“Do you know many wives who respect and admire their husbands? And yet they live happily. How many brides go to the altar with hearts open to inspection? And yet, somehow, marriage endures. The truth is, women often seek marriage as a refuge—more than they’ll admit—and they find it justified. Consider your own case. At your age, with your beauty, is it possible to condemn yourself to a life alone? Trust my experience—nothing is less possible. It’s only a matter of time. You may marry someone else years from now. Or you may marry the man who is now at your feet—one who values your respect above the love of any other woman on earth.”

“Gently, Godfrey! You’re planting thoughts in my mind I’ve never had. You’re offering me a new path—while all others are closed. I tell you again, if you say another word, I’ll marry you on your terms. Take the warning and go!”

“I won’t rise until you say yes!”

“If I say yes, we’ll both regret it—when it’s too late!”

“We’ll both bless the day I begged, and you yielded.”

“Do you feel as confident as you sound?”

“You shall judge for yourself. I speak from what I’ve seen in my own family. Tell me—what do you think of our household at Frizinghall? Do my father and mother live unhappily together?”

“Far from it—as far as I can see.”

“When my mother was young, Rachel—no secret in the family—she loved as you love. She gave her heart to a man unworthy of her. She married my father—respecting him, admiring him, but nothing more. You’ve seen the result. Is there no encouragement in that for us?”

“You won’t hurry me, Godfrey?”

“My time is yours.”

“You won’t ask for more than I can give?”

“My angel. I only ask you to give me yourself.”

“Take me.”

In those two words—she accepted him.

He erupted into another burst—this time, one of ecstatic joy. He pulled her closer until her face touched his. And then—no. I cannot bring myself to recount what followed. I tried to close my eyes—but I was a moment too late. I had expected resistance. She surrendered.

To any decent person of my sex, no further explanation is needed. Even my innocence began to grasp the conclusion. They understood each other completely. I fully expected to see them walk off together, arm in arm, to begin a new life.

Yet, according to Mr. Godfrey’s next words, one small formality remained.

He sat beside her—uninvited this time—on the ottoman.

“Shall I speak to your dear mother?” he asked. “Or will you?”

She refused both. “Let my mother hear nothing from either of us until she is better. Keep it a secret, Godfrey. Go now. Come back this evening. We’ve been alone long enough.”

She rose—and as she did, she glanced for the first time toward the little room where I was hidden.

“Who has drawn those curtains?” she demanded. “The room is close enough without blocking the air like this.”

She stepped forward, reaching for the fabric. At that instant—just as discovery seemed inevitable—the voice of the footman echoed from the stairs.

“Miss Rachel!” he cried, clearly alarmed. “Where are you, Miss Rachel?”

She recoiled from the curtains and rushed to the door. The footman entered, pale as death.

“Please come downstairs, Miss! My lady has fainted—and we can’t revive her.”

In moments, I was alone—free to descend unnoticed. Mr. Godfrey passed me in the hall, hurrying to fetch the doctor.

“Go in and help them!” he urged, pointing to the room.

I found Rachel kneeling beside the sofa, cradling her mother’s head. One glance at Lady Verinder’s face—knowing what I knew—was enough to confirm the horror.

I said nothing until the doctor arrived. He sent Rachel out first. Then he told us—Lady Verinder was dead.

And when he looked at me—no sign of remorse. Not a trace.

Later, I peered into the breakfast-room and the library. My aunt had died without reading any of the letters I had written to her.

I was so shocked by that, I didn’t realize until days later—she had also died without giving me my little legacy.
