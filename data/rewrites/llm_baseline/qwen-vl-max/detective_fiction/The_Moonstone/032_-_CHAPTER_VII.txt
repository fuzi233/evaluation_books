The events that led to Lady Verinder’s death have already been laid bare in the correspondence preceding this chapter. There is no need to dwell further on them—only to state plainly that she passed away, and that her passing marks the end of my fifth chapter. From this point forward, I shall confine myself strictly to what I have personally witnessed or experienced.

It was not until a month after my aunt’s death that I saw Rachel Verinder again. Our reunion took place under circumstances that would prove pivotal—not only for me, but for her as well. During the brief time I spent at her side, an event unfolded concerning her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite that demands full attention. Once this matter is revealed, my account will be complete. I have told all I know—as an unwilling yet undeniable witness to these unfolding truths.

Lady Verinder’s remains were removed from London and interred in the small cemetery adjacent to the church within her own estate. The family invited me to attend the funeral, but I could not bring myself to travel so soon after such a profound loss. My religious convictions made it impossible to face the service with any sense of peace. Moreover, I learned that the rector of Frizinghall—a man whose character I had long questioned—was to officiate. In fact, I had once seen him seated at Lady Verinder’s whist table, behaving more like a gambler than a clergyman. Even if I had been physically able to go, I doubt I would have felt morally justified in attending.

With her mother gone, Rachel fell under the care of her uncle, Mr. Ablewhite the elder. As named guardian by her mother’s will, he was responsible for Rachel until she married or came of age. It was natural, then, that Godfrey should inform his father of their engagement. Within ten days, the secret was known throughout the household. For Mr. Ablewhite senior—another man of questionable moral standing—the challenge became how best to impress and accommodate the wealthy young woman who was about to marry his son.

Rachel, however, proved difficult at first. She refused to live in Montagu Square, where her mother had died, or in Yorkshire, where the Moonstone scandal had taken place. Her guardian’s home at Frizinghall offered no such associations, but her presence there dampened the spirits of his daughters, the Miss Ablewhites. She requested a delay in visiting. Eventually, old Mr. Ablewhite proposed a furnished house in Brighton. His wife, their invalid daughter, and Rachel would reside there together, with the intention of joining him later in the season. They would avoid society save for a few trusted friends, and Godfrey would visit regularly by train. This constant movement—from one residence to another—this restless body and stagnant soul—I describe only to reach the outcome.

The hiring of the Brighton house was the very reason Rachel and I met again. Aunt Ablewhite, a large, quiet woman with a fair complexion and a singular flaw in character—she has never in her life done anything for herself—played a role in the arrangement. She discovered the house by stopping at a London hotel, lounging on a sofa, and summoning her son. She found servants by breakfasting in bed and instructing her maid to fetch Miss Clack. When I arrived at eleven o’clock, I found her calmly fanning herself in her dressing-gown.

“Drusilla, dear,” she said, “I want some servants. You’re so clever—please get them for me.”

I looked around the cluttered room. Church bells rang in the distance, reminding me of duty. “Oh, aunt,” I said gently, “is this truly how a Christian Englishwoman prepares for eternity?”

She responded simply: “I’ll put on my gown, Drusilla, if you’ll help me.”

There was nothing to say after that. I’ve dealt with murderers before—but never with someone so utterly passive as Aunt Ablewhite.

“Where is the list?” I asked.

She shook her head. “Rachel has it, dear—in the next room.”

I went to the next room—and there stood Rachel for the first time since our parting in Montagu Square. She was small and thin in deep mourning. If I gave weight to appearances, I might note that her complexion suffered without the contrast of white lace near the skin. But what do appearances matter? They are mere distractions on the path to higher things.

To my surprise, she rose and stepped forward, hand outstretched. “I’m glad to see you,” she said. “Drusilla, I’ve spoken foolishly and rudely to you before. I beg your pardon. I hope you’ll forgive me.”

I must have shown my astonishment. She flushed slightly, then continued: “While my mother lived, her friends weren’t always mine. Now that she’s gone, I turn to those she loved for comfort. She liked you. Try to be friends with me, if you can.”

Her reasoning shocked me. Here, in England, a grieving young woman sought solace not in prayer or reflection, but in the company of her mother’s acquaintances. A relative awakened not by conviction, but by sentiment! Deplorable—yet perhaps a sign of change.

I decided to test this transformation. I welcomed her advances warmly and sat beside her on the sofa, as she requested. We spoke of family matters and future plans—except for the one plan that mattered most: her marriage.

No matter how I tried to steer the conversation toward it, she avoided the subject. Yet I had already learned what I needed to know. She was no longer the reckless girl I had known. That alone gave me hope.

I resolved to warn her against rushing into marriage. With renewed purpose, I returned to the matter of servants.

“Where is the list, dear?” I asked.

Rachel produced it. “Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, footman.”

“These are temporary positions,” I said. “We’ll struggle to find trustworthy people in London. Has the house in Brighton been secured?”

“Yes,” she replied. “Godfrey took it. The people there suggested hiring their own staff. He thought they wouldn’t suit us and returned without settling anything.”

“And you have no experience in such matters?”

“None.”

“And Aunt Ablewhite won’t help?”

“No, poor dear. Don’t blame her. I think she’s the happiest woman I’ve ever met.”

“There are degrees of happiness,” I said. “But we’ll talk about that later. I’ll handle the servants. Your aunt will write to the household…”

“She’ll sign whatever I write,” Rachel said. “Which is the same thing.”

“Exactly. I’ll prepare the letter and leave for Brighton tomorrow.”

“How kind of you!” she said. “We’ll join you as soon as you’re ready. And you’ll stay as *my* guest. Brighton is lively—you’ll enjoy it.”

That invitation opened the door to my mission. By Saturday afternoon, the house was ready. In just three days, I had vetted every servant who applied—not just for character, but for faith. I chose those whose beliefs aligned with mine. I also called upon two serious friends in Brighton: a clergyman who agreed to welcome us in his church, and a single woman who lent me her collection of spiritual literature. I selected half-a-dozen books—carefully chosen for Rachel—and placed them where she would likely see them.

Sound doctrine in the servants, sound doctrine in the preacher, sound doctrine on her table—that was the welcome I prepared for the orphaned girl.

On Saturday afternoon, as I waited by the window, a heavenly calm filled my heart. The crowds passed by—how many, I wondered, truly felt the weight of duty as I did? A sobering thought. Let it pass.

Between six and seven, the travelers arrived. To my astonishment, they were accompanied not by Godfrey, but by Mr. Bruff, the lawyer.

“How do you do, Miss Clack?” he said. “I mean to stay this time.”

His words referenced the moment in Montagu Square when I had forced him to delay his business. His presence now meant something. I had built a paradise for Rachel—and here came the serpent.

“Godfrey was upset not to come,” Aunt Ablewhite said. “Something kept him in town. Mr. Bruff volunteered to take his place and stay till Monday.”

She added, pointing to a woman in a wheeled chair: “That’s my idea of exercise. If it’s air you want, you get it in your chair. If it’s fatigue, look at the man.”

Rachel stood silently at the window, gazing at the sea.

“Tired, love?” I asked.

“No. Just a little out of spirits,” she replied. “I’ve seen the sea before, on the Yorkshire coast, with that light. And I was thinking… of the days that can never return.”

Mr. Bruff stayed for dinner and through the evening. The more I observed him, the clearer it became—he had a purpose. He maintained his usual ease, his godless talk, until departure time. As he shook Rachel’s hand, I caught a glimpse of his sharp eyes fixed on her—intently, calculatingly. She was central to his plan.

He invited himself to lunch the next day, then left for his hotel.

The following morning, Aunt Ablewhite remained in her dressing-gown. Her daughter claimed illness—though I suspected laziness inherited from her mother. Rachel and I went to church alone.

My friend preached a powerful sermon on the world’s indifference to small sins. For over an hour, his voice echoed through the sanctuary. Afterward, I asked Rachel: “Has it touched your heart?”

“No,” she said. “It’s given me a headache.”

Some might have been discouraged. Not I. Once committed to a cause, nothing stops me.

At luncheon, Rachel refused food, citing her headache. Mr. Bruff seized the moment.

“There’s only one cure,” he said. “A walk, Miss Rachel. I’m entirely at your service.”

“With pleasure,” she said. “A walk is exactly what I wanted.”

“It’s past two,” I reminded her. “The afternoon service starts at three.”

“How can I go again?” she snapped. “With this headache?”

Mr. Bruff opened the door. In moments, they were gone.

I felt the call to interfere—stronger than ever. But there was nothing to do but wait.

When I returned from church, they had just returned. One glance told me everything: Mr. Bruff had said what he came to say.

Rachel was silent, thoughtful—more so than I’d ever seen her. Mr. Bruff paid her unusual attention, looking at her with respect. He had an engagement for dinner and left early, planning to return to London the next morning.

“Are you sure of your decision?” he asked at the door.

“Quite sure,” she replied—and they parted.

As soon as he was gone, Rachel locked herself in her room. She didn’t appear for dinner. Her maid reported a return of the headache. I rushed upstairs, offering comfort through the door. It was locked. I felt encouraged—there was now material to work with.

The next morning, I followed her tea up. I sat by her bedside and spoke earnestly. She listened politely. I noticed my friend’s books piled in the corner.

“Have you read them?” I asked.

“Yes. But they didn’t interest me.”

“Would you let me read a few passages?”

“No—not now. I have other things to think about.”

Her hands folded and refolded the frills of her nightgown.

Clearly, I needed to appeal to her worldly concerns.

“Do you know,” I said, “I had a strange thought yesterday? I wondered if Mr. Bruff told you something troubling.”

Her fingers dropped. Her dark eyes flashed.

“Quite the opposite,” she said. “It was news I cared about. I owe Mr. Bruff thanks for telling me.”

“Indeed?” I asked gently.

Her hands returned to the frills. She turned away sullenly.

I’d faced this before. It only strengthened my resolve.

Then I took the risk. “News you cared about?” I repeated. “Perhaps it’s about Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

She sat up abruptly, turning pale.

She was about to lash out as she once had—but stopped. She lay back, thought for a moment, then said:

“I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.”

I stared. “What do you mean? The whole family assumes it’s settled!”

“He’s expected today,” she said stubbornly. “Wait until he arrives—and you’ll see.”

“But Rachel—”

She rang the bell. “Penelope! My bath.”

Let me give her credit. At that moment, in my emotional state, I believe she had found the only way to make me leave.

From a worldly perspective, my position seemed compromised. I had planned to guide her through her engagement—to lead her to higher things. Now, if she spoke truth, the marriage would never happen.

Yet—ah, my friends!—a seasoned Christian sees beyond such narrow views. Suppose she broke off the engagement? What then?

It would spark conflict. Accusations. Anguish. And afterward—moral exhaustion. Her pride, her defiance, would collapse under resistance. Then she would seek comfort. And I would be there—ready with words of grace.

Never had the prospect of conversion seemed brighter.

She came down for breakfast, ate nothing, said little. Afterward, she wandered aimlessly—then suddenly played the piano. The music was vulgar, theatrical—revolting to hear.

I waited. I checked the time of Godfrey’s arrival, then left the house.

Alone, I visited my two friends. To speak with serious minds was a rare joy. Refreshed, I returned in time.

I entered the dining room—empty at that hour—and found myself facing Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

He didn’t flee. Instead, he approached eagerly.

“Dear Miss Clack,” he said, “I’ve been waiting to see you! Chance freed me earlier than expected. I arrived sooner than planned.”

Not a trace of embarrassment—despite the scene in Montagu Square. He knew I’d heard of his neglect of charity and duty. Yet here he stood, charming, smiling.

“Have you seen Rachel?” I asked.

He sighed, took my hand.

I would have pulled away—if not for the shock of his reply.

“I’ve seen Rachel,” he said calmly. “You know we were engaged? Well, she’s decided to break it off. Reflection has convinced her it’s best for both of us. She says it was rash. She wants me free to choose a happier match elsewhere. That’s all she’ll say.”

“What have you done?” I asked.

“Submitted,” he answered, unruffled.

I stood stunned, my hand in his.

It was rude to stare. Indelicate. But I did both.

“What does it mean?” I whispered.

“Permit me to explain,” he said. “And suppose we sit down?”

He led me to a chair. I recall being overwhelmed. Perhaps he embraced me—I don’t know. But I was helpless. His manner with women was deeply affecting.

At least, we sat down. That much, I remember.
