Briarmains stood far closer than the Hollow Mill that night, so gruff, warm-hearted Mr. Yorke had hauled his wounded young comrade straight to his own door, laying him in his finest guest bed with the same careful gentleness he would have shown one of his own sons. The sight of bright welling blood oozing from that treacherous, unprovoked blow sealed the tall, proud mill-owner a place right at the core of the Yorkshire gentleman’s affections. To see that straight, stalwart figure which had walked so unyielding across the highroad fall prostrate in the dust, that fine southern brow go deathly pale and slack, that youth in the prime of his strength reduced to a pale, lifeless heap at his feet—this exact set of circumstances was all it took to win Mr. Yorke’s most fierce, unwavering interest.

There was no other hand there to lift him, no other soft voice to ask after his hurts, no other clear head to plan for his care. Mr. Yorke did it all, and the utter, helpless dependence of that bleeding, silent youth on his goodwill tied that loyalty tight as a rope. Mr. Yorke had always loved holding power, and using it well: here he held a fellow creature’s life in his two hands, and it suited him perfectly.

It suited his saturnine, sharp-tongued wife no less. Some women would have screamed and fallen into hysterics to find a gory, half-dead man carried over their threshold at the dead of night, but not Mrs. Yorke. She only ever flew into hysterics when Jessie refused to leave the garden to do her knitting, or when Martin announced he was off to Australia to escape Matthew’s overbearing rules. A near-murder on her own doorstep, a half-murdered man in her best bed? It perked her right up, tied a jaunty little turban-like angle to the edge of her cap, and set her about her work like a heroine. She was exactly the sort of woman who could make a overworked little housemaid’s life a drudging misery one hour, and nurse an entire ward of plague patients through the worst fevers without a flinch the next. She almost loved Robert Moore, tough as her heart was. The second she saw him left in her arms, as small and helpless as her own youngest son in his cradle, she chased Jessie and Rose straight out of the upper floors of the house, forbade all the maids from stepping foot near the sick chamber, and guarded her charge like a ferret guarding its nest. If she caught any of them so much as handing him a sip of water or smoothing his pillow, she would box their ears before they could open their mouths.

Strangely enough, they never would have pitied him at all if he’d been attacked outside the rectory gates and taken in by old Helstone. They’d have called it well-earned payback for his stubborn mill rules and meddling. But as things stood, Robert was for the moment the apple of their eye. Even Louis Moore, his quiet, gentle brother, was allowed to sit for hours on the edge of his bed, holding his hand and pressing soft kisses to his pale forehead, without Mrs. Yorke protesting. She let him sit up all night with Robert once, even got herself up at five o’clock on a drizzly November morning to light the kitchen fire herself, cook the two brothers a hot breakfast, and serve it to them in her giant flannel wrapper and frilled nightcap, watching them eat as complacently as a hen watching her chicks peck at grain. That same day, though, she gave the cook formal warning for sneaking a basin of sago gruel up to the sickroom without her say-so, and stripped the housemaid of all her favours for airing Louis’s overcoat for him when he left, helping him into it, and accepting his thanks and a shilling tip. When two pale, anxious ladies (Caroline and Shirley, of course, whom she’d barred without a second thought) called and begged just for one single minute to see Robert, she turned them straight out the door with more than a few sharp words for their presumption.

Hortense Moore’s arrival was far more welcome. The two women found an endless, endlessly satisfying shared topic in the lazy, scheming propensities of servants, matching each other’s suspicion and severity perfectly, and Hortense never once tried to steal the role of head nurse from Mrs. Yorke. Instead she busied herself patrolling the kitchen, reporting every bit of gossip from the staff, and making herself generally useful, the two of them united in ferociously barring every last visitor from the sickroom, keeping their mill-owner captive so carefully not even a stray gust of cold air could touch him unplanned.

Surgeon Mr. MacTurk, a gruff, no-nonsense man with a short temper, declared the wound dangerous but far from hopeless, and offered to send one of his own trained nurses to sit with Moore. But neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would hear of it, promising to follow every single one of his instructions to the letter. Left to their own devices, though, the bandages got shifted and tampered with somehow, and Robert lost a dangerous amount of blood. MacTurk came galloping over, his horse lathered in foam, his temper frayed to breaking point, and let loose a stream of sharp, blistering complaints that heaped first on his stony young assistant Graves, then on his own son who worked alongside him, before landing full on the two meddling women. He and his two assistants worked straight through most of the winter night, huddled at the bedside on one side, fighting Death itself on the other, the struggle sharp and brutal, lasting until the first pale streaks of dawn broke over the hills.

At sunrise he left Graves and his son to watch the patient, then rode straight out to fetch the best nurse on his entire staff: Mrs. Horsfall. A giant of a woman, broad, strong, and unyielding as a granite boulder, she obeyed MacTurk’s orders to the letter more strictly than she followed the Ten Commandments. The moment she stepped across the threshold of the sickroom, her reign began. Hortense and Mrs. Yorke, both women who thought themselves rather imposing in their own right, were completely cowed by her bulk and sharp eye, and retreated meekly to the back parlour to grieve at being ousted. Mrs. Horsfall sat wherever she pleased, drank her daily dram three times a day, and smoked her pipe of tobacco four, and no one dared step in her way. She turned the six-foot tall, once-powerful Robert Moore in his bed as easily as a nursemaid turns a squalling infant, calling him “honey” when he did as he was told, and shaking him sharp when he fussed or tried to speak over MacTurk’s examinations. When Robert once weakly complained to the surgeon that she was a heavy drinker, MacTurk just laughed and told him not to fuss: drunk or sober, she followed his orders better than any nurse alive.

November dragged on, short, grey, dreary days melting into long dark nights, and Robert Moore lay stuck in that upper bedroom, weak, in constant pain, his only society the three sharp-edged surgeons and his dragon keeper.

Then the fogs of autumn lifted. The rains stopped, the wind swept far off to sigh over other lands, and deep clear winter settled over the hills, sharp with frost and stillness. One calm day bled into a crystalline, glittering evening, the whole world tinted like fragments of gemstone: the hills lilac-blue, the setting sun streaked purple through its red, the sky a sheet of silvered pale ice, the stars when they rose white as clear crystal, no warm gold to their light.

Down in the wood, all its green long faded to soft neutral brown and grey, a small dark blue figure wandered away from the highroad where the rest of the grammar school boys trudged home. It was fifteen-year-old Martin Yorke, sharp, prickly, tall for his age, the sort of boy ushers learned not to push around with severity or flatter with soft words. He pretended to hate all poetry and sentiment, rolling his eyes whenever anyone talked of nature or fairy tales, but he’d snuck away from the group on purpose to sit on a mossy mound at the root of his favourite old oak tree. He pulled a tattered contraband fairy tale book out of his satchel, settled in, and lost himself first in tales of green-robed fairy ladies riding white palfreys through misty mountains, then of foam-white sea nymphs dancing on sharp black reefs out in the tide.

A soft crunch of dead leaves on the path made him jump. He snapped the book shut and stuffed it back in his satchel, watching the trees part to reveal a lady in dark silk, a thin veil pulled over her face. He’d never seen a lady in this wood before. She passed him by without a word, and he was just sneering to himself that it was silly, stuck-up Caroline Helstone, when she stopped, turned back, and lifted her veil to look at his face.

“Are you one of Mr. Yorke’s sons?” she asked, soft and a little shy.

Martin would never have admitted he blushed bright red to the ears, but he did. He told her he was Martin, and the simple, unceremonious way she used his name, no “Master Martin” stuffy formality, no silly babying familiarity, made him soften immediately. She asked after his parents, his sisters, then asked after Hortense, then finally, hesitantly, after Mr. Moore.

Martin thought it would be great fun to test how much she cared, so he told her, deadpan, that Moore was dying, all hope lost, that the meddling women had messed up his bandages and finished him off.

Caroline went deathly pale. Her veil slipped from her fingers, and she stood perfectly still, as if the breath had been knocked clean out of her. Without a goodnight or another word, she turned and started walking slowly away.

The fun died in Martin’s throat immediately. He ran after her, apologizing, spilling that he’d lied, that Robert was eating mutton chops for dinner and arrowroot for supper, that there was always some sweet delicacy simmering on the kitchen fire for him, that he’d even eaten half a basin of the sago gruel once. Caroline was so shaken she could barely stand, leaning against a shivering frost-filigreed tree, trembling, and Martin’s sharp, urchin heart felt a strange twinge, like the time he’d found a blackbird crying over her nest Matthew had smashed. He offered to guide her out of the wood, leading her through a messy little short cut that made him help her climb over thorny hedges and low walls, grumbling about how helpless she was the whole time, secretly giddy to feel useful.

Before they parted at the Briarmains gate, Caroline made him promise on his honour that Robert was really recovering. Martin, feeling suddenly protective of her, promised he would sneak past Mrs. Horsfall the dragon nurse tomorrow, get a proper look at Robert himself, and meet her right here in the wood at the same hour the next evening to bring her news, so she wouldn’t have to keep walking around with that heavy, worried weight on her chest. He walked her all the way back to her cottage, ignoring the fact he’d be late and get nothing but dry bread and a long lecture for tea. The adventure was far better than muffins by the fire.

When he got home, the scolding and dry tea were duly doled out, and he was sent up to bed early. Before he climbed the stairs, he ducked into the quiet, unused formal dining room, held his candle up to the two female portraits hanging above the mantel, one soft and happy, the other sad and forlorn, and nodded at the latter.

“That’s exactly what she looked like,” he murmured to himself, thinking of Caroline leaning white and shaking against the tree, “when she thought he was dead.”
