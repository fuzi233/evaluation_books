The heat of July and August pressed down upon the Salcombe estuary, but for Ethne, the air inside the house felt stifling. Her anxieties, previously a quiet hum beneath the surface, had swelled until they forced their way onto her lips. One morning, standing at the open window of Mrs. Adair's cottage, bathed in the harsh sunlight, she voiced her fear.

"I am afraid," she said, her gaze fixed on the stone-flagged terrace below.

Behind her, Mrs. Adair sat quietly, a book open in her lap, smiling with a serene indifference that Ethne found grating.

"Afraid?" Mrs. Adair asked, her tone light. "That some accident befell Colonel Durrance in London yesterday?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly, turning her eyes downward. "Not that. Because... because he is crossing the lawn towards us now."

Below, on the grass, the figure of the colonel emerged. To Ethne, he was a silhouette of uncertainty, moving with a cane that tapped rhythmically against the earth, measuring distances she could not see.

Mrs. Adair's smile deepened, though she did not look up from her page. "I thought so," she murmured, her voice barely audible.

Ethne spun around, the sudden movement jerking her spine. "Laura, have you considered..." She stopped, searching for the words, feeling the weight of a secret pressing against her chest. "Do you truly believe he has any hope of regaining his sight?"

The question caught Mrs. Adair off guard. She closed her book, the leather cover snapping shut with a finality that silenced the room. "If he traveled to town solely to consult an oculist, surely he must hope," she reasoned coolly. "Is it not natural?"

"I am afraid," Ethne whispered, stepping away from the window and onto the terrace. "Have you not noticed? Look at him. How quickly he grows, how rapidly he adapts." She pointed a trembling finger toward the man approaching them. "He interprets your silences before you utter them. He fills out your unfinished sentences before you finish them. He reads me, Laura—read me like a child's primer."

"And you?" Mrs. Adair asked softly. "Do you read him?"

"And his eyes no longer tell me anything," Ethne confessed, the bitterness rising in her throat. "Before, when he could see me, his gaze was a shield. Now, his blindness has stripped away the barrier. He sees everything I try to hide, and I cannot see past his silence."

Mrs. Adair studied Ethne, her expression shifting from amusement to a calculating intensity. She saw the tremor in the girl's hands, the agitation that had replaced her former quiet frankness. "There is something," Mrs. Adair stated quietly, "that you intend to conceal from him?"

"Yes."

"Something significant?"

"At all costs," Ethne gasped, the confession tearing from her before she could check it. As the words left her mouth, a chill ran through her. *Could he already know?* She stepped further out onto the terrace, looking past the hedge toward the house where the blind man lived. Smoke curled from the chimneys, drifting lazily into the sky. Beyond lay the creek, where a small sailing-boat tacked slowly against the wind.

Ethne watched the boat, trying to compose herself, trying to summon the strength to face the man walking steadily toward her. Down below, on the lawn, Durrance moved with a strange, fluid grace. His cane swept the grass, not for guidance, but as a habit, a rhythmic punctuation to his steps. He did not hesitate; he knew exactly where he was going.

As Ethne prepared to descend the steps, Mrs. Adair dropped her book. The cover hit the floor with a dull thud. She moved to the window, pulling the heavy drapes aside just enough to peek out. Her face, usually composed, was drawn tight with a hungry, fierce light.

*"At all costs she must conceal,"* Mrs. Adair whispered to herself, a cruel smile playing on her lips. *"She shrinks from this blind man, terrified of the restraint marriage would impose. And I? I am glad."* The jealousy burned hot within her. Blindness, she realized with a thrill, had altered everything. It had bound the man to her care, to her influence. Every word he spoke, every step he took, depended on her interpretation. It was a power she had craved for years.

Down on the lawn, Ethne met the colonel. She walked slowly, as if approaching a battlefield.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" she asked immediately, her voice tight.

Durrance shrugged, a casual gesture that belied the gravity of his condition. "Time alone will reveal if a cure is possible," he said.

"But must *we* wait?" Ethne pressed, leaning forward, her eyes searching his pale, smooth face. "Must we simply wait?"

"Surely," he agreed gently. "Prudence dictates it." Then, unexpectedly, he turned to her. "Was it not Mrs. Adair who arranged this return to Guessens, suggesting you remain here across the fields?"

Ethne was taken aback. "Yes," she admitted, the plea ringing in her voice. "I was so distressed by your accident, Laura and I devised this plan. I wanted you home, safe."

"I know," Durrance said, a soft smile touching his lips. "But it is hard on you, Ethne. You belong elsewhere. I recall a sentence Harry Feversham spoke many years ago, before I left for Egypt. He said of you: 'She is of her country, and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy anywhere outside the reach of Donegal.'" He paused, his voice dropping slightly. "To keep you here, against your nature, seems selfish."

"I wasn't thinking of that!" Ethne cried, the words spilling out in a rush. "I was asking why you insist on waiting. Of course, we pray for your sight, but does a cure really matter? Does it change anything between us?"

She repeated the phrase, emphasizing it: "It makes no difference."

Suddenly, Durrance straightened, as if waking from a deep trance. "I beg your pardon, Ethne. I was lost in thought regarding Harry Feversham. There is something I must ask. Years ago, at Glenalla, you promised you might one day tell me what happened. What transpired that night at Lennon House? Why did you break off our engagement, sending him away an outcast?"

Ethne fell silent. The air around them seemed to grow heavy. "I would rather not," she said finally, her voice gentle but firm. "It is finished. I do not wish to speak of it."

Durrance nodded understandingly. "Very well. I will not press. Perhaps it hurts you to recall."

"It is not the pain," Ethne explained earnestly. "Mr. Feversham is a man I have known well, and now he is dead. Speaking of him feels like disturbing a grave."

They walked together toward the gap in the tree line that overlooked the creek. As Ethne spoke, raising her eyes from the grass, she saw it. The small boat had grounded near the shore. The sail hung limp, the mast propped precariously high against the bank. Standing there, staring vaguely at the house with a confused expression, was a man.

"A stranger," Ethne announced, seizing the distraction as a lifeline. "He appears lost. I shall guide him."

She rushed forward, leaving the colonel on the path. The stranger approached as she neared. He was middle-sized, with a short, blunt nose, a pair of vacant, protruding brown eyes, and a moustache that seemed to curl with aggression. He removed his hat, revealing a high, balding forehead.

"I sailed down from Kingsbridge," he said, his voice rough. "Never been this far before. Is this residence called The Pool?"

"Yes," Ethne replied, her heart hammering against her ribs. "Go up the steps to the terrace for Mrs. Adair."

"I sought Miss Eustace."

Ethne turned, surprised. "I am Miss Eustace."

The stranger regarded her silently, his eyes unwavering. "So I surmised." He twisted one of his moustaches, then the other. "Finding you proved difficult. I journeyed all the way to Glenalla—for naught. A waste of a man's limited leave."

"I apologize," Ethne said, forcing a smile. "Why did you trouble yourself?"

"You have likely forgotten my name by now," he observed. "Though I suspect you have not. Five years ago, you heard it."

Ethne froze. "Captain Willoughby?"

The color drained from her cheeks, leaving her skin stark and pale. Her lips tightened into a hard line, and her eyes hardened with a cold, defensive glare.

Captain Willoughby was unfazed. He spoke with the slow deliberation of a man granting forgiveness. "I comprehend your displeasure, Miss Eustace. None of us anticipated you would be present when the three white feathers arrived at Mr. Feversham's hands."

"The three feathers?" Ethne demanded, sweeping the remark aside. "How do you know I was there?"

"Mr. Feversham informed me."
