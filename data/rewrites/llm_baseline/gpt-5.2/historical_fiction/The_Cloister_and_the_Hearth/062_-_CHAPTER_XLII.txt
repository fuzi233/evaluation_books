Gerard came back to himself as a man comes back to a burnt house—alive, and therefore capable of counting what is gone.

The second day his mind took fire. Fever rattled in his skull; his words ran off like scalding broth that will not be held in the pot. Close at hand, on the table, lay the spoil of him: his rich auburn hair, shorn clean, long as a woman’s—coiled like a rope of bright cinnamon on a trencher, too beautiful to be only waste. From hour to hour the signs thickened; one peril succeeded another as in a kitchen where the fat has caught and the flame hunts the rafters.

On the fifth day the leech—learned, grave, and weary of trying to rescue what seemed already over-boiled—stood back, wiped his hands, and gave him up. At sunset, when the room had that coppery light which makes even clean linen look stained, Gerard fell into a sleep so deep that it frightened those who had watched him struggle.

Some said he would wake only to die. But an old gossip, whose word carried weight because she had been paid for her nursing in better houses than this, listened to his breathing as a cook listens for the first steady murmur of a pot that has settled. “His youth may save him yet,” she said, “if he sleep twelve hours.”

At that, his old landlady, who had fed more lodgers than she could count and knew the comfort that comes from simple routines—hearth, bowl, bed—cleared the room and sat alone to keep the vigil. She vowed a wax candle to the Virgin for every hour he should sleep. She marked the time by the sounds more than by any clock: the creak of a stair, the clack of a shutter in the wind, the far-off cry of a hawker, and, beneath all, that one precious, even draught of breath.

He slept twelve hours.

The good soul dropped to her knees and thanked the Virgin with her face in her apron, as if it were a clean cloth laid over a loaf.

He slept twenty-four.

Her joy began to sour into a worried wonder, as milk will turn if you cannot keep it cool.

At the thirtieth hour she could bear it no longer and sent for the woman of art. “Thirty hours!” she whispered. “Shall we wake him?”

The other came in, bent over him, and watched long—watching not only his face but the small signs that women learn without books: the moisture of the palm, the looseness of the jaw, the way the ribs lift, the steadiness between one breath and the next, like the hush between two phrases of a chant.

“His breath is even,” said she at last, “his hand moist. I know there be learned leeches would wake him to look at his tongue, and be none the wiser; but we that be women should have the sense to let bon Nature alone. When did sleep ever harm the racked brain or the torn heart?”

So they let him lie.

When he had been eight-and-forty hours asleep it got wind, as all strange things do in a street of close doors and quick ears. Curious folk pressed on the stair; they had much ado to keep them out. But they admitted only Fra Colonna, and with him the gigantic Fra Jerome.

These two relieved the women and sat silent. Fra Colonna’s eyes watered as he watched, the tears slipping down as quietly as oil on warm bread. Fra Jerome fingered his beads, and looked on as calmly as he had looked upon the sea when he and Gerard had met it hand to hand—calm, because his calm was a kind of iron.

The house itself listened. Even the hearth, usually snapping and gossiping with its own small noises, seemed chastened; and the street sounds came softened, as if the air had been thickened with wool.

At last—about the sixtieth hour of that strange sleep—the landlady touched Fra Colonna with her elbow.

He looked.

Gerard opened his eyes as gently as if he had been only dozing after a long meal. He stared, not knowing at first what he saw. He drew himself a little higher in the bed. His hand went to his head—habit, hunger, the old gesture of the living—and met the nakedness there. He found his hair gone.

He saw Colonna and smiled—an honest flash, like sunshine finding a crack in a shutter.

But in the midst of smiling his face stopped, as if some hand had dashed cold water into it. The features convulsed in a moment with anguish unspeakable. A cry tore out of him, loud and raw, and he turned his face to the wall.

His landlady wept at that; she had known what it is to wake bereaved, when the room is the same and yet the world is not. Fra Jerome began to recite canticles and prayers from his breviary, the Latin falling into the chamber like measured drops—sound meant to soothe, meant to bind a mind back into obedience.

Gerard rolled himself hard into the bedclothes as if he would smother memory.

Fra Colonna went to him, and, whimpering with grief that was half selfish—because he loved the boy and also loved his hopes—reminded him that all was not lost. The divine Muses were immortal. He must transfer his affection to them; they would never betray him nor fail him like creatures of clay.

Then the good, simple father hurried away; his own heart could not keep its footing on so steep a sorrow.

Fra Jerome remained.

“Young man,” said he, standing as if he were already at the door of a tribunal, “the Muses exist but in the brains of pagans and visionaries. The Church alone gives repose to the heart on earth, and happiness to the soul hereafter. Hath earth deceived thee, hath passion broken thy heart after tearing it, the Church opens her arms: consecrate thy gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind.”

He spoke solemnly, as if the very cadence of his voice were a chain meant to be felt; and as soon as the words were uttered he was gone, leaving behind him the echo of his own authority.

“The Church!” cried Gerard.

He sprang up with a sudden fury that made his weak limbs seem for an instant strong. He shook his fist after the departing friar.

“Malediction on the Church! But for the Church I should not lie broken here, and she lie cold, cold, cold, in Holland. Oh, my Margaret! oh, my darling! my darling! And I must run from thee the few months thou hadst to live. Cruel! cruel! The monsters, they let her die. Death comes not without some signs. These the blind selfish wretches saw not, or recked not; but I had seen them, I that love her. Oh, had I been there, I had saved her, I had saved her. Idiot! idiot! to leave her for a moment.”

The words poured out of him with the bitter salt of tears. He wept long—deep, shaking sobs, the kind that leave a man as empty as a scraped pot.

Then rage took him again, sharp and sudden.

“The Church! for whose sake I was driven from her; my malison be on the Church! and the hypocrites that name it to my broken heart. Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives; Margaret dies. Thieves, murderers, harlots, live for ever. Only angels die. Curse life! curse death! and whosoever made them what they are!”

Fra Jerome did not hear the blasphemies—only the howl of rage flung after him like a stone. It was well. Had he caught the words, he would have had his late shipmate burned in the forum with as little hesitation as he would have roasted a kid and called it duty.

The landlady, who had gone down the stair with Fra Colonna, heard the raised voice and turned back, her fear running ahead of her feet. She found Gerard dressing—his hands clumsy with weakness, his cheeks wet, his eyes bright and terrible. He had chosen his best clothes, as if for a feast-day, though his flesh looked ashy and drawn, and the cap he set on his head sat too jauntily for such a face.

She caught at him with words, as women do—trying to fold a man back into the small mercies of order. She remonstrated, begged, commanded.

“What avails my lying here?” he said gloomily, fastening a point with fingers that trembled. “Can I find here that which I seek?”

“Saints preserve us!” she cried. “Is he distraught again? What seek ye?”

“Oblivion.”

“Oblivion, my little heart? Oh, but y’are young to talk so.”

“Young or old, what else have I to live for?”

He smoothed his doublet, took his belt, made himself ready as if he were going into the street to buy spice or wine, though he was going to purchase nothing that can be weighed.

The good dame tried once more, clutching at a practical thing, the way one clings to a ladle when the kitchen tilts.

“My pretty Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday.”

“Tuesday is it?” he muttered, as if time had become a foreign tongue in his mouth. “I thought it had been Saturday.”

“Nay, thou hast slept long. Thou never wearest thy brave clothes on working days. Consider.”

“What I did, when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever erst I did not. The past is the past. There lies my hair, and with it my way of life. I have served one Master as well as I could. You see my reward. Now I’ll serve another, and give him a fair trial too.”

“Alas!” sighed the woman, turning pale, for she heard in his voice a resolve colder than any winter draft. “What mean these dark words? and what new master is this whose service thou wouldst try?”

“SATAN.”

And with that horrible declaration on his lips, the miserable creature walked out—cap and feather set on one side as if to mock his own ruin; limbs feeble, face sinister and pale as ashes, all drawn down as if by age—leaving behind him the close room, the muttered prayers, the coiled hair on the table, and the hearth that crackled on, indifferent, while the house listened to his retreating step on the stair.
