To unravel the labyrinth of the diamond's disappearance, one must first navigate the geography of secrets, beginning with the shrubbery path—a verdant artery leading from my lady's sitting room to the gardens. This was Mr. Franklin's sanctuary, a private realm where he retreated when the weight of the household pressed upon him. Yet, even within this secluded corridor, the shadow of suspicion fell thickly, binding us together in a silent conspiracy of observation.

I confess, with a heavy heart, that I am a man of stubborn convictions. When Sergeant Cuff erected mental barriers, shutting his thoughts from my prying gaze, I found myself compelled to breach them anyway. As we turned onto the path, I attempted a different strategy, voicing my own despair to match his stoicism.

"As things stand," I ventured, "if I were in your shoes, I should be at my wits' end."

"If you were in my position," the Sergeant replied, his voice steady as iron, "you would have already formed a conclusion. And now, with the facts laid bare, any lingering doubt in your mind regarding your own deductions would vanish entirely. Set aside for the moment what those conclusions may be, Mr. Betteredge. I have not summoned you here to drag me through the mud like a badger; I have brought you forth to solicit information. You could have offered it within the house, yet doors and eavesdroppers possess a peculiar affinity for gathering in clusters. In my profession, we cultivate a preference for the open air, where truths are harder to hide."

Who could withstand such a man? I yielded, waiting with bated breath for his next move.

"We shall not dissect the motives of your young lady," the Sergeant continued, his tone devoid of malice but heavy with implication. "Let us simply acknowledge that her refusal to assist renders this inquiry more arduous than necessity demands. We must now attempt to solve the enigma of the smear upon the door—which, trust me, is synonymous with the mystery of the Diamond itself—through alternative means. I intend to interrogate the servants, to probe their minds and scrutinize their actions, Mr. Betteredge, rather than rummaging through their wardrobes. But before I commence, I require answers. You are an astute observer: did you detect any anomaly among the staff following the discovery of the theft? Allow for the natural disarray of fear and panic, of course. Was there a specific discord? A servant unusually despondent? Or perhaps someone unexpectedly irritable, or afflicted by an unexplained ailment?"

I barely had time to recall Rosanna Spearman's sudden collapse at yesterday's dinner before I registered the shift in the Sergeant's demeanor. His eyes darted sharply toward the shrubbery, and I heard him murmur softly, "Hullo!"

"What is the matter?" I asked.

"A touch of rheumatism in my back," he announced loudly, seemingly inviting a third party to witness his discomfort. "The weather promises a change soon."

A few paces further led us to the corner of the house. Turning abruptly to the right, we ascended the terrace and descended the central steps into the garden below. Here, in the open expanse, we stood with visibility in every direction.

"Regarding that young woman, Rosanna Spearman," the Sergeant said, his voice cutting through the summer air. "Given her appearance, it is unlikely she harbors a lover. Nevertheless, for her own sake, I must inquire immediately: has she, poor wretch, secured a sweetheart like the others?"

What possible intent lay behind such a question under the current circumstances? I stared at him, speechless.

"I observed Rosanna Spearman concealing herself in the shrubbery as we passed," the Sergeant stated flatly.

"When you uttered 'Hullo'?"

"Precisely. If a paramour exists, her concealment matters little. But if none exists—as appears to be the case within this household—her hiding becomes a profoundly suspicious circumstance, and it shall be my painful duty to act accordingly."

What divine mandate allowed me to speak? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin's favored promenade; I knew he would inevitably turn that way upon his return from the station; I knew Penelope had repeatedly caught her fellow-servant lingering there, declaring to me that Rosanna sought only to capture Mr. Franklin's attention. If Penelope spoke the truth, the girl might well have been awaiting his return when the Sergeant spotted her. Trapped between the dilemma of attributing Penelope's fanciful speculation to myself or allowing an innocent creature to suffer the grave consequences of arousing the Sergeant's suspicion, I acted solely out of pity. On my soul and reputation, driven purely by compassion for the girl, I supplied the necessary explanation, revealing that Rosanna had foolishly set her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff never laughed. On the rare occasions amusement touched him, merely the corners of his lips curled upward. Now, they did curl.

"Would it not be wiser to suggest she is mad enough to be an unsightly girl and merely a servant?" he asked. "Falling in love with a gentleman of Mr. Franklin Blake's bearing and stature does not appear to me to be the most irrational aspect of her conduct. Regardless, I am glad the matter is clarified; clarity brings peace of mind. Yes, I shall keep this confidential, Mr. Betteredge. I delight in tenderness toward human frailty, though opportunities to exercise such virtue are scarce in my line of work. Do you suppose Mr. Franklin Blake remains unaware of the girl's affection? Ah! He would have discerned it swiftly had she been beautiful. Ugly women endure a harsh existence in this world; let us hope it is compensated for in the next. You possess a lovely garden here, with a meticulously maintained lawn. Observe how superior the flowers appear amidst grass rather than gravel. No, thank you. I shall not accept a rose; it breaks my heart to sever it from the stem. Just as it breaks yours, you know, when discord reigns in the servants' hall. Did you notice anything inexplicable among the staff when the loss of the Diamond was first revealed?"

Thus far, I had navigated reasonably well with Sergeant Cuff. Yet, the cunning subtlety with which he reintroduced that final query placed me on high alert. In plain terms, I found the prospect of aiding his investigations repugnant, especially when his inquiries threatened to ensnare my fellow-servants like snakes in the grass.

"I perceived nothing," I replied, "save that we collectively lost our composure, myself included."

"Oh," the Sergeant remarked, "is that all you have to report?"

"That is all," I answered, feigning an impassive countenance.

Sergeant Cuff's somber eyes fixed upon me with intense scrutiny.

"Mr. Betteredge," he said, "do you object to obliging me by shaking hands? I have developed an extraordinary fondness for you."

(The timing of this gesture, coinciding precisely with my deception, defies comprehension! I felt a surge of pride—a genuine sense of triumph in having bested the celebrated Cuff!)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant requested a solitary chamber, instructing me subsequently to summon the domestic servants, one by one, in descending order of rank.

I ushered Sergeant Cuff into my room and then gathered the servants in the hall. Rosanna Spearman appeared among them, indistinguishable from her usual self. She moved with the same rapidity as the Sergeant, and I suspected she had overheard his remarks concerning the servants prior to his discovery of her. Yet, there she stood, appearing utterly oblivious to the concept of the shrubbery.

One by one, I sent them into the Court of Justice, as ordered. The cook entered first. Her stay was brief. Upon exiting, her report was: "Sergeant Cuff is depressed in spirit, yet he remains a perfect gentleman." My lady's maid followed. She lingered considerably longer. Her exit report was scathing: "If Sergeant Cuff cannot believe a respectable woman, he should keep his opinions to himself!" Penelope entered next, remaining only moments. Her verdict: "Sergeant Cuff deserves pity. He must have been jilted in his youth." The first housemaid followed, staying as long as my lady's maid. Her declaration was fiery: "I did not enter my lady's service to be doubted in the face of a low police officer!" Then came Rosanna Spearman. She stayed longer than anyone else. There was no report upon her departure—only dead silence and lips pale as ash. Samuel, the footman, followed, remaining briefly. His comment: "Whoever polishes Sergeant Cuff's boots ought to be ashamed of themselves." Finally, Nancy, the kitchen-maid, entered. She stayed a short while. Her testimony: "Sergeant Cuff has a heart; he does not jest with a poor, hardworking girl."

Upon re-entering the Court of Justice to await further instructions, I found the Sergeant engaged in his customary pastime: gazing out the window and humming "The Last Rose of Summer."

"Any discoveries, sir?" I inquired.

"If Rosanna Spearman requests permission to leave," the Sergeant said, "grant it to the poor soul, but inform me beforehand."

Silence hung heavy in the room. It was futile to remain quiet about Rosanna and Mr. Franklin! The tragedy was evident; despite my efforts, the unfortunate girl had fallen under the Sergeant's suspicion.

"Do you not believe Rosanna is implicated in the loss of the Diamond?" I dared to ask.
