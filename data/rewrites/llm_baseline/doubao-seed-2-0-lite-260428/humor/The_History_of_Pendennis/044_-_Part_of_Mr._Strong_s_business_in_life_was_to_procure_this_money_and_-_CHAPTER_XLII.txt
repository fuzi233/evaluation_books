You may recall that after Pen crashed out of Oxbridge and moped around his hometown for months, chasing two very unimpressed ladies and generally wallowing in teen angst, he spent most of that time scribbling a very dramatic, very gloomy full-length novel. The thing reeked of unfiltered Byronic brooding, Werther-level suicidal despair, and Mephistopheles-adjacent edgy snark, because Pen had just picked up German and was copying his favourite poets as diligently as every overzealous clever 20-year-old with a notebook and a grudge against the universe tends to do.

He’d dripped actual tears on half the pages back then, smudging ink all over his dramatic little lines about heartbreak and cosmic unfairness. A few years on, if he picked up those old beloved poetry books he’d annotated to death, he couldn’t even summon that same over-the-top emotion — the fountain of teenage soppiness had dried up completely, like a waterhole in the desert that only leaves camel hoof prints and dead grass as proof it ever existed.

He made that very silly desert simile one morning to Warrington, who was slouched over his pipe, while Pen gesticulated so hard he slammed his decades-old manuscript down on the table hard enough to rattle the tea set and make the thin blue skim milk in the jug do a little impromptu jig. He’d fished the thing out the night before from a long-forgotten chest full of junk: ratty old shooting jackets, his crumpled Oxbridge exam scribble books, the surplice he’d worn for the mandatory chapel services, his dented graduation cap and gown, and all sorts of other embarrassing mementos of youth. He’d read half the novel in bed before falling asleep, tired out from a London party, and the first half was so boring it had actually lulled him to sleep.

“Good grief,” Pen laughed bitterly, prodding the stack of pages, “I can’t believe I wrote this just a couple years ago. I was fully convinced I’d be in love with that little minx Miss Amory forever back then! I used to hide my stupid little poems for her in a hollow tree, and dedicate them *Amori* — get it, a pun on her name? Fancy scholarship, right?”

“Clever little wordplay,” Warrington puffed around his pipe, then snatched the manuscript right out from under Pen’s nose with the fire tongs he was using to tuck a new coal into his bowl. “Let me read a bit of this rubbish.”

“‘False as thou art beautiful! Heartless as thou art fair! Mockery of Passion!’ Walter Lorraine shouts at Leonora,” Warrington boomed, barely letting Pen lunge for the book, “‘what evil spirit sent thee to torture me so? O Leonora —’”

“STOP,” Pen yelped, making a wild grab for the pages that did not work. “Don’t read that out loud! That bit’s about my other old flame, the one who’s Lady Mirabel now. I saw her last night at Lady Whiston’s party, she invited me over to her place, said we old friends should catch up more. Funny how she never spoke to me for two whole years until she saw Wenham chatting to me, and that fancy French literary guy Dubois, who was wearing so many medals he looked like he could pass for a French Marshal, all of a sudden I’m good enough for her guest list. The Claverings are going to be there too — won’t it be a riot to meet both my old crushes at the same dinner table?”

“Two flames? More like two piles of completely burnt-out ash,” Warrington snickered. “Are both of those beauties in this book of yours?”

“Sort of,” Pen admitted. “Leonora, the one who marries the duke? That’s the Fotheringay. The duke is based on my old Oxford mate Magnus Charters, a little bit, and Miss Amory is Neaera. Man, I thought of the Fotheringay the whole walk home last night in the moonlight, all those old dumb memories felt like they happened yesterday. So I dug this out, and — don’t laugh — it’s actually got some good bits! If Bungay won’t print it, I’m sure Bacon will.”

“Classic poets,” Warrington said, rolling his eyes. “Fall in love, get dumped or do the dumping, suffer a little, scream that no one has ever suffered this much in human history, then write it all down and hawk it for cash. All poets are humbugs, every last one of ’em. The second you start selling your feelings like groceries, you’re a humbug. If a poet gets a stomach ache from eating too fancy a dinner, they wail *Ai! Ai!* louder than Prometheus chained to his rock.”

“Poets just feel things sharper than other people, that’s what makes them poets!” Pen huffed, indignant. “You get all fired up writing your newspaper editorials when you catch a politician lying, right? Paley will ramble for an hour straight about a boring legal technicality he finds interesting. Why do you get to use your special brain skill for cash, but a poet can’t do the exact same with his? It’s not like money makes you spot a bad argument, it’s just the thing you trade the skill for. A painter paints a face on canvas to sell, why can’t I put my feelings on paper and do the same? If I ever think I’m good enough for an epic poem, I’ll write it. If I’m only good enough for silly jokes and stories, I’ll do that too.”

“Not bad for a speech, kid,” Warrington said, “but that still doesn’t stop all poets from being humbugs.”

“What — Homer, Aeschylus, Shakespeare? All of them?”

“Those legends are on a whole different level from you little amateurs,” Warrington scoffed. “There are great men, and then there’s the rest of us chumps.”

“Hey, Shakespeare wrote plays for cash just the same as you and me,” Pen shot back, which earned him a withering look from Warrington before the older man went back to flipping through the manuscript.

It was very obvious the whole book was 90% Pen’s own messy personal drama, the exact same heartbreaks and silly mistakes we already saw back in the first half of his life, so we won’t bore you by dumping too many excerpts from it. Pen hadn’t hidden it in his storage chest all that time out of some fancy Horatian desire to let it “ripen” like a fine wine, either — he’d just forgotten where else to put the thing, and didn’t want to look at his own teenage cringe. Anyone who hides a manuscript away for ten years hoping it’ll get better is in for a rude awakening when they dig it back up and find it’s turned as flavourless as cheap white wine that went bad.

To his credit, Pen never thought he’d written a masterpiece. When he re-read it now, he was very aware of all its flaws, and pretty humble about the bits that worked. It wasn’t great, but it was just as good as all the other trashy fashionable novels flying off circulating library shelves that season, and he knew he could write English just as well as those popular authors. Even Warrington, the grumpiest critic alive who’d gotten through two full pipes of tobacco while reading it, had to admit it wasn’t all garbage.

“I can’t read any more of this balderdash right now,” he said, yawning so wide his jaw ached, “but there’s some good stuff here, Pen. It’s got that fresh, green, unselfconscious spark that vanishes the second you start shaving. You can’t get that rosy, unforced sincerity back later, no matter how hard you try. You’ll end up primping your whiskers with curling irons and macassar oil and all that nonsense, looking all fancy and polished, but the springtime of your writing days is the best part.”

“What the hell do my whiskers have to do with anything?” Pen spluttered, very offended, because he did in fact spend an absurd amount of time curling, oiling, perfuming and fussing over his facial hair like it was a prize houseplant. “Do you think we can sell this thing? Or should we burn it in a little dramatic auto-da-fe?”

“Burning it would be fun just to punish you for being such a little humbug,” Warrington grinned, “but I’m not dumb. You love this thing way too much to actually hurt it.”

“Watch this!” Pen yelled, and tossed the whole stack of pages straight onto the coals in the hearth. But the fire had burned out ages ago after boiling their breakfast kettle, and Pen had snuck a quick glance to confirm it was totally cold before he threw the manuscript over. Warrington fished it right back out with the tongs, cackling so hard tears ran down his face.

“Pen, you are the clumsiest humbug I’ve ever met,” he wheezed. “Fine, we won’t burn the lad. We’ll take him to the publishers, sell him for cold hard cash, enough to buy beef, beer, tobacco, new shirts, the whole works. It’s a silly little book, but it’ll sell. My advice is, next time you go home on holiday, tuck Walter Lorraine in your bag, touch up the old cringey bits a tiny bit, add some comedy, some jokes, some light satire, and we’ll hawk it to Bacon or Bungay for a solid hundred quid. It’s not a masterpiece, but it’ll do.”

Pen lit up like a christmas tree — praise from cynical Warrington was higher praise than any literary award. “You silly little fool, I think it’s actually uncommonly clever,” Warrington said, softening up, and tapped Pen on the cheek playfully with the manuscript. Pen blushed so bright red his old teenage flush was back, and squeezed Warrington’s hand so tight he almost crushed his fingers, before he ran off to his bedroom to spend the whole rest of the day re-editing the book, adding bits, cutting bits, shaping it into the story you’ve almost certainly read as a respectable novel fan already.

While Pen was hard at work, Warrington pulled all the strings he could, hyping up Walter Lorraine to the two professional readers who worked for both Bungay and Bacon, telling them Pen was a total society insider who got invited to all the fanciest houses in London. He even roped silly friendly Percy Popjoy into talking to Mrs Bungay, telling her the book was going to be the biggest smash of the season, full of wit, pathos, satire, everything the public went crazy for. Bungay, who didn’t know a good novel from a Hebrew textbook, didn’t care what was in the book at all, but he trusted his wife’s opinion, and immediately invited Pen and Warrington over for dinner to negotiate.

Bacon, the rival publisher, got wind of this, panicked, and decided he wanted to outbid Bungay immediately. He sent his top man Hack to snoop around, and the next thing you know, Bacon himself is huffing and puffing his way up three flights of rickety stairs to their apartment in Lamb Court.

To call their digs un-fancy would be an understatement. The ragged carpet had gotten way more ragged over the two years they’d lived there, the whole place smelled permanently of tobacco smoke, Bacon tripped over the laundress’s buckets left in the hallway, Warrington’s shooting jacket had elbows more tattered than an old rag, and the second Bacon sat down in the chair they offered him, the whole thing collapsed under his weight. Warrington cackled so hard he dropped his pipe ash in his tea, teased Bacon that if he liked their “elegant” furniture, he could buy the whole lot for his wife’s drawing room. Bacon, who knew Warrington was a huge practical joker, later told his staff “I can never tell if that guy is messing with me or being serious.”

Bacon was this close to deciding they were both total impostors, until he glanced down at the breakfast table and saw that morning’s post: a fancy embossed invitation for Pen from the Marchioness of Steyne, another invite to a fancy dance at a duchess’s house, all super high society people. Warrington saw him staring. “Ah, Pen’s the most laid-back posh guy you’ll ever meet,” he said, deadpan. “He dines with all the lords and ladies in London, but he’s just as happy eating a mutton chop with us common blokes. Refreshing, right? He could afford a mansion in Mayfair if he wanted, but he likes slumming it here with me in our rickety old rooms.”

“Bet he doesn’t pay much rent here,” Bacon thought to himself, already salivating at the idea of a bestseller by a real live society author.

Then he got right down to business: begged to see the manuscript, offered a huge advance, said his publishing house was the most generous in London. Pen put on a very cool unbothered act, said he was already negotiating with Bungay and couldn’t promise anything, which made Bacon throw out such absurdly generous offers Pen was convinced he’d just stumbled into the land of El Dorado and made his entire fortune in one morning.

I’m not going to tell you exactly how much he got for the first edition, okay? I don’t want every 19-year-old kid with a notebook to quit their perfectly boring stable job and try to write novels for a living. Half of all novels never get published, most of the ones that do flop, Pen got absurdly lucky, don’t risk your rent money on it. That said, the sum was big enough that Pen immediately started daydreaming about opening a bank account, buying a horse and cab, moving to a fancy first-floor apartment in Lamb Court, or even relocating all the way to the fancy west end.

Uncle Major Pendennis nearly had a heart attack when Pen told him the news. He could not for the life of him figure out who on earth read these stupid novels — he only ever read Paul de Kock, the one author that made him laugh, hadn’t touched a novel in 30 years. “Gad, Pen’s the luckiest lad alive,” he thought. “He could knock one of these out in a month, that’s 12 a year, make a huge fortune in 5 years. He needs to move to a proper fancy apartment, buy a brougham, start living like a gentleman.”

Pen laughed and told Warrington about his uncle’s very dumb plan, but he had way better sense than that. He wrote a nice, sincere letter straight to Laura, enclosing almost every penny he owed her, paying off almost his entire debt. When the widow Pendennis and Dr Portman read that honest, grateful letter, where Pen thanked heaven for giving him such good friends who supported him when he was broke and miserable, old Dr Portman got so teary his voice cracked, his glasses fogged up, and before he knew what he was doing he was holding Mrs Pendennis’s hand, then gave her a big soft kiss on the cheek. Helen burst out crying happy tears all over his shoulder, and the doctor, bright red as a tomato, mumbled a little line from his favourite old poet about how he’d made “the widow’s heart to sing for joy”.
