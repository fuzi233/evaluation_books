April 14th marked Romola's return to the walls of Florence, a city that had swallowed her hopes and now demanded her presence. She had not found peace in Pistoia, where rumors of the Trial by Fire clashed like storm clouds, nor in Prato, where dread had held her in a paralyzed state. It was only the encounter with an Augustinian monk from San Spirito—a former confessor to her father—that broke her hesitation. He carried the weight of the city's collective grief, having walked beside the wagon bearing the terrible burden into the Piazza. From him, she learned the brutal finality of her husband's fate: Tito had leaped from the mob's assault into the Arno, only to be slain on the riverbank by an old enemy. Yet, the monk spoke of Savonarola with the bitter prejudice of the Black Friars, labeling the friar a deceiver, a man who had confessed his duplicity under the lash.

Romola absorbed these truths not as news, but as the heavy stones of a tomb she had already entered. That evening, she sat in a silent agony amidst the cacophony of Monna Brigida's house. The old woman, having briefly succumbed to vanity with false hair, now reverted to her piety, declaring she would rather be grey than lose her "dear child." But Romola remained unmoved by Brigida's gossipy details; the shadow of Tito's death and Savonarola's fall cast such a long darkness that minor circumstances dissolved into insignificance. Her mind, burdened by awe-stricken grief, focused on two desperate imperatives: to see Savonarola and to locate Tessa and the children.

"Tell me, cousin," Romola interrupted Brigida's rambling plans for their shared future, "has anything been seen or said of a young woman with two little children?"

Brigida recoiled, her hands rising in horror. "Cristo! No! Was he so bad as that?" she cried, her voice trembling. "Ah, then, that explains your flight. I thought you strange, flighty... 'She's got her father's temper,' I said to myself. But if you had told me the truth..."

"No, dear cousin," Romola insisted, her tone urgent. "Do not speak thus. I must find that woman and her children. They are helpless. That is my first duty."

"If that is being a Piagnone," Brigida muttered, lowering her voice, "I have been counting peas instead of prayers. Fra Girolamo says widows should not remarry; step inside the door and it is sin, but come down the chimney and you are welcome. Two children—Santiddio!"

"Cousin," Romola pleaded gently, "she has done no conscious wrong. She is ignorant. I will tell you later."

The next morning, Romola sought the house beyond San Ambrogio where she had last seen Tessa. It was empty. Old Lisa had departed a week prior with her belongings, leaving no trace of the young woman. Romola feared the worst: that Tito had sent Tessa away to a hiding place, perhaps intending to join her there. The thought was a knife twist; if Tessa were outside Florence, the chances of reunion were slim, and the image of the child waiting in helpless misery at a roadside haunted her. With no leads and a noble reserve preventing her from broadcasting Tessa's connection to Tito, days passed in anxious stillness. Every pair of round brown legs or short figure in peasant dress made her heart race.

She did not view this search as heroic charity, but as a sacred obligation. She needed to care for them, to clasp the children and make them love her, a sweet redemption for her own sorrows. Though she possessed a claim to Tito's property, she distrusted the purity of that wealth and resolved to surrender it to the State, keeping only enough to buy her father's library for Tessa's support. Brigida, however, clamored that Romola must stay with her, insisting that if Romola wished to raise others' children, she must do so alongside her own household.

Driven by a superstition of hope, Romola turned her gaze to the gate of San Gallo. She knew Tito had arranged horses there to escape Florence. Clad in her long widow's veil, she walked the Via San Gallo, scanning the crowd until she spotted Bratti haggling with a customer. A sudden movement caught her eye: something glinting from the corner of his basket. Her heart leaped.

"Bratti, my friend," she asked abruptly, "where did you get that necklace?"

"Your servant, madonna," Bratti replied, his face unreadable. "It is worth money, but my heart is too tender to trade it freely. I have pledged it."

"From a little woman named Tessa, is it not true?"

"Ah! If you know her," Bratti said, his eyes softening, "and would redeem it for her, you would be doing a great charity. She wept when she parted with it, as if running into a brook. I will charge you only a florin; I dislike to be hard-hearted."

Romola handed over the coin and retrieved the necklace, her fingers trembling with joy. "Where is she?"

"Outside the gate, at the other end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti's. Anybody knows the house."

With wings of purpose, Romola hastened to the address. Inside, the young woman and the children had been there for weeks, destitute, paying a poor widow merely with their clothes. But when Romola opened the door, Tessa did not turn. Seated on a low bed, she stared blankly at her children, who played in a corner—Lillo roaring at Ninna, then peering out with wide, fearful eyes. Expectation had long died in Tessa's heart.

Romola stepped forward, removing her veil and holding the necklace aloft. In the pure voice that once cheered her father, she called out, "Tessa!"

The woman sprang to her feet, her eyes widening in shock. "See," Romola said, fastening the beads around Tessa's neck. "God has sent me to you again."

Tessa screamed, a sound of pure release, and clung to the arms holding the necklace, sobbing uncontrollably. The children rushed from their corner, grabbing their mother's skirts and looking up at the stranger with wide, trusting eyes.

That afternoon, they returned to Monna Brigida's house in the Borgo degli Albizzi. Romola gently explained to Tessa that Naldo could never return—not out of cruelty, but because he was dead. "Be comforted, my Tessa," Romola assured her. "I am here to care for you always. And we have found Lillo and Ninna."

Brigida's face twisted in a struggle between reverence for Romola and the urge to assert her authority. *A thousand years,* she thought, *until I tell this simple country girl who Romola truly is.* Yet, before retiring, she confessed to Romola that it would be shameful not to care for such cherubs, though she requested Romola yield some control over their feeding, citing her own tragic experience with twin infants who had died at birth.
