The words echoed in Romola's mind, repeated by countless voices until they sounded like a single, piercing cry. They were the final utterances of Savonarola, spoken as he signed his confession before the brethren of San Marco: *"Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy."* These words struck her anew, casting deep shadows of doubt over the man she had revered. How could he look back upon the past with such absolute self-possession if he truly believed his divine inspiration had fled? Her uncertainty grew heavier when more pitiful reports reached her ears.

May nineteenth had arrived, and with its bright sun, Florence welcomed the two Papal Commissaries, sent to finalize the friar's trial. They entered the city amid thunderous acclamations demanding the death of the *Frate*. The populace cried out, "It is the Frate's deception that has brought on all our misfortunes! Burn him, and all shall be right; our evils will cease."

The following day, the tortured frame of the broken man endured fresh agony. Upon seeing the horrific instruments prepared for him, Savonarola, seized by convulsive agitation, fell to his knees. In brief, passionate words, he **retracted his confession**, declaring that he had lied when he denied his prophetic gift. "If I suffer," he proclaimed, "I suffer for the truth. The things I spoke, I received from God."

Yet the torture continued. When questioned why he had uttered those retracting words, the answer came, torn from his lips: "I said it to appear good. Tear me no more; I will tell you the truth."

Florentine assessors witnessed this second retraction, and the news spread quickly through the city, filling Romola with dismayed uncertainty. A sudden flash of insight pierced her gloom: *There will come a moment when he speaks freely.* When fear of falsehood vanishes, replaced only by the dread of death itself; when he is lifted above the mob, gazing upon them for the last time, they cannot stop him from speaking a final, decisive word. *I must be there,* she resolved.

Three days later, on May twenty-third, 1498, a long, narrow platform stretched across the great Piazza della Signoria, extending from the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de' Pisani. Unlike previous gatherings, there was no grove of wood ready to burn; instead, a single great heap of fuel sat on the circular area at the platform's end. Rising above this pyre was a gibbet with three halters. Though it possessed two arms, making it resemble a cross enough to unsettle some observers, one arm had been deliberately truncated to avoid the likeness.

On the marble terrace of the palace stood three tribunals. Near the main door waited the Bishop, tasked with performing the ceremony of degradation on Fra Girolamo and his two fellow brothers, who were to suffer as his accomplices. Another tribunal housed the Papal Commissaries, who would declare them heretics and schismatics, handing them over to the secular arm. Closest to the Marzocco statue, at the corner where the platform began, sat the third tribunal for the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, who would pronounce the sentence of death.

Once again, the Piazza teemed with expectant faces. A great fire was destined to be kindled. Among the crowd pressing around the gibbet, most harbored either ferocious hatred or a morbid curiosity to witness a barbarous spectacle. Yet, scattered across the wide pavement, on rooftops, and at windows, lingered many who, amidst bitter grief and the humiliation of enduring insults from the hypocritical *Piagnoni*, clung to a faint hope. Even at this eleventh hour, they prayed that God might intervene with a miraculous sign to vindicate their beloved prophet. Others, however, trembled with eagerness, just as Romola did, awaiting that final instant when Savonarola might cry out, *"O people, I was innocent of deceit!"*

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far removed from the marble terraces where the judges sat. Beside her watched Jacopo Nardi, a young Florentine of twenty-two, who would later earn honor as one of the few to write about Fra Girolamo with simple veracity. Seeing the struggle in Romola's face—a conflict between shuddering horror and a desperate yearning to witness the end—Jacopo had gently urged her earlier: *"Madonna, there is no need for you to watch these cruel things. I will tell you when he emerges from the Palazzo. Trust me; I know what you wish to see."*

Romola had covered her face, but the jeers and hoots seemed to render the hideous scene visible regardless. Finally, her arm was touched, and she heard the whisper, *"He comes."*

She turned toward the Palace. There, clad in his Dominican garb, Savonarola was led forth. Before the Bishop, he was stripped of his black mantle, his white scapular, and his long tunic, leaving him in a coarse woolen undergarment that revealed no sacred office, no rank. He had been degraded, severed from the Church Militant. The baser elements of the multitude delighted in such degradations, understanding them as pure satire. A fresh hoot of triumph rang out as the three degraded brothers marched to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries, who pronounced them schismatics and heretics. Could the prophet now pass for anything other than a heretic? It was easy to believe in the damnation of a man standing stripped and humiliated.

They proceeded to the third tribunal, where the Florentine officials awaited. From her distance, Romola could distinguish the odious figure of Dolfino Spini, dressed in his grave black *lucco* as one of the Eight. The three figures, robed in tight white garments, tramped along the platform amidst yells and grating insults.

"Cover your eyes, Madonna," Jacopo warned softly. "Fra Girolamo will be the last."

Soon, she was forced to uncover her face again. Savonarola was there. He had mounted the steps, and she could see him looking out upon the multitude. But in that same moment, her high hopes died. All she saw was what he saw: torches waving to ignite the fuel beneath his corpse, faces glowing with a glare worse than hellfire. All she heard was what he heard: gross jests, taunts, and curses. The moment had passed. She covered her face once more, knowing only that Savonarola's voice had faded into eternal silence.

On the evening of May twenty-second, 1509, five individuals, whose histories we have partially traced, sat in a handsome upper room opening onto a loggia. This loggia overlooked the Borgo Pinti and, at its right-hand corner, gazed down upon the city gate toward Fiesole and the solemn heights beyond.

At one end of the room lay an archway leading to a narrow inner recess, barely larger than a niche, where light filtered from above onto a small altar draped in fine white linen. Above the altar hung a picture, discernible only as a small, full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother from this distance. It was shaded from the overhead light by overhanging branches and wreaths of flowers, while fresh tapers below remained unlit. Yet, the decoration seemed incomplete. Part of the floor was strewn with a chaotic array of flowers and green boughs.

Seated among them was a delicate, blue-eyed girl of thirteen, tossing her long, light-brown hair aside as she selected blooms for the wreath she wove. Occasionally, she looked up at her mother's work with an air of instruction, offering guidance. For the mother was not particularly adept at flower-weaving or any craft. Tessa's fingers had grown less dexterous with the passing years—indeed, much fatter—and she moved slowly, turning her head frequently to ask Ninna's opinion with great deference. Tessa never ceased to marvel at the wisdom of her children. She still wore her countrywoman's gown, merely broader than the old one, adorned with a silver pin in her rough, curly brown hair and the memorable necklace with its red cord ending mysteriously in her bosom. Her rounded face bore an expression of childish contentment more perfect than in her youth; everyone was so good in the world, Tessa thought. Even Monna Brigida never found fault with her anymore, preferring instead to sleep—an amiable habit shared by all, one that Tessa enjoyed for herself.

Monna Brigida slept soundly in a straight-backed armchair a couple of yards away. Her hair, parted backward beneath her black hood, possessed a soft whiteness unlike snow, simply the lovely white of aged locks. Her chin rested on her bosom, her hands lying on the chair's elbow. She had not been working; she had merely been looking on, as she usually did, before succumbing to sleep.

Two other figures sat farther off, near the wide doorway opening to the loggia. Lillo reclined on the ground, his back against the door-post angle, his long legs stretched out. He held a large book open on his knee, occasionally swatting an inquisitive fly with his hand, displaying an interest stronger than that stirred by the finely printed copy of Petrarch resting nearby, which he kept open as if memorizing lines.
