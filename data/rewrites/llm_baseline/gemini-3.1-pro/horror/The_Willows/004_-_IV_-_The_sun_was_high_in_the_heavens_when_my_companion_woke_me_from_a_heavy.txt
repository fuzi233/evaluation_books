The sun was already high in the heavens when my companion roused me from a heavy, cyclical slumber, announcing that the morning porridge was ready and there remained only just enough time to bathe. Before I even opened my eyes, the olfactory landscape of the campsite washed over me: the rich, deeply comforting aroma of frizzling bacon. The scent of rendering lipids, cured with salt and hickory smoke in the ancient agrarian traditions of preservation, anchored my disoriented psyche firmly to the domestic routines of human history. 

“River still rising,” the Swede said, his voice cutting through the hiss of the frying pan. “Several islands out in mid-stream have disappeared altogether. Our own island’s much smaller.”

“Any wood left?” I asked sleepily, my mind still tracing the interwoven timelines of the river’s ancient floods.

“The wood and the island will finish tomorrow in a dead heat,” he laughed, “but there’s enough to last us till then.”

I plunged in from the point of the island, which had indeed altered its geography during the night, and was swept down in a moment to the landing-place opposite our tent. The water was icy, sweeping by like the blurred countryside from an express train. Yet bathing in that rushing current was exhilarating; the terror of the previous night seemed cleansed from my skin, evaporated from the acoustic space of my brain. The sun blazed hot without a cloud in the sky, though the wind had not abated its relentless, roaring tune.

Quite suddenly, the underlying lexicon of the Swede’s words translated themselves in my mind. He no longer wished to leave post-haste. *Enough to last till tomorrow.* He assumed we should stay on the island another night. It struck me as deeply odd, a contradiction of the hurried dialect he had spoken the evening before. 

Great crumblings of the earthen banks occurred at breakfast, throwing heavy splashes and clouds of spray that the wind carried directly into our cast-iron pan. The Swede talked incessantly about the difficulty the Vienna-Pesth steamers must face in finding the channel through such a flood. But it was his relationship to our sustenance that troubled me most. He ate very little of his breakfast, ignoring the complex, smoky flavors of the meat and the hearty texture of the oats, and for once omitted to smoke his pipe. To view a meal as mere utilitarian fuel—or worse, to reject it entirely—was entirely foreign to his nature. He kept the map spread open, studying its markings with a suspicious, shy excitement. He was profoundly frightened.

“We’d better get off sharp in an hour,” I said presently, hoping to guide him toward a confession of his shifting mind.

His answer puzzled me uncomfortably: “Rather! If they’ll let us.”

“Who’ll let us? The elements?” I asked, attempting to mask my unease beneath a dominant lexicon of casual indifference.

“The powers of this awful place, whoever they are,” he replied, his eyes locked on the map. “The gods are here, if they are anywhere at all in the world.”

“The elements are always the true immortals,” I replied, forcing a laugh, though I knew the untranslatable dread of my subjective experience must be written plainly on my face.

“We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster,” he said gravely across the smoke of the fire.

I screwed myself up to the necessity of the direct question. “Further disaster! Why, what’s happened?”

“For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone,” he said quietly.

“The steering paddle gone!” I repeated, my heart hammering, for navigating the Danube in flood without our rudder was suicide. 

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, a genuine tremor in his voice.

I stared at him. Here, in the blazing heat of the sun, upon the burning sand, a freezing atmosphere descended upon our domestic sphere. I followed him to the other side of the fireplace. The canoe lay ribs uppermost, and beside her upon the sand rested the paddle—or rather, *the* paddle, for there was only one. 

“There’s only one,” he said, stooping to pick it up. “And here’s the rent in the base-board.”

I approached to examine the damage, my fingers tracing the wood. There was a long, finely made tear in the hull where a little slither of timber had been taken clean out, as neatly as a chef might score a flank of meat. It looked as if the tooth of a sharp rock had eaten down her length, punching clean through. Had we launched, the wood would have temporarily swelled, but in mid-stream, the water would have inevitably overwhelmed us, sinking the heavily laden craft in moments.

“There, you see an attempt to prepare a victim for the sacrifice,” I heard him saying to himself. “Two victims rather,” he added, running his fingers along the slit.

I began to whistle—a defensive sonic barrier I erect when utterly nonplussed. I was determined to frame his words as foolish, to force this mythic occurrence back into the bounds of a rational, everyday accident. “We must have scratched her in landing, of course,” I said, stopping my whistling. “The stones are very sharp.”

I stopped abruptly. He turned and met my eye squarely. We both knew how utterly impossible my explanation was; there were no stones here, only yielding sand.

“And then there’s this to explain too,” he added quietly, pointing to the blade of the remaining paddle.

A new emotion, cold and untranslatable, spread over me. The blade had been scraped down all over, meticulously sandpapered to such a delicate thinness that the first vigorous stroke against the dense, heavy current would snap it at the elbow.

“One of us walked in his sleep and did this thing,” I said feebly, clinging to the fading vocabulary of reason, “or—or it has been filed by the constant stream of sand particles blown against it by the wind, perhaps.”

“Ah,” the Swede laughed quietly, turning away. “You can explain everything.”

“The same wind that caught the steering paddle and flung it so near the bank that it fell in!” I called after him, violently asserting the dominant lexicon of empirical reality against this erosion of our sanity.

“I see,” he shouted back before disappearing among the willow bushes.

Alone with these perplexing evidences of personal agency, I realized neither of us could have done this. My companion was the trusted veteran of a dozen such expeditions; his densely practical nature could not have suddenly fractured into insane purposes. Yet, he *was* changed. He was listening to a frequency I could not hear, aware of a series of secret events, waiting for an impending climax. 

I hurried to examine the tent and its surroundings. Deep hollows had formed in the sand—basin-shaped depressions varying from the size of a tea-cup to a large mixing bowl. The wind, no doubt, had formed these miniature craters. The rent in the canoe was the only inexplicable thing, and I clung to my rationalizations as desperately as one clings to a native dialect when surrounded by a hostile, foreign tongue. 

I immediately set the pine pitch melting over the fire. Soon, the sharp, highly evocative scent of boiling resin filled our olfactory space, and the Swede joined me. Under the best conditions, the canoe would not be safe for travel until the following day. I drew his attention casually to the hollows in the sand.

“Yes,” he said, “I know. They’re all over the island. But *you* can explain them, no doubt!”

“Wind, of course,” I answered without hesitation. “This sand’s loose enough to yield, that’s all.”

We worked in silence. I watched him surreptitiously; he kept turning his head, listening intently, sometimes holding his cupped hand to his ear. He was tuning into an acoustic ecology I could not perceive, searching the bushes and the sky for a sonic footprint he expected to hear. He mended the torn hull with the meticulous skill of an indigenous craftsman, and I was glad for his absorption in the physical work. 

At length, he broke the silence. “Queer thing,” he said hurriedly. “I mean, about that otter last night.”

“Shows how lonely this place is,” I replied, surprised. “Otters are awfully shy—”

“I don’t mean that. I mean—did you think it really *was* an otter?”

“What else, in the name of Heaven?”

“You know, I saw it before you did, and at first it seemed—so *much* bigger than an otter. It had such extraordinary yellow eyes,” he went on, half to himself.

“The sunset as you looked up-stream magnified it,” I laughed boisterously. “I suppose you’ll wonder next if that fellow in the boat—”

I halted. He was turning his head to the wind again, listening. Five minutes later, holding the smoking pitch, his face grew exceedingly grave. 

“I *did* rather wonder, if you want to know, what that thing in the boat was. I remember thinking at the time it was not a man. The whole business seemed to rise quite suddenly out of the water.”

Impatience and anger flared in me. “Look here now!” I cried. “This place is quite queer enough without going out of our way to imagine things! That boat was an ordinary boat, and that otter *was* an otter. And for Heaven’s sake, don’t keep pretending you hear things, because it only gives me the jumps, and there’s nothing to hear but the river and this cursed old thundering wind.”

“You *fool!*” he answered in a low, shocked voice. “You utter fool. That’s just the way all victims talk. As if you didn’t understand just as well as I do! The best thing you can do is to keep quiet and try to hold your mind as firm as possible. This feeble attempt at self-deception only makes the truth harder when you’re forced to meet it.”

He was right. Up to this point, he had easily kept ahead of me, proving more attuned to the shifting environment, while I remained deaf to the subtle power dynamics embedded in this landscape. I dropped all pretence, and thenceforward my fear increased steadily toward its climax. 

“But you’re quite right about one thing,” he added. “We’re wiser not to talk about it, or even to think about it, because what one *thinks* finds expression in words, and what one *says*, happens.”

That afternoon, while the pitch hardened, we fished for driftwood with long willow branches and watched the tremendous flood tear our island away in great, muddy gulps. Around four o'clock, the wind finally showed signs of abating, and clouds gathered in the south-west. 

The lessening of the wind was a great relief to our battered nerves, a sudden cessation of a roar that rivaled the worst of modern industrial noise pollution. Yet, the silence that followed at five o'clock was infinitely more oppressive. The acoustic ecology of the island shifted completely. The booming of the river now dominated everything; it filled the air with deep murmurs. Where the wind had held many notes—rising, falling, beating out a complex, elemental tune—the river’s song lay locked between three dull pedal notes. It held a lugubrious quality that felt ancient and foreign, sounding to my highly strained nervous state like the orchestrated music of doom.

With the withdrawal of the bright sunlight, the landscape lost all cheerfulness. The darkening outlook was alarming. The river grew blacker; the willows seemed to stand more densely together. The bushes kept up a rustling, independent movement of their own, shaking oddly from the roots upward even when no wind stirred. This bizarre, purposeful choreography stimulated the imagination far more than things of unusual appearance. Their very ordinariness masked a malignant hostility. The forces of the region were focusing upon our domestic sphere, translating their indescribable sensations into our reality.

By five o'clock, I turned to the one ritual that could ground me: I busied myself with the stew-pot and preparations for dinner. Here, amidst the pressing horror, the meticulous construction of a meal was an anchor to generational sanity. I rendered down our remaining bits of bacon fat, using the rich drippings to sweat the onions until they were deeply translucent and fragrant. I cubed the earthy potatoes, adding them to the pot along with the thick, gelatinous residue from former stews, establishing a profound historical bridge of flavor between yesterday’s sustenance and today's survival. 

With the savory foundation bubbling, I set about preparing a dessert stew of plums, their tart skins destined to burst into a rich syrup of sugar, a tribute to late-summer orchard harvests. Finally, I readied a strong brew of dark tea and dried milk. The absence of wind made my culinary duties easy, and my companion sat lazily watching me, dividing his attention between cleaning his pipe and offering the useless advice of an off-duty man. 

The main pot had just begun to bubble, releasing an incredibly evocative, gustatory-rich aroma of alliums and rendered pork, when I heard his voice from the bank. I had not noticed him wander away. 

“Come and listen,” he said, holding his hand cupwise to his ear. “See what you make of it. *Now* do you hear anything?”

We stood there, listening attentively. At first, I heard only the three deep notes of the water. The willows, for once, were silent. Then, a peculiar sound began to reach my ears—a low humming, like the resonance of a distant gong. It drifted across the darkness from the waste of swamps and willows opposite. It was struck at regular intervals, an immense, metallic note, muffled but distinctly musical, suspended somewhere in the vast acoustic space of the sky.

“I’ve heard it all day,” my companion whispered. “I hunted it down, but could never localize it correctly. Sometimes it was overhead, sometimes under the water. Once or twice, I could have sworn it was not outside at all, but *within myself*.”

I strove to associate the tone with any familiar sound—to translate it into the comforting lexicon of the known world—but failed. It shifted in direction, coming nearer, then sinking away into remote distance. It was not ominous in tone, yet it set a distressing vibration in my chest. 

“The wind blowing in those sand-funnels,” I said, stubbornly asserting reason. “Or the bushes rubbing together.”

“It comes off the whole swamp,” he answered, ignoring my feeble translations. “It comes from the willow bushes somehow—”

“But the wind has dropped. The willows can hardly make a noise by themselves!”

His answer intuitively struck the core of my fear. “It is *because* the wind has dropped we now hear it. It was drowned before. It is the cry, I believe, of the—”

I dashed back to my fire, warned by the violent bubbling of the pot that my careful culinary work was in danger of scorching. I was equally desperate to escape further conversation. I dreaded that he would speak of elemental forces again. I needed to maintain the domestic illusion of our camp. 

“Come and cut up bread for the pot,” I called to him, vigorously stirring the appetizing mixture of potatoes and onions. That stew-pot held sanity for us both; its rich, earthly sustenance was the only barrier against the mythic intrusion pressing upon our minds, and the thought made me laugh aloud.

He came over slowly, taking the provision sack from the tree. He fumbled in its depths, then emptied the entire contents upon the ground-sheet.

“Hurry up!” I cried. “It’s boiling.”

The Swede burst into a roar of laughter—a forced, mirthless sound that startled the quiet air. 

“There’s nothing here!” he shouted, holding his sides.

“Bread, I mean.”

“It’s gone. There is no bread. They’ve taken it!”

I dropped the long wooden spoon and ran up. The cultural memory of that dense, black rye loaf, meant to soak up the historical residue of our stew, was erased. Everything the sack contained lay upon the canvas, but the bread was missing. 

The entire dead weight of the encroaching untranslatable reality fell upon me. And then, I burst out laughing too. It was an explosion of psychical pressure, our linguistic and psychological defenses completely breaking down into a primitive, unnatural safety-valve of hysteria. 

And with both of us, the laughter ceased quite suddenly.

“How criminally stupid of me!” I cried, still desperately fighting to maintain a rational timeline. “I clean forgot to buy a loaf at Pressburg. That chattering woman put everything out of my head, and I must have left it lying on the counter or—”

“The oatmeal, too, is much less than it was this morning,” the Swede interrupted, pointing to our dwindling agrarian reserves. 

*Why must he draw attention to it?* I thought angrily.

“There’s enough for tomorrow,” I said, returning to stir the pot vigorously, leaning into the physical sensation of the heavy stew. “And we can get lots more at Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours we shall be miles from here.”

“I hope so—to God,” he muttered, repacking the remaining provisions. “Unless we’re claimed first as victims for the sacrifice,” he added with a foolish laugh, dragging the sack into the tent for safety. I heard him mumbling to himself in the darkness, but his words had slipped into a dialect of fear so entirely indistinct that I found it necessary, for the sake of my own survival, to ignore them completely.
