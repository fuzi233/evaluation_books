The afternoon sun slanted through the window, casting long shadows across the room where Gerard lay. He was no longer dizzy, but his mind wandered restlessly, clouded by feverish thoughts. One such fancy led him to ask Denys for a lemon to suck—something rare and exotic in these northern lands.

Denys, once a rough soldier now softened into something like a guardian, rose quickly. “Don’t worry,” he said with a grin. “Lemons shall be yours before the pot boils.” And off he went, searching every shop in town, only to return long after Gerard had grown impatient.

But it was not Denys who entered when the door finally opened.

A figure stepped in with quiet dignity—an old man draped in a long, sober gown trimmed with rich fur, wearing cherry-red hose and pointed shoes. A sword hung at his side in a morocco scabbard. Around his neck, a stiff ruff, starched and shaped by hidden wooden supports, stood rigid like armor. On his head sat a four-cornered cap lined with fur, and from his chin and chest flowed a majestic white beard. The boy behind him carried a basket filled with bottles, bandages, and surgical tools, none of them hidden from view.

Gerard recognized the man at once—not because of the sword, but because this was the full dress of a physician. And indeed, the doctor approached the bedside softly, speaking low: “How fares thee, my son?”

Gerard answered gently that his wound no longer hurt much, though his throat felt dry and his head heavy.

“A wound!” the doctor murmured, surprised. “They did not tell me of that. Let me see.” He leaned over, inspecting the injury. “Ah, yes—a clean bite. The dog must have had strong teeth.”

“Not a dog,” Gerard corrected. “A bear.”

“A bear?” the doctor replied, sternly. “Young man, think before you speak. It is unwise to mock the man of science who brings years of study and grey hairs to heal you. No bear’s jaw could make such a small wound. I say it was a dog—and not even a large one, but one of those little, vicious curs that run wild, biting men’s legs and bringing them low… if not for us learned men, who stop their mischief with knife and cautery.”

“But sir,” Gerard said slowly, “I did not say it was the bear’s jaw. I said *a bear*. It was his paw.”

“And why did you not tell me so at once?”

“Because you kept insisting it was a dog.”

“Never hide anything from your leech,” the doctor declared, more intent on lecturing than listening. “Well, it is a serious matter. All animal claws—tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, bears—and even human nails, especially children’s, carry deadly poison. You would have been better off bitten by a cur than scratched by a bear. Still, we shall apply a strong poultice to your leg. In the meantime, keep the body cool. Stick out your tongue—good! Fever. Now let me feel your pulse—yes, fever. I prescribe bloodletting. Immediately.”

“Bloodletting?” Gerard asked, frowning. “That means letting blood? Well, if it will cure me, then let it be done. I won’t lie here idle.”

The doctor nodded gravely. “Flebotomy is infallible, especially in your case.”

“Hans,” he called, “fetch the instruments. I’ll entertain our patient while you wait.”

And so began his lecture.

In sickness, he explained, the blood grows hot, corrupted, and poisonous. By removing some of this tainted fluid, nature is forced to produce fresh, pure blood in its place. Thus, bleeding serves both as cooling and cleansing—a remedy for all diseases, since all diseases are fevers, despite what empirics may claim.

“But remember,” he added warmly, “bleeding alone is not enough. Any barber can open a vein—though not all can close it again. The true art lies in knowing which vein to open for which illness. Recently, a man came to me with earache. I bled him in the right thigh, and his pain vanished. Alas, he died soon after. Another suffered toothache; I bled him behind the ear, and he was relieved instantly. He too is dead now. Our bailiff had rheumatism; I bled him between thumb and forefinger. Soon he returned, complaining of headache and ringing in the ears, holding out his hand over the basin. I smiled at his folly and bled him instead in the left ankle, against his will. His head became light as a nut.”

He paused, then shifted course, as scholars often do.

“Know this, young man: two great schools of medicine now divide Europe. The Arabian tradition—rooted in Avicenna, Rhazes, Albucazis—revived by Chauliac and Lanfranc. And the Greek school—championed today by Bessarion, Platinus, Marsilii Ficinus—but whose ancient masters were the oracles of healing: Phoebus, Chiron, Aesculapius, his sons Podalinus and Machaon, Pythagoras, Democritus, Praxagoras—who discovered arteries—and Dioctes, ‘who first gave meaning to urine.’ All taught orally. Then came Hippocrates, eighteenth in line from Aesculapius, whose writings we still possess. To him we owe the concept of vital principle. He invented the bandage, performed thoracentesis, and dissected animals—though never humans, due to pagan superstition.”

“Surely,” Gerard ventured, “God gave us our bodies, not Aristotle or any Greek.”

“Child!” the doctor snapped. “Of course God created the body. But Aristotle gave it a name. And young men always talk too much. Next came Galen, who studied in Alexandria, the center of learning. Disgusted with quadrupeds, he turned to apes, closer to man, and bled them mercilessly. Then Theophilus revealed nerves, lacteal vessels, and the pia mater.”

Gerard grew uneasy. “I cannot stay silent while you say mortal men gave names to parts Adam received directly from God.”

“Is there such stubbornness?” the doctor cried, flushing. “Who truly gives a thing to man—the one who hides it in darkness, or the scholar who reveals it? Knowledge is your only true possession. Are you convinced?”

“I am silenced,” Gerard admitted.

“Good,” the doctor said. “For noisy patients are hard to treat—especially when fevered. Now, Eristratus gave us cerebral nerves and milk vessels. He also invented lithotomy. Then came another—I forget his name. Then Ammonius, author of lithotrity. And here comes Hans with the basin—now silence!”

He turned to the boy. “Blow the charcoal, boy. Hand me the basin. Good.”

As the preparations continued, Denys returned, lemons in hand, and stopped short in surprise.

“What’s going on?” he asked, raising an eyebrow.

Gerard flushed slightly. “The doctor is about to bleed me and cauterize the wound.”

“Oh, indeed,” Denys said. “And what’s the boy doing with the coals?”

“To cauterize the vein once opened,” the doctor replied. “It’s the only safe way. Avicenna suggests tying the vein, but he says nothing how—nor does he know himself, nor any of Ishmael’s spawn. I trust no such tricks. Remember this: whatever an Arab or Arabist claims is true, must be false.”

“Oh,” Denys said, understanding now. “So you’re going to burn living flesh with red-hot iron? Have you ever held your little finger in a candle for ten seconds? This will last minutes. Do you not fear purgatory? Must you taste hell before death?”

“I hadn’t thought of that,” Gerard said quietly. “The doctor spoke of cautery, not burning. They sound different, though they mean the same.”

“Fool!” Denys scoffed. “That’s their trick—to confuse plain men with dark words until the flesh screams the truth. I’ve seen soldiers wounded in battle. These doctors cry, ‘Fever! Blood him!’ and burn the wick at the other end too. They bleed the already bled. Then fever fades into weakness. The man needs every drop of blood to live. But these prickers and burners care only for what’s before them. They drain the very blood the wound spared him to fight the weakness. And so he dies—exhausted. Hundreds I’ve seen die this way—strong men. Yet if they’re wounded where no doctor can reach, they live. I know this. Had I lived through that Brabant field without surgery? Only by luck—frost sealed my wounds. A surgeon would have pierced me once more, drained my last drop, and taken my soul with it. Seeing them thus reckless with bleeding soldiers, I trust them not. What kills a veteran can surely kill a weak civilian.”

“That sounds reasonable,” Gerard sighed. “But no need to shout. I’m not deaf.”

“Reasonable? Very common sense indeed,” the doctor snapped. “This is a soldier—a brute whose job is to kill, not heal.” He added in French, “Woe to you, ignorant man, if you interfere between a physician and his patient. Woe to you, misguided youth, if you listen to a butcher of blood.”

“Much obliged,” Denys said, mocking politeness. “But I’m a true man. I do draw blood, though not worth mentioning here. For every life I take, you take a hundred. For every spoonful I draw, you spill a tub. The world is fooled by appearances. We soldiers carry long swords and create two enemies for every one we kill. But you smooth-gowned men, with soft words and bare lancets—you are the ones who thin mankind.”

“A sickroom is no place for jesting,” the doctor thundered.

“No,” Gerard muttered, peevishly. “Nor for shouting.”

“Come, young man,” the doctor said, softer now. “Be reasonable. Each man should be trusted in his own craft. I have spent my life in this art. I studied at Montpellier—the finest school in France, and therefore Europe. There I learned Dririmancy, Scatomancy, Pathology, Therapeusis, and above all, Anatomy. At last, we disciples of Hippocrates and Galen had opportunities those ancients never dreamed of. Farewell, quadrupeds and apes, paganism and Islam. We bought corpses from churchwardens, shook gallows, undid sextons’ work under cover of night, driven by love of science and humanity. Paris sent orders to wink—and they did. Gods of Olympus, how they winked! Even the king helped—twice a year he sent a condemned criminal alive, saying, ‘Treat him as science demands. Dissect him if needed.’”

“By Herod’s liver and Nero’s bowels,” Denys roared, “he’ll make me blush for the land that bore me if he praises it any further!”

Gerard squeaked and covered his ears. Then, suddenly, he shouted back just as loudly: “You roaring, blaspheming bull of Basan—hold your tongue!”

Denys looked contrite.

“Tush,” the doctor said, waving a hand dismissively. “We rarely dissected the living—only in part. Mostly, we inoculated criminals with diseases of the time—choosing the most interesting.”

“That means the worst,” Denys whispered.

“We watched them through every stage until death.”

“Meaning the poor rogue died,” Denys whispered again.

“And now,” the doctor continued, “who deserves your trust more—the honest soldier with youth, ignorance, and prejudice—or a greybeard burdened with centuries of wisdom?”

“That is,” Denys interrupted, “will you believe a jackdaw in a long gown who heard from a starling who heard from a jay-pie who heard from a magpie who heard from a popinjay—or will you believe me, a man with nothing to gain, who sees with eyes, not ears, and has seen that fevered, bled men die, while fevered, unbled men live?”

“Who sent for this bloodsucker?” Denys demanded. “Was it you?”

“No,” Gerard said. “I thought you had.”

“No,” the doctor corrected. “The landlord told me someone was ill. I thought, ‘A stranger in need of my art,’ and came at once.”

“It was a Christian act,” Gerard said.

“A bloodhound’s act,” Denys sneered. “Do you not know landlords conspire with citizens who pay them fees for each victim? Whatever you pay this man for stealing your blood, the landlord takes a third for betraying you. And when your blood flows down the stairs, he smells it and sends to his undertaker—gets his third from that too. If he waited till the doctor left, the doctor might hire the undertaker first—and then he’d get the black thirds. Say I speak truth, old Rouge et Noir? Dites!”

“Denys,” Gerard said, “who taught you to distrust men?”

“My eyes,” Denys replied. “Eyes that see what men do, not what they say. I’ve seen it across many lands.”

The doctor used this moment wisely. “I too have eyes. I don’t rely on tradition alone, but on what I’ve seen and done. I’ve healed as many with bleeding as that Arabist has killed by neglect. Yesterday, I cured a man threatened with leprosy—bled him at the tip of the nose. Last year, a quartan ague—bled his forefinger. Our rival lost his memory. I restored it with a lance—bled him behind the ear. I bled a dull boy, and now he’s the only one in his family who knows his right hand from his left. When the plague struck, not the fake kind empyrics invent every six years, but the real Byzantine pest, I bled an alderman freely and cauterized the buboes—saved him. While our chirurgeon, a dangerous Arabist, caught it and died, calling on Rhazes, Avicenna, and Mahound—who, had they come, would have perished as he did.”

“Oh, my ears,” Gerard groaned.

“And am I so fallen that one of your presence rejects my art and listens to a crude soldier—so backward even in his own trade that he uses an arbalest, a worn-out invention German children shoot at pigeons with, mocked by German soldiers since arquebuses came?”

“You foul-mouthed charlatan,” Denys spat. “The arbalest is wielded by taller men than ever wore Rhenish hose. Even now, it kills more than your stinking arquebus, just as your lancet kills more than all our weapons combined. Go to! He was no fool who first called you ‘leeches.’ Sang-sues! Va!”

Gerard groaned again. “By the holy virgin, I wish you were both at Jericho, bellowing.”

“Thank you, comrade,” Denys said. “Then I’ll bark no more. But if need be, I’ll bite. If he pricks you with his lance, my sword-hilt knocks against his ribs. I’ve said it.”

Denys folded his arms, pale and grim.

Gerard sighed. “Now, since this is about me, let me speak.”

“Let the young man choose life or death,” the doctor said.

Gerard spoke with calm, gentle clarity—unlike either man.

“I doubt not you both mean well. But you assassinate me between you. Calmness and quiet are everything to me. Yet you are like two dogs growling over a bone. And truly, I would be bone, if this noise continues.”

Silence fell—broken only by Gerard’s soft voice as he gazed upward.

“First, venerable sir, thank you for coming—whether from kindness or trade. All trades must live.

“Your learning seems vast, and your age vouches for your experience.

“You say you’ve bled many, and many lived. I believe you.

“You say others you bled died. I believe you too.

“Denys knows less than you, but he knows what he sees. He does not guess. I’ve seen this myself. He says fevered, bled men mostly die—fevered, unbled men live. I must believe him.

“So here, all is doubt.

“But this is certain: if I am bled, I must pay you—and endure the agony of a hot iron, though I am no felon.

“To pay money and suffer for a doubtful cure—I will never do.

“Next to ease, peace is a treasure—especially in a sickroom. But it seems men cannot discuss medicine without anger and raised voices. Therefore, sir, I will try sleep. Denys, go outside and look upon the women of the town. I will not keep you from those who can afford blood and gold for flebotomy and cautery.”

The doctor’s temper flared. He had mastered it during the argument, but now it broke free. The noblest course was silence—he knew it—and so he rose, walked to the door, followed by his boy and basket.

But at the door, he choked. Swelled. Burst.

He whirled around, mouth open, forcing the boy and basket to dart sideways—*de minimis non curat Medicina*, even in rage.

“Ah! You reject my skill, scorn my art! My revenge shall be to leave you to yourself. Lost idiot—take your last look at me and the sun. Your blood be on your head!” And he stormed out.

But at the door, he turned again, tail twitching like a cat’s.

“In twelve hours, you’ll enter the second stage of fever. Your head will split. Your carotids will thump. Aha! And if a pin drops, you’ll jump to the ceiling. Then send for me—and I’ll not come.”

He departed. But at the handle, fury seized him. He spun back, pale-faced, boy trembling, basket swaying.

“Next—CRAMPS of the STOMACH. Aha!

“Then—BILIOUS VOMIT. Aha!

“And after that—nothing can save you. Not even I. And if I could, I would not. Farewell!”

Even Denys paled. But Gerard merely clenched his teeth in rage and gripped his bolster tightly.

This lit the fire anew. The insulted elder returned from the threshold, whisking train in tow.

“And after that—MADNESS!

“And after that—BLACK VOMIT!

“And then—CONVULSIONS!

“And then—THAT CESSATION OF ALL VITAL FUNCTIONS THE VULGAR CALL 'DEATH,' for which thank your own Satanic folly and insolence. Farewell.”

He left. He returned. He roared: “And think not to be buried in a Christian churchyard! The bailiff is my friend. I shall tell him how and why you died: *felo de se!* *Felo de se!* Farewell!”

Gerard sprang up, somehow lifted by fury, and seeing him move, the furious orator charged forward, fist raised, ready to unleash some final threat—lost to history.

But as he came, bolster flew—hurled by Gerard like a missile—striking the doctor square in the face. He crashed down, the boy’s head cracked beneath him, and the doctor tumbled into the basket, wedged upside-down, crushing phials one after another. The boy, lighter, scattered far, landing in a squat, howling.

The doctor’s face twisted in horror. He shrieked—a sound louder and more unearthly than any before—and kicked wildly, struggling to escape.

He was sitting on the hot coals.

They had singed his robe. Now they bit his flesh.

Struggling in vain, he rolled sideways with the basket—then came a great hissing. Gerard rushed forward, wrenching the basket off with effort. The doctor lay face down, groaning, his robe beautifully painted with various poisons—some green, some blue, some purple—by the very medicines he’d brought.

Gerard and Denys helped him up.

“Courage, man,” Gerard said. “It’s but cautery. Balm of Gilead—why, you recommended it just now to my comrade.”
