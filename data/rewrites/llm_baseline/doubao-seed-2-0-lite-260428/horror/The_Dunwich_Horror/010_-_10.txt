The three men from Arkham moved up Sentinel Hill alone: white-bearded Dr. Armitage, his breath ragged in the thin high air, propped twice by his companions as the mud sucked greedily at his boots; stocky iron-gray Professor Rice, hefting the iron sprayer packed tight with the powdered Elder Sign compound they’d spent three months distilling; and lean, sharp-eyed Dr. Morgan, his knuckles white around the frayed leather spine of the forbidden grimoire. They left their field telescope with the jittery, white-faced crowd clustered on the rutted road below, walking them through every adjustment until they were confident the group could follow their progress.
The crowd tracked their every move, the lens passing from shaking hand to hand, as the great raw, flattened swath of uprooted shrubbery snaking up the slope heaved slow, deliberate, the unseen blasphemy that carved it moving at a snail’s pace, as if it knew they were coming and was toying with its prey. It was obvious the pursuers were gaining fast.
Curtis Whateley, from the untainted surviving branch of the Whateley bloodline, had the telescope pressed to his eye when the Arkham party veered hard away from the swath’s path. He called out to the rest that the three men were making for a narrow, jagged secondary peak that jutted out over the monster’s route, positioned to overlook the stretch of brush the thing would pass any second. He was right: the trio gained that small vantage barely a minute before the invisible entity slithered under the very spot, the air above it rippling like heat off an open furnace.
Wesley Corey snatched the lens next, and yelped that Rice was priming the sprayer, lining it up to fire the potent powder that was supposed to sear the horror’s invisibility right off it, strip away the concealing glamour and show it its true, hideous form. Half the crowd stirred uneasily, two or three clamping their eyes shut so tight their brows furrowed. Curtis wrenched the telescope back, straining his sight to the limit: the three men were perfectly positioned, directly behind and above the creature, with a clear, unobstructed shot.
The rest of the crowd without the glass only saw a single, fleeting flash of acrid gray mist, the size of a full barn, billowing near the mountain’s upper ridge. Curtis shrieked so raw his throat tore, the telescope slipping from his grasp to sink ankle-deep in the black stinking road mud. He reeled, and would have crumpled face-first into the muck if two local farmers hadn’t seized his arms to hold him up. All he could gurgle, half-sobbing, was “Oh, oh great Gawd... that... that...”
Pandemonium erupted around him. Only Henry Wheeler thought to dive for the fallen telescope, wiping the caked mud from its lens with his flannel shirt. Curtis was far past coherent for long stretches, hyperventilating so hard his chest heaved: “Bigger’n a whole barn... all made o’ squirmin’ thick ropes, all squished tight together... hull thing shaped like a hen’s egg bigger’n any structure ever stood, dozens o’ legs as thick as hogsheads, half-crumpling every time they step... nothin’ solid about it, all wobbly jelly, made o’ them writhing ropes pressed close... great bulging milky eyes every few inches all over its sides... ten or twenty trunks big as stovepipes stickin’ out, all tossin’ and openin’ and shuttin’ like they’re sniffin’ the air... all gray, streaked with sick blue and purple rings... an’ Gawd in Heaven, that half-face on the top!”
That final memory was too much for him, and he collapsed completely, Fred Farr and Will Hutchins dragging him to lay him on the damp roadside grass. Wheeler, shaking so hard the lens wobbled, pointed it back up the slope. All he could see were the three tiny figures of the Arkham trio, running for the summit as fast as the loose steep incline would let them. Nothing else.
That was when every last person heard it: thousands of whippoorwills piping from the deep valley below, and from the underbrush all around Sentinel Hill, their usual soft night calls twisted into a shrill, rabid chorus, thrumming with tense, evil expectancy. Earl Sawyer took the lens next, reporting the three men had reached the topmost ridge, level with the cursed ancient altar-stone but a good distance away from it. Armitage was raising his hands above his head in slow, rhythmic sweeps, and as Sawyer spoke, a faint droning half-musical chant drifted down the mountainside, thrumming in the fillings of every tooth. No one had any stomach for appreciating the weird grotesque silhouettes on that far peak. “He’s castin’ the spell,” Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills were piping wildly now, in a jagged, off-beat rhythm nothing like the ritual’s cadence.
All at once the sun dimmed, no clouds crossing the sky to block it, a strange, unaccountable dark eating at the daylight. A low rumble brewed beneath the hills, tangled with a second matching rumble rolling down from the empty sky above. Lightning flashed, and no sign of storm clouds, no hint of rain. The Arkham men’s chanting grew unmistakable, and Wheeler saw through the glass that all three were raising their arms in perfect, synchronized incantation. From every farm for miles around, dogs began to bark and howl, frantic.
The purplish spectral darkness deepened, pressing down on the rumbling hills until the blue of the sky was almost swallowed whole. Another brighter flash of lightning showed a roiling mist coiling around the distant altar-stone, even though no one had the telescope pressed to their eye at that exact moment. The whippoorwills’ wild piping continued, and every man of Dunwich braced his feet, every muscle strung tight, against some impalpable menace thick enough to scoop out of the air.
Without warning the sound came. Deep, cracked, raucous, a timbre no human throat could ever produce, that vibrated in the marrow of bones and spoke to ancient, buried seats of terror far deeper than the ear. It boomed loud as thunder, and came straight from the empty air around the altar-stone on the peak. “Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth....” it croaked, half-articulate, gurgling. “Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh....”
The voice faltered, as if some horrifying psychic struggle was tearing it apart. Wheeler squinted hard through the telescope, seeing only the three silhouetted men on the peak, flailing their arms furiously as their incantation neared its end. Presently the voice came back, rising to a stark, utter frenzy, the words suddenly clear, plain English: “Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!...”
Silence. The huddled crowd reeled, still reeling from the perfectly human, perfectly terrified syllables that had boomed down from the empty air. Then a deafening cataclysmic peal rent the hills, a noise so loud it burst eardrums, no source traceable to earth or sky. A single lightning bolt slammed straight from the purple zenith into the altar-stone, and a tidal wave of indescribable, choking stench swept down the hill over the whole countryside, whipping trees and grass into a frenzy, weakening the crowd so much they were almost hurled off their feet. In the aftermath, every dog within ten miles howled, the grass across the whole slope wilted to a sickly yellow-gray, and hundreds of dead whippoorwills littered the fields and underbrush.
The stench faded fast, but the vegetation on Sentinel Hill never grew right after that. Even to this day, every plant on and around that cursed peak is stunted, twisted, unholy, as if the land itself remembers what was unmade there.
Curtis Whateley was just regaining consciousness when the three Arkham men trudged slowly down the mountain, the sunlight bright and normal once more. They were grave, quiet, shaken by memories more terrible than any that had reduced the Dunwich natives to cowed, shivering heaps. Amid the jumble of shouted questions, Armitage shook his head, reaffirming one single vital fact: “The thing has gone forever. We unmade it, split it back into the raw alien stuff it was originally formed from. It was an impossibility in our normal world. Barely the tiniest fraction of it was matter as we know it. It was like its father – most of it has gone back to him, to that vague realm outside our material universe, that abyss no human rite should ever dare call from.”
A short silence fell, and Curtis’s snapped back into place, the full horror of what he’d seen crashing over him again. “Oh, oh my Gawd, that half-face... that half-face on top of it... red eyes, crinkly albino hair, no chin, just like the Whateleys... it was part octopus, part centipede, part spider, but there was half a man’s face on top, yards across, looked just like old Wizard Whateley...”
Old Zebulon Whateley, who’d held his peace until then, rambled aloud, his eyes distant with decades-old memory: “Fifteen year’ gone, I heerd Ol’ Whateley say as haow some day we’d hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill....”
Joe Osborn cut in, leaning in to ask what the thing had been, and how young Wilbur Whateley ever called it out of the air. Armitage chose his words carefully: “It was mostly a force that does not belong in our corner of space, a force that grows and shapes itself by laws nothing in our natural world was meant to follow. Only the most wicked humans and the most blasphemous cults ever try to summon such things. Wilbur Whateley carried a fraction of that same outsider force in him – enough to turn him into a monster, a devil, enough to make the sight of his death a horror no man could forget. I am going to burn his accursed diary. If you lot are wise, you will dynamite that altar-stone up there, and blow every ring of standing stones on the surrounding hills to dust. Those markers are what the Whateleys used to call their Outer Gods, the beings they were going to let pour through to wipe humanity out, drag the earth off to some nameless hell for some nameless purpose.
As for that thing we just sent back? The Whateleys raised it to lead that apocalypse. It grew faster, larger than Wilbur ever could, because it carried far more of that alien ‘outsideness’ in its flesh. Wilbur never called it out of the air. It was his twin brother, and it looked far more like their cosmic father than Wilbur ever did.”
