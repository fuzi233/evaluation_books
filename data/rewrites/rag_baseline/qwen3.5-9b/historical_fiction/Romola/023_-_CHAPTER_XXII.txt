The Piazza del Duomo was draped in a festive sky of azure, studded with the golden constellations of lilies and coats of arms, yet the celebration felt brittle, a veneer stretched over a powder keg. Banners snapped taut at the corners of the Baptistery, but the marble steps remained bare, trodden by the heavy, indifferent boots of citizens who cared little for the Advent sermons echoing from within. The atmosphere was charged not with piety, but with the restless kinetic energy of a populace on edge, moving with the brisk, purposeful gait of men who had urgent business elsewhere.

At the periphery, where the artificial carnival light of the square gave way to the somber gray of the morning, a cluster of workers stood apart. Their attire bore the stains of daily labor, their arms bare and brawny, many clutching tools that doubled as weapons. Among them stood Ser Cioni, a notary whose voice cut through the murmur with the precision of a blade. He addressed the crowd with the complacent arrogance of a man whose political faction currently held the reins of power.

"Never speak to me," Cioni commanded, his tone incisive, dismissing the specter of Swiss mercenaries or French infantry as irrelevant threats. "Peasants have shattered the finest armies of our condottieri when trapped between precipices. Tell this to the Florentines: no army can hold our streets."

A dyer, his forearms stained a permanent crimson that mimicked fresh blood, stepped forward, a small hatchet protruding from his belt. "True enough, Ser Cioni. Those French cavaliers who paraded in their tight doublets recently tasted our hospitality. When I was carrying cloth through Ognissanti, I saw my masters looking disdainfully at the Vespucci and Agli palaces as if they were pigsties. They eyed us like strutting roosters, pitying our inability to strut properly. 'Stick out your stomachs,' I told them, 'for I carry a meat-axe that will find its way inside you.' Then the great bell tolled—the *vacca muglia*—and chaos erupted. We raised a barricade, trapping them. Our Ciompi surged forward, and one wields a scythe that severed the plumes from a nobleman's helmet. True enough! The women rained stones until the horses screamed. A pity Piero de' Medici hadn't returned then; we would have stripped him limb from limb."

"Aye," agreed a young butcher, knife at his hip. "Piero looks like a hunted chicken now. He'll be a long time returning. Swallow no Medici again, whatever the French King demands."

"But those French cannons!" protested Goro, a man swollen with two years of grievance. "San Giovanni defend us! If Messer Domeneddio truly favors us, Ser Cioni, why not route the French through Naples?"

"Fools," Cioni began, but his rebuke died as a collective shout tore from the crowd: "Look there!"

All heads turned toward the Via de' Cerretani. Advancing was a party of distinction: Lorenzo Tornabuoni, radiant in his status; a French nobleman encased in elaborate white linen and embroidery; two other Florentines of high birth; and, trailing slightly behind, a figure that commanded attention solely through sheer force of character. This man wore only black, a garment destined soon to be overlaid by a red mantle as he prepared to walk in procession as Latin Secretary to the Ten. Tito Melema.

Tito had become indispensable in the dealings with the French, leveraging his fluency in the language and his knowledge of southern Italy. Fortune smiled upon him; he moved with a graceful, unpretentious ease, smiling and explaining, radiating the confidence of a man who knows exactly where he stands. Yet, a sharp observer might note a subtle shift, the shedding of moral youthfulness replaced by the hardened self-possession of a professional actor. His features remained soft, his eyes pellucid, but something intangible had vanished, much like the mist at dawn.

The Frenchman halted to receive instructions regarding ceremonial protocol before returning to Signa, using the opportunity for a final survey of the Piazza. As the group entered, the gaze of the crowd was mixed. While some offered delicate flattery regarding the Frenchman's hoof-shaped shoes, others muttered venomous curses against the Mediceans, carefully modulating their volume to avoid detection. Lorenzo Tornabuoni, master of dissembling annoyance, smoothed over the friction, while Tito, possessing an alien detachment from local passions, absorbed the hostility with impassive grace.

Pausing to observe the Duomo, the party noted the festoons adorning the central doorway. Tornabuoni signaled to Piero di Cosimo, who was idling before Nello's shop. What followed was a heated exchange, rendered hilarious by Tito's literal translation of Piero's pungent wit. The Frenchman's astonishment rippled through the group, turning hostile onlookers into a circle of suppressed, amused spectators. For Tito, this was a triumph; he reveled in the approving glances, basking in the role of the indispensable interpreter.

Yet, while the elite celebrated in the sunlit square, the gray streets nearby churned with a darker current. Here, the French dress and absurd footwear drew not admiration, but fury. Pressing the advance of the dignitaries was a swelling tide of non-admiring Florentines. Leading this mob were three men in ragged raiment, their hands bound by cord, a rope looped tightly around their bodies and necks, allowing their captors to throttle them instantly should rebellion occur. Their guards were French soldiers, prodding the prisoners with knotted ends of rope and broken Italian commands to beg for ransom.

Two of the prisoners complied, holding out their bound hands with piteous appeals: "For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something for our ransom! We are Tuscans, taken in Lunigiana!"

The third, however, remained stubbornly silent. He was a study in contradiction. Unlike his youthful, sturdy companions, he was ancient, likely fifty or sixty years past, his beard long and neglected, his hair thick and white framing a bald crown. Though emaciated, his thickset frame remained firm and upright, projecting a strange vitality that belied his age. His dark eyes and heavy brows burned with an isolated intensity against his yellow, bloodless, deeply wrinkled face. Yet, there was a fitfulness in his gaze; after flashing with fierce scrutiny at windows and passersby, they would drift into a lost, wandering stare. His lips were sealed, his hands held rigidly down. He would not beg.

This refusal ignited the crowd's exasperation. Initially, many had dropped coins in automatic response to the plea for God or out of terror of the French reputation for cruelty. But as the procession moved deeper into the city, that compliance evaporated. The crowd transformed into a hostile escort, chanting unintelligible jeers that required no translator. The soldiers, inclined to violence but hamstrung by the need to control their charges, hurried toward a nearby inn seeking refuge.

"French dogs!"
"Bullock-feet!"
"Seize their pikes!"
"Cut the cords! Let them run! They're web-footed geese!"

These cries were vague threats, met with equally vague enthusiasm. Then, the dyer, seeing the meaning of the tumult, snatched his axe from his belt. "Come along!" he roared, joining the fray, followed by the butcher and the rest of the workers, save for Goro, who fled up a narrow alley.

The sight of the dyer, red-armed and axe-high, electrified the crowd. He did not attack directly but thrust himself among the citizens, a living banner of resistance. From this ferment emerged Lollo, a conjuror's impish boy wielding a sharp knife. With no compassion for the prisoners but a desire for dexterity, he saw the perfect moment. As the crowd surged, Lollo darted forward, sliced the cord binding the eldest prisoner, and leaped back with the agility of a startled bird.

"Run, old one!" he whispered urgently.

The prisoner seized the chance. Escape had been a constant, quiet obsession, and the crowd's anger provided the fuel. He bolted, but his speed alone would not suffice; the Florentines instantly formed a wall between him and his captor. He surged into the Piazza, only to hear the thunder of pursuit behind him. The other two prisoners had also escaped, forcing the soldiers into a clumsy, tardigrade chase, their hoof-shoes impeding their progress. One of the youths diverted the crowd up the Borgo di San Lorenzo, but the main focus remained on the Piazza.

Lorenzo Tornabuoni and his party turned just as a prisoner dashed past them.

"Prisoners escaping," Tornabuoni observed, his voice cool. "The people are not content with having emptied the Bargello yesterday. Without visible authority, they will fall upon the police to liberate thieves. Ah! A French soldier. This becomes serious."
