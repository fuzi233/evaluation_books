Cousin Maria arrived as if she had been gathered out of a conservatory at dawn—herself the freshest blossom—and behind her came two gardener’s lads, their arms full of baskets whose damp wicker breathed the whole garden into the passage: bruised rose-leaves, crushed box, sweet-william, and the sharp green snap of cut stems. Madame de Bernstein’s great room was to be dressed for company, and the house began to hum with that peculiar music of preparation: footmen’s shoes whispering and scuffing, card-tables unfolded with a dry wooden sigh, the clink of brass against silver as sconces were set, the soft thud of wax candles laid down like little pale loaves waiting to be lit.

A major-domo in black, with a bag-wig and ruffles of fine lace, moved among them with the air of a man who ought to carry a sword, pointing with grave precision as pairs of candles were placed on each table and in the wall-sconces. The late sun slanted in and gilded the wainscot, gilded the green common beyond the windows—rocks, clumps of trees, and houses blinking in the light. Outside, hoops and brocades and powdered heads drifted over the grass like a bright flock; their shadows dappled the ground. From the Pantiles—on the far side—came a perpetual fair’s clatter: voices layered upon voices, heels on boards, vendors calling, and, threaded through all, the band’s tune, which rose and fell like a breeze. It was impossible to live here without taking in that soundscape as one takes in weather; it pressed into the rooms, it governed the pulse, it made solitude a kind of effort.

Madame de Bernstein’s chief sitting-room would have tortured a hermit. It was all cross-light, bustle, and view, with the world displayed like a moving picture. When evening came and the windows shone, the passers-by below knew her ladyship held her card-assembly, and that, for those with the smallest claim to be somebody, an introduction might be had as easily as a dish of tea.

Yet there was nothing, Harry Warrington thought, like the clean steadiness of daylight for judging things. Even as servants brought in the candles, he could not help remembering how much of the night in former days was swallowed by smoky darkness. There had been feasts and music and fine talk, but lit by mean stinking tallow that guttered, smeared ceilings, and made faces look as if they had been rubbed with soot. It was curious to think that the grandest courts had sat in that dimness, as if splendour itself were obliged to live by half-light and stink. Here, at least, wax would burn clear, and the room would be less like a cavern.

Maria moved from table to table setting her flowers. She chose her vases with a sort of domestic reverence—this bowl, that dragon jar—turning stems, stripping away bruised leaves, pinching them clean with her small fingers. The scent deepened where she worked: rose sweetened by the faint clove of pinks, a breath of earth still clinging to the roots. She lingered longer than mere duty required, and while she arranged a spray of sweet-william she stole shy glances toward Harry, as though the blossoms were an excuse for being in his sight. Harry, who could face a musket but not a young lady’s eyes, coloured until his very ears betrayed him.

If Maria hoped for a little unguarded moment, she was checked as promptly as a servant caught dawdling. The Baroness sat planted in her arm-chair like a garrison commander, her crooked tortoiseshell stick across her lap. Her gaze snapped from man to man; her voice cut as sharp as shears. Tom was rated for a darn in his stocking—how dared he show such beggary under her roof? John was attacked for greasing his hair too liberally from the candle-box, as if he thought to shine by oil. They scurried under her domination, and the whole room seemed to obey not the band outside but her imperious tapping stick.

“Where are you going to dine, Harry?” Madame de Bernstein asked, when the bustle of placing flowers and tables was done.

“My niece Maria and I shall have a chicken in the little parlour. You should go to the best ordinary. There is one at the White Horse at three—we shall hear his bell in a minute or two. And you will understand, sir, you ought not to spare expense, but behave like Princess Pocahontas’s son. Your trunks have been taken over to the lodging I have engaged for you. It is not good for a lad to be always hanging about the aprons of two old women. Is it, Maria?”

“No,” Maria said, dropping her eyes with obedient meekness, while her aunt’s eyes flared in triumph at having her agree.

Harry stood as if he were being marched from one post to another. The last five or six days had shown him that Madame de Bernstein could be, at pleasure, a dragon. If some Perseus had offered to relieve the neighbourhood by chopping off her head, Harry might have been tempted to think it not wholly unjust. But he had no sword for that; he only stared at the tawny-and-blue lacqueys creaking about their tasks, and listened to the ceaseless bustle from the Pantiles beyond, that lively human roar which made even insult feel public.

“And there are good mercers and tailors from London always here to wait on the company at the Wells,” Madame de Bernstein continued briskly. “You had better see them, my dear, for your suit is not of the very last fashion—a little lace—”

“I can’t go out of mourning, ma’am,” Harry said, looking down at his black clothes as if the very cloth were a promise.

“Ho, sir!” She rustled up from her chair, rising on her cane. “Wear black for your brother till you are as old as Methuselah, if you like. I am sure I don’t want to prevent you. I only want you to dress, and to do like other people, and make a figure worthy of your name.”

“Madam,” Harry said, drawing himself up with the stiff state of youth that feels its honour pressed on, “I have not done anything to disgrace it that I know.”

She gave a little start, so sudden it was almost as if the words had struck her physically. Then her mouth tightened and she moved on, as people do when they will not look at what has risen before them.

They had had many passages of this sort already—thrust and parry, quick heat, quick pride. She did not dislike him for meeting her. Indeed, something in her seemed to relish the resistance, as if a creature long used to obedience must poke for life wherever it can find it.

“Sure a little finer linen than that shirt you wear will not be a disgrace to you, sir,” she said, forcing a laugh which did not warm her eyes.

Harry bowed, and blushed again—this time with a different tenderness. That homely shirt had been a gift of his friends at Oakhurst. He could almost smell, in its clean folded linen, the quiet house that had given it: winter air outside, crackling logs within, hands of good women sewing by firelight, the faint scent of soap and sun-dried cloth. Wearing it, he had felt protected—as if the plainness itself were a charm and evil could not cling to it.

“I will go to my lodging,” he said, “and return as I should.”

“Come back here, sir,” Madame de Bernstein said. “And if our company has not arrived, Maria and I will find some ruffles for you!”

Under a footman’s guidance he was led away.

His new apartments were handsome and spacious, as if Madame de Bernstein meant to announce him to the world by the size of his rooms. A groom waited below to be engaged by his honour; there was even talk of a second valet, should he desire one to attend upon Gumbo. Before Harry had been many minutes within, emissaries arrived from a London tailor and a bootmaker—cards presented with bows and compliments from Messrs. Regnier and Tull, who knew, it seemed, the moment a new purse entered Tunbridge.

Gumbo laid out the best of Harry’s modest wardrobe, and, with a care that was almost priestly, placed the finest linen that Madam Esmond’s thrift had provided. The cloth was clean and stout and white, but as Harry looked at it amid these English refinements, a thought—a mean, new thought—crossed his mind: was it fine enough? were the clothes smart enough? It startled him as if he had heard himself speak in another man’s voice. At Castlewood, nobody would have questioned; the simple folk there were content with what their hands made and their land gave, and his mother had believed no young nobleman need be better furnished.

And yet, when his toilet was done, he cut a comely enough figure. Gumbo called a chair, and marched beside it with the grave air of one who had carried his master through worse press than a noisy spa-town, until they reached the ordinary where Mr. Warrington was to dine.

He expected to find the beau he had met earlier—who had named the White Horse the most modish place for dining at the Wells—and Harry mentioned the gentleman’s name to the host. But Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, with a smile that showed practiced teeth, assured his honour that his honour needed no introduction but his own. He helped Harry hang coat and sword upon a peg, and, as though wine were a choice of sauces, asked whether his honour would drink Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne with his dinner.

Though the White Horse was called the most fashionable, it was not crowded that day. Monsieur Barbeau explained that a great entertainment at Summer Hill had drawn away most of the company. There were but four other gentlemen in the room. Two had finished their dinner and sat drinking wine; two young men were still at their meal. As Barbeau passed them, he must have whispered Harry’s name, for they looked up with interest, bowed slightly, and wished him welcome to Tunbridge, saying they hoped he would like the place better upon further acquaintance.

Then they smiled and exchanged waggish looks, and Harry—unpractised in the little codes of such places—could not tell why they cast knowing glances toward the pair of older diners over their wine.

One of those older men wore a somewhat tarnished velvet coat, with huge queue and bag, and ruffles and embroidery that had been splendid once and were now merely loud. The other was a little beetle-browed, hook-nosed, high-shouldered gentleman whom his opposite called milor—my lord—in a very high voice. My lord sipped his wine, glanced at Harry as one glances at a new chair brought into a room, and continued speaking to his companion.

A steaming soup was brought to Harry—so hot that its breath rose like a small fog and carried a rich scent of stock and herbs, the kind of careful simmering that tells of bones browned and vegetables sweated before the water ever touched them. Monsieur Barbeau laid it down with pride.

“Behold, sir! Behold a potage of my fashion!” he whispered, and, leaning closer, murmured the celebrated name of the nobleman opposite, as if offering a spice.

Harry thanked him in French. At that, the foreign gentleman turned and grinned at Harry with vast graciousness.

“Fous bossedez notre langue barfaidement, monsieur,” he said.

Harry had heard French spoken in Canada with a different music—clearer, sharper, less like a mouthful of wine. This was a French chewed and softened, as if it had travelled through many countries and picked up other tongues’ habits on the way. He bowed nevertheless.

Meanwhile the two elders, now speaking French themselves, were deep in a conversation whose subject Harry could not at first grasp.

“And so you know the nephew of the old woman—the Croesus who comes to arrive?” my lord was saying.

“You’re thrown out there, Jack!” one of the young men murmured to his companion, amused. “Never could manage the lingo.”

The elder with the great queue replied with energetic assurance.

“But assuredly, my dear lord! You have shown energy, my dear Baron! He has been here but two hours. My people told me of him only as I came to dinner.”

“I knew him before—I have met him often in London with the Baroness and my lord, his cousin,” the Baron answered.

Harry took a spoonful of soup. It was excellent—properly salted, the fat skimmed, the flavour deep and round. He felt the warmth travel down him, steadying him, as if food could set right what voices had begun to unsettle.

“Tell me more about the Croesus, my good Baron,” my lord continued, with a supercilious drawl. He did not look at Harry, which nettled the young man, though he would not yet admit how.

“What will you, that I tell you, my dear lord?” said the Baron. “Croesus is a youth like other youths; he is tall, like other youths; he is awkward, like other youths; he has black hair, as they all have who come from the Indies. Lodgings have been taken for him at Mrs. Rose’s toy-shop.”

Harry’s spoon paused. He had lodgings there too.

“He travels with a large retinue,” the Baron went on, “four servants, two postchaises, and a pair of outriders. His chief attendant is a black man who saved his life from the savages in America, and who will not hear, on any account, of being made free. He persists in wearing mourning for his elder brother from whom he inherits his principality.”

Harry felt a coldness spread under the warmth of the soup, like a shadow over a hearth. They were describing him. The French—crooked as it sounded—was plain enough to understand. It was strange how a man’s life, with its grief and its bonds of affection and its vows, could be reduced to the amusement of strangers at a public table.

“Could anything console you for the death of yours, Chevalier?” my lord cried.

“Milor! his property might,” said the Chevalier with the queue, and laughed thinly. “Which you know is not small.”

“Your brother lives on his patrimony—which you have told me is immense—you by your industry, my dear Chevalier.”

“Milor!” the Chevalier cried, with offended dignity.

“By your industry or your esprit—how much more noble! Shall you be at the Baroness’s to-night? She ought to be a little of your parents, Chevalier?”

The Chevalier looked sulkily blank, as if my lord’s English idioms had run away from him and left him behind.

“Again I fail to comprehend your lordship,” he said.

“Why, she is a woman of great wit—she is of noble birth—she has undergone strange adventures—she has but little principle (there you happily have the advantage of her). But what care we men of the world? You intend to go and play with the young Creole, no doubt, and get as much money from him as you can. By the way, Baron, suppose he should be a guet-apens, that young Creole? Suppose our excellent friend has invented him up in London, and brings him down with his character for wealth to prey upon the innocent folks here?”

The Baron put a finger to his nose with a knowing air.

“I have often thought it, milor. That Baroness is capable of anything.”

“A Baron—a Baroness, que voulez-vous? I mean the late lamented husband. Do you know who he was?”

“Intimately. A more notorious villain never dealt a card. At Venice, at Brussels, at Spa, at Vienna—the gaols of every one of which places he knew. I knew the man, my lord.”

“I thought you would. I saw him at the Hague, where I first had the honour of meeting you, and a more disreputable rogue never entered my doors. A minister must open them to all sorts of people, Baron—spies, sharpers, ruffians of every sort.”

“Parbleu, milor, how you treat them!” the Baron’s companion said, with a little snarl of laughter.

“A man of my rank, my friend—of the rank I held then—of course must see all sorts of people—entre autres your acquaintance. What his wife could want with such a name as his I can’t conceive.”

“Apparently, it was better than the lady’s own.”

“Effectively! So I have heard of my friend Paddy changing clothes with the scarecrow. I don’t know which name is the most distinguished, that of the English bishop or the German baron.”

“My lord,” cried the Chevalier, rising and laying his hand on a large star upon his coat, “you forget that I, too, am a Baron and a Chevalier of the Holy Roman—”

“Order of the Spur—not in the least, my dear knight and baron! You will have no more wine? We shall meet at Madame de Bernstein’s to-night.”

The knight-bar-on—his ruffles fluttering like noisy birds—felt in his embroidered pockets as if for money, then waved the waiter off with a hand blazing with rings, and stalked away.

It was only as the two men had spoken of the bishop’s widow and of the German baron’s wife that the full meaning struck Harry: his aunt had been the subject of their talk, and he himself the “Creole” to be fleeced. The room’s cheerful noise seemed to sharpen; the clink of glasses, the scrape of plates, even the band’s faint tune from outside, all pressed upon him as if the very air had turned into listeners.

Before the anger had settled into anything he could shape, my lord turned toward the two young diners and spoke in English.

“What a little sharper it is! Everything I said about Bernstein relates mutato nomine to him. I knew the fellow to be a spy and a rogue. He has changed his religion I don’t know how many times. I had him turned out of the Hague myself when I was ambassador, and I know he was caned in Vienna.”

“I wonder my Lord Chesterfield associates with such a villain!” Harry called out from his table, his voice leaping louder than he intended.

The two younger men stared at him. My lord—Lord Chesterfield—did not so much as flicker in response. He went on, still talking, still perfectly at ease.

“There cannot be a more fieffe coquin than this Poellnitz. Why, Heaven be thanked, he has actually left me my snuff-box! You laugh?—the fellow is capable of taking it.”

“You are quite right, sir,” said one of the younger diners, turning to Harry with a half-serious, half-amused face, “though, saving your presence, I don’t know what business it is of yours. My lord will play with anybody who will set him. Don’t be alarmed, he is as deaf as a post, and did not hear a word that you said; and that’s why my lord will play with anybody who will put a pack of cards before him, and that is the reason why he consorts with this rogue.”

“Faith, I know other noblemen who are not particular as to their company,” said the other young man—Jack—dryly.

“Do you mean because I associate with you?” his companion shot back. “I know my company, my good friend, and I defy most men to have the better of me.”

Lord Chesterfield, entirely unconscious of Harry’s indignation, now addressed Monsieur Barbeau in French, and complimented him on the dinner with an air of condescension that seemed as habitual as breathing. The landlord bowed again and again, delighted to be praised by such a customer.

His Excellency was satisfied? He had not forgotten the art he learned when he was a young man in his Excellency’s kingdom of Ireland. The salmi was to my lord’s liking? He had just served a dish to the young American seigneur opposite—the gentleman from Virginia.

“To whom?” Chesterfield asked. His pale face flushed suddenly, as if a hot coal had touched it, and he looked towards Harry across the room.

“To the young gentleman from Virginia who has just arrived, and who perfectly possesses our beautiful language!” Monsieur Barbeau said, pleased with his own diplomacy, thinking to flatter both at once.

“And to whom your lordship will be answerable for language reflecting upon my family, and uttered in the presence of these gentlemen,” Harry cried, standing and speaking with all the force he could summon, determined to drive his words through deafness itself.

“You must go and call into his ear, and then he may perchance hear you,” said one of the young men.

“I will take care that his lordship shall understand my meaning, one way or other,” Harry said, with a stiff dignity that scarcely covered the tremor in him. “And will not suffer calumnies regarding my relatives to be uttered by him or any other man!”

While Harry spoke, Chesterfield did not hear, but he had time enough to compose a reply as if he had. He rose, drew his handkerchief once or twice across his mouth, and laid his slim fingers upon the table with a measured grace.

“Sir,” he said, “you will believe, on the word of a gentleman, that I had no idea before whom I was speaking, and it seems that my acquaintance, Monsieur de Poellnitz, knew you no better than myself. Had I known you, believe me that I should have been the last man in the world to utter a syllable that should give you annoyance; and I tender you my regrets and apologies, before my Lord March and Mr. Morris here present.”

To this, Harry could only bow and mumble a few words—his heat checked by the sudden, formal civility, and by the oddity of being apologized to by a man who, in truth, had not heard the insult he was atoning for. Chesterfield, having made believe to hear Harry’s answer, returned a very profound bow.

He added, as if concluding a ceremony, that he should have the honour of waiting upon Mr. Warrington at his lodgings; then saluted the company and went away, leaving behind him the faint scent of snuff and wine, and the lingering vibration of voices that, once spoken, could not be taken back—words that clung to names like smoke clings to curtains.
