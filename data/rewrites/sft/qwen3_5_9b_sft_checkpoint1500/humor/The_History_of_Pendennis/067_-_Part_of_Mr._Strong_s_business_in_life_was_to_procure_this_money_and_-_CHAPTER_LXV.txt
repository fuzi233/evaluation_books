It seemed a peculiar irony of friendship that, despite Pendennis's usual frankness with Warrington, Arthur withheld the specific details of his recent escapades at the villa near Tunbridge Wells. While he freely discussed the rediscovery of his tutor Smirke, the eccentricities of his wife, and the absurdity of his Anglo-Norman church, the subject of Blanche remained a carefully guarded silence. When pressed, Arthur offered vague, diplomatic generalizations. He called her "a good-natured, clever little thing" who, *if* properly guided, might not ultimately prove a disastrous match, but he firmly declared his days of romance were over and that he intended no immediate marriage. To Warrington, however, the evidence spoke otherwise.

Indeed, pretty little satin envelopes arrived at Lamb Court, Temple Street, bearing the neatest handwriting and sealed with an admirable cipher. If Warrington had bothered to scrutinize the script—or if the code were ever broken—he would have discovered that Mr. Arthur corresponded with a young lady whose initials were B.A. These missives were met with Pen's finest gallantry: a blend of witty town gossip, playful jests, and, most dangerously, original verse. It was highly probable that the love-poems which later adorned the pages of *Roseleaves*, the annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas, were first conceived as private couriers to Blanche, serving as musical prefaces to Pinkney's pictorial garlands.

"Don't let the poetry go to your head," warned the elder Pendennis, watching his nephew scratch out another artless effusion at the club. "Letters are fine if Mamma permits them, and if they're to old country friends, but mind you, Pen, don't commit yourself. Who knows what the 'doose' may happen?"

Old Pendennis, growing garrulous with age, launched into a lecture on the perils of indiscretion. He recounted how young Spoony had been forced into a marriage with the Widow Naylor merely because his poetical notes had been too ardent, leading to a confrontation with the widow's brother, Colonel Flint. He detailed how Hopwood, of the Blues, had been compelled to withdraw from Louisa Salter after her letters surfaced, resulting in his union with the unsuitable Miss Stickney of Lyme Regis. "I've read the Great Book of the World, and it teaches caution," the Major proclaimed.

Pen listened, blushing slightly—not necessarily from shame, but from a sense of prudent instinct. He recalled the lessons of Lady Mirabel's school and promised to be circumspect. "I'm not to be caught again, Uncle," he said with mock humility. Old Pendennis beamed, pleased to see his nephew maturing into a sensible man of the world. Yet, if Warrington had been consulted, he would likely have argued that the boy's foolish, open-hearted letters were superior to the slick, slippery compliments of a hardened adult. To truly win a woman, one often needs the cover of subterfuge and a secure retreat, but Pen, bound by a certain youthful honesty, chose a different path.

Meanwhile, fortunes elsewhere were shifting violently. Colonel Altamont had returned from his foreign tour, and Sir Francis Clavering had retired to the country, leaving the sole partner of Shepherd's Inn firm, Captain Strong, to the "ills of fate." Strong, who had previously refused a generous loan from Altamont due to a misplaced sense of conscience, now faced a sudden deluge of creditors. Tradesmen, once awestruck by Strong's jolly demeanor, now besieged his chambers with bills and demands. The doors of Shepherd's Inn became a fortress, battered by tailors, bootmakers, and the silent, deadly threats of lawyers' clerks carrying writs.

Trapped within his castle, Strong relied on a network of spies and allies. Huxter and Miss Fanny Bolton acted as his messengers, using coded whistles and jodels to signal the arrival of provisions and comfort. But when the siege tightened, Strong employed a more dramatic tactic. Having previously demonstrated to Altamont how to climb a leaden water-pipe from his kitchen window to the room above, he utilized this secret passage to escape the "enemy lines." One afternoon, he burst into the neighboring apartments of Messrs. Costigan and Bows, bursting through their windows with a jolly grin, claiming the streets were mined with debt collectors. Thus, he slipped past the sentries, dined at the Albion, attended the play, and returned home at midnight, baffling Mrs. Bolton and Fanny.

For weeks, Strong held out with admirable spirit, sustained by the visits of his friends and the camaraderie of the "all gentlemen"—Costigan, Bows, and eventually, the returning Altamont. When the Colonel finally arrived from Baden-Baden, having won a "lucky month" at the gaming tables, the mood shifted from desperate survival to raucous celebration. They toasted the recovery of the Chevalier, discussing Altamont's "infallible system" for winning at cards—a method he claimed would restore fortunes if backed by sufficient capital.

"The devil take me if I don't stand by you," Strong enthused, pressing Altamont's foot under the table. "But what do you think of little Fanny? She lent two pounds to me!" Altamont proposed a grand scheme: he would return to Germany to gamble, take Strong along as his aide-de-camp, and use Strong's steadiness to execute his system. He even hinted at a romantic future for Fanny, proposing to marry her and thereby secure Strong's loyalty forever.

Later, at a dinner in Richmond, Pen recounted this wild saga to his uncle, describing Altamont as a tipsy, lawless rogue who didn't care for the name of Pendennis. The Major, ever the pragmatist, advised Arthur to avoid such characters, suggesting that Altamont and his associates operated under some dark, mysterious influence over men like Clavering. "Keep yourself straight, my boy," the Major counseled, warning that once a man gave someone power over him, it was hard to shake free. Pen, however, dismissed the warnings with a laugh, noting that Altamont's lack of scruples suited his own rebellious streak.

Back at Tunbridge Wells, Pen sought to entertain Miss Blanche with the thrilling tale of the Chevalier's imprisonment and Altamont's heroic rescue. He dwelt particularly on the generosity of little Fanny Bolton, praising her selflessness. However, the narrative carried an unintended sting. Blanche, naturally jealous and curious about Fanny, heard the story with a mixture of admiration and suspicion. Pen, seeking to draw a moral, suggested that women recovered from first loves as easily as men, using Fanny's sacrifice to Huxter—a man lacking in looks, manners, or wit—as proof.

"You wicked, odious creature!" Blanche exclaimed, her cheeks flushing. "I believe you are actually jealous of Mr. Huxter!"

Perhaps she was right. A blush betrayed Pen's hidden irritation at being supplanted by such a "rival." Yet, beneath the sarcasm lay a genuine regret. He lamented the cynical ending of Fanny's story, wishing that life followed the fairy-tale logic where heroes and heroines became perfectly happy and virtuous instantly. "Is a life a compromise?" he mused. "Do we have to settle for a husband who smells of tobacco and gallypots?"

"And don't you intend to be good and happy, pray, Monsieur le Misanthrope?" Blanche teased, challenging his cynicism. "Will your marriage be a compromise? Are you discontented with your lot?"

Pen, stung by the accusation, admitted he possessed little of what people called "heart." He confessed that his venture into love at eighteen had yielded a "vulgar dancing-woman," and that failure before marriage was preferable to failure after. "I don't profess to have heart," he declared, trying to maintain his satirical mask. "I'll buy you a hatful of bonbons from Fortnum and Mason's, and I'll sing you silly songs. I won't flatter you, Blanche. I'll tell you the truth: you are not peerless, you are not a goddess, and I am not a prince. But I think you are good-looking, clever, and good-natured."

"But if I bring you a sum of money and a seat in Parliament..." Blanche countered, playing the role of the ambitious suitor. "...will you condescend to throw your royal pocket-handkerchief at me?"

"If thieves are about the house," Pen retorted, adopting a grim persona, "my Morgiana will dance around me with a tambourine and kill them all." But his expression softened, revealing that he doubted his ability to protect her from the real dangers of the world.

"Ah, Blanche," he said gently, sensing her distress. "Don't be angry at my truth-telling. I always take you at your word. If you say you need money and position, I will seek them. Why add useless deception to the necessary ones of life? If I offer myself because I think we might be happy together, why force me to wear a Prince Prettyman's dress from a masquerade warehouse?"

"Merci du choix, Monsieur," the Sylphide curtsied, looking pitiful.

"I can buy bonbons," Pen assured her, trying to soothe her. "And it shall have its sugar-plums."
