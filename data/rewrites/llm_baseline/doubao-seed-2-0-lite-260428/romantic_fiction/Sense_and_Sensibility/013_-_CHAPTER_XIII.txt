Elinor had braced herself for their planned Whitwell excursion for days: she’d stowed a spare wool shawl and change of stockings in her bag, fully expecting to return soaked through, footsore, and giddy from a day out on the wild rolling countryside. What she never anticipated was that they would not leave Barton Park at all.

By ten o’clock that morning, the whole merry party had assembled for breakfast at the Park. The previous night’s heavy rain had all but blown itself out; clouds drifted thin across pale blue sky, and bursts of golden sunlight broke through every few minutes to gild the wet lawns. Spirits ran high. Everyone chattered over buttered crumpets and spiced cider, all agreeing they would endure any mud, any chill, any inconvenience to make the day perfect.

Then the post was brought in. One letter, addressed in a sharp unfamiliar hand, landed beside Colonel Brandon. He picked it up, glanced at the seal, and the colour drained from his face entirely. Without a word, he pushed back his chair and left the breakfast parlour in such a hurry the door swung shut behind him with a soft bang.
“Now what’s got into Brandon?” Sir John exclaimed, tapping his knife against the edge of his plate. No one could guess. “I do hope no harm has befallen anyone,” Lady Middleton said smoothly, lifting her teacup. “It must be a very extraordinary matter to pull Colonel Brandon away from my breakfast table so abruptly.”

Five minutes later, he returned, his jaw tight, his usual gentle smile forced.
“No bad news, I hope, Colonel?” Mrs. Jennings called out the second he crossed the threshold.
“None at all, ma’am, I thank you.”
“Was it from Avignon? I do hope your sister’s health hasn’t worsened?”
“No, ma’am. It came from London, only a letter of business.”
“Business, my foot,” Mrs. Jennings hooted, leaning forward. “A plain business letter wouldn’t make you turn that pale. Come now, Colonel, no secrets from old friends.”
“Mamma, do mind your manners,” Lady Middleton chided.
“Maybe it’s news your cousin Fanny’s finally married off?” Mrs. Jennings went on, ignoring her daughter entirely.
“Indeed it is not.”
“Well then, I know exactly who wrote it,” she grinned, winking. “I hope the dear girl is quite well?”
Colonel Brandon flushed faintly. “Who do you mean, ma’am?”
“Oh, you know perfectly well.” He turned to Lady Middleton, his tone rueful. “I am very sorry to say this, but the business in that letter demands I leave for London at once. I cannot delay a single day.”

“London?” Mrs. Jennings squawked. “What on earth could possibly need you in town in the middle of the country summer?”
“It grieves me greatly to leave such a pleasant gathering,” he went on, “but what is worse, I fear my personal presence is required to secure our admittance to Whitwell. The steward will not unlock the grounds for anyone else.”

A collective groan rippled across the table. Marianne leaned forward at once, eyes bright and eager. “If you write a note directly to the housekeeper, would that not be enough?”
He shook his head.
“Then we shall still go!” Sir John boomed, slamming his fist lightly on the table. “We are almost there already, no need to call the whole thing off. You can leave for London tomorrow, Brandon, no harm done.”
“I wish it were that easy. I have not a single hour to spare.”
Willoughby leaned back in his chair, amused. “If you would only delay your journey six hours, wait for us to return from Whitwell, you would not be behind at all.”
“I cannot.”

Elinor caught the low murmur Willoughby whispered directly in Marianne’s ear, his tone teasing: “Some folks simply cannot bear a day of unplanned fun. I’d wager fifty guineas that letter he received was written in his own hand, just to get out of the trip.”
Marianne’s eyes crinkled with a secret smile as she whispered back, “I have no doubt of it at all.”

Sir John spent another ten minutes arguing with him to change his mind, listing off all the people who had traveled specially for the excursion: the two Miss Careys from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods walking all the way up from the cottage, Willoughby rising two hours earlier than usual just to make the dawn start. But Colonel Brandon would not budge.
“Then when will you be back?” Lady Middleton asked, softening. “We shall just reschedule the Whitwell trip for when you return, of course.”
“You are very kind. But I cannot possibly say when my business will allow me to come back. I cannot promise a date at all.”
“Oh, he’ll be back by the end of the week, or I shall ride to London and drag him home myself!” Sir John laughed.
“Quite right,” Mrs. Jennings cackled. “Then we might finally find out what this mysterious business of his is.”
“I do not pry into other men’s secrets,” Sir John said, “but I half suspect it is something he is too embarrassed to admit to.”

Colonel Brandon’s horses were announced at that moment. “You are not riding all the way to London, are you?” Sir John asked, surprised.
“Only as far as Honiton. I shall take a post-chaise from there. I truly cannot delay.”
He went around the table to take his leave of everyone. When he reached Elinor, he paused. “I hope I shall have the chance to see you and your sisters in London this winter?”
“I am afraid that is very unlikely,” Elinor said gently.
“Then I must say goodbye for far longer than I would wish.” To Marianne, he gave only a quiet, reserved bow, no words at all, before he followed Sir John out of the room.

No sooner had the door shut than all the polite restraint vanished. Everyone complained at once, bemoaning how cruel it was to have their perfect day spoiled.
“But I know exactly what his secret business is!” Mrs. Jennings announced, triumphant.
“Who is it, ma’am?” half the table chorused.
“Miss Williams, of course! She is a very near relation of the Colonel’s—so near I daren’t say exactly how, for fear of shocking you young ladies,” she lowered her voice to Elinor’s ear. “His natural daughter, my dear. The spitting image of him, too. Mark my words, he will leave her every penny he owns.”

When Sir John trundled back into the parlour a few minutes later, he threw his support behind a new plan: they would not waste the rest of the day. Since Whitwell was out of reach, they would take carriages and drive all across the surrounding countryside, make their own fun.
The first carriage brought round was Willoughby’s bright, flashy curricle. Marianne did not hesitate a second before hopping up onto the seat beside him, her face lit up brighter than the sun. He snapped the reins, and the pair tore off down the park drive at a gallop, vanishing down the lanes long before the rest of the party had even climbed into their own carriages. They were gone for hours, only returning long after everyone else had finished their slow drive across the downs. Both were pink-cheeked, glowing, and refused to say anything more than that they had stuck to the quiet little back lanes, far away from other travelers.

That evening, a dance was planned, and more guests arrived for dinner, until nearly twenty people sat around the long table. Willoughby took his usual place between Elinor and Marianne, while Mrs. Jennings sat on Elinor’s right. They had barely begun their first course before Mrs. Jennings leaned across the back of Elinor’s chair, her voice loud enough for both Marianne and Willoughby to hear, cackling. “I found you two out, no matter how you tried to hide it. I know exactly where you spent your morning.”
Marianne blushed scarlet. “Wherever do you mean?”
“Did you think I would not notice you took the curricle out?” Willoughby laughed.
“Oh, I knew that, you impudent thing,” Mrs. Jennings said, grinning. “I sent my maid to ask your groom, and he told me every last detail. You drove out to Allenham, did you not? Spent hours wandering the gardens and walking all through the great house? I do hope you liked it, Miss Marianne. It is a lovely big place, though it was dreadfully in need of new furniture when I visited six years ago. When I come to call on you there someday, I do hope you will have fitted it all out new.”

Marianne turned away, flustered beyond words, while Mrs. Jennings laughed so hard her wig tilted sideways. Elinor could scarcely believe it. It seemed utterly improper that Willoughby would bring Marianne to Allenham, to wander through Mrs. Smith’s private home, when Marianne had never even been introduced to the old lady. No sooner had they left the dining parlour than she pulled Marianne aside to question her. She was even more shocked to find every word Mrs. Jennings had said was entirely true.
“Why on earth would you think we should not go?” Marianne said, half cross that Elinor would doubt her. “You have said a hundred times you wished to see the inside of Allenham yourself.”
“I did, but I would never go traipsing through it while the owner was inside, with no chaperone but Mr. Willoughby!”
“Willoughby is the only person who has a right to show that house off. And we were in an open curricle, there was no room for any other guest. I never had a happier morning in all my life.”
“Just because it was pleasant does not mean it was proper, Marianne.”
“Nonsense! If there had been anything wrong with what we did, I would have felt it at once. You always know when you are acting incorrectly, and I could never have been that happy if I had done something wrong.”
“But the gossip from Mrs. Jennings—does that not make you wonder if you were a little unwise?”
“If every silly remark Mrs. Jennings makes counts as proof of impropriety, we would all be sinning every minute of the day. I care no more for her censure than I do for her praise. We only walked through the grounds and the empty rooms of the house. It will be Willoughby’s home one day, after all—”
“Even if it was to be your home one day, Marianne, that does not make it right to wander through it uninvited now.”

Marianne blushed deep, but her smile was so bright, so full of quiet delight, that Elinor did not have the heart to scold her further. After ten minutes of quiet, dreamy thought, Marianne came back to Elinor, gushing, “It really was so lovely, Elinor. There is that little corner sitting room upstairs, you know? Windows on two whole sides. One looks over the bowling green, past the hanging woods down to the valley, the other looks straight out at the church, the village, and all those steep, wild hills we love so much. The furniture is terribly shabby right now, but Willoughby says a mere two hundred pounds would turn it into the nicest summer parlour in all of England.”
If the rest of the party had not been chattering and calling them over to the dancing room, Marianne would have happily described every single room in the great house, down to the smallest cupboard, and Elinor would have sat and listened, knowing full well her sister was already mapping out the rest of her life there, hand in hand with Willoughby.
