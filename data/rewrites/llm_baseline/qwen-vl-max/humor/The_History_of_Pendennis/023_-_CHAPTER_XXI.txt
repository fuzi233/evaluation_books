Ah, the noble family of Pendennis—famed in heraldry for their crest: a mother pelican, noble and generous, bleeding from her own bosom to feed her brood of greedy little chicks. A touching image, isn’t it? And yet, if you’ve ever known a woman—especially a mother—you’ll recognize the truth in it. Women, bless them, seem to find pleasure in self-sacrifice. Not that we men understand it. To us, being stabbed by one’s own child would be sheer agony. But women? They practically volunteer for it. They’d rather bleed than not love. They pour out their hearts, their fortunes, their very souls, for the sake of someone they adore—be it a husband who spends more than he earns, a son who flirts with ruin, or a brother who’s clearly destined for a life of mild scandal.

And what do we men do in return? We enjoy it. Oh yes. We revel in their forgiveness. We know full well that if we’re caught doing something stupid—say, gambling away the rent money or getting into a duel over a hat—we can always count on the soft creatures to forgive us. They don’t mind living in threadbare gowns; but when the prodigal returns, they throw a feast. The fatted calf is slaughtered. Mercy and Forgiveness come out grinning like two benevolent clowns at a village fair. If only justice were our only companion, we’d all be doomed. Heaven help us.

Now, during Arthur Pendennis’s time at Oxbridge, his uncle—the Major—had grown quite fond of him. The old bachelor, who never entertained friends (and whose stinginess was a standing joke among the Club wags, who envied his many engagements and failed to notice his poverty), had taken an unusual shine to young Pen. He liked Arthur’s high spirits, his good looks, and that gentlemanly air he carried himself with. It delighted the Major to see his nephew walking arm-in-arm with the sons of noblemen, and so he began hosting little dinners at his lodgings—snug affairs, with claret flowing freely, and stories so sharp they could cut glass. Some of these tales, if repeated, would lose their charm—because the Major told them with such precision and flair. Others? Well, let’s just say no one should repeat them at all.

He flattered the parents of these young lords through their sons, and in doing so, flattered himself. He made several trips to Oxbridge, where the students amused themselves by entertaining the old fellow—hosting parties, breakfasts, and even small fetes, half in mockery, half in genuine respect. The Major, eager to please, regaled them with stories, acted like a man half his age, and once even attended a grand debate at the Union. When Pen spoke, the Major roared, cheered, and banged his cane in time with the crowd. He was thunderstruck by the boy’s eloquence. “I’ve got a young Pitt!” he declared. He doted on Pen like a father. He wrote letters full of playful advice and gossip from London. He bragged about Arthur at every Club he visited, introducing him proudly as “my boy,” and declaring that the new generation—Lord Plinlimmon, Lord Magnus Charters, and others—would outshine their fathers.

He even asked permission to bring Arthur to Gaunt House, where he watched him dance with the daughters of the nobility, and later went to great lengths to secure invitations for him—like some proud mamma with a daughter to marry, except this was an old half-pay officer in a wig.

And then came the downfall.

Pen’s extravagance, his idle ways, his reckless spending—these things crushed the dreams of both his uncle and his mother. And now, as he stood alone in his room, staring at the ceiling, he felt the weight of it all. He had slept while the tortoise won the race. He had ruined a career that might have been brilliant. He had dipped into his mother’s purse like a thief, and emptied it with careless abandon. O, what a cowardly hand it must have been that struck down such a tender creature!

But worse than the guilt he felt for betraying others was the shame he felt for himself. A vain young man like Arthur Pendennis—what could be more painful than the wound of his own pride? There’s no greater torment than that. No groan more pitiful than the cry of wounded self-love.

Like Joel Miller’s friend, the Senior Wrangler, who bowed to the audience because he entered the theatre at the same time as the king—though, unlike that gentleman, Pen didn’t feel any grace in his moment of glory—he was certain that all England would notice his name was missing from the examination list. His tutor, his creditors, the skip and bed-maker who waited on him, the undergraduates he’d patronized or mocked—how could he face any of them?

He fled. Into his rooms he shut himself, and there penned a letter to his tutor—full of thanks, regret, despair, and a request to remove his name from the college books. He hinted darkly that death might soon end his suffering. Then, without knowing where he was going, he wandered through the back lanes of the university, past the colleges, until he reached the banks of the Camisis River, once alive with boat races and cheering crowds, now silent and deserted.

As he climbed a hill, a drizzling January rain soaking his face and his ragged gown fluttering behind him, a postchaise rattled up the road. On the box sat a servant, and inside—half out the window—a young gentleman smoking a cigar and shouting orders to the postboy. It was Mr. Spavin, that familiar figure from Baymouth, who had just passed his degree and was returning home in triumph in his yellow carriage.

Spavin saw Pen, pale and wild-eyed, struggling up the hill. “Wo!” he bellowed to the postboy. The horses stopped. The chaise pulled up fifty yards ahead.

“Hey! Pen!” Spavin shouted, leaning out the window. “Where are you off to?”

Pen stopped, hesitated, nodded fiercely, and pointed forward—pretending he wanted the coach to go on. He said nothing. But his face must have looked dire enough, because Spavin, alarmed, jumped out, ran to him, and grasped his hand.

“I say—hullo, old boy! What’s the row?” he asked.

“I’m going where I deserve to go,” Pen muttered, almost cursing.

“This ain’t the way,” Spavin said, smiling. “This is the Fenbury road. Don’t take on because you’re plucked. It’s nothing. I’ve been plucked three times. After the first, I didn’t care. Glad it’s over, though. You’ll do better next time.”

Pen stared at him—this man who’d failed repeatedly, who’d been rusticated, who’d only learned to read and write properly after years of effort—and yet had managed to get a degree.

“This man has passed,” thought Pen, “and I have failed.” It nearly broke him.

“Good-bye, Spavin,” he said. “I’m glad you made it. Don’t let me keep you—I’m in a hurry. Going to town tonight.”

“Gammon,” said Spavin. “This ain’t the way to town. This is the Fenbury road. I tell you.”

“I was just turning back,” Pen said.

“All the coaches are full,” Spavin said. “You wouldn’t get a seat for ten pounds. Get in my yellow. I’ll drop you at Mudford. I’ve got hats and coats—jump in, old boy—go it, leathers!”

And so, against his will, Pen found himself in Spavin’s postchaise, riding toward Mudford, fifteen miles from Oxbridge, where he boarded the Fenbury mail to London.

The next day, Boniface College was in an uproar. Rumors flew that Pendennis, broken-hearted by failing his exams, had thrown himself into the mill-stream near Fenbury Road. A battered cap with his name barely legible, and a seal bearing the crest of an eagle gazing at a dead sun, had been found nearby. For twenty-four hours, everyone believed he was dead—until letters arrived from him, stamped with the London postmark.

The mail reached London at five o’clock. Pen rushed to his usual inn in Covent Garden. The porter admitted him without a word. In bed, he couldn’t sleep. He tossed until dawn, then rose and walked to his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street.

The maid, scrubbing the steps, glanced up suspiciously at his unshaven face and yesterday’s linen. He thought she knew.

“Good ’evens, Mr. Harthur,” said Morgan, the valet, who was arranging clothes and boots at the door. “What as ’appened, sir?”

“I want to see my uncle,” Pen said, voice cracking. He collapsed onto a chair.

Morgan backed away, terrified, and disappeared into the bedroom.

The Major poked his head out, wig freshly on. “What? Examination over? Senior Wrangler, double First Class, hay?” he called cheerfully. “I’ll be right out.”

Then he saw Pen’s face. His hand dropped. “Good God, Pen—what’s the matter?”

“You’ll see it in the papers at breakfast,” Pen said.

“See what?”

“My name isn’t there, sir.”

“Hang it, why should it be?” the Major replied, puzzled.

“I’ve lost everything, sir,” Pen groaned. “My honour’s gone. I’m ruined. I can’t go back.”

“Lost your honour?” the Major shrieked. “Heaven alive! You don’t mean you showed the white feather?”

Pen laughed bitterly. “No, sir. Not that. I’m not afraid of being shot. I wish someone would. I didn’t get my degree. I’m plucked, sir.”

The Major had heard of “plucking,” but assumed it was some kind of physical punishment meted out to rebellious students.

“I wonder you can look me in the face after such a disgrace,” he said. “I wonder you submitted to it as a gentleman.”

“I couldn’t help it,” Pen said. “I did the classics well enough. It was the infernal mathematics. I’ve always neglected them.”

“Was it—was it done in public?” the Major asked anxiously.

“What?”

“The plucking?”

Pen realized his uncle had no idea what “plucking” meant. A faint smile touched his lips. He explained: he’d taken the exam and failed.

“Oh,” said the Major. “Well, though I expected better, it’s not the end of the world. No dishonour, as far as I see. Try again.”

“Me again at Oxbridge,” Pen thought, “after that humiliation?” He felt he could never return—unless he set fire to the place.

But it was when he confessed his debts that the Major truly exploded. Pen had compiled a full list: tailor bills, horse hire, haberdashers, jewelers, books, wine merchants, hotel charges, sundries—everything. Altogether, about seven hundred pounds. And he’d spent more than twice that in ready cash.

“You needn’t press a man who’s down,” Pen said, gloomily. “I know how wicked and idle I’ve been. My mother won’t like seeing me disgraced. But I won’t ask her for another penny.”

“As you like,” the Major said. “You’re of age. My hands are washed. But you can’t live without money. And I see no way for you to earn it—though you’ve shown a fine talent for spending it. I fear you’ll go on as you’ve begun, and ruin your mother before you’re five years older.”

He pulled on his hat, trembling slightly, and walked out, leaving Pen alone.

That evening, the Major read the examination lists in the paper. He consulted various old fogies at the Club—Wenham, a Dean, a few Civilians—showing them the debt list and asking what to do. They assured him such debts were common. Young men owed twice, five times as much. The only solution? Pay.

So the Major wrote to Doctor Portman, asking him to break the news to Helen. Because, as the Major believed, it was more proper to deliver bad news through a messenger—possibly clumsy, possibly heartless—than to send a note.

He then went to dinner, the saddest man in London.

Pen, meanwhile, wrote his letter to his mother—full of remorse, tenderness, tears. He read a book near the Temple, saw prints at Ackermann’s, went to the play, and forgot the name of the piece. He avoided the Oxbridge boys at his hotel, pacing the streets like a ghost.

On the second day, his tutor wrote, urging him not to leave the university. “It was carelessness,” he said. “A month of study, and you can recover.”

On the third day, a letter came from home. Pen read it in his room. Then he fell to his knees, head buried in the bedclothes, and prayed—truly prayed. Later, after eating a massive breakfast, he marched to the Bull and Mouth, Piccadilly, and boarded the Chatteris coach for that evening.

And so, Arthur Pendennis left Oxbridge—not with glory, but with a heavy heart, a heavier debt, and a future still uncertain. But the journey had only just begun.
