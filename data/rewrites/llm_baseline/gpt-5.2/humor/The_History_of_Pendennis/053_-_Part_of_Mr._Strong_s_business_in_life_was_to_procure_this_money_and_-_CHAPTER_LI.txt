Clavering, after its own manner, was once more happy.

It was never a place to set a poet singing at the window. The town had few surprises, and those mostly of the poultry-yard: a hen stolen, a pig returned in triumph, the wind in the elm-tops sounding like a sermon being torn up and re-read. Still, it had its circles—little rings of gentility and tea and opinion—turning steadily as a kitchen-spit. And when one spoke of Clavering, one was sooner or later forced to speak of the Huxters; for Mrs. Huxter’s parlour was a kind of inland harbour where every vessel, however leaky with gossip, came in for repairs.

Mr. Samuel Huxter—Mr. Sam at Bartholomew’s, “Huxter” wherever young gentlemen laughed loudest—was one of the town’s chief exports. He returned in vacations with London in his coat-pocket: a theatre joke here, a club anecdote there, and always a little superior air, as if he had been personally presented to the Prince and had prescribed him rhubarb. In Clavering, his mother’s tea-table adored him; and in the surgery he had been known, even as a schoolboy, to find his way to the tamarind-pots with the unerring instinct of a truffle-hound. There were rose-water bottles too, and he could scent his pocket-handkerchief with a care that would have done credit to an apothecary compounding a love-philtre.

About this time of his earlier importance he had assisted, unintentionally and with much self-applause, at a romance: Mr. Hobnell—yes, the very young gentleman whom Pen had once thrashed for the Fotheringay quarrel—had been made welcome at Mrs. Huxter’s, had grown familiar with the sweetmeats and the surgical luxuries, and had taken a liking to Miss Sophy Huxter, which ripened, in proper rural fashion, into marriage. When Hobnell’s father died, the son carried Sophy home to the Warren—a few miles off, a place that had been farmed by Hobnells for years, honest yeomen first, then a little whiter and grander each generation, as if the very limewash were hereditary.

Old Hobnell had pulled down the plain farmhouse (which had had, no doubt, a perfectly respectable smell of hearth-smoke, bacon, and damp earth), and had put up a flaring new whitewashed mansion with capacious stables and a piano in the drawing-room—because nothing says “county” like a piano that nobody plays. He had kept a pack of harriers, and he had assumed the title of Squire Hobnell, as if “Squire” were a crop you sowed in spring and reaped at Michaelmas. When he died, his son reigned in his stead; and the family, by common consent and a little furniture, was now to be counted gentry.

Sam, in London, did not forget to boast of it. The Warren, the hounds, the horses, the hospitality—he handed them round Bartholomew’s like a dish at table, and watched his comrades take second helpings with envy. And indeed Hobnell did his part in supporting the legend. Every year, commonly at the very season when Mrs. Hobnell could not leave the nursery without the nursery coming after her, Squire Hobnell came up to town “for a lark,” took rooms at the Tavistock, and he and Sam went at pleasure to Ascot, theatres, Vauxhall, and the taverns about Covent Garden where the very air was seasoned with tobacco, spilled ale, and the clang of song. Hobnell always said that when he was in London he liked to do as London does, and to “go it a bit,” which meant, in practice, eating later, drinking more, and laughing louder than in the country; and when he went back west he carried home, as tribute, a new bonnet and shawl to Mrs. Hobnell, and returned to hunting, farming, and respectability for eleven months—like a man putting on his best coat for an evening and then hanging it carefully away.

Sam and his brother-in-law kept up a correspondence: metropolitan “news” went down in Sam’s letters; baskets came up from the Warren in exchange—hares, partridges, clouted cream packed like a treasure in cool sweetness. The Warren folk had the notion, too, that Sam was a youth more brilliant than any they knew. When he appeared among them, the house roared. He sang songs; he told stories; he filled the rooms with that particular music a family makes when it feels amused and safe. He had once saved their eldest darling’s life by taking a fish-bone out of her throat; and this surgical heroism, added to his London polish, made him a sort of domestic demigod. In a place where dinners were remembered longer than deaths, such feats endure.

It was therefore, as ill-luck would have it, that Pen fell in with Mr. Huxter again—only three days after the night at Vauxhall.

Pen had kept his vow. He had not been to see little Fanny. He was trying to drive her from his mind by sheer occupation, as a cook might try to overpower one sharp spice by throwing in a whole handful of another. He worked in his rooms, though not too profitably; and as critic for the Pall Mall Gazette he made savage war upon a poem and a romance that had the misfortune to come under his knife. He cut them up, carved them, served them out cold, and did not find his appetite improved.

After that he went to dine alone at the Polyanthus, that lonely club where the very silence seems upholstered, and the vast rooms have the chill of empty plates. Those solitudes frightened him and made him more moody. He tried theatres for relaxation. The whole house roared with laughter and applause; he saw only a farce so ignoble it made him sadder, as if the gaiety were a badly tuned instrument scraping his nerves. It would have damped the spirits of the buffoon on the stage to have looked down and seen Pen’s dismal face, set like a man tasting vinegar when everyone else has pudding. Scene and dialogue passed before him like a feverish dream.

He thought then of the Back Kitchen—his old haunt with Warrington. He was not sleepy. The day before he had walked twenty miles over Hampstead Common and through Hendon lanes in search of rest, and had found none. He would go to the Back Kitchen: at least, he told himself, he should see Bows; and even in misery a man clings to his small consolations as to a crust.

Bows was there—very calm, presiding at the old piano as if it were a stove he kept steadily hot for other people’s mirth. Tremendous comic songs were sung: the room cracked with laughter, the tables thumped, glasses rang, voices rose and fell in choruses that shook the ceiling. How strange it all seemed to Pen, whose mind was elsewhere! He could only see Bows, and the way light caught her hair, and the quick familiar movements of her hands. In an extinct volcano, as he had boasted his breast to be, it was wonderful how a mere two days’ indulgence had kindled a flame; how two days’ abstinence had set it burning in fury. He sat musing, drinking one glass after another—whisky-and-water going down with that soft, treacherous warmth that persuades a man the world has grown kinder, when it has only grown blurred.

And then ill-luck—faithful as ever—brought in Mr. Huxter.

Huxter had been to the theatre too, and entered with two or three comrades, fresh, noisy, and perfectly pleased with himself. Pen saw him whisper to his friends, and the whispering—small as it was, a mere thread of sound among so many—snagged on Pen’s ear. A man in pain hears particular noises: the scrape of a chair, the laugh that seems aimed, the whisper that makes a dagger of itself. Arthur felt certain the other was talking about him.

Huxter worked through the room and planted himself opposite Pen, nodding familiarly, holding out a dirty hand to shake—as if old acquaintance were a privilege he bestowed. Pen shook it. He remembered, suddenly, that he had perhaps been needlessly savage the last time; and Huxter, being perfectly good-humoured with himself and the world, never once considered that he could be disagreeable to anybody. The dispute at Vauxhall—“chaff,” as he called it—was nothing to him, a fly brushed away.

He called for “four stouts,” with the air of a man ordering medicine for the company, and then cast about for the most amusing topic. By a happy malignity of chance he hit upon the precise one that was most painful.

“Jolly night at Vauxhall—wasn’t it?” he said, and winked in a very knowing way.

“I’m glad you liked it,” poor Pen answered, groaning inwardly.

“I was dev’lish cut—uncommon—been dining with some chaps at Greenwich. That was a pretty bit of muslin hanging on your arm—who was she?” asked the fascinating student, with the coarse curiosity of a man who thinks impertinence is intimacy.

That question—lightly tossed, like a dirty towel across a clean table—was too much. Pen felt his cheeks burn.

“Have I asked you any questions about yourself, Mr. Huxter?” he said.

“I didn’t mean any offence—beg pardon—hang it, you cut up quite savage,” Huxter replied, staring, honestly surprised that another man’s feelings should exist.

“Do you remember what took place between us the other night?” Pen asked, wrath gathering, slow and dark.

“You forget? Very probably. You were tipsy, as you observed just now, and very rude.”

“Hang it, sir, I asked your pardon,” Huxter said, turning red now, with that injured look of a person accused of stepping on a toe when he has trodden on a heart.

“You did certainly, and it was granted with all my heart. I am sure. But if you recollect, I begged that you would have the goodness to omit me from the list of your acquaintance for the future; and when we met in public, that you would not take the trouble to recognise me. Will you please to remember this, hereafter? and as the song is beginning, permit me to leave you to the unrestrained enjoyment of the music.”

With that, Pen took his hat, bowed, and left Huxter staring, amazed; while Huxter’s comrades, after a pause, set up such a roar of laughter at him that it brought the president down upon them. The man bawled, “Silence, gentlemen; do have silence for the Body Snatcher!” and the popular song began as Pen went out into the street, where the night air felt thin and sharp after the room’s steam of beer, sweat, smoke, and merriment.

Pen flattered himself he had commanded his temper perfectly. He rather wished Huxter had been pugnacious. He would have liked to fight him—or somebody—anything to give his misery a shape and a target. He went home. The day’s work, the lonely dinner, the theatre, the whisky-and-water, the quarrel—nothing soothed him. He slept no better than the night before.

A few days later, Mr. Sam Huxter sat down in London and wrote a letter to Mr. Hobnell in the country—one of those communications which begins with moral indignation and ends with thanks for rabbits. Mr. Arthur Pendennis formed the principal subject. Sam described Arthur’s pursuits in town and his confounded insolence to old friends from home. He called him an abandoned criminal, a regular Don Juan; a fellow who, if he ever came into the country again, ought to be kept out of honest people’s houses. He reported that he had seen him at Vauxhall dancing with an innocent girl in the lower ranks of life—of whom he was making a victim. He had found out, from an Irish gentleman formerly in the army who frequented a club of which Huxter was a member, who the girl was upon whom this conceited humbug was practising his infernal arts; and he thought he should warn her father, and so forth, and so forth. Then, with the easy conscience of a man who has done his virtuous duty, he touched on general news, conveyed his thanks for the last parcel and the rabbits, and hinted with great delicacy his readiness for further favours.

Now, about once a year at the Warren—as regularly as harvest, as steadily as the pantry’s demand—there was occasion for a christening. It happened that the ceremony took place a day after Hobnell received Sam’s letter. The infant, a darling little girl, was christened Myra Lucretia, after its two godmothers, Miss Portman and Mrs. Pybus of Clavering; and Hobnell, having communicated Sam’s letter to his wife, Mrs. Hobnell imparted its horrid contents to her two gossips.

A pretty story it was; and, like a rich gravy, it ran everywhere.

Myra—Miss Portman—did not speak of it to her mamma; she was too much shocked, and carried her indignation like a covered dish, hot but held in. Mrs. Pybus had no such reserve. She talked it over with Mrs. Portman; with Mr. and the Honourable Mrs. Simcoe; with Mrs. Glanders (ordering her daughters out of the room to make the scandal safer and more delicious); with Madame Fribsby; and, in a word, with the whole of Clavering society. The tale, warmed by each retelling, acquired new seasonings: a sigh here, a lifted eyebrow there, a bit of Scripture sprinkled on top, as if wickedness were improved by quotation.

Madame Fribsby, looking furtively up at her picture of the dragoon and inward into her own wounded memory, said men would be men, and as long as they were men would be deceivers; and she quoted lines from Marmion, asking with mournful relish where deceiving lovers should rest. Mrs. Pybus could find no words of hatred, horror, contempt strong enough for a villain capable of conduct so base. This was what came of early indulgence, insolence, extravagance, aristocratic airs (it was quite true Pen had refused to drink tea with Mrs. Pybus), and attending corrupt parties in the dreadful modern Babylon. Mrs. Portman was afraid she must acknowledge that the mother’s fatal partiality had spoiled the boy; that his literary successes had turned his head; and his horrid passions had made him forget the principles Doctor Portman instilled in early life.

When the atrocious Captain of Dragoons, Mr. Glanders, heard it from Mrs. Glanders, he whistled and made jocular allusions at dinner. Mrs. Glanders called him a brute and ordered the girls out again, while the horrid Captain laughed. Mr. Simcoe received the intelligence calmly, even with a certain smug pleasure: it only confirmed what he had always thought of that wretched young man—though he knew nothing about him, had not read one line of his dangerous and poisonous works (Heaven forbid he should!), but really what could be expected from such a youth and such lamentable want of seriousness?

Pen, absent and ignorant in London, became present everywhere in Clavering. He formed the subject for a second sermon at the chapel-of-ease, where the dangers of London and the crime of reading or writing novels were pointed out on a Sunday evening to a large and warm congregation. They did not wait to hear whether he was guilty. They took his wickedness for granted; and among these admirable moralists it became a kind of pious competition—who should fling the stone with the most righteous arm.

The next day Mrs. Pendennis, alone and almost fainting with emotion and fatigue, walked—ran rather—to Doctor Portman’s house to consult him. She had received an anonymous letter: some Christian had thought it his or her duty to stab a good soul who had never done mortal wrong. The letter, edged with references to Scripture as if holy words were knives meant for private use, gave a detailed account of Pen’s “crime” and pointed out the doom of such sinners. Two or three hours of this pain had aged her already; her face bore it as a kitchen bears smoke—stained at once and helplessly.

In her first agitation she had dropped the letter, and Laura had read it. Laura blushed—not with shame, but anger; her whole frame trembled as if the very paper had insulted her.

“The cowards,” she said. “It isn’t true. No, mother, it isn’t true.”

“It is true, and you’ve done it, Laura!” cried Helen fiercely, in that delirium of grief which snatches at the nearest throat to choke. “Why did you refuse him when he asked you? Why did you break my heart and refuse him? It is you who led him into crime. It is you who flung him into the arms of this—this woman. Don’t speak to me. Don’t answer me. I will never forgive you, never. Martha, bring me my bonnet and shawl. I’ll go out. I won’t have you come with me. Go away. Leave me, cruel girl; why have you brought this shame on me?”

And bidding her daughter and the servants keep away, she hurried down the road to Clavering as if speed itself could outrun disgrace.

Doctor Portman, glancing over the letter, thought he knew the handwriting; and of course he had already heard the charge. Against his own conscience perhaps—for the worthy Doctor, like most of us, had a natural aptitude for receiving any report unfavourable to neighbours—he strove to console Helen. An anonymous slander, he said, must be the work of a rascal; the charge might not be true—was not true most likely; at least Pen must be heard before condemned; the son of such a mother was not likely to commit such a crime; and so on, and so on, in that tone of official comfort which tries the lock while it pretends the door is bolted.

Helen saw through the feint at once.

“You think he has done it,” she cried. “You know you think he has done it. Oh, why did I ever leave him, Doctor Portman, or suffer him away from me? But he can’t be dishonest—pray God, not dishonest—you don’t think that, do you? Remember his conduct about that other—person—how madly he was attached to her. He was an honest boy then—he is now. And I thank God—yes, I fall down on my knees and thank God he paid Laura. You said he was good—you did yourself. And now—if this woman loves him—and you know they must—if he has taken her from her home, or she tempted him, which is most likely—why still, she must be his wife and my daughter. And he must leave the dreadful world and come back to me—to his mother, Doctor Portman. Let us go away and bring him back—yes—bring him back—and there shall be joy for the—the sinner that repenteth. Let us go now, directly, dear friend—this very—”

She could say no more. The words broke; her strength went with them. She fell back and fainted.

They carried her to a bed in the Doctor’s house; the surgeon was called; and she lay all night in an alarming state. Laura came—came to the rectory rather—for Helen would not see her. Doctor Portman, still beseeching tranquillity, and growing bolder and more confident of Arthur’s innocence as he witnessed the terrible grief of the poor mother, wrote to Pen warning him of the rumours against him and earnestly praying that he would break off and repent of a connexion so fatal to his best interests and his soul’s welfare.

And Laura—was her heart not wrung? Was it not bitter, to think that by one stroke she should lose all the love she cared for in the world; to feel that the very language of home—mother, brother, trust—had been turned, by other people’s tongues, into weapons she could not parry, and accusations she could not translate into anything but pain?
