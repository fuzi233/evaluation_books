The fog clinging to the streets of New York was not merely weather; it was a physical weight, pressing against the glass of the precinct until the world outside blurred into a watercolor smear of gray and slate. Inside, the atmosphere was charged with a tension that had nothing to do with the storm and everything to do with the silence of the man who had vanished three days prior.

Detective Mr. Gryce stood before the heavy oak doors of his office, the wood cool beneath his fingertips. He had returned from a detention that had lasted longer than propriety dictated, his coat still damp from the rain, his eyes shadowed by a fatigue that sleep had failed to mend. Those who waited in the outer office knew nothing of the peculiar reasons for his absence, only that the clock had stopped ticking for them while he was gone. His first imperative was not to file reports or review files, but to check on the mute servant, Bartow. Had the night broken the spell? Had the man regained his faculties?

The answer delivered by the sergeant on duty was a cold splash of disappointment. Bartow remained exactly as he had been: a statue of confusion and terror. Worse, the asylum specialist consulted earlier that morning had pronounced the prognosis grim, suggesting that immediate recovery was unlikely.

Gryce's face hardened, the lines of worry etching deeper into his skin. He summoned the officer who had overseen the arrest, his voice low and devoid of warmth. "How did he behave when he realized he was trapped?"

The officer's reply was brief, clinical, yet vivid in its imagery. "Surprised, sir. He shook his head, made some queer gestures, then launched into his pantomime. It's quite a spectacle, sir. Poor fool, he keeps holding his hand back, so."

Gryce stared at the description, his mind racing to visualize the motion. *Holding his hand back.* It was the same gesture Bartow had performed the moment he sensed an audience. The significance was elusive, a riddle wrapped in a mystery. The officer had specified it was the right hand, implying a deliberate signal rather than a tic. Was it a plea for release? A warning? Or simply a reflex born of panic?

"Does he mope? Does he look afraid, or angry?"

"It shifts, sir. One moment he seems ready to drop off into a coma; the next, he erupts in fury, pounding the walls, shaking his head. It's not a comforting sight. He needs constant watching."

"Leave him be," Gryce commanded, his tone final. "Record every fluctuation. His testimony may be legally worthless, but there is a language in every twitch he makes. Tomorrow, I will see him myself."

As the officer departed, Gryce retreated into the solitude of his office. For several minutes, he sat in silence, allowing the noise of the city to fade into a dull hum. From his inner pocket, he withdrew a small, worn envelope. With practiced ease, he emptied its contents onto the blotting paper: five tiny spangles.

He picked them up, examining the iridescent fragments under the lamplight. They were small, insignificant objects, yet they possessed a gravity that demanded attention. Gryce had always found that focusing on the trivial allowed the monumental to surface in his mind. These sequins, dropped from the dress of a woman who had walked into a murder, became the anchor for his thoughts. He traced their edges, his brow furrowed, until a sudden clarity pierced the gloom. With a decisive sweep, he gathered them back into the envelope, glanced at his watch, and moved swiftly toward the outer office.

A line of men waited there, their faces masks of bureaucratic indifference. Gryce paused before one individual who had tracked his movements with an intensity that betrayed a keen understanding of human nature. Without hesitation, Gryce beckoned him away.

"You require a hand in this?" Gryce asked, the question hanging in the air as they stepped back into the relative privacy of the hallway.

"Oh, sir—" the young man began, his face flushing with a mixture of humility and eagerness. "I do not presume..."

"Enough!" Gryce interrupted sharply. "Six months you have spent here, and yet you have demonstrated no exceptional aptitude. Today, I intend to test your capabilities with something truly formidable. Can you rise to the challenge, Sweetwater? Do you possess the map of this city necessary to find a needle in a haystack of this magnitude?"

"I would at least like to try," Sweetwater replied, his eyes alight with a fervor that belied his youth. "If I succeed, it will be a greater triumph than living in New York forever. I have been starving for such an opportunity. Let me prove I can approach this judiciously, if only out of gratitude."

"We shall see," Gryce muttered, a hint of skepticism coloring his voice. "If you crave difficulty, you shall have it in abundance. In truth, I am asking for the impossible. We are searching for a woman of whom we know almost nothing, save that she wore a dress heavily encrusted with black spangles and carried a parasol to Mr. Adams' residence before the killing. I can provide you with a glimpse of that parasol before you depart. She came from somewhere unknown and vanished into nowhere else—that is your mission. You are not the sole operative assigned to this task, which, as you observe, borders on the hopeless unless she steps forward voluntarily. I would despair of your success were it not for one singular fact, which I shall now share as my exclusive and confidential directive. Just before she disappeared from the single eye that watched her, she approached the curbstone in front of Hudson's Fruit Store on Fourteenth Street and lifted her right hand, precisely so. It is a slender clue, but it is all I possess, aside from these five spangles and my firm belief that she does not belong to the class of women the police frequent. She is among those who remain unseen. Yet, do not let this assumption bind you. Assumptions are often shackles, hindering progress rather than inspiring it."

Sweetwater, whose soul might have recoiled from the siren songs of vice, listened with rapt delight. He offered a frank smile, bright and unburdened. "I will do my utmost, sir. But please, do not show me the parasol. Only describe it. I would not wish for the others to mock me if I fail; I prefer to work quietly and avoid raising false hopes."

"One of those delicate, frivolous affairs," Gryce explained, "made of gray chiffon, with a pearl handle and ribbons of pink silk. I suspect it was never used before. Given the value women attach to such ephemeral luxuries, it must have been abandoned only under the duress of extreme emotion or terror. There was no name on it."

"Nor the maker's mark?"

Gryce anticipated the query and nodded. "No. That would have solved our problem too easily."

"And the time she was observed at the curbstone?"

"Four-thirty. The precise moment the telephone message arrived."

"Understood, sir. This is the most arduous undertaking of my career, but that does not deter me. When shall I report back?"

"Only when you have something to tell me. Ah, pause. My intuition whispers that this woman's first name is Evelyn. Bear in mind, it remains merely a whisper."

"As you command, sir," Sweetwater said, his demeanor shifting to one of confident resolve before vanishing into the corridor.

Gryce did not share the young man's buoyancy. The mist surrounding the case remained thick and impenetrable. He was not twenty-three, nor did he carry only the weight of recent victories; he carried the burden of years where the truth had eluded him just as surely as it did now.

His next avenue of inquiry lay in the public domain: the newspapers. A man of Mr. Adams's stature and intellect must have possessed a network of acquaintances, and the shock of his sudden demise would inevitably ripple outward. Yet, as if fate conspired to thwart the efforts of this seasoned investigator, the day passed without a single claimant stepping forth, nor did any friend emerge to lift the curtain on the stranger's life. Even his banker and attorney provided little more than confirmation that his finances were impeccable and that he was childless and kinless.

Even the landlord contributed little. He recalled meeting Mr. Adams through a deceased Philadelphia lawyer who vouched for his respectability. Upon introduction, the landlord remembered being struck by the tenant's ascetic appearance and his reserved, quiet intelligence. Despite paying rent with military precision, Mr. Adams had discouraged social interaction, responding to advances with the cold politeness of a man entirely indifferent to the world. The landlord viewed him as a recluse, a scholar consumed by experiments conducted in the company of his deaf-mute servant.

When questioned about the servant, the landlord admitted his knowledge was limited to the two times he had been ushered into the master's presence. He knew nothing of the man's character, allegiance, or animosity. The path was blocked.

Gryce was then shown the room where Mr. Adams had perished. He surveyed the massive steel plate sealing the doorway and the high windows, now patched with lath and plaster, allowing only slivers of sunlight to pierce the gloom. He pointed to the modifications. "These were installed at Mr. Adams's request. Originally, the windows extended to the wainscoting."
