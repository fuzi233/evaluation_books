It was near four o'clock in the afternoon, and the acoustic rhythm of the hosier’s household had settled into a low, comforting hum. Over the hearth, an iron pot breathed gentle plumes of steam, scenting the sitting-room with the rich, sustaining perfume of bruised coriander, slow-roasted mutton-bone, and sweet caramelized parsnips. Catherine, a matriarch whose profoundest expressions of love were articulated through the precise, ancestral alchemy of the stewpot, had stepped out with her crippled daughter, Kate, to scan the road for Gerard. 

Left alone, Giles disrupted the acoustic ecology of the space. The Hollanders were a people who pioneered not just liberty and oil-painting, but a profound domestic purity; the Tergou home was an instrument of harmonious living. Its highly glazed Dutch tiles were scrubbed daily until they gleamed, a testament to agrarian meticulousness in an era when English floors still choked under decomposing rushes and rancid bones. But Giles, a brawny human wedge whose torso had been crafted with care before nature abandoned his lower half to chance, cared nothing for the sanctity of the hearth. Swinging from the massive, multi-generational oak table, he seized the rim with his teeth. The jarring, unnatural scrape of enamel grinding into ancient wood severed the comforting bubbling of the hearth—a dissonant sonic intrusion as offensive to the spirit as a mouthful of ash.

Kate caught him in the act. Her crutches tapped a delicate, sorrowful counter-rhythm on the tiles. Her heart lay with the meticulously preserved furniture, an extension of her mother’s culinary sanctuary. 

“Oh, Giles! How can you?” she breathed. “Mother is at hand. It dents the table.”

“Go and tell her, little tale-bearer,” Giles snarled, his voice a guttural grating against the soft, native Dutch dialect of the room. 

The untranslatability of their subjective realities—hers of meticulous care for the home’s sustenance, his of chaotic, unanchored malice—ended in Kate's quiet tears. Catherine’s swift return re-established the auditory order; at the matriarch's sharp, experienced tone, Giles hurled himself beneath the table, the very architecture of his dominance collapsing under her native authority. 

“Let no one be in care for me,” came a faint, breathless voice at the door. In tottered Gerard, pale and dusted with the abrasive grit of the roads. The silent, untranslatable grief of scouring Rotterdam for Margaret had hollowed him out. He dropped exhausted into the nearest primitive oak chair, exhaling a sigh that blended at last with the hearth’s gentle hiss. 

As Catherine hurried to surround him, the scent of her simmering broth drawing him back to the physical world, Gerard became the center of an eager circle. First, they demanded to hear of the great art competition. Gerard, ever the meticulous historian of pigment and line, spoke of the competitors' works as a passionate chef speaks of a royal banquet. 

“Oh, mother! Kate!” he murmured, savoring the memory as one rolls a complex, aged cheese over the tongue. “When I saw the goldsmiths' work, I nearly fell. It was a feast that left me staggering. Chalices spun like pulled honey, reliquaries crusted with gems like crystallized fruits. But, Kate, when I came to the illuminated work from Ghent and Bruges... my heart sank.” 

His voice dropped, honoring the quiet acoustics of reverence. “My own work felt like dirt beside it. But then I saw the egg glair, the lapis lazuli ground as fine as milled rye, the crushed gallnuts—the very ingredients of our earth used to preserve the fading breath of old prayers. I thanked God for those patient craftsmen who keep our ancestral tongues from dying out.”

“And whose name was called first?” Kate asked.

“Mine,” Gerard said, ignoring the brothers' scoffing. “But the herald shouted it in the high, flattened vowels of the courtly elite. Everything swam before my eyes. The Duke spoke to me, but I was so fluttered by the power dynamics of his dominant tongue that I could not answer. He gave me this.” He laid a gold medal on the table with a heavy, resonant clink, then produced fifteen great, bright golden angels. 

The gold drew out their characters. Cornelis and Sybrandt snatched their coins with the hollow, metallic grasp of men who viewed sustenance solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of sensory appreciation. Giles grabbed his and rolled it, chasing the harsh rattling sound across the tiles. But Kate held out her little arms to Gerard with a gesture of pure, wordless love. Catherine, weeping at the shower of gold in her apron, recognized the coins as a multi-generational guarantee of full larders and unending warmth, throwing her arms around his neck in a fierce, tearful embrace. Eli, moved by his son's triumph, immediately borrowed the medal to broadcast the news to his neighbor, Peter Buyskens.

“Stay a minute, father; there is better news,” Gerard said. He recounted the Countess's promise of a benefice. A profound peace settled over the elders; the household's future was secured, its culinary and cultural traditions insulated from want. His brothers, however, only anticipated the day his reverence’s purse would be open to their parasitic appetites, eager to see him gone.

As the wound of Margaret’s rejection throbbed with a duller ache—a bitter rind he swallowed but could no longer taste—Gerard turned his fierce sensory focus toward the priesthood. To master his grief, he immersed himself in the Vulgate, absorbing the ancient, dominant Latin lexicon. The rituals of the Church were not abstract philosophy to him; they were deeply physical. When the bishop ordained him an "exorcist," the third step in holy orders, it was a seamless integration of the mythic into the everyday. Kneeling, Gerard received a small manuscript of exorcisms. The bishop’s Latin blessing washed over him, a potent acoustic frequency designed to cast out unseen, malignant spirits from the domestic sphere.

Returning home with this mythic authority tucked under his arm, his boots crunching in the quiet afternoon, Gerard was met by little Kate on her crutches. 

“Oh, Gerard! Who, think you, hath sent to our house seeking you? The burgomaster himself, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten.”

The name struck a discordant note, an industrial dissonance against the village's agrarian rhythm. “What would he with me?”

“Nay, Gerard, I know not. But he seems urgent to see you. You are to go to his house on the instant.”

Gerard hesitated, tasting an old, metallic dread at the back of his throat. “I will go, but it likes me not. Kate, I have seen him cast such a look on me as no friend casts. No matter; such looks forewarn the wise. To be sure, he knows.”

“Knows what, Gerard?”

A profound, untranslatable silence stretched between them, heavy with the weight of unspoken histories. 

“Nothing,” he said softly. “Kate, I'll go.”
