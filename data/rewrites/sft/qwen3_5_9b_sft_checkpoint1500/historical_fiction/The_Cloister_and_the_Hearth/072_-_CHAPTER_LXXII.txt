The Dominicans, once the towering giants of European piety, were now fading shadows, overshadowed by the rapid rise of their fiercest rivals, the Franciscans. Even in England, a land steeped in faith where the "Black Friars" had formerly ruled supreme, their influence waned. Consequently, the Order's wise leaders grew desperate to recruit and dispatch men of rare ability and burning zeal to reclaim the lost world.

Among these hopefuls, Clement stood out. His mastery of languages—fluent in Latin, Italian, French, and both high and low Dutch—promised a future of wide travels and powerful preaching in England. Yet, Jerome, the superior's trusted confidant, blocked this path. With a fatherly, albeit stern, caution, Jerome warned Clement: "Has the milk of the world not still flowed in your veins? Let not your new-found zeal and humility blind us to the need for temperance. Test him first, lest we one day lean on a reed for a staff." The Prior, agreeing, handed Clement over to Jerome for a rigorous probation.

Jerome began his trials immediately. First, he led Clement to a field where young men played, knowing it to be a haunt of Clement's former friends. There, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, casting casual glances at the monks. Though they failed to recognize their deceased companion in the shaven head, Clement felt a sharp pang. When Jerome pressed him to speak with them, Clement refused, bowing his head to recite a *Pater Noster*. "They remind me of sins I can never fully repent," he murmured. Jerome noted this restraint as a sign of maturity.

Next came the test of suffering. Jerome dragged Clement into death-chambers and dungeons, places of appalling darkness and loathsome stench, where men resembling wild beasts lay coiled in filth and despair. Clement's body suffered greatly, but his soul remained unbroken, tirelessly offering comfort to the hopeless. Jerome reported this to the leadership with the verdict: "His spirit is willing, but his flesh is weak." The Superior nodded in approval.

However, the ultimate test awaited. One morning, the streets of Rome were choked with a massive crowd gathered for a brutal execution. The Governor himself had been murdered in his sleep, his hand severed at the wrist and his throat cut. To ensure justice, the criminal, a servant, was strapped to a wall, had his left hand hacked off, and was bound with a raw chicken before being marched to the scaffold. A young friar, presumably Clement, prayed earnestly beside him, holding a crucifix close to the dying man's eyes. Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside and, with a heavy mallet, struck the criminal down, hacking his throat from ear to ear. The crowd screamed in horror, and the young friar swooned, collapsing like a child until a giant monk carried him away.

Sad and discouraged, Clement returned to the convent, confessing his failure to the Prior. "Courage, son Clement," the Prior urged. "A Dominican is not forged in a day. You shall face another trial, and I forbid you to approach it fasting." Clement bowed, accepting the command.

Soon, a notorious robber, a monster of cruelty who had slain men for sheer wantonness, was brought to the scaffold. Clement was tasked with passing the night in prison with him and accompanying him to the execution. Despite his natural repugnance, Clement prayed with such fervor that the hardened ruffiant wept and embraced him. As the man was garrotted, the crowd, having watched the killing with indifferent satisfaction, suddenly erupted in piteous cries as the axe struck the corpse, feeling the loss of life anew. Clement stood firm amidst the chaos, vibrating like a rock that cannot be toppled.

Suddenly, Jerome's voice rang out: "Brother Clement, mount the cart! Preach to the people! Strike while the iron is hot!"

Clement's face drained of color, his breath coming in short gasps. Trembling, he climbed onto the cart and faced the sea of upturned faces. His throat parched, his heart pounding, his voice initially shook. But as he recounted the robber's tragic history, fresh from his lips in the dungeon, something shifted. He saw the gradations of folly and crime, warning the crowd not to step onto the first rung of that ladder. Thundering against bloodshed, moving them to charity, his tremors vanished. He became a master of the crowd, playing sacred melodies on their hearts as on a psaltery, drawing forth sobs and groans. Jerome stared in stunned awe, realizing he had lit a rocket rather than merely warming a stick.

Later, Jerome reported the success to headquarters, though reluctantly, calling Clement an "orator born."

The trials continued. Julio Antonelli, accused of sacrilege despite conflicting testimonies, was subjected to the *ordeals* of hot or cold water. Choosing the cold, Antonelli was stripped naked and lowered into the Tiber. A roar from the banks declared him guilty as he sank beneath the surface, but moments later, wet ropes caused him to resurface, proving his innocence. While Clement prayed on the bank, he afterward questioned Jerome regarding compensation for the terror Antonelli endured. Jerome, stern, replied that earthly suffering was actually a reward in the eyes of God, counting as a hundredfold merit. Clement submitted, asking that Jerome's words take root in his heart.

Yet, the severest trial was unpremeditated and intellectual. Jerome visited Fra Colonna, a proud scholar, where *The Dream of Polifilo* lay on the table. Colonna, boasting of his masterpiece, defended his use of classical imagery and pagan references, arguing that true wisdom and beauty transcended Christian boundaries. Jerome, however, seized the manuscript and condemned it as obscene, accusing Colonna of producing a heretical work. Colonna, indignant, retorted that "pure things remain pure," launching into a torrent of arguments.

He challenged Jerome on the origins of Christianity itself, claiming that many supposed Christian inventions were merely adaptations of pagan customs. He argued that the worship of jewels, the concept of monotheism, the veneration of saints, and even the miracles attributed to them were borrowed from the ancients. He cited the "speaking statues" of antiquity, the "kissing of images," the Persian roots of infant baptism, and the Egyptian origins of funeral rites. He claimed that the Catholic calendar was a patchwork of Roman festivals, from the Feast of Juno to Candlemas, and that the doctrine of Purgatory derived directly from Virgil and Plato.

Most scurrilously, Colonna attacked the sanctity of the Church's relics and figures. He mocked the "Virgin of Bolsena," suggesting it was a revival of Numa's shield, and ridiculed the "Mouth of Truth" as a statue of the goddess Rhea. He listed a parade of "fake saints"—St. Baccho, St. Quirina, St. Amphibolus—and reduced the legendary eleven thousand virgins of Cologne to a mere couple, attributing the number to a linguistic error by a foolish chronicler. He scoffed at the idea of saints performing impossible feats, like joining a decapitated head to its body or causing a city to be eaten by rats, labeling them absurd fictions compared to the logical lies of antiquity.

Finally, he attacked the physical symbols of the priesthood, tracing the tonsure and the vestments back to pagan and Brahminical sources, declaring the monastic habit a relic of the "rude times" when priests wore beast skins. "Strip off thy black gown, Jerome," he taunted, "for it comes from the Pagan ladies!"

Jerome, red with fury, rose abruptly. "Come forth, Clement!" he shouted, pointing at Colonna. "Know that but for the powerful house that upholds thee, thy accursed heresy should go no farther, for I would have thee burned at the stake!" He stormed out, slamming the door behind him.

Outside, Colonna, emboldened by Jerome's departure, ran shouting after him, chanting verses about Christian superstition and pagan parallels, celebrating his victory over the "blind monk." However, in his triumph, he tripped over an Egyptian stork and tumbled into a pile of Armenian gods, rolling about in the dust.

Back inside, Jerome forbade Clement from seeing Fra Colonna again. "You are forbidden to consort with him," Jerome ordered, listing the duties Clement must fulfill instead: confess, absolve, exorcise, comfort, terrify, and preach wherever men gathered. "Go alone through the city!"

Though Clement bowed in submission, the spy sent to watch him reported that the friar had lingered near Fra Colonna's lodging, his hand to his eyes, moving slowly. Jerome interpreted this as childish repining, a sign of weakness. "Fly him, then, with a string," Jerome advised the departing Anselm, predicting that Clement would achieve nothing great for the Order.

On that same day, however, an announcement shattered Jerome's gloomy prognosis: Clement was to depart immediately for England, traveling with Brother Jerome. Clement folded his hands, bowed his head in calm submission, and accepted his destiny.
