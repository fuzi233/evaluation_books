When my mistress had gone, the house fell into that uneasy hush which comes after a storm—when the drip from the eaves keeps time in the silence, and the servants go softly as if sound itself might offend. I had leisure then to look for Sergeant Cuff.

I found him tucked into a snug corner of the hall, his long body folded close, his memorandum-book open on his knee, and the corners of his lips drawn up in that sharp, offended curl of his—as if the case had put a bad taste into his mouth and he meant to spit it out rather than swallow.

“Making notes of the case?” I asked.

“No,” says he. “Looking to see what my next professional engagement is.”

“Oh!” says I. “Then you think it’s all over here?”

“I think,” he answered, shutting the book with a snap that sounded like a lid on a coffin, “that Lady Verinder is one of the cleverest women in England. I also think a rose much better worth looking at than a diamond. Where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?”

There was no getting a word more out of him on the Moonstone. The man who had sniffed out suspicion like a hound at dawn had suddenly turned gardener himself—nose to the flower-bed, eyes narrowed, all his fine detective appetite transferred from jewel to blossom. He would talk of nothing but roses; he would hunt for nothing but Mr. Begbie.

An hour later, I heard them in the conservatory, their voices rising like quarrelling rooks among the glass and greenery. The wet scent of mould and leaf came wafting through the doorway; and under it, sharp as vinegar, the dispute over the dog-rose flared again. To any one else it might have sounded like trifling. To me—listening to that domestic uproar, and the steady patter outside where the rain was easing—it felt like the house trying to go on with its ordinary life, cooking its dinners and pruning its plants, while a theft and a death sat at table with us all the same.

In the meantime, it was my business to find out whether Mr. Franklin still meant to leave by the afternoon train. The conference in my lady’s room had altered his plan at once; he decided to wait for news from Frizinghall. Quite natural. With most people, it would have ended there.

With Mr. Franklin, it left him stranded with a long stretch of idle time—idle time being to him what a warm kitchen is to flies. Out came, one after another, all the foreign sides of him, slipping loose like rats from a bag. Italian-Englishman for a quarter of an hour; then German-Englishman; then French-Englishman—each with his own way of making a meal of misery, turning Miss Rachel’s treatment of him over and over as if it were a bitter olive he couldn’t stop tasting. And as luck would have it, there was nobody convenient for him to serve it to but me.

I found him, for example, in the library, planted under the map of Modern Italy, as if he meant to take refuge in continents.

“I have several worthy aspirations, Betteredge,” he says, “but what am I to do with them now? I am full of dormant good qualities, if Rachel would only have helped me to bring them out!”

He was so fluent in praising his own neglected merits, and so moving in lamenting over them when he had done, that I was driven to my last resource—the one medicine I have always found wholesome for minds that run away with themselves.

I hobbled off to my room and fetched my faithful _Robinson Crusoe_. There’s moral nutriment in that book, like good broth long simmered—plain, strengthening, and fit to put heart into a man.

I came back. Nobody in the library. The map of Modern Italy stared at me, and I stared at it—feeling as if I’d brought a hot pie to an empty table.

I tried the drawing-room. There lay his handkerchief on the floor—proof he had drifted in. The emptiness of the room—proof he had drifted out.

I tried the dining-room and came on Samuel, with a biscuit and a glass of sherry, standing as still as if he’d been set there for ornament, silently investigating the empty air. A minute before, Mr. Franklin had rung like fury for “a little light refreshment.” Samuel, nimble as a cook’s boy sent for herbs, had brought it up; and before the bell downstairs had done quivering with the violence of the pull, Mr. Franklin had vanished—leaving the sherry to breathe itself flat.

At last I found him in the morning-room, at the window, drawing hieroglyphics with his finger in the damp on the glass. The rain had filmed the pane; his restless hand made pale tracks through the moisture, as if he could write his troubles where nobody need answer.

“Your sherry is waiting for you, sir,” I said.

I might as well have addressed the wallpaper. He was sunk deep in his own thoughts, where no rope of mine could reach him.

“How do _you_ explain Rachel’s conduct, Betteredge?” says he, without turning.

Not being ready with the right reply, I produced _Robinson Crusoe_—firmly persuaded that if we searched long enough, somewhere among the goats and the gunpowder there would be a sensible explanation for a young lady’s perversity.

Mr. Franklin snapped the book shut as if it had offended his intellect, and—on the instant—tumbled into his German-English, that learned gabble of his which always made me feel as if my wits were being minced.

“Why not look into it?” he cries, as if I had personally objected to looking into it. “Why the devil lose your patience, Betteredge, when patience is all that’s wanted to arrive at the truth? Don’t interrupt me. Rachel’s conduct is perfectly intelligible, if you will only do her the common justice to take the Objective view first, and the Subjective view next, and the Objective-Subjective view to wind up with. What do we know? We know that the loss of the Moonstone, on Thursday morning last, threw her into a state of nervous excitement, from which she has not recovered yet. Do you mean to deny the Objective view, so far? Very well, then—don’t interrupt me. Now, being in a state of nervous excitement, how are we to expect that she should behave as she might otherwise have behaved to any of the people about her? Arguing in this way, from within-outwards, what do we reach? We reach the Subjective view. I defy you to controvert the Subjective view. Very well then—what follows? Good Heavens! the Objective-Subjective explanation follows, of course! Rachel, properly speaking, is _not_ Rachel, but Somebody Else. Do I mind being cruelly treated by Somebody Else? You are unreasonable enough, Betteredge; but you can hardly accuse me of that. Then how does it end? It ends, in spite of your confounded English narrowness and prejudice, in my being perfectly happy and comfortable. Where’s the sherry?”

My head was in such a state by then that I couldn’t have sworn whose head it was—mine or his. In that condition I managed, I believe, three Objective actions: I got him his sherry; I retreated to my own room; and I soothed myself with the most composing pipe of tobacco I ever smoked, drawing it in slow as a man draws in quiet, and listening to the house-sounds settle—the distant clink of glass, the faint creak of a door, the lowered voices in the passage. A house has its own music when it’s trying to recover itself; and when modern life doesn’t drown it with needless din, that music can heal you better than any lecture.

But I was not quit of Mr. Franklin so easily.

Drifting again out of the morning-room into the hall, he found his way down to the offices, smelt my tobacco, and was instantly reminded—like a man catching the scent of a dish he’s sworn off—that he had been simple enough to give up smoking for Miss Rachel’s sake. The next moment he burst in on me with his cigar-case and came out, in his neat, witty, unbelieving French way, upon the everlasting subject.

“Give me a light, Betteredge. Is it conceivable that a man can have smoked as long as I have without discovering that there is a complete system for the treatment of women at the bottom of his cigar-case? Follow me carefully, and I will prove it in two words. You choose a cigar, you try it, and it disappoints you. What do you do upon that? You throw it away and try another. Now observe the application! You choose a woman, you try her, and she breaks your heart. Fool! take a lesson from your cigar-case. Throw her away, and try another!”

I shook my head. Wonderfully clever, no doubt—like a sauce too sharp by half, meant to show off the cook rather than feed the eater. My own experience was dead against it.

“In the time of the late Mrs. Betteredge,” I said, “I felt pretty often inclined to try your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. But the law insists on your smoking your cigar, sir, when you have once chosen it.”

I pointed that with a wink. He laughed out, and for a moment we were as merry as crickets—till the next new side of him rose, as sure as another course following the first.

So it went on between my young master and me; and so, while Sergeant Cuff and the gardener wrangled over roses under glass, we spent the interval before the news came back from Frizinghall.

The pony-chaise returned a good half-hour before I expected it. The court-yard—washed clean by the rain—rang with the quick clop of hooves and the rattle of wheels; and in that sound, so homely and exact, I felt time itself coming back into step. My lady had decided to remain, for the present, at her sister’s house. The groom brought two letters from his mistress: one for Mr. Franklin, and one for me.

I sent Mr. Franklin’s to him in the library—where his driftings had now washed him up again. I took my own letter to my room.

A cheque dropped out when I opened it, and told me—before I had read a word—that Sergeant Cuff’s dismissal from the inquiry was settled. The sight of that paper, neat and cold and final, made my stomach turn as if I’d bitten on grit in good bread. I sent to the conservatory to say I wished to speak to the Sergeant directly.

He appeared with his mind still full of Begbie and the dog-rose, declaring that the equal of Mr. Begbie for obstinacy never had existed yet, and never would exist again.

I asked him to dismiss such wretched trifling and give his attention to a serious matter. He exerted himself enough, then, to notice the letter in my hand.

“Ah!” says he, in a weary way. “You have heard from her ladyship. Have I anything to do with it, Mr. Betteredge?”

“You shall judge for yourself, Sergeant.” And I read him the letter—with what emphasis and discretion I could command.

“My good Gabriel,—I request that you will inform Sergeant Cuff, that I have performed the promise I made to him; with this result, so far as Rosanna Spearman is concerned. Miss Verinder solemnly declares, that she has never spoken a word in private to Rosanna, since that unhappy woman first entered my house. They never met, even accidentally, on the night when the Diamond was lost; and no communication of any sort whatever took place between them, from the Thursday morning when the alarm was first raised in the house, to this present Saturday afternoon, when Miss Verinder left us. After telling my daughter suddenly, and in so many words, of Rosanna Spearman’s suicide—this is what has come of it.”

I looked up. “What do you think of that, Sergeant?”

“I should only offend you if I expressed _my_ opinion,” he answered. “Go on, Mr. Betteredge,” he says, with resignation enough to provoke a saint.

My tongue itched to “go on” in words of my own choosing. But my Christianity held firm, and I proceeded:

“Having appealed to Miss Verinder in the manner which the officer thought most desirable, I spoke to her next in the manner which I myself thought most likely to impress her. On two different occasions, before my daughter left my roof, I privately warned her that she was exposing herself to suspicion of the most unendurable and most degrading kind. I have now told her, in the plainest terms, that my apprehensions have been realised.

“Her answer to this, on her own solemn affirmation, is as plain as words can be. In the first place, she owes no money privately to any living creature. In the second place, the Diamond is not now, and never has been, in her possession, since she put it into her cabinet on Wednesday night.

“The confidence which my daughter has placed in me goes no further than this. She maintains an obstinate silence, when I ask her if she can explain the disappearance of the Diamond. She refuses, with tears, when I appeal to her to speak out for my sake. ‘The day will come when you will know why I am careless about being suspected, and why I am silent even to _you_. I have done much to make my mother pity me—nothing to make my mother blush for me.’ Those are my daughter’s own words.

“After what has passed between the officer and me, I think—stranger as he is—that he should be made acquainted with what Miss Verinder has said, as well as you. Read my letter to him, and then place in his hands the cheque which I enclose. In resigning all further claim on his services, I have only to say that I am convinced of his honesty and his intelligence; but I am more firmly persuaded than ever, that the circumstances, in this case, have fatally misled him.”

There the letter ended.

Before I handed over the cheque, I asked if he had any remark to make.

“It’s no part of my duty, Mr. Betteredge,” he says, “to make remarks on a case, when I have done with it.”

I tossed the cheque across the table to him. “Do you believe in _that_ part of her ladyship’s letter?” I asked, hotly.

He looked at the cheque and lifted his dismal eyebrows—acknowledging my lady’s liberality as if it were an unavoidable weather.

“This is such a generous estimate of the value of my time,” he said, “that I feel bound to make some return for it. I’ll bear in mind the amount in this cheque, Mr. Betteredge, when the occasion comes round for remembering it.”

“What do you mean?” I asked.

“Her ladyship has smoothed matters over for the present very cleverly,” says he. “But _this_ family scandal is of the sort that bursts up again when you least expect it. We shall have more detective-business on our hands, sir, before the Moonstone is many months older.”

If his words meant anything, and his tone meant anything, it came to this: that my mistress’s letter had convinced him Miss Rachel was hardened enough to resist the strongest appeal—and that she had deceived her own mother by lies.

I answered plain. “Sergeant Cuff, I consider that observation an insult to my lady and her daughter!”

“Mr. Betteredge, consider it as a warning to yourself, and you will be nearer the mark.”

Hot as I was, the infernal confidence of the man shut my mouth. I walked to the window to compose myself.

The rain had ceased. The air outside had that rinsed smell—stone cooled, earth newly opened. And who should I see waiting in the court-yard but Mr. Begbie, fixed there like a stake in the ground, ready to go on with the dog-rose war the instant the Sergeant showed his face.

“My compliments to the Sairgent,” says Begbie the moment he saw me. “If he’s minded to walk to the station, I’m agreeable to go with him.”

“What!” cries the Sergeant behind me, “are you not convinced yet?”

“The de’il a bit I’m convinced!” says Begbie.

“Then I’ll walk to the station!” says the Sergeant.

“Then I’ll meet you at the gate!” says Begbie.

I had been angry enough to bite iron; but no man’s anger can hold out long against such an interruption. The absurdity of it—this solemn officer and this stiff-necked gardener, hauling roses into court as if petals were evidence—worked on me like a sudden change of wind, cooling what had been too hot.

Sergeant Cuff saw the change and gave me a word in season.

“Come! come!” says he. “Why not treat my view of the case as her ladyship treats it? Why not say, the circumstances have fatally misled me?”

To take anything as her ladyship took it was a privilege worth having, even when it was offered by Sergeant Cuff. I cooled down to my customary level. I held any opinion of Miss Rachel, other than my lady’s opinion or mine, in lofty contempt.

The only thing I could not do was keep off the subject of the Moonstone.

My sense should have told me to let it rest. But sense, like good bread, takes time to learn; and the virtues the present generation boasts of weren’t baked in my oven.

He had hit me on the raw. The tender place still tingled. So I perversely led him back.

“I am quite satisfied myself,” I said. “But never mind that. Go on, as if I was still open to conviction. You think Miss Rachel is not to be believed on her word; and you say we shall hear of the Moonstone again. Back your opinion, Sergeant. Back your opinion.”

Instead of taking offence, he seized my hand and shook it till my fingers ached.

“I declare to heaven,” says he solemnly, “I would take to domestic service tomorrow, Mr. Betteredge, if I had a chance of being employed along with you! To say you are as transparent as a child, sir, is to pay the children a compliment which nine out of ten don’t deserve. There! there! we won’t begin to dispute again. You shall have it out of me on easier terms than that. I won’t say a word more about her ladyship, or about Miss Verinder—I’ll only turn prophet, for once in a way, and for your sake. I have warned you already that you haven’t done with the Moonstone yet. Very well. Now I’ll tell you, at parting, of three things which will happen in the future, and which, I believe, will force themselves on your attention, whether you like it or not.”

“Go on!” says I—airy as ever, though my stomach had begun, in spite of me, to tighten at the sound of the word “prophet.” Prophecies have a way of lodging in a house like a draught: you can’t see them, but you feel them in your bones.

“First,” says the Sergeant, “you will hear something from the Yollands—when the postman delivers Rosanna’s letter at Cobb’s Hole, on Monday next.”

If he had flung a bucket of cold water over me I don’t think it would have struck me more unpleasantly. In that instant, like a taste remembered too late, I felt what I had not faced: Miss Rachel’s solemn denial left Rosanna’s strange doings—the new nightgown, the hidden stained nightgown, all of it—hanging in the air without sense or savour. The question had been in my mouth for days, and I had never bitten down on it, not once, till Cuff forced it between my teeth.

“In the second place,” he went on, “you will hear of the three Indians again. You will hear of them in the neighbourhood, if Miss Rachel remains in the neighbourhood. You will hear of them in London, if Miss Rachel goes to London.”

I had lost all interest in the jugglers, and I had persuaded myself of my young lady’s innocence. This second prophecy I swallowed easily enough.

“So much for two of the three things,” says I. “Now for the third!”

“Third, and last,” says Sergeant Cuff, “you will, sooner or later, hear something of that money-lender in London, whom I have twice taken the liberty of mentioning already. Give me your pocket-book, and I’ll make a note for you of his name and address—so that there may be no mistake about it if the thing really happens.”

He wrote on a blank leaf, careful as any clerk weighing out measures:

“Mr. Septimus Luker, Middlesex-place, Lambeth, London.”

“There,” says he, pointing to it, “are the last words, on the subject of the Moonstone, which I shall trouble you with for the present. Time will show whether I am right or wrong. In the meanwhile, sir, I carry away with me a sincere personal liking for you, which I think does honour to both of us. If we don’t meet again before my professional retirement takes place, I hope you will come and see me in a little house near London, which I have got my eye on. There will be grass walks, Mr. Betteredge, I promise you, in _my_ garden. And as for the white moss rose—”

“The de’il a bit ye’ll get the white moss rose to grow, unless you bud him on the dogue-rose first!” cried a voice at the window.

We both turned. There was Mr. Begbie—eternal as a church clock—too eager for controversy to wait at the gate.

The Sergeant wrung my hand once more and darted out into the court-yard, hotter still.

“Ask him about the moss rose, when he comes back, and see if I have left him a leg to stand on!” he cried, hailing me through the window.

“Gentlemen, both!” I called after them, trying—once again—to moderate their noise as I had moderated it before. “In the matter of the moss rose there is a great deal to be said on both sides!”

I might as well have whistled to a milestone. Away they went together, battling over roses without asking or giving quarter. The last I saw, Mr. Begbie was shaking his obstinate head, and Sergeant Cuff had him by the arm like a prisoner in charge.

Ah, well—well. I own I couldn’t help liking the Sergeant, though I hated him all the time. Explain that, if you can. There’s more in a man’s nature than the words he has for it; and sometimes the truest things in us won’t fit the dominant language we’re forced to speak.

You will soon be rid of me and my contradictions. When I have reported Mr. Franklin’s departure, the history of the Saturday’s events will be finished at last. And when I have next described certain strange things that happened in the course of the new week, I shall have done my part of the story, and shall hand over the pen to the person appointed to follow my lead.

If you are as tired of reading this narrative as I am of writing it—Lord, how we shall enjoy ourselves on both sides a few pages further on!
