Brother Clement had spent more than a year teaching and preaching in Basel when Jerome appeared before him one day, dust-covered yet triumphant.

"Give glory to God, Brother Clement," Jerome declared. "The time has come—you may now join me on our journey to England."

"I am ready," Clement replied. "Expecting your arrival these many months, I have devoted my spare hours to mastering the English tongue."

"A wise preparation," Jerome acknowledged. He explained that his delay had been purposeful—he had sought and secured extraordinary authority from the Pope to gather alms for the Church in England and to hear confessions even within secular monasteries. "Now let us gird ourselves and go forth to strike a blow for Holy Church—and against the Franciscans."

Their path led them down the Rhine, preaching as they went. In larger towns, both spoke; in smaller villages, they often parted ways, each taking a side of the river before reuniting at an appointed place.

Their styles differed. Jerome’s oratory was lofty, commanding, though narrower in scope, his delivery steady as a deep river. Clement’s, by contrast, was a shifting stream—now gentle, now fierce, now intimate, now soaring. Where Jerome stood firm as an oak, Clement bent like a reed to the hearts of his listeners, then rose like a flame when the spirit moved him. He could thunder like a storm, blaze like lightning, and sweep all before him in a torrent of eloquence.

(To recount his words here would be a task both difficult and unnecessary—others have captured such fire better than I. Yet know this: Clement had a gift Jerome lacked—he listened before he spoke. He learned the hidden sins of each town, the wounds beneath its surface, and shaped his sermons to strike true.)

In one city, they shared a pulpit. Jerome preached against vanity in dress, his voice sharp with satire. The people listened, unmoved—for vanity was not their vice.

Then Clement spoke. He spoke of drunkenness.

It was the sin they cherished, the sin their clergy ignored, the sin that birthed theft, violence, even murder. He reminded them of a drunken parricide, of men who had roasted a comrade alive. He spoke of their late prince, dead not of apoplexy but of wine; of an alderman drowned in a puddle of his own making.

"Your children shiver in rags," he cried, "while you drown your souls and your coin in drink! You become beasts—foolish as calves, savage as boars. How long before your wives and daughters follow? In Bohemia, they do already. Would you see the mothers who bore you, the wives you vowed to cherish, sunk as low as yourselves?"

A roar of horror rose from the crowd.

"Ah, it wounds you to imagine it!" Clement thundered. "Yet think—how much more must it wound the pure eyes of God to see His image in man debased to a swinish thing?"

He painted two pictures then: the drunkard’s home, squalid and weeping; the sober man’s, bright with peace. The women wept and murmured, "True, true!"

The sermon stirred an uproar. Men grumbled; women feigned defiance, praising their husbands’ "right to a drop." Yet that very evening, a hundred of those same women sought Clement, pressing gifts into his hands, kissing his robe, blessing him for daring to speak against their "sots."

Jerome frowned. "Drunkenness is no heresy, Brother. Must a whole sermon be wasted on it?"

Yet as they traveled, Jerome could not deny the truth: Clement’s words sank deeper than his own. They made men think, weep, even change.

"It is some trick of northern speech," Jerome told himself, uneasy. "He lowers himself to their level." Yet at times, Clement’s words rose higher than Jerome ever could. The contradiction nettled him—until he chided himself for petty pride.

"Souls are as varied as locks," he conceded. "And preachers must be as varied as keys. This novice, it seems, holds the key to these northern hearts."

So they journeyed on, sometimes drifting downstream, more often walking, preaching, confessing, until at last they reached Düsseldorf.

There stood the little quay where Gerard and Denys had once boarded a boat up the Rhine. The friars stepped onto the stones. The streets were unchanged, the Silver Lion still stood—yet the man who walked through them now was not the same. Barefoot, hands folded, eyes cast down, his heart was cool as stone. A true son of Dominic. A servant of Holy Church.
