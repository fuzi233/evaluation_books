On the appointed day for Madame Esmond's entertainment to the General, the estate of Castlewood was draped in a splendor that bordered on the theatrical. Madame Esmond, arrayed in a gown far more magnificent than her custom, endeavored to cloak the inherent sadness of the occasion with a veneer of radiant hospitality. Her guest, the General's new aide-de-camp, arrived first. This young man, whom she received beneath the shelter of the covered gallery, was none other than Mr. Washington. In the colony, few equaled him in the mastery of horse and saddle; his mount was a creature of pure spirit, matching the vigor of its rider.

Before retiring indoors to remove his riding-boots, the Major and his hostess traversed the length of the gallery, engaged in a discourse that wove together personal news and public affairs. She sought his confirmation regarding his appointment to General Braddock's staff and expressed her anxiety concerning her son's imminent departure. As they spoke, the silence was punctuated by the rhythmic tread of negro servants carrying dishes for the impending feast. They moved from the gallery down to the rough lawn, pausing briefly in the shade before proceeding to the house. Mr. Washington announced the speedy arrival of His Excellency, accompanied by Mr. Franklin of Pennsylvania.

Mrs. Esmond recalled hearing that Mr. Franklin had begun life as a printer's boy. To her mind, it was a curious trajectory for a man to ride in the carriage of the Commander-in-Chief. Yet, Mr. Washington countered with a defense of the printer's character, describing him as a shrewd, sensible, and highly meritorious gentleman. Madame Esmond, however, remained convinced that the Major was too generously disposed toward a man of such humble origins. "At least," she declared, in a tone of stately resolve, "since my boy is to embark on this campaign, he will be surrounded not by tradesmen, but by gentlemen of honor and fashion."

Mr. Washington, having witnessed the vices of the courtly set, wisely refrained from exposing the flaws of "honorable" men to a young mind entering the world. Instead, he posited that Harry and George must accept the full spectrum of human experience, listening to both the wise and the foolish. The widow, trembling slightly, invoked the image of a veteran officer and friends of the Duke, pleading with her guest to ensure her son's safety. She emphasized the deep trust her father, Colonel Esmond, had placed in Mr. Washington, urging him to act as a surrogate father figure.

"You know I would have done anything to win Colonel Esmond's favor," the gentleman replied, bowing with a grace that belied his internal turmoil. "Madam, what venture would I not make to deserve the affections of your daughter?" The lady blushed, executing a curtsey of unrivaled elegance, and promised that so long as he cherished her children, he would hold her eternal gratitude. As they embraced, George emerged from within, meticulously powdered and attired in his finery. Unlike the modern youth who might swagger with a cigar in hand, George approached with low, respectful bows. Yet, despite his outward deference, his countenance betrayed a storm. A polite smile masked the watchfulness and wrath burning in his eyes.

"What manner of game?" the Colonel asked, seeking to lighten the mood. "None," George replied curtly, turning his gaze to the portrait of his father hanging in the parlor. The Colonel, usually taciturn, launched into a lengthy account of his morning hunt. He described the absurdity of General Braddock's heavy frame and massive boots as he floundered through the Virginian undergrowth, pursued by a motley pack of dogs and Negro hunters who barked with equal fervor. "Great God!" Braddock had exclaimed, panting, "what would Sir Robert say in Norfolk to see a man hunting with a fowling-piece in hand, chasing a turkey!"

Madame Esmond laughed gently, mistaking the anecdote for local trivia, unaware that the Colonel referred to Sir Robert Walpole's estates in England. Young Esmond, however, seized upon the moment with a sneer. "And yet," he scoffed, "there is good sport here."

"What sport?" the Colonel demanded, his temper rising.

"Why, surely you know," George interjected, feigning innocence, "without staring at me so fiercely, as if preparing to draw a foil. Are you not the premier sportsman of the countryside? Do you not command the fish of the stream, the beasts of the forest, and the fowl of the sky?" He listed the myriad creatures of the wild—shad, salmon, roe-deer, buffalo—with a listlessness that suggested he found the very concept repulsive. "I am no sportsman," he admitted. "I am a milksop, bred at my mother's knee. Behold these apron-strings!" He gestured to the ribbon pinned to his coat. "Who would not cherish such charms? Remember, they were once black—for my grandfather."

"And who would not mourn for a gentleman so tender-hearted?" the Colonel asked, though his expression remained stern.

"In truth, I wish my grandfather were here," George continued, his voice dropping to a whisper, "to rise from his tomb and restore my father, the Ensign."

"Oh, Harry!" Madame Esmond cried, her composure breaking as her younger son entered the room, dressed in a gold-corded frock and silver-hilted sword, a mirror of his elder brother. She flew to embrace him, tears streaming down her cheeks. Harry, confused by his mother's distress, asked what troubled her, turning to the Colonel for explanation. The Major, biting his lip, confessed his inability to decipher the source of her sorrow.

"A mere inquiry about pink ribbons," George explained, attempting to diffuse the tension. "Ribbons that seem to possess a magical power over our mother's heart, don't they, Colonel?"

"Speak for yourself, sir!" the Colonel snapped, his patience wearing thin. "Do you speak for yourself?"

"He speaks too much for himself," the widow wept, overwhelmed by the emotional turbulence of her household.

"I confess," George retorted, spinning on his scarlet heels, "I know not the source of these tears any better than the source of the Nile. If the portrait of my father began to weep, I would marvel at paternal sorrow as greatly as I do at hers." He picked up one of Madame Esmond's bright pink ribbons, treating it with a strange reverence. "I hate sport," he declared, "which you and the Colonel love. I would kill nothing alive—not a turkey, nor a titmouse, nor an ox. I care for nothing but roses."

The Colonel, taken aback by the sudden shift from aggression to effeminacy, retreated to the balcony, taking a modest draught from a bowl of apple-toddy to cool his agitation. Inside, the atmosphere shifted again. Almost reconciled, the mother offered a hand to each son. George placed his free hand on Harry's shoulder. "If you dislike sport, and prefer quietude, why not remain here?" Harry proposed, his face flushing. "Let me go forth with Colonel Washington and Mr. Braddock. Honneur oblige, after all; my name comes first."

"One must stay," George argued, "to tend to Mother. We cannot afford to be both scalped by Indians or fricasseed by the French."

"Fricasseed by the French!" Harry exclaimed, indignation rising. "They are the best troops in the world! Englishmen! I long to witness their defeat! What a thrashing you will deliver!"

As George sat at the harpsichord, playing and singing *"Malbrouk s'en va-t-en guerre"* with a mechanical precision, the gentleman from the balcony returned. "I play 'God Save the King' in tribute to the expedition," George announced, looking over his shoulder with a mischievous glint.

"The Colonel takes his leave," George continued, refusing to move. "He needs to prepare himself for the General's reception."

"Let me escort the Colonel to his chambers," Madame Esmond insisted, her voice sharp with irritation. She swept out of the room, followed closely by the bewildered Major, leaving George alone at the instrument. Harry, observing his brother's strange behavior, asked what the trouble was. "Why wouldn't he kiss her hand?" he wondered, recalling the previous scene.

"Common kindness!" George shrieked, brandishing a piece of paper he had hidden. "Look at this, Hal! Is that common kindness?" It was a fragment of a letter, yet its meaning was stark. "...is older than myself, but I, again, am older than my years... consider a sober person... Friend and guardian! Curse him!" George clenched his fists. "...The flattering offer which General Braddock hath made me... postpone this matter until after the campaign... return to repose under my own vine and fig-tree."

"He means Castlewood!" George shouted, pointing a shaking finger at the creeping ivy covering the walls outside the window. "'Under my own vine and fig-tree!' Where he hopes to present my dear brother to his new sister-in-law. She has a pretty Scripture name..."

"Rachel," George finished bitterly. "Rachel is not weeping for her children, and she longs to be comforted." Turning to Harry, he urged, "Upstairs at once, kneel down, and pray, 'Dear Papa, welcome to your house of Castlewood.'"
