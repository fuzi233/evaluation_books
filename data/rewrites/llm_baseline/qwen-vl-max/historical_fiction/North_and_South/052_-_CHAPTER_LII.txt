“For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine.”

Edith moved through the house on tiptoe, her voice hushed, checking Sholto whenever he raised his tone. It was as if every sudden sound might shatter the quiet tension that had settled over the drawing-room, where two figures sat in silence behind closed doors. The clock struck two. Still no movement. Then, a swift footstep descended the stairs, and Edith slipped to the doorway, peering cautiously inside.

“Well, Henry?” she asked, her eyes searching his face.

“Well,” he replied, curtly. “Come in to lunch.”

“No, thank you,” he said, turning away. “I’ve already wasted too much time.”

Her brow fell. “Then it’s not settled?”

“No,” he answered flatly. “Not at all. And it never will be, if by ‘it’ you mean what I think you do. That won’t happen, Edith. So stop dreaming about it.”

“But it would be so good for us all,” she pleaded. “I’d feel at ease knowing Margaret was close by. As it is, I’m always afraid she’ll vanish off to Cadiz again.”

“I’ll try to find a wife who understands children,” he said with a hint of weariness. “That’s all I can promise. Miss Hale refuses me. And I won’t press her.”

“Then what did you talk about?” she asked, stepping forward.

“A thousand things you wouldn’t care about—investments, leases, land values.”

“Oh, go away then,” she snapped. “You and she must be terribly dull if you’ve spent all morning on such tedious matters.”

He smiled faintly. “Very well. I’ll return tomorrow—with Mr. Thornton. We’ll have more to discuss with Miss Hale.”

“Mr. Thornton? What does he have to do with this?”

“He’s Miss Hale’s tenant,” Henry said, walking away. “And he wants to give up his lease.”

“Oh,” she said, dismissively. “Well, I don’t understand these details. Don’t bother explaining them to me.”

“The only detail I want you to know,” he said, pausing at the door, “is that we need the back drawing-room undisturbed—just as it was today. With the children and servants coming and going, I can never get anything properly explained. And the plans we have for tomorrow are important.”

No one ever learned why Henry Lennox failed to appear the next day. But Mr. Thornton arrived punctually. After waiting nearly an hour, Margaret entered the room, pale and visibly agitated.

“I’m so sorry Mr. Lennox isn’t here,” she began quickly. “He could have handled this far better than I can. He’s my advisor in such matters.”

“I’m sorry I came if it troubles you,” Thornton said gently. “Shall I go to his chambers and see if I can find him?”

“No, thank you,” she replied. “I wanted to tell you how deeply I regret losing you as a tenant. But Mr. Lennox says things will improve…”

“Mr. Lennox knows little of what I feel,” Thornton interrupted quietly. “Happy and fortunate in everything a man values, he cannot grasp what it means to stand at the edge of life, no longer young, yet forced to begin again—back at the starting point that demands the energy of youth. To feel half a lifetime gone, nothing achieved, nothing left but the bitter memory of wasted chances… Miss Hale, I would rather not hear Mr. Lennox’s opinion on my affairs. Those who are content often trivialize the struggles of others.”

“You’re unjust,” she said softly. “Mr. Lennox only believes there’s great likelihood you’ll recover—perhaps even surpass—what you’ve lost. Please—don’t interrupt me.”

She gathered herself, then hurriedly flipped through legal documents and financial statements, her hands trembling slightly. “Ah! Here it is!” she exclaimed. “He drew up a proposal—I wish he were here to explain it—but it shows that if you took eighteen hundred fifty-seven pounds from my bank account—money currently earning only two and a half percent—you could repay me at a higher interest rate and continue operating Marlborough Mills.”

Her voice had steadied. Thornton remained silent. She searched for another paper—the one detailing security terms—eager to frame the entire matter as a business transaction, one clearly favorable to her.

As she looked, her breath caught. His voice, when it came, was thick, raw with emotion.

“Margaret.”

She glanced up—then lowered her head, pressing her forehead into her hands to hide her face.

He stepped closer. “Margaret!”

Her head sank lower, her face almost touching the table.

He knelt beside her, bringing his face level with her ear, and whispered—almost gasped—his words:

“Take care. If you don’t speak, I shall claim you—some strange, presumptuous way. Send me away now, if I must go. Margaret!”

At the third call, she turned her face toward him, still hidden beneath her small white hands, and rested it against his shoulder, burying her cheek in his coat.

It was too sweet to see—too sweet to bear—to feel her soft skin against his. He held her tightly. They said nothing.

After a long moment, she murmured, brokenly: “Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough.”

“Not good enough?” he whispered. “Don’t mock my own sense of unworthiness.”

A minute passed. Then, gently, he lifted her hands from her face and placed her arms around him just as she had once done to shield him from the mob.

“Do you remember, love?” he murmured.

“And how I repaid you with my insolence the next day?”

“I remember how wrong I was,” she said. “That’s all.”

“Look here,” he said. “Lift your head. I have something to show you.”

Slowly, she faced him, glowing with tender shame.

“Do you know these roses?” he asked, pulling out his pocketbook and revealing dried flowers carefully preserved within.

“No,” she replied, curious. “Did you give them to me?”

“No,” he said. “Vanity. You didn’t. But you may have worn similar ones.”

She studied them, then smiled faintly. “They’re from Helstone, aren’t they? I recognize the deep indentations on the leaves. Oh—have you been there? When?”

“I went there,” he said, “on my return from Havre. I needed to see the place where Margaret grew into who she is—even at the darkest time, when I had no hope of ever calling her mine.”

“You must give them to me,” she said, reaching for them with gentle urgency.

“Only if you pay me for them,” he teased.

After a while, in delicious silence, she whispered: “How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?”

“Let me speak to her,” he said.

“Oh, no,” she murmured. “I owe her so much—but what will she say?”

“I can guess,” he said. “Her first words will be, ‘That man!’”

“Hush,” she whispered, smiling. “Or I’ll try to imitate your mother’s voice saying, ‘That woman!’”
