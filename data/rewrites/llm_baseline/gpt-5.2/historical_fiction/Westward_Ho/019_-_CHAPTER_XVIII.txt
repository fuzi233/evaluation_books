They slipped by the southern horn of Grenada in the black hours before dawn, and by sunrise were deep within that ring of islands where the sea seems to have remembered every color it ever wore, and the land to have forgotten every scruple it ever learned. Here the trade-wind came steady and kindly, as if it too had been trained to household service; it laid a cool hand on the sweaty necks of the men, filled the canvas with a soft drum, and kept the ship’s timbers talking in their homely creaks and sighs. No hurricane voice worried these waters; the great roar went prowling elsewhere, far northward, while here the world kept a perpetual, dangerous smile.

Sky, sea, and islets melted into one another like a thin sherbet—blue upon blue, then sudden bars of green, then a bright dash of sand, and over all a haze that turned distance into a mirage-pantry where shapes wavered like heat above an oven-mouth. The sailors, practical as butchers and as hungry in spirit as they were in belly, cared less for the rainbow than for what might be salted and stowed: Spanish gold, and—more whispered, more coveted—pearls. Amyas, who had seen tropic beauty before and would not be bewitched into idleness, watched the coasts with a colder appetite: not for meat, but for conquest; he weighed in his mind, as another man might weigh flour, the chances of stripping these islands from Spain and laying them as a dish before Queen Elizabeth.

Yet even the hardest eyes on board could not look without a certain hush at seas so famous and so little known to English keels. Here a failure cost more than drowning. The thought of the Inquisition sat on their tongues like too much pepper—hot, bitter, not to be swallowed without water. The old mariners of Hawkins’s ventures were plied all day, as if they were cook-books for a strange kitchen: the names of isles and capes, the habits of fish and birds, where fresh water might be drawn, where a reef would rip a boat like a careless knife.

Frank listened most of all, grave and quiet. A sort of awe, not sorrowful but shining, grew on him; he stood long at the rail and seemed to drink light as if it could nourish. At times he murmured, half to himself, “This is the gate of heaven,” and forgot the very call to mess, as though bread and beef were poor things beside the spectacle. The islands hung in the air with inverted doubles below them; sand-hills rolled and weltered in the mirage; slopes blazed with yellow flower-beds and huge thorny cacti, like giant candelabra set out for some pagan feast. Even the sun, as he went down, was warped into odd oval and pear-shaped flames through crimson mist, as if the sky itself were a distorting glass.

While Frank wondered, Yeo rejoiced; for to the southward of the sinking sun rose a cluster of tall peaks out of the sea. Unless his reckonings lied, those were the mountains of Macanao at the western end of Margarita—the Isle of Pearls, whose very name had rung through Mediterranean ports and German fairs, and whose riches were spoken of with the same mouth that spoke of sin.

Next day they ran along the north side of the island, having passed—so far as their eyes could judge—undiscovered by the castle the Spaniards had built at the eastern end to guard the pearl-fishery. Toward afternoon they opened a deep, still bight wooded down to the water’s edge. There in the roadstead lay a caravel, and three boats by her.

At that sight the deck filled as if a bell had rung. Every man had a counsel, every mouth a plan. Some would have sailed straight in on the fresh afternoon breeze that always seemed to blow shoreward in those parts. But the white lines of breakers fretted here and there at the bay’s mouth; coral, they feared, and not in a mood to forgive. Better to run by quietly and send in boat and pinnace.

Yeo would have had Spanish colors shown, to keep the caravel from starting like a startled hare; but Amyas refused, square and stubborn. To lie with a flag, he said, was a mean thing, unless driven by extremity or commanded by great statecraft.

So they held on till the next point shut them from the roadstead, and then lowered away. Cary went in the largest boat with twenty men; Amyas took the smaller with fifteen. Among them, because he would not be denied, came John Brimblecombe in cassock and bands, and with an old sword of his uncle’s that he treasured as another man might treasure a carved spoon from his grandmother’s chest.

At the bight’s mouth they found the reef as thick as warned—coral heads everywhere, pale and treacherous under the clear water, like bones under skin. They edged along it a long way hunting a passage. Those new to the Indies leaned over and stared down into gardens no gardener on English soil could have imagined: living flower-beds, branching fans, soft waving fronds, and fish flashing through as bright as spilled spice—turmeric-yellow, saffron, a sudden pepper-red. Even the sea had its own music here: the hush of water sliding, the tick of the reef, the distant boom where surf struck the outer rocks like steady hand-drumming.

Then, below, the tone of the place changed. Shapes moved, long and thick, with an easy cruelty. A “school of sharks,” Yeo called it—some of them nearly as long as the boat—tilting up their wicked scowling eyes as if measuring what might be had for supper.

“Jack,” said Amyas, sitting near him, “look how that big fellow eyes thee. He hath surely taken a fancy to that plump hide of thine, and thinks thou wouldst eat as tender as any sucking porker.”

Jack went pale to the lips but said nothing.

Just then the biggest brute shot upward after a parrot-fish. The little creature, finding no other way, sprang clean into the air and almost aboard. Where it had burst from the water, three or four great brown shagreened noses clashed together within two yards of Jack. Each opened to show those horrible rows of saw teeth, and then sank again, sulky as hungry dogs, to wait.

Jack breathed very softly, “In manus tuas, Domine,” and turned his eyes inboard. Whatever wonders the Indies held, he had no lust to look at sharks.

At last they found their opening through the reef and ran in on the fair wind. The caravel lay now not a musket-shot away. Cary laid her aboard before the Spaniards could get to their ordnance. Standing up in the stern-sheets he shouted to them to yield. The captain answered boldly enough, asking, in whose name?

“In the name of common sense, ye dogs!” cried Will Cary. “Do you not see you are but fifty strong to our twenty?”

And up he scrambled. The Spaniard fired a pistol at him. Cary knocked him over rather than kill him; and the crew, seeing the thing done, yielded—some on their knees, some leaping overboard, splashing like frightened sheep.

Meanwhile Amyas had pulled round under the stern and boarded the second boat, the nearest being fast alongside and therefore a sure prize. In that boat the Spaniards cried “Misericordia” and yielded without a blow; the negroes leaped overboard and swam ashore like sea-dogs.

The third boat, not an oar’s length away, began to pull off. Here John Brimblecombe, fired by a need to be seen brave, seized a boat-hook and clawed it into her stern, shouting, “Stay, ye Papists! Stay, Spanish dogs!”

They were ten to his one. In a moment they hauled him over; he fell full-length, nose-first, into the sea, and the hook stayed fast behind them.

What seized him then no man could tell—perhaps the memory of those sharks, for fear breeds its own creatures and populates the water with teeth. He roared like a bull in a market, and instead of turning for the English boat struck out after the Spanish, cassock floating behind him so that he looked for all the world like some great black monk-fish. He howled and puffed with his mouth full of salt water, and his words came broken as if chewed.

“Stay, ye Spanish dogs! Help, all good fellows! See you not I am a dead man? They are nuzzling already at my toes! He hath hold of my leg! My right thigh is bitten clean off! Oh that I were preaching in Hartland pulpit! Stay, Spanish dogs! Yield, Papist cowards, lest I make mincemeat of you; and take me aboard! Yield, I say, or my blood be on your heads! I am no Jonah; if he swallow me, he will never cast me up again! It is better to fall into the hands of man, than into the hands of devils with three rows of teeth apiece. In manus tuas. Orate pro anima—!”

And so on, in a frenzy that might have made Panurge himself blush. The English, expecting every moment to see him snapped up by a shark or brained by an oar, fired a volley at the fugitives. The Spaniards, terrified, leaped overboard as the others had done. Jack scrambled into the empty boat, and drew sword with one hand while wiping water from his eyes with the other, and began to lay about him like a lion, cutting at the air and crying, “Yield, idolaters! Yield, Spanish dogs!” Only when his breath failed and his wits returned did he see there was no one left to strike. He sat panting, then began stripping off his hose, swearing his thigh was bitten through and his leg full of blood, and would have swooned for that imaginary wound had not the men, with much laughter and some anger, persuaded him at last that he was whole.

They overhauled the prizes. The caravel held hides and salt pork—heavy stuff, fit for stowage and barter, with a smell of brine and old animal oil that clung to the fingers. But there was more. In the captain’s cabin, and in the stern-sheets of the boat Brimblecombe had “boarded,” were frails of leaves packed neatly. When they opened them, the leaves crackled like dry bay, and within lay goodly pearls, somewhat browned. The Spaniards, in haste and greed, had damaged the color by opening the shells with fire, instead of leaving them to decay slowly after the Arabian fashion. Still, pearls were pearls. Though no one could tally their worth exactly, content spread through the men like warmth after a swallow of strong liquor.

Then some malicious fellow set the caravel on fire. Hides burn with an acrid, nauseous stench; it was no nosegay that rose over the bight, but a black reek like scorched hair and tallow. Amyas was very angry at that wanton waste—Drake, his model, had never made a pastime of destruction. Cary had his jest ready.

“Ah!” said he. “‘Lutheran devils’ we are, you know; so we are bound to vanish, like other fiends, with an evil savor.”

Once aboard again Amyas rounded on Mr. Brimblecombe and boxed his ear, telling him he would do better to play the man with less roaring—roar less before he was hurt, and keep his breath for his strokes, if he wished the crew to listen much to his sermons. Frank, hearing, bade Amyas leave the offender to him, and set upon Jack with a scholar’s whip.

“Come hither, thou recreant Jack, thou lily-livered Jack, thou hysterical Jack. Tell me now—thou hast read Plato’s Dialogues, and Aristotle’s Logic?”

Jack, meek as a scolded boy, answered, “Yes.”

“Then I will deal with thee after the manner of those ancient sages, and ask whether the greater must not contain the less?”

“Yes, sure.”

“And that which is more than a part contain that part, more than which it is?”

“Yes, sure.”

“Then tell me—is not a priest more than a layman?”

Jack, who was ever loud on the dignity of the priesthood, answered boldly, “Of course.”

“Then a priest containeth a man, and is a man, and something over—viz., his priesthood?”

Jack, seeing the snare, hesitated. “I suppose so.”

“Then if a priest show himself no man, he shows himself all the more no priest.”

“I’ll tell you what, Master Frank,” says Jack, sore but stubborn, “you may be right by logic; but sharks aren’t logic, nor don’t understand it neither.”

Frank pressed him, asking if it were manly to howl like a pig in a gate because he thought there was that which was not there; and still more, if there had been real peril, whether it were brave to keep his mouth open and swallow half the sea. Jack had no answer at first. Then, in plain sincerity, he confessed it was not Spaniards he feared. Spaniards were Heaven’s handiwork; he knew how to deal with them. But those “fiends’ spawn of sharks”—the sight of that great fellow taking fish alongside had upset him clean.

“Oh, Jack, Jack,” cried Frank, “behold how one sin begets another! Just now thou wert but a coward, and now thou art a Manichee; for thou imputest to an evil creator that which was formed only for a good end—namely, sharks, which were made on purpose to devour useless carcasses like thine. Moreover, as a brother of the Rose, thou wert bound by the vow of thy brotherhood to have leaped joyfully down that shark’s mouth.”

Jack retorted that perhaps if Mistress Rose had been in the beast’s stomach he might have shown more devotion; but he had wanted to fight Spaniards, not to be shark-bitten. Frank called it self-will, and with mock solemnity pronounced him cut off and deprived—no more chaplain, unless within seven days he did some deed fit for the Seven Champions or Orlando himself; and even then, no iron about him beyond what would mend his pen.

Jack protested, asking since when Frank had been Bishop of Exeter. Frank answered that Jack’s wit was blinded by gross vapors raised by fear, and that the bishop might deal with his Manicheeism; but his howling—scandalous in a preacher—deserved censure.

Then Amyas joined in, and between them they pounded him with blunt truths: that no wonder young men refused the Gospel when it was preached by those whom they could not help but look down upon; that for a priest of the English Church, whose work was to make men good Christians by making them good men and good Englishmen, to show the white feather in need was to unpreach in a minute what he had preached for years. Jack, overwhelmed, clapped hands to ears and fled, and Frank laughed so heartily that even Amyas’s sad steadiness softened for a moment, glad after all for the smile.

Next day was Sunday. After divine service—Jack could hardly be persuaded to read it, so shamefaced was he, and would not hear of preaching afterward—Amyas read aloud, as was their custom, the articles of their agreement. Then, seeing a sloping beach abreast with a shoot of clear water running into the sea, he resolved they should land, wash clothes, and water the ship again, for water had been scarce at Barbados.

To this party Jack Brimblecombe must needs go, taking sword and a great arquebuse. He had dreamed, he said, that Spaniards set upon him, and was sure the dream would come true; and moreover he cared little if it did, or if he ever got back alive. Better, he declared in a wild bitterness, to die than be made an ape and a scarecrow, laughed at by the men and badgered with Ramus’s logic and Plato’s dialectical devilries, till he was made to confess himself a Manichee, and for aught he knew a turbaned Turk or a Hebrew Jew. So he flung himself into the boat like a desperate man.

They went ashore under strict command from Amyas that no firearms should be let off, for fear of alarming Spaniards. The place was fair beyond telling: trees crowding down to the bright edge, shade cool as a cellar, and the thin music of the rivulet pouring into the sea with a constant, clean chatter that soothed the mind after the ship’s groaning and the men’s loud talk. They washed their clothes, stretching limbs with joy, and then shot the seine they had brought. The net came up alive and strange, heavy with creatures that flashed and wriggled like a spilled casket of jewels.

Among them they took a sea-cow—seven feet long—barnacled and limpeted on her back as if she had been drift-timber. She was a fond, foolish beast, and yet, men said, pious withal: if she found a corpse she would watch it day and night until it decayed or was buried. The Indians called her manati; she carried her young under her arm and gave it suck like a woman; and wounded, she lamented aloud with a human voice, and was said at seasons to sing melodiously—perhaps, some argued, the very melody that had given birth to tales of sirens and Tritons. They did not rashly deny it; for in those seas Nature bred wonders as a kitchen breeds steam, and the man who calls all strange things lies may prove only ignorant.

One of the men also brought in two great black fowls shot with a crossbow—bodied and headed like capons, but bigger than any eagle—the Spaniards called them curassos. Later these, with the sea-cow, made them good cheer, roast and sodden. The manati’s flesh was dainty meat, as good as a four-months’ calf, tender and fat; the smell of it as it browned—clean, sweet, with a mild richness—made the men forget danger for a little, and talk with their mouths full, as men will.

Then they set to filling casks and barricos, hauling the boat up to the outflow of the rivulet so the water ran clear and cold into their staves. Lucky for them it fell out that they were all close together at that work, and not abroad skylarking as they had been half an hour before.

John Brimblecombe, as soon as they landed, had gone apart with a shamefaced and doleful face. Under a great tree he sat, plucked a Bible from his bosom, and read steadfastly, girt with his great sword, his arquebuse laid by him. That too was well—for him and for the rest.

They had not finished watering when a cry rose sharp as a cracked bell: the enemy was upon them. Out of the wood, not twenty yards from the parson, came full fifty shot, with a multitude of negroes behind, and an officer in front on horseback, plume of feathers nodding in his hat, sword drawn in his hand. The suddenness of it broke the shoreline’s gentle music; the birds fled with startled clatter, the rivulet’s constant voice seemed to grow thin under the harsh new sounds—boot-thuds, shouted Spanish, the metallic cough of match and pan.

“Stand, for your lives!” shouted Amyas; and only just in time. Ten minutes were lost in running to and fro before he could get them into any order. But when Jack beheld the Spaniards—almost as if he had expected them—he plucked a leaf and set it in his Bible for a mark, laid the book down soberly, caught up his arquebuse, and ran like a mad dog straight at the Spanish captain. He fired once and shot him through the body, stark dead. Then, flinging the arquebuse at the head of the next man, he fell on with his sword like a very Colbrand, breaking in among the arquebusiers and striking right and left such ugly strokes that the Spaniards, who took him for a fiend—or Luther himself come to plague them—gave back in confusion.

They fired at him five or six at once, but whether for fear of him or fear of hitting one another, made such bad play that he took only one shrewd gall in his thigh, enough to make him limp for many a day. Still he pressed on. By then the English had run up in better order, near forty well armed. The Spaniards turned and fled as fast as they had come.

Cary, with breath to jest even then, hinted that the dogs had tasted the parson and had no stomach to wait for clerk and people.

“Come back, Jack! Are you mad?” shouted Amyas.

Jack, who had not spoken one word all this while, followed the Spaniards as fiercely as ever, until, reaching a great blow at one arquebusier, he caught his foot in a root. Down he went, struck his head, and knocked all breath out of himself—which between fatness and fighting was not much. He lay.

Amyas, seeing the Spaniards gone, would not pursue. He lifted Jack up. Jack stared about as if waking from a fever and cried, “Glory be! glory be!—How many have I killed? How many have I killed?”

“Nineteen, at the least,” quoth Cary, “and seven with one back stroke,” and then showed him the dead captain and two arquebusiers, and three or four more limping away wounded—some by their fellows’ shot.

“There!” said Jack, pausing and blowing, “will you laugh at me any more, Mr. Cary, or say I cannot fight because I am a poor parson’s son?”

Cary took him by the hand and asked pardon for his scoffing, owning that Jack had that day played the best man of them all. Jack, who never bore malice, began laughing too.

“Oh, Mr. Cary,” he said, “we have all known your pleasant ways, ever since you used to put drumble-drones into my desk at Bideford school.”

So they went to the boats and pulled off, thanking God—as well they might—for their deliverance. All the crew rejoiced over Jack; yet after a while he grew very faint, having bled more than he knew, and made as little of his real wound as he had made much the day before of his imaginary one.

That evening Frank asked him, with the curious seriousness of one who keeps a ledger of men’s souls and manners, how it came to pass that he had shown so cool and approved a valor in so sudden a mishap.
