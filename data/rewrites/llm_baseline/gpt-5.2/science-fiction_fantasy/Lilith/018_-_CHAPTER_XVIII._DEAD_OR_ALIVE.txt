I went on, keeping my face to the moon as if she were a lantern I had once trusted and now did not dare to turn my back upon. She was not yet high. She stared straight into the forest with a look that made me think of old kitchen copper left too long in the rain—dented, darkened, and tired of shining. No cloud came near her; the stars were too sharp and bright, as if they belonged to some newer coinage. She seemed to travel one road while I took another, yet for a long while we kept pace through the trees, she the dulled disc above, I the moving shadow below. The forest had its own speech—needles whispering, trunks creaking in slow syllables, night insects ticking like tiny spoons against glass—and over it all, the silence carried a weight. No wheels, no engines, no iron clatter; the absence of modern noise felt like a clean, strict diet after a lifetime of greasy din.

My eyes were mostly on the ground. It is the way of anyone who has ever foraged: you learn to read the floor for what it offers, what it hides, what it has dropped. Under a spreading tree something pale caught the moon’s meagre shine. At first it was only a whiteness, an irregular patch among needles and shadow, but as I drew nearer it took the terrible shape the mind recognizes before it admits.

“Another skeleton!” I said, and knelt.

My hand found not bone, but body—body so wasted it might have been only a skin stretched over a frame. It was cold; not the honest cold of stone, but the cold of something that had once held warmth as naturally as bread holds steam, and now would not. The more I looked, the more I touched, the more my senses insisted on death. For a bewildered instant the fancy came that it was one of the wild dancers—some ghostly Cinderella of that strange revel—lost on the way home and spent at last in the open night.

She was quite naked. Even in the shadow under the boughs I could have counted each rib without laying a finger between them. The skin clung like tight parchment. The lips had drawn back from the teeth, and those teeth—beautiful in shape, terrible in their bareness—gleamed through the dark. Her hair was longer than her body, thick, fine as sifted flour to the touch, and black as the space between stars. She must have been tall; she might once have been graceful. How had she come here? Not of herself, surely, and already so far gone. Had she staggered until her strength failed, fallen, and lain until hunger finished the work? But even hunger… how could it carve a person so clean? And naked—how? No decent thought fitted. If savages had stripped her, why leave her? If beasts had taken cloth, why not flesh? Yet there she was, abandoned to any irreverent eye, any brutal claw, to be worried and tossed until the friendly rains, year upon year, washed her into the soil.

I rose, stood over her, and tried to think like a man who still belonged to men. I must not—could not—leave her like that. Even the garment of a woman claims a kind of reverence; her uncovered body demanded it with a sharpness that made my throat tighten. But the ground was hard, knotted with roots, and I had only my hands. Burial would be slow work, perhaps impossible.

Then another question, small and fierce, began to stir: What if she were not dead?

This world had already taught me that the ordinary rules did not always hold. At first I had thought she could not have been dead long—there was no sign of decay—but then what was there in her to decay? Her wasting had left her almost nothing but form. Could life, reduced to some ember, still be hiding in that pitiful hearth?

As I left the forest-hall, I had seen a bunch of ripe grapes in the doorway and had taken it, eating as I came. A few still hung on the stalk, their skins tight, their scent a dark sweetness that seemed indecently alive beside her. If anything could persuade a spark to flare, it might be that sharp, clean juice.

Her mouth was a little open. The head lay awkwardly. To move her I slid my arm under the shoulder she lay upon—and felt warmth in the pine-needles beneath her. Not much, but unmistakable: the bed under her had not yet cooled to the forest’s temperature. She could not have been long as I found her. She might still be—alive, though no breath stirred my cheek, no heart declared itself to my searching hand.

One of her hands was clenched hard around something small. I could not see what it was. I tried not to force it; the grip looked like the last stubborn habit of a will that refused to let go.

I squeezed a grape into her mouth, letting the juice wet the teeth and tongue. No swallowing followed. No flutter of the throat. Still, I could not stop. Doubt may be poor encouragement, but it is a worse excuse for doing nothing.

I made a nest as if for a loaf that must not lose its heat: a thick layer of pine-needles and dry leaves, then one of my garments, warm from my body. I lifted her onto it—so light she seemed scarcely to weigh more than her hair—and covered her with my clothes and a great heap of leaves. I wanted to keep whatever little warmth remained, to hoard it until the sun returned like an oven door opening.

I tried another grape. Nothing.

So tight was the skin upon her bones that I dared not rub her, as one might rub frozen hands. I crept into the heap of leaves and got as close to her as I could, drawing her into my arms. I had not much heat in me, but what I had I would share. Her cold seemed to breathe outward, as if death were a kind of climate. No answering warmth came back from her; only the steadiness of her stillness.

And there, under that sad, slow-setting moon, I lay with the dead—or the nearly dead—and watched for dawn.

When the darkness began to thin, the forest’s sound changed with it. A faint chorus of waking stirred in the understory: the quick shiver of small wings, the soft patter of something light-footed, the deliberate creak of a bough adjusting its load. The acoustic world felt like weather—pressure and release—and I found myself listening as if the right combination of sounds might mean her life had returned.

Instead I saw motion close to the ground: the low undulating glide of a large snake passing in an unswerving line. Then, as if pulled by the same invisible thread, a creature like a roebuck-doe went by with her calf. After that, two beasts like bear-cubs, with three or four smaller ones behind them. The light came quickly now, and a troop of horses trotted past—no larger than the smallest Shetland pony, yet full-grown in their perfection. Some had the build of cart-horses, others the carriage of chargers or racers. Their hooves made a soft, numerous drumming in the grass, not the harsh ring of iron on road, but a sound like hands on packed earth.

Dwarf cattle followed. Small elephants—ridiculous and solemn—moved with their private thunder. The procession had the feel of an old habit, a domestic routine of the wild, as if this migration were as regular as morning bread.

“Why are the children not here!” I thought, suddenly pierced with the memory of the Little Ones. “The moment I am free of this poor woman, I must go back and fetch them!”

But I could not leave her yet. I laid my hand on her. It seemed—only seemed—a trifle warmer. Perhaps she had stolen a degree from my body. She could hardly have made any of her own. Still, hope did not lessen.

The horizon flushed. The sun came up peering, innocent, as if he had never seen this world before and had risen early just to stare at it. His light struck through me like a draught of clean hot broth after days of hunger. I rose full of life, braced against death.

I lifted the handkerchief I had arranged to keep pine-needles from her mouth and eyes and looked, trying to decide whether I had found a priceless jewel or only its empty case. She lay motionless. In the new light her face was worse than I had known: the hollows were deeper, the bones sharper, every tooth insisting through the drawn lips. The human garment was worn to threads; yet perhaps the bird of heaven still nested within. Perhaps it would wake to motion and song.

But the sun shone on her face. I covered her again, light leaves over the handkerchief, and went to see where the animals were going.

Their track was well beaten, old with use. Other paths joined it like tributaries, widening it. The trees retreated. Grass thickened. Then the forest ended, and a plain of the loveliest green stretched away until it met the sky. Along the forest’s edge a small river flowed, and to it the track led.

At sight of the water a new hope leapt in me—hot, immediate. Mist, bluish and thin, rose and vanished as it rose. On the far side, in abundant grass, small animals fed as if breakfast were the one sure law. It seemed they slept in the forest and in the morning swam the river to the plain.

I knelt to drink. The water was hot.

It shocked me more than cold would have. I tasted it: it had a strange metallic savour, like water that has slept in copper, or like the lick of a knife’s edge. I sprang up. Here was warmth in plenty—warmth that ran, constant, like a fire that never needed feeding. Warmth was the first necessity of life. This might be the difference between her ember and her ash.

I ran back.

Without understanding my solitude no one will know what seemed to hang upon the redemption of this woman. “Prove what she may,” I thought, “I shall at least be lonely no more.” In that thought was a selfishness I could not clean away, as one cannot clean hunger out of a starving man. I had found myself such poor company that only now did I seem to know what hope was. This blessed hot water would expel the cold death—and drown my desolation.

I lifted her. Tall as she was, she weighed almost nothing; her bones were delicate, scarcely clothed. Yet she was not stiff. She yielded like a sleeping child. That looseness made me more hopeful than any warmth in the needles had done. I carried her on one arm, her head against my shoulder, and moved softly, as if even the wind of my passing might frighten away the last flutter of life.

At the stream I hesitated. The water was too hot to plunge her into at once. A sudden shock might startle whatever remained and drive it out, like steam escaping when you lift a lid too fast. I laid her on the bank and soaked one of my garments, then began to bathe her slowly.

As I worked, I could not even guess her age. Youth and age were both erased by starvation. Only the astonishing abundance of her black hair argued for life—hair that looked as if it drank darkness and stored it. Her eyelids were not quite shut, which made her seem more dead: a crack in the clouds of her night with no dawn behind it.

I kept bathing her continuously, as a careful cook keeps a sauce from breaking by never letting one part cool while another is heated. I would not allow any section of her poor frame to lose what the water gave it. Gradually her skin felt less like winter. At last, when the heat had soaked through as much as it could, I ventured to submerge her. I stepped into the stream and drew her in, holding her face above the surface while the swift, steady current flowed around the rest of her.

Her clenched hand did not loosen, though the heat swaddled her. I noted it and could conclude nothing.

After about ten minutes I lifted her out, laid her on the bank, dried her, covered her as well as I could, and ran to the forest for leaves. The soil and grass were dry and warm. When I returned she had scarcely cooled. I spread leaves over her and ran for more—again and again, until she lay like some strange seed buried shallow, warmed from below, waiting.

Now I could leave her briefly to seek shelter. I ran upstream toward rocky hills not far off. There the river issued full-grown from a rock at the base of one hill, as if it had travelled hidden through stone and chosen that spot to break into day. It did not fill the opening; I crept through and found a little cave.

Inside, the stream did not roar down any stair as I had imagined. It rose quietly from the ground at the back like the base of a thick column and ran along one side in a deep, narrow channel, almost filling it. The air in that cave smelled of wet rock and warm mineral—an odour like heated iron with the sting filed off—and the sound of the water was steady, a living hush that calmed the nerves, not like the punishing noise of machines. It was a place where a mind could heal simply by listening.

I saw at once what might be made: if I could find fallen boughs long enough to span the channel, stout enough not to bend, then with smaller branches and leaves I could build a couch above the warm stream—a bed kept constantly heated from beneath, like a hearth that never smoked.

I ran back to her. She lay as I had left her. Heat had not awakened her, but neither had it summoned decay. Hope remained possible.

I set boulders near her feet and along her sides to keep her from rolling. Then I went to the wood and found boughs fit for my purpose, many of beech, dry and pale, with yellow leaves still clinging as if unwilling to forget their summer language. I laid them across the channel, then crossed them with smaller branches, interlaced twigs, and buried everything deep under leaves and dry moss until the bridge became a warm, dry, soft couch.

When at last it was finished, after not a few journeys, I lifted her again and carried her to the cave. She was so light that more than once I had the irrational fear that when I set her down she would have become a skeleton after all, emptied while I walked. When I laid her gently on the leaf-deep bridge, it was a relief to let go of that fancy even more than to set down the weight.

I covered her thickly with leaves. Then I tried again with a grape. This time I could open her mouth a little farther. The grape lay there, unheeded, but I hoped the juice might creep down of its own accord, like rain into dry ground.

After an hour or two on that warmed couch, she was no longer cold. The brook’s heat had seeped into her—truly she was but a frame—and she was warm to the touch. Not, perhaps, with the warmth of life, but with a warmth that made life more possible. I remembered having read of one in a trance lying motionless for weeks.

So in that cave I stayed.

Day after day, night after night—seven long days and nights—I sat or lay, now waking, now sleeping, but always watching. Each morning I went out and bathed in the hot stream. Each morning, stepping from it, I felt as if I had eaten and drunk. That strange nourishment gave me courage to lay her in it also every day, carefully, as if curing her were a slow cooking that must not scorch. After her bath I always put a fresh grape in her mouth.

Once, lifting her, I saw a shadow of discoloration on her left side. Terror struck me like a cleaver’s blow. But the next morning it had vanished, and I continued. I too ate grapes and other berries from the forest, their skins bursting with sharp sweetness, their scents marking the hours better than any clock. Yet I believed that with my daily bath in that river I could have lived well enough without food at all.

Every time I slept I dreamed I had found a wounded angel. Unable to fly, she stayed with me until at last she loved me and would not leave. Every time I woke it was to see, instead of bright eyes, the white motionless wasted face on the couch—lips drawn, eyelids cracked open upon nothing.

But Adam himself, when first he saw her asleep, could not have watched more anxiously for Eve’s waking than I watched for this woman’s. Adam, perhaps, knew nothing of his own need; I, an alien from my fellows, had learned to love what I had lost. If this shred of womanhood vanished—if she proved only a husk—there would be nothing left in me but a consuming hunger after life.

I forgot even the Little Ones. Things were not amiss with them, I told myself; they had each other, and their own harmless customs. Here lay something else: what might wake and be a woman—might actually open eyes, and look out of them upon me. Now first I knew what solitude meant: not being without voices in the world, but gazing on one who neither saw nor heard, neither moved nor spoke, while the cave filled with the tender sound of water that spoke in a tongue I could not translate into comfort.

A man alone is not a man, but a creature that may become one—need and possibility. To be enough for himself, a being would have to be some eternal self-existent worm. Man is too superbly made, too simply intricate; he rises from and stands upon a pedestal of lower lives and higher structures, and he cannot breathe long in any atmosphere less divine than that offered by other souls. Only in the reflex of other lives can he ripen into himself.

Save for the faint prospect of dawn in the form beside me, I should have fled for fellowship to the beasts that grazed and did not speak. Better to go about with them—infinitely better—than to live alone. But with even the smallest chance that the silent mouth might yet shape a word, in whatever native dialect her spirit still remembered, I—the poorest of creatures—was yet a possible man.
