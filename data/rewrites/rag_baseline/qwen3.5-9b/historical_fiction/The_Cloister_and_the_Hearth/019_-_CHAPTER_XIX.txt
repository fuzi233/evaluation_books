Dawn broke not with a gentle light, but with the sharp, metallic tang of impending violence, waking Gerard before the sun had fully claimed the sky. He roused Martin, the old soldier, whose eyes widened in astonishment at the hour. In his mind, Gerard had already vanished through the window during the chaos of the previous night. Yet, reality demanded action. As they conferred on the only viable route to safety—a narrow passage through the great forest leading to a known bridle-road and a hostelry that might offer a swift steed—they moved with urgent precision. Their goal was clear: to cross the border of Holland before the populace stirred from their slumber.

Peter's dwelling stood but a short distance from the tree line. They departed immediately, Martin armed with his bow and three arrows, while Gerard carried only a stout oak staff, a gift from Peter for the journey ahead. Margaret, practical and quick, pinned up her kirtle and farthingale against the wetness of the road. Peter accompanied them only to the edge of his garden hedge, where, with an emotion rarely seen on his stoic face, he bestowed his blessing upon the young man.

As they crossed the stony field toward the wood, the sun merely peeked over the horizon, casting long, uncertain shadows. Halfway across, Margaret's composure shattered. Her eyes darted nervously backward, catching something unseen, and a cry tore from her throat. Driven by primal instinct, she turned and sprinted toward the forest, screaming with terror. Behind her, the thunderous hoofbeats of Ghysbrecht and his men echoed like judgment. Resistance was folly; flight was the only option. Martin and Gerard followed suit, their hearts pounding in unison. Though the pursuers gained ground, Martin's shouts pierced the rising panic: "Only win the wood! Only win the wood!"

Hope surged within them. With their superior start, the great trees seemed to stretch their branches like welcoming arms, their leaves forming a living shield against the open field. But nature offered no sanctuary from human malice. The fiery old burgomaster, Ghysbrecht, flung himself upon his mule, spurring the beast into a gallop that defied reason. He did not pursue his own men alone; he targeted the fugitives. His intent was to cut them off, to crush them between his force and the timberline.

Ghysbrecht rode in a wide semicircle, positioning himself directly before Gerard, leaving the others exposed to mercy. Margaret shrieked, throwing herself against Gerard in a futile attempt to shield him, but he shook her off without ceremony, driven by a singular focus. In his ardor, Ghysbrecht forgot the ancient truth that hunted beasts turn upon their hunters, nor did he consider that hatred breeds a reciprocal desire to destroy. Expecting Gerard to dodge, the burgomaster prepared to strike. Instead, Gerard flew straight at him, issuing a savage, exulting cry. With all the heart, soul, and strength he possessed, he swung the oak staff.

The weapon descended upon Ghysbrecht's face with a frightful crash. The impact knocked the old man beneath his own mule, which beat the devil's tattoo with its heels as it stumbled forward. Blood streamed from the burgomaster's face, spattering his collar, marking the end of his command. In that same instant, the three fugitives vanished into the tree line. The yells of dismay and vengeance that erupted from Ghysbrecht's men signaled a shift from pursuit to a desperate race for survival.

"Why run?" Gerard panted, turning to his companions. "You have your bow, and I have this," shaking the blood-stained staff.

"Boy!" Martin roared, his voice cutting through the din. "The GALLOWs! Follow me!" He fled deeper into the woods.

Soon, the baying of dogs, like a pack tearing at fresh meat, filled the air. The pursuers had entered the wood, spotting the fugitives flitting among the trunks. Margaret moaned and panted, her lungs burning, while Gerard clenched his teeth, gripping his staff until his knuckles turned white. They reached a stiff hazel coppice, where Martin dashed in, shouldering aside the young wood as if it were standing corn. Within fifty yards, they faced four blind paths. Martin chose one, commanding, "Bend low." They glided along, half-creeping, until their path intersected with other tortuous trails. Taking one, they expected to circle back, but the path twisted, eventually depositing them in a thick pine grove where the walking was hard and the visibility nil. The young fir-trees grew so densely that one could not see three yards ahead.

Exhaustion finally claimed Martin. Having learned in war to dissociate from danger amidst its presence, he sat down, pulled a piece of bread and a slice of ham from his wallet, and began to eat his breakfast calmly. The younger pair stared at him with dismay.

"All Sevenbergen could not find you now," Martin said, chewing deliberately. "You will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you reach Italy; is that the way to carry a purse?"

Gerard looked down. Entangled by its chains to the buckle and strap of his wallet hung a large triangular purse. "This is none of mine," he muttered, attempting to free it. But the friction of the coppice had rendered it inextricable.

"It seems loath to leave me," Gerard sighed, resorting to his knife to sever the connection. Upon examination, the purse was well-provided with silver coins of various sizes, though its bloated appearance was largely due to numerous folded sheets of brown paper. A light dawned in Gerard's eyes. "Why, it must be that old thief's! See! Stuffed with paper to deceive the world!"

The mystery of its acquisition solved itself quickly. The purse had rested at Ghysbrecht's saddle-bow; in his rush to strike, Gerard had unconsciously torn it away, felling his enemy and robbing him in a single, fatal gesture. Delighted by this feat, Gerard hesitated to discard the spoils.

"Throw it away, Gerard, or let Martin take it back," Margaret urged, her voice trembling. "Already they call you a thief. I cannot bear it."

"Throw it away? Give it back? Not a stiver!" Gerard retorted fiercely. "This is spoil lawfully won in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?"

"Of course," Martin agreed readily. "Send him back the brown paper, but the purse or the coin—that were a sin."

"Oh, Gerard!" Margaret pleaded. "You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for that if we take what is not ours?"

But Gerard viewed the situation through a different lens. "It is Heaven that gives it me by a miracle, and I shall cherish it accordingly," he declared, invoking scripture. "Thus the favored people spoiled the Egyptians, and were blessed."

"Take your own way," Margaret submitted humbly, recognizing his resolve. "You are wiser than I am. You are my husband," she added softly, "is it for me to gainsay you?"

These words, coming from a woman who had previously held the whip-hand, surprised Martin momentarily. Later, they would surprise him less. Gerard kissed her tenderly for her wife-like docility, and they continued their journey hand in hand, led by Martin into the depths of the massive forest.

With every step, the feeling of security grew. The townspeople never ventured so far into the trackless wilds. Impetuous natures repent quickly, and Gerard, once clear of immediate danger, felt his conscience prickle.

"Martin, would I had not struck quite so hard," he confessed.

"Whom? Oh! Let that pass; he is cheap served," Martin dismissed.

"I saw his grey hairs as my stick fell on him," Gerard insisted, haunted by the image. "I doubt they will not fade from my sight this while."

Martin grunted with contempt. "Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy is, the older; and the older the craftier, and the craftier the better for a little killing."

"Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!" Gerard shuddered.

"I am much mistaken if you have not," Martin replied cheerfully. "Now Heaven forbid!"

"The old vagabond's skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!"

"Heaven and the saints forbid it!"

"He rolled off his mule like a stone shot out of a cart. Said I to myself, 'There is one wiped out,'" Martin grinned ruthlessly.

Overcome by guilt, Gerard fell to his knees, praying fervently for his enemy's life. Martin's patience snapped.
