"Marriage is a sacred thing," declared Gorgibus, his voice rising with the fervor of a man defending a holy relic. "And it is the mark of a gentleman to begin with it."

"Oh, my dear sir!" Madelon laughed, the sound light and dismissive, like a bird shaking off rain. "If everyone were like you, a novel would end before the first page was turned! Imagine if Cyrus married Mandane immediately, and Aronce wed Clélie right then and there! Let us weave our story at our own leisure, and do not rush us toward the conclusion."

The scene shifted, the French wit dissolving into the quiet, dust-moted air of a country rectory. It would be easy to fault the rector of Pennicote for viewing his niece, Gwendolen, through the lens of a brilliant match. Was he not merely a man of his time, expecting a young woman of such charm to secure a prosperous union? Yet, his intentions were pure, devoid of the cynical calculus that might have suggested she dress in rags to tempt a marquis or hide away to be saved by a stranger. His plans were rational, grounded in the sensible desire to present her advantageously within the neighborhood's best circles.

These plans aligned perfectly with Gwendolen's own heart. But one must not mistake her ambition. She did not dream of marriage as the ultimate prize, nor did she view it as the direct reward for her graces on horseback or her social poise. While she accepted that matrimony was inevitable, and that it would certainly be above the mundane mediocrity of most women, she did not see it as the fulfillment of her soul's yearning. To be the object of desperate sighs and legal suits was a delightful proof of her power; but to become a wife, bound by the heavy chains of domesticity, seemed a tedious necessity.

To Gwendolen, marriage appeared a dreary state where a woman lost her freedom, bore too many children, and sank into a humdrum existence. Of course, it offered social elevation, a promotion she could not refuse, yet promotions often come with bitter herbs. A title does not replace the need for leadership. This delicate, twenty-year-old sylph intended to lead. Though the passions that drive such ambition usually dwell in the hearts of men, they resided here too, nestled among her feminine trappings. Her knowledge, however, was not of the world-shaking variety; she possessed no leverage to move the machinery of state or science. Instead, she sought to do what pleased her in a striking manner, or rather, whatever she could do to dazzle others and feel, through their admiration, a more intense pulse of living.

"Gwendolen will not rest until she has the world at her feet," whispered Miss Merry, the meek governess. These were hyperbolic words, now softened to mean simply that she commanded the attention of a few polite acquaintances in a genteel suburb. Yet, for Gwendolen, standing high in her young self-exaltation, the phrase carried a vast, hazy promise. Others allowed themselves to be swept away like empty ships, tossed by winds they could not control. Not so she. She would not be sacrificed to lesser creatures; she would master the chances life offered, conquering circumstances with her exceptional cleverness.

Certainly, her position at Offendene, marked by the notice of Lady Brackenshaw, the archery club, and dinner invitations from the Arrowpoints, did not scream of extraordinary opportunity. But Gwendolen's confidence rested entirely on herself. She felt fully equipped to rule her destiny. Regarding her past, she believed she had been treated harshly, yet her education had left her unscathed. In the schoolroom, her sharp mind had absorbed the rigid starch of unexplained rules and disconnected facts, shielding her from the pain of ignorance. Through novels, plays, and poetry, she knew enough of the rest of the world. Her French and music were flawless, the standard justifications for a young lady's station. Add to this the innate sense of capability that some are born with—the feeling that any subject they touch reveals their own power to judge it—and who could wonder why Gwendolen felt ready to steer her own ship?

There were countless subjects in the world, perhaps the majority, that bored her, appearing as dull as dim light to the elderly. Yet, if they arose in conversation, she would not falter. No one had ever disputed her power or her general superiority. From her arrival at Offendene onward, the first thought of everyone around her was always, *What will Gwendolen think?* If a footman's boots creaked, if the laundry was subpar, if the wood smoke filled the bedroom, apologies were offered directly to her. If she arrived late to breakfast, the concern was solely that her coffee and toast remain hot and crisp. When she entered, her light-brown hair brushed fresh, her large brown eyes gleaming like a wave-washed onyx beneath long lashes, she was the one who had to exercise tolerance. She had to gently chide Alice for her stiff posture and tell Isabel to retreat, leaving the room to Miss Merry.

She was always the princess in exile, entitled to a breakfast roll made of the finest flour from seven golden ears of wheat, and to have her silver fork preserved during a general evacuation. How was this to be explained? The answer seemed obvious: her beauty, her unusualness, her decisive will that animated her graceful movements and clear tones. On a rainy day when everyone else felt flaccid and the purpose of things faded, her presence alone seemed sufficient reason to maintain the forms of life. Even hotel waiters moved with greater alacrity to clear crumbs and creases when she was near. This potent charm, combined with her status as the eldest daughter—toward whom her mother had always felt a penitent sorrow for the hardships inflicted by a stepfather—seemed a complete explanation for her domestic empire. To seek another cause would be to ask for the reason of daylight when the sun shines.

But beware of drawing conclusions without comparison. I have witnessed the same assiduous, apologetic attention paid to those who were neither beautiful nor unusual, whose firmness lacked grace, and who were not eldest daughters with tender, compunctious mothers. Some were merely common men. The only thread connecting them was a strong determination to have what they desired, paired with a total fearlessness in becoming disagreeable or dangerous when denied. Who is cajoled and served with trembling by the weak females of a household more than the unscrupulous male, capable of doing worse elsewhere if he lacks freedom at home? Thus, I am forced to doubt whether, even without her charm and her special filial position, Gwendolen might not still have played the queen in exile, had she merely kept her inborn energy of egoistic desire and her power to inspire fear regarding what she might say or do.

However, she possessed the charm. Those who feared her were also fond of her; the fear and the affection were perhaps heightened by the iridescence of her character—the play of various, nay, contrary tendencies. Macbeth's rhetoric about the impossibility of being many opposite things at once referred to the clumsy necessities of action, not the subtler possibilities of feeling. We cannot speak a loyal word and remain meanly silent in the same breath; we cannot kill and refrain from killing simultaneously. But a moment is wide enough to hold the loyal and the mean desire, the outlash of a murderous thought, and the sharp, sudden stroke of repentance.
