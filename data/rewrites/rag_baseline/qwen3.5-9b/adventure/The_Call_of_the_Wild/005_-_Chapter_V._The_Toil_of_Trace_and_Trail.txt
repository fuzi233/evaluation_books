Thirty days after departing Dawson, the Salt Water Mail arrived in Skaguay, dragging Buck and his companions into the town in a state of absolute depletion. Buck, once a solid one hundred and forty pounds, had shrunk to one hundred and fifteen. While his mates were naturally lighter, they had shed proportionally more mass than he. Pike, the habitual malingerer who had spent years feigning injury to avoid labor, was now limping with genuine, bone-deep agony. Sol-leks bore a limp of his own, and Dub suffered from a severely wrenched shoulder blade.

Every animal was terribly footsore. The elasticity of their tendons was gone; their paws struck the trail with a heavy, thudding impact that jarred their skeletal structures and compounded the exhaustion of the day's march. Nothing was physically wrong with them save for a profound, systemic collapse. This was not the acute fatigue resulting from brief, excessive exertion, recoverable within hours. It was the slow, relentless drainage of strength accumulated over months of unceasing toil. There remained no power of recuperation, no reserve strength to summon. Every muscle fiber, every cell, was drained dry. The cause was evident: in less than five months, they had traversed two and a half thousand miles, enduring only five days of rest during the final eighteen hundred. Upon reaching Skaguay, they appeared to be on their last legs, barely capable of keeping the traces taut, managing only to stay out of the sled's path on downward slopes.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers operated under the confident expectation of a lengthy stopover. Having covered twelve hundred miles with merely two days of rest, they believed common justice entitled them to an extended period of loafing. However, the influx of prospectors rushing into the Klondike had created a logistical bottleneck of Alpine proportions. Official orders mandated that fresh batches of Hudson Bay dogs replace the current team, deemed worthless for the trail. Since dogs counted for little against dollars, the plan was to sell them.

Three days elapsed, allowing Buck and his mates to fully register the extent of their weakness. On the morning of the fourth day, two men from the United States approached and purchased the entire team, harness included, for a trivial sum. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man of light complexion, possessing weak, watery eyes and a mustache that curled aggressively upward, mocking the limply drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a youth of nineteen or twenty, distinguished by a massive Colt's revolver and a hunting knife strapped to a belt that bristled with cartridges. This belt was his defining feature, advertising a callowness that was sheer and unutterable. Both men were manifestly out of place, their presence in the North a mystery that defied comprehension.

Buck observed the transaction, watching the money change hands between the buyer and the government agent, knowing that the Scotch half-breed and the mail-train drivers were being permanently excised from his life, replaced by Perrault, François, and the others who had preceded them. When driven to the new owners' camp, Buck encountered a scene of slovenly chaos: a tent half-stretched, dishes unwashed, and total disorder. Most notably, he saw a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

Buck watched them apprehensively as they attempted to dismantle the camp. Their movements lacked businesslike efficiency, characterized instead by frantic effort. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times larger than necessary. Tin dishes were packed away dirty. Mercedes constantly obstructed her men, offering a ceaseless stream of remonstrance and unsolicited advice. When they placed a clothes-sack on the front of the sled, she insisted it belong on the rear; once moved, she discovered overlooked items that could fit nowhere else but that specific sack, forcing them to unload again.

Three men from a neighboring tent emerged, grinning and winking at one another.

"You've got a right smart load as it is," one remarked. "It's not me should tell you your business, but I wouldn't tote that tent along if I was you."

"Undreamed of!" cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in dainty dismay. "How in the world could I manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime; you won't encounter any more cold weather," the man replied pragmatically.

She shook her head decisively. Charles and Hal piled the remaining odds and ends atop the mountainous burden.

"Think it'll ride?" one of the bystanders asked.

"Why shouldn't it?" Charles demanded shortly.

"Oh, that's all right, that's all right," the man hastened to say meekly. "I was just a-wonderin', that is all. It seemed a mite top-heavy."

Charles turned his back and secured the lashings as poorly as his incompetence allowed.

"And of course the dogs can hike along all day with that contraption behind them," affirmed a second observer.

"Certainly," Hal replied with freezing politeness, gripping the gee-pole with one hand and swinging his whip from the other. "Mush!" he shouted. "Mush on there!"

The dogs lunged against their breast-bands, straining hard for several moments before relaxing completely. They were incapable of moving the sled.

"The lazy brutes, I'll show them," Hal cried, raising his whip to strike.

Mercedes intervened, seizing the whip and wrenching it from his grasp. "Oh, Hal, you mustn't," she pleaded. "The poor dears! Promise you won't be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won't go a step."

"Precious lot you know about dogs," her brother sneered. "Leave me alone. They're lazy, I tell you. You've got to whip them to get anything out of them. That's their way. Ask anyone."

Mercedes looked at the onlookers imploringly, her pretty face etched with a repugnance at the thought of inflicting pain.

"They're weak as water, if you want to know," one of the men stated flatly. "Plum tuckered out. They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked," Hal spat, his beardless lips curling. Mercedes gasped, "Oh!" in pain and sorrow at the profanity. Yet, being a clannish creature, she immediately rallied to her brother's defense. "Never mind that man," she said pointedly. "You're driving our dogs; do what you think best with them."

Once again, Hal's whip cracked against the animals. They threw themselves against the breast-bands, digging their feet into the packed snow, lowering their bodies to the ground, and pouring forth every ounce of their strength. The sled remained immovable, anchored by friction and inertia. After two futile efforts, they stood still, panting heavily. The whip whistled savagely until Mercedes intervened a third time. Dropping to her knees before Buck, tears streaming down her face, she wrapped her arms around his neck.

"You poor, poor dears," she cried sympathetically. "Why don't you pull hard?—then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck disliked her, yet he felt too miserable to resist her touch, accepting it as part of the day's miserable routine.

One of the onlookers, previously holding his tongue to suppress hot words, finally spoke up.

"It's not that I care a whoop what becomes of you, but for the dogs' sakes, I just want to tell you: you can help them a mighty lot by breaking out that sled. The runners are froze fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, right and left, and break it free."

On the third attempt, following this advice, Hal leveraged his body weight against the pole and freed the runners frozen to the snow. The overloaded, unwieldy sled lurched forward, Buck and his mates struggling frantically under the rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the path curved sharply and sloped steeply toward the main street. Maintaining control of a top-heavy sled required an experienced handler; Hal was neither. As they swung around the turn, the sled tipped over, spilling half its contents through the loose lashings. The dogs never faltered. The lightened sled bounded on its side behind them. Fueled by anger at their mistreatment and the unjust burden, Buck erupted into a run, the team following his lead. Hal screamed "Whoa! whoa!" but they paid him no heed. He tripped, pulled off his feet, and the capsized sled ground over him. The dogs dashed on up the street, scattering the remainder of the outfit along the chief thoroughfare, adding to the chaotic gaiety of Skaguay.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the fleeing dogs and gathered the scattered belongings. They also offered counsel. "Half the load and twice the dogs, if you ever expect to reach Dawson," was the consensus. Reluctantly, Hal, his sister, and his brother-in-law listened, pitched the tent, and overhauled the gear. Canned goods were produced, eliciting laughter from the crowd; canned provisions on the Long Trail were a subject of dreams. "Blankets for a hotel," quoth one of the men who laughed and assisted. "Half as many is too much; get rid of them. Throw away that tent, and all those dishes—who's going to wash them, anyway? Good Lord, do you think you're traveling on a Pullman?"
