Captain Shandon, usually a man of action rather than administration, found himself unexpectedly thrust into the role of editor-in-chief by his wife's persistent nagging. Reluctantly agreeing to the arrangement, he secured for John Finucane, Esquire—a lawyer from the Upper Temple with a penchant for dramatic loyalty—the position of sub-editor at the newly launched *Pall Mall Gazette*. It was a fitting reward; Finucane had long been Shandon's sanctuary, a place where the Captain hid from bailiffs and debt collectors. Though the Sheriff's men eventually learned the secret location of this refuge, turning Finucane's staircase into a regular checkpoint, the bond remained unbroken.

Finucane's chambers became a haven for Mrs. Shandon. Often veiled and trembling, she would descend the stairs to recount her woes, seeking strategies to liberate her "adored" husband. Finucane treated her visits as a high honor, leaning over the balustrade to ensure no rogue "Temple Lovelaces" intercepted her on her way home. In fact, he secretly hoped a villain might attack her, allowing him to rush forward and break the intruder's bones—a fantasy born of genuine affection. He even attended to their child, little Mary, tucking her into her crib in his cramped quarters. While Mrs. Shandon prayed for her husband's safety, Finucane, despite being a Roman Catholic, would fervently add "Amen" to her petitions, his heart swelling with sympathy.

With the editorial structure settled, Finucane departed at six o'clock to meet Mr. Bungay, the publisher, for a strategic dinner. Dressed in his finest (if somewhat garish) attire and carrying four pounds and two shillings—his weekly salary minus the cost of new gloves—he headed to Dick's Coffee House. There, alongside Mr. Trotter, Bungay's business manager, Finucane laid out a master plan for the *Gazette*. With dazzling speed, he outlined the staffing needs: who should cover the markets, the turf, church news, and society gossip. He claimed intimate knowledge of every journalist in London and possessed a genius for saving capital through "ingenious economic contrivances." His calculations left Bungay bewildered yet impressed, leading the publisher to admit privately that the Irishman was remarkably clever.

Seizing the moment, Finucane pivoted to his true objective: freeing Captain Shandon. He detailed the Captain's current debts and argued passionately that confinement hindered productivity. He painted a heartbreaking picture of Mrs. Shandon wasting away and the child falling ill in the Fleet Prison. Moved by this appeal, Bungay agreed to consider the matter the following morning. The meeting concluded with rounds of brandy-and-water, though Bungay insisted on paying the bill himself, leaving Finucane with his full salary intact.

That evening, Finucane arranged a special outing for Mrs. Shandon and Mary. When the Captain expressed concern about his headache, urging them to stay, Mrs. Shandon gently insisted they go. "Go along with you, and Polly," the Captain relented with good humor, handing over his medical texts to Finucane before retreating to his "abominable devices"—his writing.

As the trio walked from the prison gates toward Paternoster Row, they encountered Mrs. Bungay. Dressed in a flamboyant shot-silk gown of red and purple, complete with a yellow shawl and a parasol, she looked every bit the wealthy matron compared to Mrs. Shandon's worn black silk. Despite the contrast, the two women exchanged polite curtseys. Mrs. Bungay, feigning interest in the child, offered to watch Mary while Mrs. Shandon met with her husband. However, the sight of the frightened little girl clinging to her mother softened the stern housewife. Seeing the opportunity, Mrs. Shandon kindly offered to accompany them upstairs, where a second batch of biscuits quickly won Mary's trust.

In the drawing room, the dynamic shifted entirely. While Finucane faced a cold reception downstairs—Mrs. Bungay having lectured her husband earlier on the futility of aiding a "good-for-nothing" Irishman—the charm of the mother and child proved irresistible. Mrs. Shandon spoke with an artless sweetness, detailing her husband's virtues and the child's frailty. Mrs. Bungay, though initially grim, began to melt, recalling her own daughter, Bessy, who bore a striking resemblance to little Mary.

When the couple returned to the drawing room, Mrs. Bungay revealed her change of heart. "The Captain is an Irishman," she explained, "and I've always disliked them. But his wife is a lady... and look at that little girl!" Reminded of her past sorrows, Mrs. Bungay's resolve crumbled. She instructed her husband to assist Mrs. Shandon.

Mr. Bungay, though clumsy in his delivery, announced that he would advance sixty-five pounds to clear the Captain's remaining debt, deducting the sum from future salaries. On the condition that Mrs. Shandon personally negotiated with the creditors, the deal was struck.

The celebration was immediate and raucous. "Bedad, Bungay, you're a trump!" shouted Finucane, tossing Mary into the air and jumping with joy. The ladies, accompanied by the delighted Finucane on the back seat of a one-horse carriage, drove triumphantly away from Paternoster Row. Back at his desk, Bungay reflected wistfully on the transaction, noting that while helping the Captain wasn't strictly wise financially, it soothed his wife's conscience, especially given the recent loss of his own daughter.

True to form, Mrs. Shandon proved herself a skilled negotiator. Armed with the advance payment, she bribed the creditors with ten shillings in the pound, securing their release. By the following Sunday, Captain Shandon was finally free, marking the end of his imprisonment for the foreseeable future.
