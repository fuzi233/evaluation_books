“Moses, though he opposed art, was himself a great artist, possessing the true spirit of creation. Yet his artistic vision—like that of his Egyptian kin—was directed only toward the colossal and enduring. But unlike the Egyptians, who shaped their monuments from brick and granite, he built human pyramids, carved human obelisks. He took a poor shepherd tribe and forged it into a people meant to defy the centuries… He created Israel!”—HEINE: _Geständnisse_.

Imagine Daniel Deronda’s inner world when he left England and when he returned. Then, he had set out for Genoa uncertain whether the desires stirring within him would be nurtured—or whether the truths revealed to him might lead him down paths far removed from the quiet direction his thoughts had lately followed, a path he had half-willingly embraced, yet one whose uncertainty made his longing dangerous. Now, he returned with something like a discovered birthright, a confirmation of the ambition that had begun to stir in his soul. He came back not merely free—but bound by a duty that felt more natural than freedom itself, a bond his experience had prepared him to accept willingly, even without the promise of fulfilling a secret yearning long too fragile to name. But now, at last, he could confess it: his heart had chosen.

Since that moment when he left the Chelsea house in silence, moved deeply by Mirah’s farewell glance and words—those quiet, pleading expressions that stirred in him a deep care for womanhood, awakened as it was when his own lips were still soft as a girl’s—she had held a place in his soul so quietly powerful that it had helped him remain steadfast in word and deed through every trial we know of. There seemed no chance he could ever court her—the woman who had grown dear amidst circumstances that forbade such pursuit. Yet she had become a beloved ideal, dimming the appeal of others, reshaping his affections until absence felt like a lack. That influence had only deepened over time.

Gwendolen’s fate had drawn Deronda closer to her not with love, but with a passionate pity that felt like self-sacrifice. His tenderness, less constrained, flowed freely toward another image—an inner presence entirely unlike Gwendolen. And then there was Mordecai. Their growing connection brought Mirah closer still, not because their relationship changed outwardly, but because she became entwined in all the thoughts that made him hesitate before actions that might wound her brother. This transformation had not passed unnoticed by Deronda. He felt it keenly—like an unspoken desire that he knew better to keep hidden than to confess, even to himself. But when Hans’s attentions flared, the jealousy ignited. When his mother accused him of loving a Jewess, any denial suddenly felt like betrayal. She forced him into a declaration, just as Joseph Kalonymos had compelled him to state his resolve. This clarity, once achieved, transformed him. A surge of energy broke free—the suppression, the hesitation, the endless caution—all released. He laughed at his own impatience as he approached England from Mainz, feeling each mile a burden. It was as if he had found not just ancestry, but a new soul—his judgment no longer drifting in impartiality, but choosing with purpose, embracing kinship as strength, exchanging cold detachment for warm fellowship. He wanted to return to Mordecai—to speak openly, to argue, to agree, to feel again the closeness he’d been denying. And above all, to see Mirah—not as a distant hope, but as someone whose presence he could meet without fear, interpreting her eyes and voice from a new beginning. He wasn’t afraid of Hans’s advances, but he sensed Mirah’s feelings had always flowed along a different course—one unlikely to turn into love. The thought of surprising her by becoming her lover, when she saw him only as a nobleman or guardian, chilled him. He longed for gentler transitions.

No wonder, then, that Deronda went straight from the London station to the small square in Brompton. Every reason urged haste. He had promised Lady Mallinger he’d visit her at the Abbey the next day, and sunset was already falling. He wished to deliver the precious chest to Mordecai—its contents waiting to be studied, both alone and together—and this visit would please Mordecai deeply. So it pleased Deronda too. The currents of his nature were converging: his deep affection for those near him, his need for meaning beyond daily acts, his hunger for connection. In this grand, historic moment—uncovering his heritage—he wore the ordinary clothes of his age, like any hero of old. He didn’t consider how well they suited him; he rarely gave such matters thought. But his own inner glow, the warmth of his being, shone brighter now, visible in his eyes, in the fleeting flush on his skin, as he entered the house wondering what awaited him. He stepped in as quietly as he could.

It was the evening of the same afternoon Mirah had spoken with her father. Mordecai, overwhelmed by her sorrow and the memories it stirred, had abandoned his work. Papers lay scattered across the floor, forgotten. Neither he nor Mirah had bothered to tidy them. They sat motionless, lost in silence, while the clock ticked on the mantel and twilight crept in. Mirah, unable to eat, had not moved since she’d thrown off her cloak and taken her seat beside him, hand clasped in his. He leaned back, eyes closed, breathing labored—Mirah thought he looked as if his soul could no longer endure its frail prison. The idea of his death haunted her whenever she saw him like this, drained of life. Now, to her grief, came the regret that she hadn’t controlled her outburst, which had shaken him so deeply. She watched him—her cheeks pale, her eyes bright with recent tears, her curls tousled like a child’s—watching that thin face as if a veil had fallen forever, as if her joy had died, leaving her strong enough only to mourn. Life stretched before her not just with sorrow, but with double loss—one living, one dead.

Then the door opened. A familiar voice called softly: “Daniel Deronda—may he come in?”

“Come! come!” Mordecai said, rising instantly, his face alight, eyes wide—as if he’d expected this visit all along. Mirah started up, blushing, caught between surprise and alarm.

When Deronda entered, the room seemed to clear after rain. No storm could darken the light of that moment. He reached out to Mirah, standing near her brother’s side, and placed his other hand gently on Mordecai’s shoulder, holding both men at once. He said nothing, reading their faces, then asked anxiously, “Has anything happened? Any trouble?”

“Speak not of trouble,” Mordecai said, shielding her. “There is joy in your face—let it be ours.”

Mirah thought, *It must be something he can’t tell us.* But they sat down, Deronda drawing a chair close to Mordecai.

“That is true,” he said, firmly. “I have a joy that will endure even in the worst sorrow. I did not tell you why I went abroad, Mordecai—because… never mind. I went to learn my origins. And you were right. I am a Jew.”

The two men clasped hands—a gesture born of Mordecai’s sudden spark, passing through Mirah like lightning. But Deronda continued without pause, speaking as much from Mordecai’s mind as his own:

“We share the same blood. Our souls are called to the same purpose. We shall not be divided by life or death.”

Mordecai whispered in Hebrew, barely audible, using the sacred words of covenant: “Our God and the God of our fathers.”

The weight of emotion silenced his usual fluent speech.

Mirah knelt beside her brother, gazing at his face—once so deathly, now radiant. It was an involuntary act, a release from despair into a joy so solemn it felt like a ritual. For a moment, she thought only of her brother’s happiness.

“And it is not only that I am a Jew,” Deronda went on, relishing this rare moment when desire and deed were one, when reality matched his deepest dream. “I come from a line that has fiercely preserved our people’s unity—a family of Spanish Jews, scholars and leaders. And I possess something that will bind us to them. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, kept manuscripts and records, hoping they would reach his grandson. That hope is fulfilled—despite attempts to hide my parentage. I carry the chest containing them, his writings, and it rests below in this house. I mean to leave it with you, Mordecai, so we may study them together. Some I can read—Spanish, Italian. Others are in Hebrew, Arabic, perhaps Latin translations. I only glanced at them in Mainz. We’ll read them side by side.”

He ended with a smile—bright, sudden, breaking through his usual gravity, like sunlight after dusk. But when it turned to Mirah, it struck her too sharply. She rose, returning to her usual posture—hands folded, feet crossed—trying to appear calm. She had knelt instinctively, without thought of herself. But now, under Deronda’s gaze, she remembered Mrs. Grandcourt, the social expectations, and flushed. Deronda, equally sensitive, imagined his own feeling had shown too plainly, and frightened her. He feared any boldness might ruin her regard for him—and thus endanger the bond he shared with brother and sister. If Mirah could not love him, then his declarations might only bring her pain, given how closely they would remain tied.

While these thoughts raced through both minds, Mordecai saw only blessing in Deronda’s presence.

“Daniel,” he said, his voice swelling with meaning, “from the first, I told you—we do not know all the pathways. Has there not been a meeting among them, as in one soul, where a thought is born and draws all things to it, fed and glowing? All things are bound in that Omnipresence which holds the world, and events are like glass through which we see some of the paths. And if it seems that human error and unloving wills have prepared you—as Moses was prepared—to serve your people better, that depends on a higher order than the laws guiding our steps. For man’s evil will does not make a people’s good except by awakening righteous will. Beneath all clouds around the Eternal, this is clear: a people can be blessed only by wise counselors and many whose hearts obey justice and love. See now—it was your loving will that formed the chief pathway, resisting evil. By caring for my sister, seeking her brother, your soul was ready to receive the message: ‘Behold, the multitude of your brethren.’”

“It is true,” Deronda replied. “You and Mirah have been my teachers. Had this truth come before I knew you, I might have resisted it. Perhaps I would have said, ‘If I could choose, I would not be a Jew.’ But now—I feel my whole being consent to it. It is your mind, gradually shaping mine, that has brought this full acceptance.”

At that moment, the first evening in the bookshop flashed vividly in his memory—the distance he’d once felt from Mordecai’s prophetic certainty. He loved satisfying the eager soul before him, like a watcher who finally sees the signal flame on the mountain. He spoke with greater fervor:

“It is through your inspiration that I see my life’s purpose. You have given shape to what I believe was an inherited longing—echoes of passionate thoughts in generations past, especially in my grandfather. Imagine a stolen child of a mountain tribe raised in a city, or a painter born blind—yet the ancestral life remains within, a dim yearning for unknown things, a body like a silent instrument, trembling with latent music. That, I think, has been my experience. Since I learned to read, I’ve longed for an ideal task—where I might be the heart and mind of a people, a leader not sought for glory, but called by duty. You have shown me such a task: to unite our race despite division. You said, ‘Our religion united us before it divided us—it made us a people before Rabbanites and Karaites.’ I will try to build that union. I will work in your spirit. Failure would not be dishonor—but not to try would be.”

“Even as my brother who nursed at my mother’s breast,” Mordecai said, sinking back in his chair, exultant, as if after a finished labor.

To grasp the power of Deronda’s words, one must remember his former restraint, his careful avoidance of premature promises. This pledge carried a sacramental weight—for him, for Mordecai. For Mirah, the effect was profound but different: surprise at the sudden intimacy, a sense of dawn breaking through darkness. But after a pause, Mordecai spoke again:

“It has begun—the marriage of our souls. Only the flesh must pass, and then the betrothed shall unite in a deeper bond. Call nothing mine that I have written, Daniel. Though our masters taught that all should be quoted in the speaker’s name—good rule—that does not exclude the willing union of souls, where thought grows fuller, like waters mingling, inseparable in depth and clarity. I have judged my writings. I wish the body of my thought to die as this flesh will. Let it be reborn from our fuller soul—yours.”

“You cannot ask me to promise that,” Deronda smiled. “I must first see reason in the writings themselves. I am still a slow learner. That blending must happen without choice. But what we cannot stop should not dictate what we ought to do. Our duty is faithful tradition. And you would insist on that for anyone but yourself. Do not ask me to deny my spiritual roots when I find my life’s clue in recognizing my natural ones.”

“I ask no promise until you see the reason,” Mordecai said. “You speak truly—I would follow the Master’s law for another. But for years, my hope, my confidence, has been not that my imperfect words live, but that my vision and passion enter yours—yes, yours. For the one I longed for afar was not another, but you, whom I recognized as mine when you came near. Still, you shall judge. For my soul is satisfied.” He paused, then shifted tone, recalling Deronda’s revelation: “What drove your parents——?” He stopped himself. “No, I ask not about others unless you wish.”

“Some time—gradually—you will know all,” Deronda said. “But now, tell me of yourselves. How has the time passed? I know there’s been trouble. Mirah has suffered.”

He looked at Mirah, but she turned to her brother, pleading silently for him to answer. She hoped he wouldn’t reveal the truth about her father tonight—when Deronda had just drawn so close, identified with her brother. To hear of their disgrace now, as if it were his too, wounded her. To escape, she rose to fetch her hat and cloak, thinking she’d go to her room. Perhaps they’d speak more freely without her. But Mordecai said:

“Today, we faced a grief. A duty long buried has returned, bringing no joy—only dread. But for now, we are free from visible chains. Let us delay speaking of it, as if this evening, deepening around us, were the start of a festival where we offer our first fruits of joy—no mourning mixed in.”

Deronda understood the hint, and said nothing. As Mirah rose, he stood too, saying, “Are you going? I must leave soon—after I and Mrs. Adam lift the chest, and I give the key to Mordecai—no, Ezra—may I call him Ezra now? I’ve come to think of him as Ezra, hearing you say it.”

“Please call him Ezra,” Mirah whispered, faintly, touched by his nearness, his gaze. Was he truly changed? Or was it only her own heart? The emotions of the past hours had drained her. She was weak, hungry, trembling. Deronda saw her pallor, longed to comfort her—but dared not. She reached out, trying to smile. He opened the door for her. That was all.

A man of refined pride avoids courting a woman whose wealth or rank might make his advances seem presumptuous. But Deronda faced a subtler difficulty—one where appearances suggested the opposite, yet love demanded humility. To a reverent heart, the beloved always possesses a kind of nobility. And here, Mirah’s gratitude bound her to him, making every wish of his feel like a claim. Her inability to fulfill it would haunt her, especially in their shared care for Ezra. Thus, Deronda’s fears were not only of pride, but of tender compassion. To be seen as a benefactor—this very role—seemed an insurmountable barrier to declaring love, unless somehow Mirah’s heart had already accepted him. And the agitation within him was immense.

Even a man trained in romance, hardened by practice, may one day be seized by the awe of love—tremble, stutter, show signs of raw feeling beyond his control. How much more so a man whose inner life had guarded his sensibilities, keeping the language of passion alive, fresh as spring leaves on the hillside!

And so, Deronda stood at the threshold, not knowing what lay ahead—only certain that everything had changed.
