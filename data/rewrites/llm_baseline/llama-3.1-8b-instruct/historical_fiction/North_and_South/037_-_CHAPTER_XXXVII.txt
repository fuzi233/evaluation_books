Their visit to the Bouchers was a sobering experience, one that left Margaret feeling disheartened. The widow's thoughts were consumed by her own suffering, and her selfishness extended even to her children, whom she saw as a burden rather than a blessing. As they sat in the cluttered room, Margaret tried to engage the children in conversation, but their words were laced with a deep sadness. But the widow's focus remained fixed on her own pain, and Margaret watched in dismay as she took her children upstairs to view their father's body. The scene was a jarring mix of sorrow and horror, and Margaret felt a pang of regret for the children's innocence being marred by such a cruel sight. She tried to steer their thoughts towards more positive channels, suggesting ways they could help their mother, ways they could honor their father's memory. And to her surprise, the children responded eagerly, setting about the task of tidying the room with a newfound sense of purpose.

But she was resolute in her self-pity, blaming everyone and everything except herself for her husband's tragic fate. The masters, the union, the children – all were guilty in her eyes, and she saw no reason to question her own role in the events that had led to her husband's downfall.
