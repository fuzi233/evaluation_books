How relentless shall the vanity of mankind prove itself, nay, how particularly so among the French? Can it truly be that following the skirmish at Saint-Cast—a mere affair of severing a rear guard, as you know—they proved so unfeeling as to waste countless pounds of powder upon the Invalides in Paris, boasting and blustering over our misfortune? Does any true magnanimity exist in cheering and shouting because five or six hundred brave souls were taken by ten thousand on a shore, and met that fate which awaits the last of the line? I once fancied designing a faithful portrait of the rejoicings in London upon our "glorious" success at Saint-Malo. I pictured the polished cannons drawn in procession by our gallant tars; the sturdy horse-grenadiers prancing past; the rabble waving hats, screaming cheers, pickpocketing, while our friends watched from a balcony in Fleet Street, blessing this scene of British triumph. But now that the French Invalides have been so vulgar as to mimic the Tower, erecting their Saint-Cast against our Saint-Malo, I scorn to touch upon this stale subject. I declare *nolo*, not *malo*: content, for my part, if Harry returns from one expedition and another with his skin intact. Have I ever said he was bruised? Have I not, for fear of distressing my fair young reader, declared he was as well as he had ever been in his life? The sea air had bronzed his cheeks, and the ball whistling past his side-curl had missed its mark. The ocean had soaked his gaiters and garments, yet it had not swallowed his body. True, he had shown the lapels of his coat to the enemy; but for as brief a moment as possible, withdrawing from their gaze with all speed. And what, pray, are lapels but reverses? Coats possess them, as do men; and our duty is to wear them with courage and good humor.

"I can tell you," said Harry, "we were all forced to run for it; and when our line broke, it was whoever reached the boats first who was most fortunate. The French horse and foot pursued us down to the sea, intermingled among us, hacking down our men and bayoneting them where they lay. Poor Armytage was shot ahead of me and fell; I took him up and staggered through the surf to a boat. It was fortunate that the sailors in our vessel were not terrified, for the shots were whistling around their ears, shattering the blades of their oars, and riddling their flag with bullets; yet the officer in command remained as cool as if he were drinking a bowl of punch at Portsmouth, which we shared upon landing, I can assure you. Poor Sir John was less fortunate than I. He never lived to reach the ship, and the service has lost a fine soldier, while Miss Howe has lost a true gentleman to her husband. Such casualties, you see, must happen; and thus his brother secures the promotion—the baronetcy."

"It is of the poor lady I am thinking," said Miss Hetty, to whom our volunteer was recounting his tale. "And the King. Why did the King encourage Sir John Armytage to go? A gentleman could not refuse a command from such a quarter. And now the poor gentleman is dead! Oh, what a state his Majesty must be in!"

"I have no doubt his Majesty will be in a profound state of grief," said Papa, shaking his head.

"Now you are laughing! Do you mean, sir, that when a gentleman dies in his service, almost at his feet, the King of England feels nothing?" Hetty asked sharply. "If I thought that, I vow I would support the Pretender!"

"The sauce-box would make a charming little headpiece for Temple Bar," remarked the General, seeing clearly the mixture of remorse, consternation, and gratitude swirling within Miss Hetty's little heart now that the danger had passed. "No, my dear. Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a burden you would place upon them! I hold a higher regard for his Majesty than to suppose him so weak; and if Miss Hester Lambert obtained her Pretender, I doubt whether she would find any greater happiness. That family was never renowned for excessive sentiment."

"But if the King sent Harry—I mean Sir John Armytage—directly into the war wherein he lost his life, ought not his Majesty repent deeply?" the young lady pressed.

"If Harry had fallen, no doubt the court would have gone into mourning: as it stands, gentlemen and ladies wore colored clothes yesterday," observed the General.

"Why should we not light bonfires for a defeat, and don sackcloth and ashes after a victory?" asked George. "I protest I do not wish to thank Heaven for assisting us to burn the ships at Cherbourg."

"Yes, you do, George! Though I have no right to speak, and you are not even half as clever. Yet when your country wins, you are glad—I know I am. When I flee before the French, I am ashamed; I cannot help it, though I did it," Harry admitted. "It does not seem right to me, somehow, that Englishmen should have to do this," he added gravely. George smiled but chose not to ask his brother what, on the other hand, was the Frenchman's opinion.

"'Tis a bad business," continued Harry, solemnly; "but 'tis lucky 'twas no worse. The story regarding the French is that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was something akin to what you call a dampened chicken. Our entire retreat might have been cut off, only, to be sure, we ourselves were in a great haste to move. The French local militia behaved famously, I am happy to report; and there were numerous gentleman volunteers with them, who displayed themselves in the front exactly as they ought to. They say the Chevalier of Tour d'Auvergne engaged despite the Duke of Aiguillon's orders. Officers brought us a list of our prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe. He is a lord now, since news of his brother's death reached home, George. He is a brave fellow, whether lord or commoner."

"And his sister, who was to have married poor Sir John Armytage—think what her state must be!" sighed Miss Hetty, who had lately become quite sentimental.

"And his mother!" cried Mrs. Lambert. "Have you seen her ladyship's address in the papers to the electors of Nottingham? 'Lord Howe being now absent upon public service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favor of your votes and interests that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother as your representative in Parliament.' Isn't this a gallant woman?"

"A laconic woman," said George.

"How can sons fail to be brave who have been nurtured by a mother like that?" asked the General.

Our two young men exchanged glances.

"If one of us falls defending his country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write thus," said George.

"If Sparta lies anywhere near Virginia, I reckon we have it here," remarked Mr. Harry. "And to think that we should both have met the enemy, and both have been beaten by him, brother!" he added pensively.

Hetty looked at him, recalling him only as he had been the other day: tottering through the water toward the boats, his comrade bleeding on his shoulder, the enemy in hot pursuit, bullets flying everywhere. It was she who had driven him into that peril. Her words had stung him. Now that he had returned, he never rebuked her. Unless directly questioned, he spoke scarcely of his adventures at all. He was exceedingly grave and courteous with Hetty; with the rest of the family, especially frank and tender. Yet those taunts of hers had wounded him. "Little hand!" his looks and demeanor seemed to say, "Thou shouldst not have been lifted against me! It is ill to scorn anyone, much more one who has been so devoted to thee and all thine. I may not be swift of wit, but insofar as the heart goes, I am the equal of the best, and the best of my heart thy family has known."

Harry's grievance, coupled with his magnanimous endurance of it, served to restore him in Miss Hetty's esteem to the place he had lost during the preceding months of inglorious idleness. The reverence the fair pays to the brave she granted him. She was no longer pert in her answers, nor sarcastic in her observations regarding his conduct. In a word, she was a humbled, altered, and improved Miss Hetty.

And all the world seemed to turn toward Harry, as he turned toward the world. He was no longer sulky or indolent; he no longer despaired of himself or defied his neighbors. The colonel of his regiment reported his behavior as exemplary and recommended him for one of the commissions vacated by the casualties during the expedition. Although its conclusion was unlucky, it was, for him, fortuitous. His brother-volunteers, upon returning to St. James's Street, spoke highly of his conduct. These volunteers and their actions became the theme of universal praise. Had he been a general commanding, slain in the moment of victory, Sir John Armytage could scarcely have garnered more sympathy than the nation showed him.
