Until the enemy—otherwise known as Miss Fotheringay—had retreated from the field of battle, Major Pendennis maintained a strict, if subtle, occupation of Fairoaks. He did not *officially* monitor Pen’s every move, but through a network of informants (gardeners, grooms, and the ever-gossipy Mrs. Pybus), the Major kept a ledger of the boy’s sighs, strolls, and ill-advised sonnets.

Ah, the exquisite agony of young love! If you, dear reader, have ever been jilted, deceived, or merely left standing at a dance with a wilted corsage, you may sympathize with Pen’s plight. Nights spent tossing like a ship in a storm! Days wasted composing verses so dreadful they’d make a gravestone weep! The boy was a walking tragedy—or at least, a moderately entertaining farce.

His mother, ever the devoted spectator of his melodrama, fretted silently. The Major, meanwhile, offered sage advice between sips of claret: *"My dear madam, the boy will recover. A woman’s rejection is merely Nature’s way of preparing him for the next one. Vanity, not heartbreak, is the true culprit."*

Mrs. Pendennis, whose own romantic history was as brief as a curtsy, did not argue. She simply knitted, sighed, and occasionally hid sharp objects—just in case.

Fairoaks, though picturesque, was a prison to a man of the Major’s metropolitan tastes. His days were a grim parade of backgammon with the widow (who cheated), dinners with Dr. Portman (who lectured), and the thrilling arrival of the evening post. To stave off boredom, he took up self-doctoring, studied the *Annual Register* for its gripping accounts of the American War, and practiced his cane-twirling in the terrace walk.

Pen, meanwhile, alternated between brooding like Byron and fishing like a man who hoped the carp might offer better conversation than the locals. His poetic output soared—unfortunately, quality did not match quantity. (Sample line: *"O cruel nymph! Why dost thou spurn my heart? / As spurned as eels in Major’s frying pan."*)

Enter Mr. Foker, stage left, in a tandem that scattered chickens and dignity alike. His visits brought levity—and fresh gossip. The village, already a simmering pot of intrigue, now boiled over with the news of Pen’s theatrical misadventures.

Clavering’s high society (if one could call it that) reacted as if Pen had proposed to a fire-breathing dragon rather than an actress. Mrs. Portman, that indefatigable herald of doom, spread the tale with evangelical zeal. Soon, even the butcher’s boy was quoting *Hamlet* at Pen, though with less eloquence and more cabbage-stinking breath.

The climax came when young Hobnell of Wapshot’s Academy, emboldened by cider and stupidity, staged an impromptu graveside parody of Pen’s woes. Pen responded as any self-respecting romantic hero would: with a right hook that sent Hobnell tumbling into an open grave.

The Major, ever the strategist, dispatched Foker to negotiate peace terms. A note was sent. Hobnell, after consulting his fellow scholars (and, presumably, a smuggled flask), agreed to a truce—or at least, to stop reenacting Shakespeare in Pen’s presence.

And so, the village returned to its usual pastimes: judging, whispering, and pretending not to judge. Pen, though bruised in pride and knuckles, lived to brood another day.

**Moral:** *A broken heart mends faster when you’ve a good horse, a bad poem, and at least one friend willing to punch your enemies.*
