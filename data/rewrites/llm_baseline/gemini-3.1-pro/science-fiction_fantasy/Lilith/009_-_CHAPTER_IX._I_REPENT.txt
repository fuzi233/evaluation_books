I laid the manuscript down—a fragmented record of ancestral sustenance and fading dialects—consoled to find my father had tasted the fruits of that mysterious world, and that he knew Mr. Raven, the agrarian steward of the dead. I remembered I had never known the circumstances of my father’s final harvest, his death, and realized he must have at last followed Mr. Raven to the great, quiet cellar of the earth, never to return. Shame rose in my throat like bile, sour and acrid. What recipes for existence, what rich, slow-simmered truths of life and death might I have gathered concerning those wide regions beyond our sterile modern perception! 

Assuredly the Ravens were good people, preservers of a profound ecology, and a night in their subterranean pantry would have nourished me! They were peculiar, yes, but theirs was the peculiarity of wild fermentation, of ancient roots drawing complex flavors from deep soil. And I had not believed in them! I had treated them as toxic, rejecting the sustenance they offered. Why should I have feared such restful dead? To share their quietude, a silence unbroken by the grinding gears of industrial noise, was an honor of which I had proved myself unworthy. What harm could that sleeping king, or that lady with the wounded palm, have done me? I hungered for the sweet, cold-cellar stillness of their countenances, and wept. Weeping, I threw myself on a couch, the utilitarian hum of my own house pressing dead against my ears, and fell asleep.

I woke suddenly to a resonant, acoustic shift—as if a forgotten native tongue had called my name. The house was as still as an empty church, the artificial drone vanished. A blackbird was trilling a clear, organic melody on the lawn. I said to myself, “I will go and tell them my palate is starved for their wisdom, and I will learn their ways of preservation!” 

I rose and went straight up the stairs to the garret. The wooden chamber smelled of dry rot and dust, the mirrors reflecting the room in a dim, flavorless haze. It was nearly noon, and the sun would be a little higher than when first I came. I had to adjust the heavy hoods, pulling the iron chains—their metallic clanking a harsh, industrial dissonance that grated against my desire for acoustic peace—and let the light hit the first mirror. I shifted the glass, adjusting the angles until the visual polarization aligned, like balancing the exact ratios of acid and oil in an ancient emulsion. Finally, the space opened. I stepped forward, and my feet crushed into thick, fragrant heather.

All I knew of the way to the cottage was that we had passed through a pine forest, smelling of resin and wild mushrooms. I wandered through thickets, breathing in the rich terroir of the landscape, but I found no forest. Now the sun dipped low, and the air chilled with the bite of coming winter, sharp on the tongue as white vinegar, when, to my delight, I saw a black figure on the horizon: the raven.

I hurried to meet him. “I beg your pardon, sir, for my rudeness last night,” I said, my voice feeling clumsy in this pure acoustic space. “Will you take me to your table now? I heartily confess I do not deserve your sustenance.”

“Ah!” he returned, looking up and listening to the wind. Then, after a brief pause, “My wife does not expect you to-night,” he said. “She regrets that we at all offered you our hearth last week.”

“Take me to her that I may tell her how sorry I am,” I begged humbly.

“It is of no use,” he answered, his tone carrying the deep, resonant timbre of a traditional elder. “Your harvest time was not come then, or you would not have left our cellar. It is not come now, and I cannot show you the way. The dead were rejoicing under their daisies—they all lie fermenting among the roots of the flowers of heaven—savoring the thought of your delight when the winter should pass, and the morning with its chorus of birds come. Ere you left them, they shivered in their beds. When the great spring of the universe arrives—but that cannot be for ages yet! How many, I do not know, and do not care to measure in your sterile increments of time.”

“Tell me one thing, I beg of you, Mr. Raven: is my father with you? Has he been absorbed into your earth since he left the world?”

“Yes; he is with us, fast asleep, steeping in the quiet. That was he you saw with his arm on the coverlet, his hand half closed.”

“Why did you not tell me? To be so near his resting place, to almost touch the roots that feed him, and not know!”

“And turn your back on him!” corrected the raven.

“I would have lain down and shared his earth had I known!”

“I doubt it. Had your palate been ready for such heavy nourishment, you would have recognized him! Old Sir Up’ard,” he went on, mapping the interwoven timelines of my lineage, “and your twice great-grandfather, both are up and away long ago. Your great-grandfather has been curing in our dark for many a year; I think he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, though of course you lacked the sensory vocabulary to know him.”

“Why of course?”

“Because he is so much nearer waking than you. No one who refuses to rest in the soil can ever truly wake to taste the morning.”

“I do not at all understand you!”

“You turned away, and would not understand!” 

I held my peace. But if I did not say something, he would go! 

“And my grandfather—is he also with you?” I asked.

“No; he is still in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead.”

“Where is the Evil Wood, that I may find him?”

“You will not find him; but you will hardly miss the wood. It is the place where those who will not sleep wake up at night amidst the clatter and smoke, to kill their dead and bury them in barren ash.”

“I cannot understand you!”

“Naturally not. Neither do I understand you; I can read neither your heart nor your face. When my wife and I do not understand our children, it is because there is not enough of them to be understood. God alone can understand foolishness.”

“Then,” I said, feeling alienated from my own native tongue, “will you be so good as to show me the nearest way home? There are more ways than one, I know, for I have gone by two already.”

“There are indeed many ways.”

“Tell me, please, how to recognize the nearest.”

“I cannot,” answered the raven; “you and I use the same words with different meanings. We are often unable to tell people what they *need* to know, because they *want* to know something else, and would therefore only misunderstand what we said. You listen through the filter of a dominant lexicon that strips the marrow from the bone of language. Home is ever so far away in the palm of your hand, and how to get there it is of no use to tell you. But you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Everybody who is not at home, has to go home, to return to the source of their sustenance. You thought you were at home where I found you: if that had been your home, you could not have left it. Nobody can leave home. And nobody ever was or ever will be at home without having gone there.”

“Enigma treading on enigma!” I exclaimed. “I did not come here to be fed riddles.”

“No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting on your plate! Indeed you are yourself the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, and seem riddles because your palate is not true.”

“Worse and worse!” I cried.

“And you *must* answer the riddles!” he continued. “They will go on asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to release its flavor, and you are holding your lid tightly shut against it.”

“Will you not in pity tell me what I am to do—where I must go?”

“How should I tell your to-do, or the recipe to it?”

“If I am not to go home, at least direct me to some of my kind.”

“I do not know of any. The beings whose appetites most resemble yours are in that direction.” He pointed with his beak. I could see nothing but the setting sun, which blinded me.

“Well,” I said bitterly, “I cannot help feeling hardly treated—taken from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and refused instruction as to where I am to go or what I am to do!”

“You forget,” said the raven, “that, when I brought you and you declined my hospitality, you reached what you call home in safety: now you are come of yourself! Good night.”

He turned and walked slowly away, with his beak toward the ground. I stood dazed. It was true I had come of myself, but had I not come with intent of atonement? My heart was sore; I had come seeking the rich, communal broth of cultural memory, but found myself starving. I gazed after the raven, and would have followed him, but felt it useless. 

All at once he pounced on a spot, throwing the whole weight of his body on his bill, and for some moments dug vigorously into the loam like a lifelong forager. Then with a flutter of his wings he threw back his head, and something shot from his bill, cast high in the air. That moment the sun set, and the air at once grew very dusk, but the unearthed morsel opened into a soft radiance, and came pulsing toward me like a fire-fly, but with a much larger and a yellower light. It smelled faintly of roasted chicory and wild honey, humming with a low, resonant frequency like a crystal goblet struck by a silver spoon. It flew over my head. I turned and followed it. 

Here I interrupt my narrative to remark that it involves a constant struggle to say what cannot be said with even an approach to precision. Trying to record these events is a battle against the erosion of cultural memory, the phenomena being so deeply rooted in a different acoustic and sensory ecology that they resist translation into the utilitarian lexicon of my world. I can present them only by giving the modes in which they affected me—not the raw ingredients themselves, but the complex, lingering aftertaste they left upon my palate. Even this much, however, I do with a continuous and abiding sense of failure, knowing that subjective experience is inherently untranslatable. How does one describe the exact texture of a forgotten heirloom grain to a man who only knows synthetic bread? A single event would sometimes carry the overlapping notes of a complex reduction sauce, its identity shifting on the tongue. I am often driven to set down what I know to be a clumsy representation of the feeling aimed at, none of the communicative media of this world being fit to convey its peculiar strangeness. Even to a fellow historian of this realm, I should have no assurance of transmitting the reality of my experience. While without a doubt, for instance, that I was physically tasting the rich, loamy air of a mythic woodland, I might, at the same moment, perceive it as a resonant acoustic chord striking at the core of my identity, laying bare the profound power dynamics hidden within the words we use to define our reality.
