Fra Colonna had free run of the Pope’s vast, cobwebbed library, and often wrapped up his work at the same hour as Gerard, so the two would wander the sun-warmed streets of Rome together. For the young Dutch artist, every step was glowing: the great domes of the churches, the carved marble facades of palaces, the half-buried relics of ancient glory all stretched before him like a page of living legend, wrapped in the soft gold Roman light.

The friar would grant the ruins their grandeur, but pour cold common sense over the rest of Gerard’s starry-eyed wonder.
“This place you see as Rome? It is no more than the tomb of the true, mighty Rome of old.” He pointed out how twenty or thirty feet of the famous triumphal arches lay buried under muck and later construction, how the noisy modern streets ran straight over the tops of broken columns and the foundations of palaces that had stood tall a thousand years prior. Comparing the cramped, small bounds of the current city to the scattered, gargantuan traces of the old civilization peeking through the cobblestones here and there, he offered a sharp, memorable simile: “I tell you, this little village they call Rome is no different from one of those swallows’ nests you must have seen built tucked under the eaves of a long-decayed abbey.”

“Then old Rome must have been beyond beautiful,” Gerard breathed.
“Judge for yourself, my son. Look at the great Cloaca Maxima, the sewer the Romans built when their civilization was still in its infancy – that will outlast Vesuvius itself. Look at the broken fragments of the Temple of Peace. What do you think you would feel if you could see the old Capitol with its full twenty-five temples? This Monte Savello hill? It is nothing but the crumbled ruins of Marcellus’s ancient theatre. And Testacio, one of the highest hills in modern Rome? It is nothing more than an old rubbish heap: the women of old Rome tossed their shattered pots and pans there for generations, and lo – a mountain. *Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem* – judge Hercules by his foot, the lion by his claw.”

Gerard listened respectfully, but when the friar drifted into claiming that the pagan Romans had been proportionally morally superior to their modern descendants, Gerard pushed back hard. “Then do you mean the world lost something when Christ came to us?” He blushed the second he said it, ashamed to speak so sharply to his benefactor.

“Saints forbid!” Colonna said at once. “That would be rank heresy to say so.” But after that quick, formal concession, he wandered off into winding, subtle circumlocution, creeping towards that forbidden idea of pagan virtue via a spiral back staircase of classical references. Mid-debate, they rounded a corner to find a small church porch crammed with onlookers: a demon was being exorcised inside.

Fra Colonna had a curious habit of letting out a soft, quiet moan whenever he was presented with claims no philosopher like Zeno or Epicurus would ever have accepted as fact. The sound, for those who knew him well, carried equal parts unmistakeable dissent and gentle pity for human credulity, ignorance, and error – especially when that error blinded people to the merits of the old pagan world. He moaned now. “Come away, then.”
“Nay, father, please, please,” Gerard begged. “I have never seen a devil cast out in my life.”

The friar sighed, shrugged heavily, and followed Gerard inside. They found the man said to be possessed forced to his knees before the altar, a scarf tied tight around his neck so the officiating priest could hold him fast like a chained dog. Not many onlookers had dared turn up: word had spread that the last demon cast out of that very church had not fled far at all, but hopped straight into one of the onlookers gathered there, the way a ferreted rabbit bolts from one burrow straight into the next.

By the time Gerard and Colonna arrived, the priest decided he had just enough spectators, and began. He turned to the struggling demoniac, breviary in hand, and started calling out names to identify the spirit he was chasing:
“Come out, Ashtaroth. Oho, not you, then. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No, he does not tremble. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul. Aha! I have found you, you crawling reptile, back to your old tricks. Let us pray!
“O Lord, we pray you drive the foul fiend Nebul out of this your creature: out of his hair, and his eyes, out of his nose, out of his mouth, out of his ears, out of his gums, out of his teeth, out of his shoulders, out of his arms, legs, loins, stomach, bowels, thighs, knees, calves, feet, ankles, fingernails, toenails, and soul. Amen.”

The priest rose from his knees, turned to the gathered crowd, and said genially, “Good folks, this is as stubborn a devil as you will see on any summer’s day.” He rounded back on his captive, addressing first the man, then the demon that supposedly spoke through his mouth: one minute the voice screamed hatred for the holy names he kept shouting, the next the demon whined that it felt dreadfully cramped and miserable inside the man’s body.
First the priest blurred the line between the possessed victim and the supposed demon, cuffing the man soundly, kicking him, and spitting repeatedly in his face. Then he lit a candle, held it upside down to burn the flame against his own fingers until he yelped and dropped it. Next he showed the man the ciborium holding the Consecrated Host, then lit another candle to burn his fingers a little more cautiously. At last he spoke gently to the man’s human self, dismissed him, and accepted the warm compliments of the crowd.

“Good father,” Gerard said, wide-eyed, “How well you know all their names. Priests back in my northern Holland have no such exact knowledge of the ranks of hell.”
“Ay, young man, here we know all their names, and all their tricks, the vile reptiles. This Nebul is one of the hardest fiends to ever hunt out.” The priest went on to tell the crowd a string of his exorcism stories, ending with his feat the day before, when he had driven a huge, hulking fiend out of a woman’s mouth. The demon had left behind a mess of nails, pins, and a tuft of its own hair, and shrieked in anguish as it fled: “It is not you that has conquered me. Look at that stone on the window sill! The Archangel Gabriel once landed on that very stone when he came down to earth – it is that that defeated me!”

Fra Colonna moaned again. “And you all believed that story?”
“Of course! Who but an infidel would doubt a revelation that precise?”
“What, believe the father of lies? That is stretching credulity past all reasonable bounds.”
“Oh, a liar does not lie every single time.”
“He does when he tells you an absurd story first, then waves a supposed holy relic at you to arm you against his own kind. If demons existed, they would never be that stupid. You should have answered him with a line from the ancients: *Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes* – I fear the Greeks, even when they bring gifts. Some scoundrel murdered his wife on that window sill stone, young man, mark my words.” He herded Gerard quickly out of the church.

“Alas, father, I fear you embarrassed that good priest,” Gerard said.
“Ay, by Pollux!” Colonna chuckled. “I pricked him all over with a single jab of Socratic interrogation. What modern man can parry the weapons of the ancient world?”

One afternoon, when Gerard had finished his day’s work, a liveried, fine-dressed lackey arrived to summon him to the Cesarini Palace. He went, and was led into a grand, high-ceilinged apartment where a young girl sat working at a tapestry. She rose at once, excused herself, and said she would go alert her mistress.

Gerard cooled his heels there for a full hour, and soon grew fretful. “These nobles think nothing of wasting a poor man’s time.” Just as he had decided to slip out and go back to his work, the door swung open, and a strikingly beautiful young woman entered, followed by two maids. It was the young princess of the house of Cesarini. She had been talking loudly and haughtily to her servants as she came in, but the second she spotted Gerard, she softened her voice to a gentle, feminine lilt. “Are you the scribe, messer?”
“I am, signora.”
“Good.” She sat down, leaving Gerard and her maids standing. “What is your name, good youth?”
“Gerard, signora.”
“Gerard? By Bacchus’s body, is that even a proper name for a human being?”
“It is a Dutch name, signora. I was born in Tergou, in Holland.”
“Such a harsh name for such a handsome young man, girls,” she laughed to her servants, who nodded eagerly in agreement. “Why did I send for him, again?” she said, leaning back on her throne-like armchair in lazy hauteur. “Ah, I remember. Sit down, Ser Gerardo, and write a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover – at least, that is what he claims he is.”

Gerard sat, pulled out his paper and ink, and looked up for her instructions. She looked down at him from her high chair, her eyes just as puzzled as his.
“Well, Gerardo.”
“I am ready, your excellence.”
“Then write.”
“I am only waiting for your words to write down.”
“And who, pray, do you think is supposed to give them to me?”
“Who but your grace, since the letter belongs to you?”
“Mercy on us! You scribes can’t even find the words yourselves? What is the point of your craft if you can’t think of the words for me? I think you might be an impostor, Gerardo.”
“Nay, signora, I am no impostor. I could easily put all your highness’s speech into proper grammar, as well as write it down. But I cannot read your silence. Speak whatever is in your heart, and I will set it down on paper before your very eyes.”
“But there is nothing in my heart. Sometimes I think I have no heart at all.”
“What is in your mind, then?”
“Nothing there, either. Nor in my head, for that matter.”
“Then why write the letter at all?”
“Now that is the first sensible thing either of us has said all afternoon, Gerardo. A pox on that man! Why did he not write to me first? Then I could have replied saying nay to this and ay to that without all this headache. And besides, it is not a lady’s place to say the first word in such matters, is it?”
“No, signora – only the last.”
“Very well spoken, Gerardo. Ha ha! You get a gold coin for that wit. Hand me my purse!” She paid him on the spot, quick as was the custom of the Middle Ages. Gold in his hand only made Gerard eager to help her out of her difficulty. “Signorina,” he said, “these tasks only feel hard because people try too hard, are too fancy, and labor over flowery phrases. Just imagine that the gentleman you love –”
“I do not love him.”
“Very well, the gentleman you do not love, is sitting right at this table, and tell me exactly what you would say to his face.”
“If he sat there, I would tell him, ‘Go away.’”
Gerard, who had been flourishing his pen in preparation, set it down with a groan.
“And once he was gone,” one of her maids Floretta put in, “your highness would say, ‘Come back.’”
“Likely enough, you little minx. Now all of you be quiet, let me think. He pestered me to write, and I promised, so my honour is at stake. What lie can I give Gerardo to pass along to that fool?” She turned her head away, rested her noble chin on her half-clenched white hand, and sank into deep thought.

She was so lovely, statuesque, looking almost like some divine figure lost in lofty meditation, that Gerard forgot everything but his artist’s eye. He picked up his pen and began rapidly sketching that perfect profile on a spare sheet of paper. He was nearly done when the fair “statue” snapped her head around and caught him in the act.
“Nay, Signora,” he said, a little peevishly. “For heaven’s sake, do not shift your posture – it was perfect. See, you are almost finished.”

All eyes turned at once to the drawing, and everyone began chattering at once.
“How lifelike! And done in a minute – wait, I think her highness’s chin is just a little softer than that.”
“Oh, one small touch will fix that.”
“What a shame it is not coloured! I adore colour, nothing beats it. Black and white is so dull. And her highness has such lovely skin – take that away, and half her beauty is gone.”
“Quiet. Can you colour this, Ser Gerardo?”
“Ay, signorina. I am not very skilled at oils, that is where my friend Pietro shines, but in this small scale I can tint it so you would swear it was alive, if you have time to spare for such vanity.”
“Call this vanity? And as for time, it hangs on me like lead. Go get your colours right now – this instant, for the love of all the saints.”
“Nay, signorina, I need time to prepare them. I can come back tomorrow at this same hour.”
“So be it. And you, Floretta, see that he is let in at any hour. Oh! Wait, do not finish her head right now, leave it just as it is!”
“Forgive me, Signora, I was only going to prep the lines at home before I tint it. But I will leave it be. Now let us finish that letter.”
“What letter?”
“The one to Signor Orsini.”
“And do you think I will waste my time writing silly letters to that empty fool, who cares for me no more than he cares for the moon? Nay, not no care – I have just realised my true feelings. I hate him and despise him. Girls, I forbid you ever to mention that man’s name to me again, or I will whip you until the blood runs. You all know how hard I can hit when I am roused.”
“We do, we do,” they said, bobbing curtsies.
“Then do not tempt me.” Her eyes flashed for a second like a sharpened blade, before she turned back to Gerard all soft honey warmth. “Addio, Gerardo.” Gerard bowed and backed out of what he could not help but think of as a velvet tiger’s den.

He returned the next day to colour the portrait, and soon after that the princess commissioned a half-length portrait, then a full-length one. He found himself forced to set aside two hours every afternoon to draw and paint the famously beautiful, famously vain young noblewoman, who had a whole line of suitors begging for portraits of her. Suddenly Gerard’s income was growing steadily, and Margaret felt closer than ever, waiting for him back home.

It was Holy Thursday, and no work was permitted for the day. Fra Colonna and Gerard sat at a high window watching the religious processions wind past below. Their numbers, and the glowing pious ardour of the crowds, thrilled Gerard: the whole city, recently given over to ordinary worldly joys, seemed transformed by that deep devotion.

Before long, the Pope himself paced majestically past at the head of his cardinals, wearing a red hat, white cloak, and a capuchin of red velvet, riding a stunning white Neapolitan barb draped in red velvet fringed and tasselled with gold. A hundred fully armed horsemen rode behind him, lances held upright, the butts resting on their thighs. The cardinals all rode bareheaded – all but one, de Medicis, who rode right beside the Pope chatting to him like an equal. Every fifteen steps the Pope paused for one brief moment, raised his hand to bless the crowd, then moved on again.

Gerard and Colonna climbed down from their window, wound their way through side streets, and reached the portico of one of Rome’s seven great basilicas. The whole facade was hung with black cloth, and soon the Pope and his cardinals, who had entered the church by a different door, stepped out onto the steps holding torches, separated from the crowd by wooden barriers. A canon then read out a Latin papal bull, excommunicating several named people – most notably any princes who were keeping the Church from any of its temporal lands.
The awed ceremony made Gerard tremble, and the rest of the crowd trembled right along with him – but two of the cardinals ruined the whole effect, laughing openly and unreservedly the entire time.

Once the reading was done, the black cloth was stripped away to reveal bright, richly coloured hangings. The Pope blessed the assembled crowd, then tossed his torch down into their midst – and two of his cardinals did the same. Immediately a wild scramble broke out over the burning torches: people fought over them, tore them to pieces, competing so fiercely for small fragments of the sacred flame that many walked away with black eyes, bloody noses, and burnt fingers. While the crowd was caught up in that chaos, the Pope and his entire retinue withdrew peacefully.

Then a cry went up, and the crowd surged towards a square where a large open stage had been set up, with a group of priests praying on top. They rose, and with great ceremony pulled on bright red gloves. One of them knelt, and with every sign of deep reverence drew a square frame like a mirror out of a shrine. Inside was what looked like the faint impression of a human face.
It was the Verum Icon, the true image of Our Saviour’s face, taken at the very moment of His most terrible mortal agony. Received by every Christian there without a single grain of doubt, the sight moved every heart in the crowd. People threw themselves face down on the ground the second the priest held it high, cries of pity burst from every mouth, and tears stood in almost every eye.
