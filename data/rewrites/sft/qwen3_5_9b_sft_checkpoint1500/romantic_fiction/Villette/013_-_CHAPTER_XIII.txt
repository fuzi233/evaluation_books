I found myself compelled to smile, nay, to laugh, at Madame once more within the span of forty-eight hours following our last encounter.

Villette possesses a climate as fickle, though perhaps less damp, as any English town. A night of fierce winds gave way to a subsequent day of dry tempest; the air was thick with darkness and cloud, yet devoid of rain. The streets were dim, choked with sand and dust swirling from the boulevards. Even if the weather had been fair, I doubted I would have relished spending my evening of study and leisure in the garden where I had resided yesterday. My alley, and indeed every walk and shrubbery within the grounds, had acquired a novel, albeit unwelcome, interest. Their seclusion had become precarious; their tranquility insecure. That casement, which had previously showered me with billets, had vulgarized the once cherished nook it overlooked. Elsewhere, the eyes of the flowers seemed to have gained sight, and the knots in the tree-trunks listened like secretive ears. Some plants, alas, had been trampled by Dr. John in his frantic search, his hurried and heedless progress leaving chaos in its wake. Though I desired to prop up, water, and revive what he had disturbed, I found some plants crushed. Furthermore, he had left footprints on the flower beds. Despite the strong wind, I managed to erase these traces with a brief moment's leisure early in the morning, before prying eyes could discover them. With a pensive sort of contentment, I seated myself at my desk to tackle my German as the pupils settled into their evening lessons, while the other teachers picked up their needlework.

The setting for the "evening study" was invariably the refectory, a significantly smaller apartment than any of the three schoolrooms. Here, access was restricted solely to the boarders, numbering merely a dozen. Two lamps hung from the ceiling above two tables; their lighting at dusk signaled the abandonment of schoolbooks, the adoption of a grave demeanor, the enforcement of general silence, and the commencement of "la lecture pieuse." As I soon discovered, this so-called "pious reading" was primarily designed as a wholesome mortification of the intellect and a useful humiliation of reason—a heavy dose for Common Sense to digest at her leisure.

The volume produced (which was never changed, but recommenced upon finishing) was a venerable tome, as old as the hills and grey as the Hôtel de Ville. I would have given two francs for the chance to seize that book, turn over its sacred yellow pages, ascertain the title, and read with my own eyes the monstrous fabrications that, as an unworthy heretic, I was only permitted to consume with bewildered ears. This book contained legends of the saints. Good heavens! (I speak these words reverently) what legends they were. What brazen rascals those saints must have been if they first boasted of these exploits or invented these miracles. These legends were nothing more than monkish extravaganzas, over which one laughed inwardly; alongside them were priestly matters, and the priestcraft of the book was far worse than its monkery. My ears burned on either side of my head as I listened, forced to endure tales of moral martyrdom inflicted by Rome; the dread boasts of confessors who had wickedly abused their office, trampling high-born ladies into deep degradation, making countesses and princesses the most tormented slaves under the sun. Stories like that of Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary recurred again and again, laden with dreadful viciousness, sickening tyranny, and black impiety—nightmares of oppression, privation, and agony.

I sat out several evenings of this "pious reading" as quietly and patiently as possible, breaking off the points of my scissors only once by inadvertently stabbing them deeply into the worm-eaten wood of the table. But eventually, the heat became unbearable. My temples, heart, and wrist throbbed so violently, and my sleep afterwards was shattered by excitement, that I could no longer remain. Prudence dictated henceforth a swift clearance of my person from the premises the moment the guilty old book was brought out. Never did Mause Headrigg feel a stronger call to testify against Sergeant Bothwell than I did to voice my opinion regarding the popish "lecture pieuse." Nevertheless, I managed somehow to curb and rein in my impulses. Though I always shot from the room quickly as soon as Rosine lit the lamps, I did so quietly, seizing the vantage moment afforded by the slight bustle preceding the dead silence, vanishing while the boarders stowed their books away.

When I vanished, I entered darkness. Candles were not permitted to be carried about, and the teacher who abandoned the refectory possessed only the unlit hall, schoolroom, or bedroom as a refuge. In winter, I sought the long classrooms, pacing them rapidly to maintain warmth—fortunate if the moon shone and stars were present, soon reconciled to their dim gleam or even to their total absence. In summer, it was never truly dark, and I ascended to my quarter of the long dormitory, opened my casement (that chamber was illuminated by five casements as wide as great doors), and leaned out to gaze upon the city beyond the garden, listening to band music drifting from the park or palace square, meanwhile entertaining my own thoughts, living my own life, within my own still, shadow-world.

That evening, fleeing as usual before the Pope and his works, I mounted the staircase, approached the dormitory, and quietly opened the door, which was always kept carefully shut and revolved noiselessly on well-oiled hinges. Before I *saw*, I *felt* that life was present in the great room, usually void. Not that there was stir, breath, or rustle of sound, but vacuum lacked; solitude was not at home. All the white beds—the "lits d'ange," as they were poetically termed—lay visible at a glance; all were empty; no sleeper reposed therein. The sound of a drawer sliding out cautiously struck my ear. Stepping slightly to one side, my vision took a free range, unimpeded by falling curtains. I now commanded my own bed and my own toilet, with a locked workbox upon it and locked drawers underneath.

There stood a dumpy, motherly little figure, clad in a decent shawl and the cleanest possible nightcap, working diligently before this toilet, apparently performing me the kindness of "tidying out" the "meuble." The lid of the workbox stood open, the top drawer ajar; duly and impartially was each succeeding drawer opened in turn. Not a single article of their contents was lifted and unfolded, not a paper glanced over, not a little box unlidded; and beautiful was the adroitness, exemplary the care with which the search was conducted. Madame worked at it like a true star, "unhasting yet unresting." I will not deny that I watched her with a secret glee. Had I been a gentleman, I believe Madame would have found favor in my eyes; she was so handy, neat, and thorough in everything she did. Some people's movements provoke the soul by their loose awkwardness, but hers satisfied it by their trim compactness. I stood, in short, fascinated; but it was necessary to make an effort to break this spell, for a retreat must be beaten. The searcher might have turned and caught me; there would have been nothing for it then but a scene, and she and I would have had to confront each other suddenly, stripped bare of conventions and disguises. We would have looked into each other's eyes and known that we could work together no more, and parted for eternity.

Where was the use of tempting such a catastrophe? I was not angry, nor did I harbor any wish to leave her. I could hardly find another employer whose yoke would be so light and easy of carriage; truly, I liked Madame for her capital sense, whatever I might think of her principles. As to her system, it did me no harm; she might work me with it to her heart's content, and nothing would come of the operation. Loverless and inexpectant of love, I was as safe from spies in my heart-poverty as the beggar from thieves in his destitution of purse. Turning, then, I fled, descending the stairs with progress as swift and soundless as that of a spider running down the banister at the same instant.

How I laughed when I reached the schoolroom. I knew now she had certainly seen Dr. John in the garden; I knew what her thoughts were. The spectacle of a suspicious nature so far misled by its own inventions tickled me greatly. Yet, as the laugh died, a kind of wrath smote me, followed by bitterness. It was the rock struck, and Meribah's waters gushing out. I had never felt such a strange and contradictory inward tumult as I did that evening: soreness and laughter, fire, and grief shared my heart between them. Hot tears welled up—not because Madame mistrusted me (I did not care twopence for her mistrust)—but for other reasons. Complicated, disquieting thoughts disrupted the entire repose of my nature. However, that turmoil subsided; by the next day, I was again Lucy Snowe.

Upon revisiting my drawers, I found them all securely locked; the closest subsequent examination could not discover any change or apparent disturbance in the position of a single object.
