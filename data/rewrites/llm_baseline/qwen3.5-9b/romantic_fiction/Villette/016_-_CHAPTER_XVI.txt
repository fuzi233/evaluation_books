My soul's journey during that swoon remains a mystery to me. Whatever visions she witnessed, or where she traveled within her trance on that strange night, she guarded her secrets fiercely, never whispering a word to Memory and baffling imagination with an absolute silence. Perhaps she ascended toward her eternal home, hoping for permission to rest, believing her painful union with matter had finally dissolved. Yet, as she thought this, an angel may have barred her entry at heaven's threshold, guiding her weeping downward until she was bound once more—shuddering and unwilling—to that frail, cold body she had grown weary of inhabiting.

I know she returned to her prison with pain, reluctance, a moan, and a long shiver. The divorced mates, Spirit and Substance, were difficult to reunite; they greeted one another not in an embrace, but in a racking struggle. When my sense of sight returned, everything appeared red, as if swimming in blood; hearing rushed back loudly like thunder; consciousness revived in terror. I sat up appalled, wondering into what strange region, among what bizarre beings, I was waking. At first, I recognized nothing. A wall was not a wall; a lamp was not a lamp. I should have understood what we call a ghost as easily as I understand the commonest object, which implies that everything my eyes rested upon struck me as spectral. But soon the faculties settled into their places, and the life-machine resumed its regular work.

Still, I did not know where I was. Only later did I realize I had been moved from the spot where I fell. I lay not on a portico step; night and tempest were excluded by walls, windows, and a ceiling. I had been carried into a house—but what house?

My thoughts drifted immediately to the pensionnat in the Rue Fossette. Half-dreaming, I tried to determine which room I occupied: the great dormitory or one of the smaller ones? I was puzzled because the glimpses of furniture I saw did not match my memory of any of those apartments. The empty white beds were missing, along with the long line of large windows. "Surely," I thought, "they have not carried me to Madame Beck's own chamber!" Then my eye fell upon an easy-chair covered in blue damask. Other matching cushioned seats appeared gradually, revealing the complete fact of a pleasant parlor: a wood fire on a clear-hearth, a carpet where bright blue arabesques relieved a fawn-colored ground, and pale walls adorned with an endless garland of azure forget-me-nots tangled among golden leaves and tendrils. A gilded mirror filled the space between two windows, curtained in ample blue damask. In this mirror, I saw myself lying not in bed, but on a sofa. I looked spectral; my eyes appeared larger and more hollow, my hair darker by contrast with my thin, ashen face. It was obvious, from the furniture and the layout of the room, that this was an unknown chamber in an unknown house.

No less evident was the unsettled state of my brain. As I gazed at the blue armchair, it seemed to grow familiar, as did a certain scroll-couch and a round center-table covered in blue cloth bordered with autumnal foliage. Most striking were two small footstools with worked covers and a small ebony-framed chair, its seat and back decorated with brilliant floral groups on a dark background.

Struck by these details, I explored further. Strangely, old acquaintances surrounded me, and "auld lang syne" smiled from every nook. Over the mantelpiece hung two oval miniatures, known to me by heart: the pearls around the high, powdered heads; the velvets circling the white throats; the swell of the full muslin kerchiefs; the pattern of the lace sleeve-ruffles. On the shelf sat two china vases, relics of a diminutive tea-service smooth as enamel and thin as eggshell, alongside a white alabaster centerpiece preserved under glass. I could have identified the peculiarities of all these items, numbering flaws and cracks like a clairvoyant. Above all, there was a pair of handscreens with elaborate pencil drawings finished like line engravings. My eyes ached to behold them again, recalling hours spent following the tedious, feeble, finical strokes of a schoolgirl's pencil held in fingers now so skeleton-thin.

Where was I? Not only in what part of the world, but in what year of our Lord? For all these objects belonged to past days and a distant country. Ten years ago I had bid them goodbye; since my fourteenth year, they and I had never met. I gasped audibly, "Where am I?"

A shape previously unnoticed stirred, rose, and came forward: a figure inharmonious with the surroundings, complicating the riddle further. It was merely a native *bonne* in a commonplace cap and printed dress. She spoke neither French nor English, and I gained no intelligence from her dialectic phrases. Yet she bathed my temples and forehead with cool, perfumed water, raised the cushion beneath me, signaled that I should not speak, and resumed her post at the foot of the sofa.

She was busy knitting. With her eyes drawn away from me, I could gaze at her uninterrupted. I wondered greatly how she had come there, or what she could possibly have to do among the scenes and days of my girlhood. Even more, I marveled what those scenes and days could possibly mean to me now.

Too weak to scrutinize the mystery thoroughly, I tried to dismiss it as a mistake, a dream, or a fever fit. Yet I knew there could be no mistake, that I was not sleeping, and that I believed I was sane. I wished the room were less brightly lit so I might not see so clearly the little pictures, the ornaments, the screens, and the worked chair. All these objects, along with the blue-damask furniture, were precisely the same in every minutest detail as those I so well remembered from the drawing-room of my godmother's house at Bretton. It seemed only the apartment itself had changed, differing in proportion and dimension.

I thought of Bedreddin Hassan, transported in his sleep from Cairo to the gates of Damascus. Had a Genius stooped his dark wing down the storm to which I had succumbed, gathering me from the church steps and bearing me over land and ocean, to lay me quietly beside a hearth of Old England? But no; I knew the fire of that hearth had burned before its *Lares* no longer—it had gone out long ago, and the household gods had been carried elsewhere.

The *bonne* turned to survey me again. Seeing my eyes wide open and presumably perturbed, she set down her knitting. I watched her briefly at a small stand, pouring out water and measuring drops from a phial. Holding a glass, she approached me. What dark-tinged draught might she offer? Some elixir of Genii or distillation of Magi?

It was too late to inquire. I had swallowed it passively and instantly. A tide of quiet thought gently caressed my brain; softer and softer rose the flow, with tepid undulations smoother than balm. The pain of weakness left my limbs; my muscles slept. I lost the power to move, but losing the wish to do so, it was no privation. The kind *bonne* placed a screen between me and the lamp. I saw her rise to do this, but do not remember seeing her resume her place. In the interval between the two actions, I "fell on sleep."

When I woke, everything had changed again. High daylight surrounded me—not a warm summer light, but the leaden gloom of raw, blustering autumn. I felt sure now that I was in the pensionnat, certain by the beating rain on the windowpane, the "wuther" of wind among the trees indicating a garden outside, and the chill, whiteness, and solitude in which I lay. I say *whiteness*, for dimity curtains dropped before a French bed bounded my view.

I lifted them and looked out. My eye, prepared to take in the range of a long, large, whitewashed chamber, blinked baffled upon encountering the limited area of a small cabinet with sea-green walls. Instead of five wide, naked windows, there was one high lattice shaded with muslin festoons. Instead of two dozen little stands of painted wood holding basins and ewers, there was a toilette-table dressed like a lady for a ball in a white robe over a pink skirt, crowned by a polished large glass and a pretty pin-cushion frilled with lace. This toilette, together with a small, low green-and-white chintz armchair, a washstand topped with a marble slab, and supplied with utensils of pale-green ware, sufficiently furnished the tiny chamber.

Reader, I felt alarmed! Why? You will ask. What was there in this simple, somewhat pretty sleeping closet to startle the most timid? Merely this: These articles of furniture could not be real, solid armchairs, looking-glasses, and washstands—they must be the ghosts of such articles. Or, if this were denied as too wild a hypothesis—and, confounded as I was, I *did* deny it—there remained but to conclude that I had passed into an abnormal state of mind; in short, that I was very ill and delirious. And even then, mine was the strangest figment with which delirium had ever harassed a victim.
