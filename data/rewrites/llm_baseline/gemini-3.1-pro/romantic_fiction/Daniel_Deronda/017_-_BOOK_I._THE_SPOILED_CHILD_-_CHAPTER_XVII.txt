On a fine evening near the end of July, Deronda was rowing himself on the Thames, seeking refuge from the iron-throated clamor of the city. It had been a year since his return to England, a period spent ostensibly studying law in deference to Sir Hugo, but largely consumed by a profound alienation from the superficial commercialization of his social sphere. London’s industrial noise pollution—the grinding gears of factories, the incessant clatter of carriages, the cacophony of a society built on rapid extraction—battered at his psychological quietude. He had found that his only true solace lay in the acoustic ecology of the river, where the rhythmic, fluid resistance of the oars offered a healing sonic environment. 

Here, the cyclical pacing of the tides mirrored the ancient, multi-generational sagas of human movement. He rowed in a dark-blue shirt and skull-cap, taking deliberate breaths of the complex olfactory landscape: the sharp, loamy scent of riverbank moss, the metallic tang of moving water, and the faint, sweet decay of willow leaves. In his boat, wrapped meticulously in a damp linen cloth to preserve its crumb, sat his evening sustenance: a dense loaf of rye bread fermented slowly by a traditional agrarian baker in Surrey, paired with a wedge of raw-milk cheese that tasted vividly of summer pastures and clover. Deronda possessed a meticulous reverence for such food, recognizing the profound meaning and historical continuity embedded in its creation. To view a meal as mere utilitarian fuel was, to him, a tragic erasure of the human hands that coaxed life from the soil.

As he approached the neighborhood of Kew Bridge between six and seven o’clock, the auditory sanctuary of the river was disrupted. A great coal barge advanced toward him, its mechanical churning a violent intrusion that masked the delicate insect-murmurs and avian calls of the evening. Deronda guided his boat aside, resting on his oars to let the behemoth pass. To counteract the abrasive roar, he unconsciously engaged in a restorative acoustic practice, his throat humming a low-toned chant that had haunted him all day—the gondolier’s song from *Otello*, where Rossini had worthily set the immortal words of Dante: *“Nessun maggior dolore Che ricordarsi del tempo felice Nella miseria.”*

As the barge’s wake subsided, the pianissimo fall of his melodic wail—*nella miseria*—echoed distinctly against the water’s brink. It was then he turned and saw a figure who seemed to have stepped out of a forgotten myth into the everyday reality of the riverbank. She was a girl of hardly eighteen, fragile and slight, with a delicate face framed by dark curls pushed beneath a large black hat. A heavy woolen cloak hung over her shoulders. She stood completely immobile, her hands clasped, staring at the water with a despair whose subjective depths defied translation. 

The abrupt arrest of his attention silenced his song. Without the protective resonance of his voice, the girl seemed to shatter out of her trance. She turned with the frightened, trembling posture of a fawn. Her gaze met Deronda’s—a long, searching look across the water, carrying the untranslatable weight of a sorrow that the dominant English lexicon could never adequately name. She seemed only half-conscious of her physical surroundings. Deronda felt an immediate, overwhelming surge of compassion, a recognition of shared humanity that transcended their silence, but she quickly turned and retreated to a bench under a nearby tree. 

Unwilling to intrude upon her vulnerability, he rowed away, his mind swept into the circular currents of historical tragedies. He fell to musing on the countless unrecorded girl-tragedies hidden in the hedgerows of time, where the helpless drag wounded wings, their stories erased from cultural memory. The image of her delicate, pale face and brown-black drapery remained etched in his mind like an ancient cameo. He could not merely float on the tide of the world’s routine apathy. The shadows were lengthening, the mellow light turning the river into a canvas of copper and bruised plum, and his thoughts coalesced into a passionate vision of responsibility. 

He allowed the tide to carry him back toward Kew Gardens, seeking a solitary bend where he could lodge his boat and partake of his humble, grounding meal while the stars began their ancient call-and-response in the darkening sky. But as he settled, the rustle of willow branches across the water disrupted the stillness. It was the girl. 

The dying sunlight painted her small face with an ethereal, tragic gold. Deronda watched with motionless attention as she took off her woolen cloak, seated herself, and deliberately submerged the heavy fabric in the river. She held it there, letting the thirsty fibers drink the water, preparing a drowning shroud. The precise, heavy sound of the wet wool being hauled from the river struck Deronda’s ears with the force of a physical blow. 

He seized his oars, the rhythmic splash cutting a desperate trajectory across the water. The girl, overcome with terror at his approach, sank down on the brink, crouching and covering her face. 

He steadied the boat against the bank, modulating his voice to its gentlest, most resonant timbre so as not to fracture her fragile state. "Don’t be afraid. You are unhappy. Pray, trust me. Tell me what I can do to help you."

She raised her head. The light caught his face, and she recognized him. When she finally spoke, her low, sweet voice carried a distinct, melodic accent—an echo of overlapping diasporas, suggesting a native dialect that had been partially overwritten but not entirely subdued by the English tongue. 

"I saw you before," she murmured, and then added, dreamily, "nella miseria."

"It was you, singing?" she continued, hesitatingly. "Nessun maggior dolore." 

Her utterance of the Italian syllables carried the precise, aching musicality that Deronda had been striving for. "Ah, yes," he said, understanding the connection. "I am often singing them. But I fear you will injure yourself staying here. Pray let me take you in my boat to some place of safety. And that wet cloak—let me take it."

She looked at him, her eyes searching for the hidden motives so often buried in the language of strangers. "You look good. Perhaps it is God’s command."

"Do trust me. Let me help you. I will die before I will let any harm come to you."

She dragged the saturated cloak, letting it fall heavily to the earth, and stepped backward. Her hands, chilled and delicate, rested against her waist. The raw, unmediated grief of her posture drew from Deronda an awe-struck whisper: "Great God!" It was a reverence for inexorable calamity, a sudden, piercing thought that perhaps his own unknown mother had once looked like this—an intersection of personal identity and mythic repetition.

He extended his hand, and her tiny fingers closed around his. But she paused. "I have nowhere to go—nobody belonging to me in all this land."

"I will take you to a lady who has daughters," Deronda said instantly, visualizing the warm, restorative sanctuary of Mrs. Meyrick’s home in Chelsea. 

"Do you belong to the theatre?" she asked timidly, as if fearing the superficial artifice and commercial exploitation of the stage.

"No; I have nothing to do with the theatre," he assured her firmly. "I will put you in perfect safety at once; with a lady, a good woman... Life may still become sweet to you."

She stepped into the boat, yielding to the ancient domestic routine of being sheltered. Deronda retrieved her hat from the bushes and hauled the dripping cloak into the hull. He took off his own coat, offering it with a gentle smile. She wrapped it cleverly around her shoulders, accepting the tactile warmth. 

"I have some biscuits," Deronda offered, opening a small tin to reveal hard-tack baked with rolled oats, wild honey, and toasted coriander seed—a meticulously sourced recipe meant to sustain the body and spirit. "Should you like them?"

"No; I cannot eat. I had still some money left to buy bread."

He began to ply his oars. The twilight deepened into the indigo of a midsummer night. She leaned forward, watching the wooden blade break the surface of the water, evidently finding solace in the acoustic rhythm. 

"I like to listen to the oar," she broke the silence at last, her voice harmonizing with the fluid environment.

"So do I."

"If you had not come, I should have been dead now... I cannot see how I shall be glad to live. The *maggior dolore* and the *miseria* have lasted longer than the *tempo felice*." She paused, her mind wrestling with the untranslatability of her past. "Dolore—miseria—I think those words are alive."

Deronda remained mute, profoundly moved by her recognition of how cultural memory breathes within language. To force her into the sterile confines of English interrogation would be a violation. 

"I thought it was not wicked," she mused. "Death and life are one before the Eternal. I know our fathers slew their children and then slew themselves, to keep their souls pure. I meant it so. But now I am commanded to live."

"You will find friends. I will find them for you," he promised.

She shook her head. "Not my mother and brother. I cannot find them."

"You are English? You must be—speaking English so perfectly."

She strained to see him in the dim, double light of the moon and the river, her sorrowful isolation making the boundary between internal memory and outward reality difficult to distinguish. The question of her origin highlighted the subtle power dynamics of the tongue they shared. 

"You want to know if I am English?" she asked, while Deronda reddened, realizing the clumsiness of his dominant lexicon. "Yes, I will tell you. I am English-born. But I am a Jewess."

Deronda was silent, absorbing this revelation, noting how it intertwined with the exotic, untranslatable cadences of her grief. 

"Do you despise me for it?" she asked, her voice a piercing cry of a creature accustomed to the harsh judgments of a homogeneous society.

"Why should I?" he said. "I am not so foolish."

"My mother and brother were good. But I shall never find them. I am come a long way—from abroad. I ran away... This morning when the light came, I felt as if one word kept sounding within me—Never! never! But now... perhaps we are going to her." 

She buried her head on her knees, surrendering to an outburst of passionate weeping. Deronda steered the boat, his mind rejecting his initial thought of taking her to his home in Park Lane. The glaring gaslights, the staring lackeys, and the chilling suspicions of the housekeeper would act as an acoustic and visual violence against her already susceptible mind. Instead, the Meyricks’ small house in Chelsea presented itself as a haven. There, traditional domesticity was elevated to an art; tea was steeped with precision, bread was broken with reverence, and the three daughters lived with a romantic, generous appreciation for history. They would fold this lovely, sorrowful Jewish girl into their care just as the mythic figures in their beloved sagas would. 

When they transferred to a cab, the rumbling, industrial jolting of the carriage replaced the soothing rhythm of the river, making it difficult for her tired head to rest. Yet she dozed, her sweet head hanging helpless. Watching her, Deronda recalled the mythic story from Plutarch about the Delphic women—how, when the exhausted, wandering Maenads collapsed in the marketplace, the matrons of the city stood a silent, protective guard over their slumbers, seamlessly integrating a profound act of divine rescue into their everyday domestic routine. 

He knew the women of Chelsea would do the same. As the carriage rattled through the darkened streets, Deronda felt himself stepping into a multi-generational cycle, discovering that the act of rescuing a fellow creature was as much about preserving the delicate, untranslatable poetry of a life as it was about drawing breath.
