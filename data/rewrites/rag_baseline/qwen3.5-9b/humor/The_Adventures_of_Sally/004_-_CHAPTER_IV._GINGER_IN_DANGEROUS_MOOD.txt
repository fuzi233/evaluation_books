The air in Piccadilly was thick with the static of recent travel. Bruce Carmyle intercepted his cousin, Ginger, on their separate returns from Roville. Ginger, preferring the distance, offered a hurried nod, but Carmyle halted him. "Just the man," Carmyle stated, extending a cigarette case. Ginger accepted it with the wary suspicion of a passenger accepting a card from a stage magician. It was a display of geniality so foreign to their history that it startled him; the affair Scrymgeour still bled, and the Family was already in session over it.

"Back in London?" Carmyle asked.
"A day or two."
"I heard you'd returned. Staying at the club. Also, thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas."

Ginger recoiled. "What!"
"In that compartment at Roville. You threw her onto me. We agreed it counted as an introduction. She's attractive."

Carmyle was not entirely decided on Sally, but he was certain she should not vanish from his orbit. Her departure had left him with a baffled dissatisfaction, a hunger to prove he possessed more depth than she suspected. He was piqued. A future without her felt flat.
"A very attractive girl. Pleasant talk."
"I bet you did," Ginger said, envious. "Did she give you her address?"
"Why?" Ginger asked, guarding the information like a collector protecting a rare artifact.
"Well, I promised to send her some books..."
"She doesn't get much time for reading."
"Books not published in America."
"Oh, pretty nearly everything is."
"These particular ones aren't," Carmyle said, his tone sharpening. Finding Ginger's reserve tedious, he pressed further. "Give them to me; I'll send them."
"Good Lord, man!" Carmyle snapped. "Where does she live?"

Ginger yielded the sacred street name, grudgingly, knowing escape was impossible against a persistent devil like his cousin. Carmyle noted the address in a dapper, morocco-bound notebook, a man who never relied on the backs of envelopes.
"I saw Uncle Donald this morning," Carmyle said, his geniality evaporating instantly.
"Yes?" Ginger replied moodily.
Uncle Donald was the managing director of the Family, a man who approximated a human blister.
"He wants you to dine tonight at Bleke's."

Ginger's depression deepened. A dinner with Uncle Donald was a sobering experience regardless of location; at Bleke's, a relic of Old London catering to patrons who hadn't missed an evening in fifty years, it touched bed-rock.
"To-night?"
"Don't be a fool. You know you've got to go."
"If you have another engagement..."
"All right," Ginger sighed. "Seven-thirty sharp."

They parted ways, Carmyle heading east to his chambers at the Temple, Ginger westward toward Hyde Park Corner. Little sympathy existed between them, yet their thoughts converged on the same object. Carmyle threaded through the circus crowds thinking of Sally; Ginger loafed aimlessly, bumping through pedestrians in a sort of coma. Since returning, Ginger had been in bad shape. Hopeless love had reduced him to an absolute onion. Even the financial crash had not bruised him deeply until now. Things irritated him acutely—the moustache of Uncle Donald, used as a zareba against soup.

"If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup-strainer tonight, I'll slosh him with a fork!" Ginger thought, stopping abruptly.
Hard thoughts grew harder. Nothing expands faster than a mood of rebellion. By the time he reached his club, he was a menace to society, specifically the sections comprising his uncles and aunts. The rage persisted as he dressed for the dismal festivities. He scowled, struggling with an obstinate tie. He was warming up.

Then, fate applied the finishing touch. A knock at the door. A waiter entered with a telegram. Readdressed and forwarded from the Hotel Normandie, it was a wireless sent aboard the Olympic.

Ginger sat heavily on the bed. Twenty-five minutes past seven, a taxi pulled up at the dingy door of Bleke's Coffee House in the Strand. The driver, observing his fare's manner and appearance, remarked that he was a determined-looking sort of young bloke.
