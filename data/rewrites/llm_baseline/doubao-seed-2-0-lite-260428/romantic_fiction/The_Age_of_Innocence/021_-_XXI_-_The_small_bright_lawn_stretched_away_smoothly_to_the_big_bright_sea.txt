The small, sun-drenched lawn stretched smooth and bright as polished linen all the way down to the glinting wide blue sea.

The turf was hemmed with a vivid border of scarlet geranium and deep crimson coleus, and chocolate-painted cast iron vases spaced at intervals along the winding gravel path strung loops of soft pink petunia and trailing ivy geranium above the neatly raked stones. Halfway between the cliff edge and the square chocolate-stained wooden house (its verandah tin roof striped yellow and brown to mimic an expensive canvas awning), two large archery targets stood propped against a thicket of shrubbery. Opposite them, across the width of the lawn, a crisp white canvas tent had been pitched, with benches and garden seats clustered around its entrance. Ladies in gossamer summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock coats and tall top hats milled on the grass or lounged on the seats, and every few minutes a slender girl in starched muslin would step out from the tent, bow in hand, and loose her shaft at the targets, the whole party pausing their chatter to watch its flight.

Newland Archer, leaning against the verandah rail of the house, looked down on the scene with a quiet, unnameable disorientation. Shiny blue china flower pots on bright yellow stands flanked the painted steps, each holding a spiky sharp green palm, and below the verandah ran a wide sweep of blue hydrangeas edged with more scarlet geraniums. Behind him, the French windows of the drawing rooms swayed with lace curtains, giving glimpses of glossy parquet floors dotted with plush chintz poufs, tiny velvet armchairs, and low tables heaped with little silver trinkets.

The Newport Archery Club always held its annual August meet at the Beauforts'. Archery had long reigned unrivaled besides croquet as the most genteel outdoor social amusement, and while lawn tennis was gaining in popularity it was still deemed far too rough, far too unladylike for polite gatherings. As an excuse to show off pretty gowns and graceful, artfully poised movements, the bow and arrow still held complete sway.

Archer stared down at the familiar scene wondering how life could still be going on in its old, unbroken rhythm when his own whole understanding of the world had shifted completely. Newport was the place that drove home just how deep that shift ran. Back in New York the previous winter, after he and May had settled into their new greenish-yellow house with the bow window and the Pompeian vestibule, he'd fallen back with relief into the familiar routine of his law practice, and that daily work had felt like a link to the man he'd been before his wedding. There had been the pleasant thrill of picking out a sleek, showy grey stepper for May's brougham (a gift from the Wellands), and the absorbing, long-dreamed-of work of arranging his new library, which he'd built exactly as he'd wanted against all family doubts: dark embossed wallpaper, heavy Eastlake bookcases, and solid, "sincere" armchairs and tables. He'd met his old friend Winsett again at the Century Club, run with his set of fashionable young men at the Knickerbocker, and between work hours, dinner parties, entertaining at home, and the occasional night at the opera or the theater, his life had felt like the real, inevitable, ordinary future he was supposed to be living.

But Newport was a place with no duties, no excuses, only unbroken, unthinking holiday. Archer had tried to persuade May to spend the summer on a remote wild island off the Maine coast called Mount Desert, where a handful of hardy Boston and Philadelphia families camped in rough local cottages, surrounded by wild woods, rocky shores, and reports of a rough, trapper-like freedom far from New York society's strictures. But the Wellands always summered in Newport, in one of their square, boxy cliffside homes, and Archer could give no good reasonable argument why he and May should not join them there. As Mrs. Welland tartly pointed out, May had spent months wearing herself out trying on summer gowns in Paris only to be forbidden to show them off where everyone could admire them, and Archer had no counter for that kind of logic.

May herself could not understand his vague, unspoken reluctance to fall in with what she thought was such a pleasant, sensible summer plan. She reminded him he had loved Newport in his bachelor days, a fact he could not deny, so he had lied and said he was sure he would love it far more now that they were there together. But standing on the Beaufort verandah, looking out over the bright, chattering crowd on the lawn, he knew with a cold little shiver that he would not like it at all.

It was not May's fault, poor dear. Though they had sometimes fallen slightly out of step during their travels, harmony had always settled back over them once they returned to the familiar social rules May had known her whole life. He had always known she would not disappoint him, and he had been right. He had married her, as most young men of his circle did, because he had met this perfectly charming girl just as a string of aimless, unsatisfying sentimental adventures had left him tired and disgusted. She had represented peace, stability, easy comradeship, the steady, comforting pull of an unavoidable, honorable duty. He could not say he had made a mistake in his choice, for she had fulfilled every single thing he had expected of her. It was undoubtedly a source of quiet pride to be the husband of one of the most beautiful, popular young married women in New York, especially one as sweet-tempered and reasonable as she was, and Archer had never been blind to those advantages. He had trained himself to think of that foolish, feverish last-minute madness on the eve of his wedding, the dream that he could ever run off and marry the Countess Olenska, as the last of his discarded boyish follies, and Ellen had long existed in his memory only as the softest, most plaintive of all his discarded ghosts. But all those deliberate erasures had left his mind an empty, echoing place, and the chattering, laughing people on the Beaufort lawn shocked him now, like children playing carelessly on top of a graveyard.

He heard the soft rustle of skirts beside him, and the Marchioness Manson fluttered out of the drawing room window, as festooned and bedizened as ever: her limp Leghorn hat tied to her head with yards of faded gauze, a tiny black velvet parasol on a carved ivory handle balanced absurdly over its wide brim. "My dear Newland! I had no idea you and May had arrived! You only got here yesterday, you say? Ah, business, professional duties... I understand perfectly. So many husbands can only join their wives here for weekends, you know." She cocked her head to one side, languishing at him through half-screwed eyelids. "Marriage is one long sacrifice, as I always used to remind my Ellen--"

Archer's heart gave that strange, sharp jolt it had once before, as if a heavy door had slammed shut between him and the whole rest of the world, but the split second of disorientation must have passed fast, for he soon heard Medora answering a question he must have spoken out loud. "No, I'm not staying here, I'm at the Blenkers', in their delicious little rural solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort was kind enough to send his famous trotters for me this morning so I could catch at least a glimpse of one of Regina's famous garden parties, but I go back to country life this evening. The Blenkers, such dear original people, have rented a primitive old farmhouse at Portsmouth where they gather all the most interesting, representative people around them..." She drooped a little under the shade of her parasol brim, adding with a faint blush: "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding a whole series of Inner Thought meetings there. What a contrast to this giddy worldly pleasure, but I have always lived for contrast! To me the only real death is monotony. I always tell Ellen: beware of monotony, it is the mother of every deadly sin. But my poor child is going through a phase of high-mindedness, of utter abhorrence for society. You know, of course, that she has turned down every invitation to stay in Newport this summer, even her grandmother Mingott's? I could barely persuade her to come to the Blenkers' at all, if you can believe it! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if only she had listened to me when things could still have been different... when the door was still open... But shall we go down and watch this absorbing match? I hear your May is one of the competitors."

Beaufort strolled toward them from the tent across the lawn: tall, heavy, straining a little too tight at the seams of his tailored London frock coat, a rare orchid pinned to his buttonhole. Archer, who had not seen him in two or three months, was struck by how much he had changed. Under the harsh summer sun, his usually ruddy complexion looked heavy and bloated, and if he did not walk so straight and square-shouldered he would have looked like an overfed, overdressed old man.

There were endless rumors swirling around Beaufort. That spring he had gone on a long cruise to the West Indies on his brand new steam yacht, and word had spread that a lady who looked exactly like Miss Fanny Ring had been seen in his company at every port they stopped at. The Clyde-built yacht, fitted with tiled bathrooms and other unheard-of luxuries, was said to have cost him half a million dollars, and the enormous pearl necklace he had given his wife on his return was the very picture of the lavish expiatory gifts men gave their wives to make up for very public affairs. Beaufort's fortune was substantial enough to absorb that kind of expense, but the uneasy whispers persisted, not just on Fifth Avenue but all through Wall Street. Some said he had made terrible bad speculations in railway stock, others that he was being bled dry by one of the most greedy mistresses in the city, and to every new rumor of looming insolvency Beaufort responded with some new, even more extravagant display: a whole new row of orchid houses, a new string of racehorses, a new Meissonnier or Cabanel painting for his gallery.

He walked up to the Marchioness and Newland with his usual half-sneering smile. "Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters treat you well? Forty minutes flat, eh? Not bad at all, considering we had to go slow to spare your nerves." He shook Archer's hand, then stepped to Medora's other side, and muttered a few quiet words that Archer could not catch. Medora replied with one of her odd, jerky foreign gestures, a "Que voulez-vous?" that deepened Beaufort's frown, but he put on a perfectly convincing congratulatory smile as he turned to Archer. "You know May is going to carry off the first prize, no question."

"Ah, then the prize will stay right in the family," Medora trilled, just as they reached the tent and Regina Beaufort stepped out to meet them, wreathed in girlish clouds of mauve muslin and floating veils.

May Welland was just stepping out of the tent then. In her white dress, with a pale green ribbon around her waist and a wreath of ivy in her hat, she had the same cool, Diana-like aloofness she had worn the night she walked into the Beaufort ballroom for their engagement party. Not a single thought or heavy feeling seemed to have left a mark on her since that night, and though her husband knew she was capable of both, he marveled again at how easily every experience seemed to slide right off her.

She held her bow and arrow in her hand, stepped onto the chalk mark traced on the turf, lifted the bow to her shoulder, and took aim. The attitude was so full of classic grace that a soft murmur of appreciation rippled through the onlookers, and Archer felt that familiar warm glow of pride and proprietorship that so often tricked him into momentary happiness. Her rivals -- Mrs. Reggie Chivers, the Merry girls, a whole cluster of rosy Thorleys, Dagonets, and Mingotts -- stood behind her in a soft, lovely anxious group, brown heads and golden heads bent over their score cards, pale muslin and flower-wreathed hats blending into a tender rainbow. All of them were young and pretty, bathed in the soft glow of summer, but not one had that nymph-like easy grace his wife had when she tensed her muscles, a faint happy frown on her face, and poured all her focus into hitting her mark.

"Gad," Archer heard Lawrence Lefferts say, "not one of the lot holds the bow half as well as she does." Beaufort laughed and retorted, "Yes, but that's the only kind of target she'll ever hit."

Archer felt a burst of irrational anger. In any other circumstance, a coarse man calling his wife too nice, too unapproachable, would have felt like a compliment, proof of her purity. But Beaufort's words sent a faint shiver through his chest. What if that "niceness" carried to that perfect, unassailable degree was nothing but a negation, a thick curtain dropped tight in front of an emptiness he had never once managed to see past? He watched May walk back, flushed and calm after her final perfect bullseye, and for a second he felt like he had never once lifted that curtain to see what was behind it.

She accepted the congratulations from her rivals and the rest of the party with the simple grace that was her greatest charm. No one could ever be jealous of her triumphs, because she gave off the quiet, unmistakeable feeling that she would have been exactly as serene and happy if she had missed every shot. But when her eyes met her husband's, her face lit up with the clear, unmistakeable pleasure she took in his approval.

Mrs. Welland's wicker pony carriage was waiting for them, and they drove off among the dispersing line of other carriages, May holding the reins and Archer sitting at her side. The late afternoon sunlight still gilded the bright lawns and shrubbery, and up and down Bellevue Avenue rolled a steady double line of victorias, dog-carts, landaus and vis-à-vis, carrying well-dressed ladies and gentlemen away from the Beaufort garden party, or home from their daily turn along the Ocean Drive.

"Shall we go visit Granny?" May suggested suddenly. "I want to tell her myself that I won the prize. There's plenty of time before dinner."

Archer agreed, and May turned the ponies down Narragansett Avenue, crossed Spring Street, and drove out toward the rocky, moorland stretch beyond the fashionable district. It was there, in this unfashionable, cheap stretch of land overlooking the bay, that Catherine the Great Mingott, who had always cared nothing for precedent and hated wasting money, had built her gaudy, many-peaked, cross-beamed cottage-orné when she was young. In a thicket of stunted oaks, her wide verandahs jutted out over the bay dotted with small islands. A winding drive led up between cast iron stags and blue glass balls half buried in mounds of geraniums, to a front door of highly polished walnut under a striped verandah roof. Behind it ran a narrow hall with a black and yellow star-patterned parquet floor, opening onto four small square rooms lined with heavy flock wallpaper, their ceilings covered in every god and goddess of Olympus that an Italian house painter could possibly dream up. One of those rooms had been turned into her bedroom when her weight grew too great to easily climb stairs, and she spent all her days in the adjoining sitting room, enthroned in a huge armchair between the open door and open window, forever waving a palm-leaf fan that the great swell of her bosom held so far from the rest of her that the air it moved only ever stirred the fringe of the anti-macassars on her chair arms.

Since she had been the one to speed up his marriage, old Catherine had treated Archer with the same cordial warmth one feels for someone you helped to get exactly what they wanted. She was fully convinced that irrepressible passion was the reason for his hasty wedding, and as someone who adored impulsiveness (as long as it did not lead to anyone wasting money unnecessarily) she always greeted him with a genial, conspiratorial twinkle and little playful allusions that May, luckily, seemed completely impervious to.

She examined the diamond-tipped arrow that had been pinned to May's dress as her prize, remarking that in her day a simple filigree brooch would have been thought more than enough, but there was no denying that Beaufort did things extremely handsomely. "Quite a proper heirloom, my dear," the old lady chuckled, pinching May's soft white arm. "You have to leave it in fee to your eldest girl." The color flooded May's face so fast it was like a flag being raised. "Well well, what did I say to make you blush so red? No daughters, only boys, eh? Good gracious, look at her blushing over her blushes! What, I can't even say that either? Mercy me, when my children beg me to paint all those gods and goddesses off the ceiling I always tell them I'm far too grateful to have people around me that nothing can shock!"

Archer burst out laughing, and May echoed him, crimson all the way to the corners of her eyes.

"Now tell me all about the party, you two, because I will never get a straight word of it out of that silly Medora," the old ancestress continued. When May exclaimed, "Cousin Medora? But I thought she was going back to Portsmouth?" Mrs. Mingott answered placidly: "Oh, she will, she will, but she has to stop here first to pick up Ellen. Ah -- you didn't know Ellen was coming to spend the day with me? Such nonsense, her refusing to come stay for the whole summer, but I gave up arguing with young people about fifty years ago. Ellen -- Ellen!" she cried in her shrill old voice, trying to lean far enough forward to catch a glimpse of the lawn down past the verandah.

There was no answer, and Mrs. Mingott rapped impatiently with her stick on the shiny parquet floor. A mulatto maid in a bright turban answered the summons, and told her mistress she had seen "Miss Ellen" walking down the path to the shore. Mrs. Mingott turned to Archer. "Run down and fetch her, like a good grandson; this pretty girl here will describe the whole party to me," she said, and Archer stood up like he was moving in a dream.
