The air in Grace Church was thick with the dust of spring, a dry, lively suspension that coated the silence. The old ladies of both families had pulled forth their faded sables and yellowing ermines, creating a wall of texture that nearly suffocated the faint, green scent of the lilies lining the altar. Camphor from the front pews hung heavy, a chemical barrier against the season.

Newland Archer stood on the chancel step, flanked by his best man, waiting for a signal. The sexton's cue meant the brougham was visible, but the lobby remained a zone of suspended animation where bridesmaids hovered like a cluster of forced-bloom flowers. In this gap, the protocol demanded the groom stand exposed, a solitary specimen for the assembly to inspect. Archer endured this performance with the same resigned fatigue he applied to every other ritual of a nineteenth-century marriage—a rite that felt less like a union and more like a fossilized event from the dawn of history. To him, the path ahead offered no relief; it was simply a series of obligations, equally easy or equally painful depending on the observer's mood. He had obeyed his best man's frantic instructions with the pious deference of a man repeating a script written by ancestors.

He checked his list mentally. The bouquets of lilac and lily-of-the-valley had arrived. The gold and sapphire links for the ushers, the cat's-eye pin for the best man—all accounted for. He had spent half the night agonizing over the phrasing of his thank-you notes to male friends and ex-lovers. The fees for the clergy were safe in his best man's pocket. His luggage waited at Mrs. Manson Mingott's, along with the traveling clothes he would soon discard. Even the railway compartment was secured, a private box for the journey to an undisclosed destination, adhering strictly to the ancient taboo of concealing the location of the bridal night.

"Got the ring?" whispered Van der Luyden, the best man, his voice trembling under the weight of his novice role.

Archer repeated the motion he had witnessed a thousand times: the ungloved right hand diving into the breast pocket of his dark grey waistcoat. He confirmed the presence of the small gold circle, engraved inside with *Newland to May*, before returning to his posture. Tall hat held firmly, pearl-grey gloves with black stitching gripped in the left hand, he stared at the church door.

Above, Handel's March swelled through the imitation stone vaulting, a pompous tide carrying the echoes of countless previous ceremonies. He recognized the faces in the boxes—the same Mrs. Selfridge Merry with her towering ostrich plumes, the same Mrs. Beaufort with her diamonds and her practiced smile. He wondered if the theater of judgment continued beyond death, if suitable seats awaited them in the next act.

His gaze drifted to the first rows. The women wore sharp expressions of curated excitement; the men looked sullen, burdened by the necessity of maintaining their frock coats until lunch. He pictured Reggie Chivers complaining about the breakfast venue, imagining Sillerton Jackson asserting the new English custom of small tables.

For a fleeting moment, his eyes rested on the left-hand pew. His mother sat there, weeping softly beneath her Chantilly veil, clutching her grandmother's ermine muff. Beside her, his sister Janey strained her neck, her vision limited to the few front pews, surrounded mostly by dowdy Newlands and Dagonets.

Across the aisle, separated by the white ribbon marking the family section, Beaufort stood tall and flushed, scanning the congregation with an arrogant intensity. Next to him, his wife shimmered in silvery chinchilla and violets. On the far side, Lawrence Lefferts's sleek head seemed to guard the invisible deity of "Good Form." Archer wondered how many flaws Lefferts's keen eyes would find in the ritual, then realized with a jolt that he, too, had once believed such scrutiny mattered. The arguments over whether wedding gifts should be displayed had darkened the morning, reducing grown adults to agitated children. It was inconceivable now that anyone should care, yet he remembered a time when the manners of his tribe seemed to hold the fate of the world.

*"Real people,"* he thought, *"were living somewhere else, doing real things..."*

"There they come!" the best man breathed, his voice cracking.

Archer knew better. The door's cautious opening was merely Mr. Brown, the stable keeper acting as sexton, conducting a preliminary survey. The door closed softly. After a pause, it swung open again, and a murmur rippled through the nave: "The family!"

Mrs. Welland entered first, supported by her eldest son. Her large pink face was solemn, her plum-colored satin and blue ostrich plumes meeting with general approval. Before she settled into the pew opposite Mrs. Archer's, the crowd leaned forward, anticipating the arrival of Mrs. Manson Mingott. Rumors had circulated that the matriarch, despite her physical limitations, intended to attend. Bets had been placed at the clubs regarding her ability to navigate the nave. She had even sent a carpenter to dismantle the front pew panel, though the measurements had proven discouraging. The family had briefly considered wheeling her up in her massive Bath chair, a notion so grotesque it caused collective recoil. The idea of exposing her person to the mob of dressmakers and reporters outside the awning was unbearable. Old Catherine had relented, accepting the compromise that the wedding breakfast would be held under her roof, a concession purchased only by the promise of hospitality.

Despite the reports, a sporting minority still hoped to see her. The temperature dropped visibly when it became clear she had been replaced by her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott. She possessed the high color and glassy stare typical of her age, her black Chantilly over lilac satin contrasting sharply with her mother-in-law's plum. But the impression shifted drastically with the arrival of the gaunt, mincing figure following Mr. Mingott, dressed in a chaotic disarray of stripes, fringes, and floating scarves. As this apparition glided into view, Archer's heart seized.

He had assumed the Marchioness Manson was still in Washington, accompanying her niece, Madame Olenska. Their departure had been attributed to Olenska's desire to escape the persuasive eloquence of Dr. Agathon Carver, who had nearly recruited her for the "Valley of Love." No one expected them to return. For a moment, Archer stood frozen, staring at Medora's fantastic silhouette, straining to see who followed. But the procession ended; the lesser family members took their seats, and the eight ushers, gathering like birds preparing for migration, slipped through the side doors.

"Newland—I say: *she's here!*" the best man whispered urgently.

Archer started awake.

Time seemed to have dilated. The white and rosy procession was halfway up the nave. The Bishop, the Rector, and two white-winged assistants hovered near the flower-banked altar. The first chords of the Spohr symphony strewed their floral notes before the approaching bride.

Archer blinked, unsure if he had truly closed his eyes. His heart resumed its labor. The music, the scent of lilies, the drifting cloud of tulle and orange blossoms, the sudden convulsion of joy on Mrs. Archer's face, the low murmur of the Rector, the synchronized movements of the pink bridesmaids and black ushers—it was all a confusing mixture of the familiar and the utterly meaningless in his new capacity as husband.

"My God," he thought, "*do* I have the ring?" He performed the convulsive check once more.

Then, instantly, May was beside him. Radiance streamed from her, warming his numbness. He straightened and smiled into her eyes.

"Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here," the Rector began.

The ring was on her finger. The Bishop's blessing had been spoken. The bridesmaids poised to resume their places. The organ showed signs of swelling into the Mendelssohn March, the essential soundtrack for any New York debut.

"Your arm—I say, give her your arm!" Van der Luyden hissed nervously.

Once again, Archer felt the sensation of drifting far from shore. What had propelled him there? Perhaps it was the glimpse, among the anonymous spectators in the transept, of a dark coil of hair under a hat. A moment later, the hat lifted, revealing a face with a long nose, laughably unlike the ideal he had constructed. Was he suffering from hallucinations?

Now, he and his wife paced slowly down the nave, carried forward by the light Mendelssohn ripples. The spring day beckoned through the widely opened doors. At the far end of the canvas tunnel, Mrs. Welland's chestnuts, adorned with white favors, pranced and showed off.

A footman, wearing an even larger white favor, wrapped May's white cloak around her. Archer stepped into the brougham at her side. She turned to him with a triumphant smile, their hands clasping beneath her veil.

"Darling!" Archer said. Suddenly, a black abyss yawned before him, and he felt himself sinking into it, deeper and deeper, while his voice continued smoothly and cheerfully. "Yes, of course I thought I'd lost the ring; no wedding would be complete if the poor devil of a bridegroom didn't go through that. But you *did* keep me waiting, you know! I had time to think of every horror that might possibly happen."
