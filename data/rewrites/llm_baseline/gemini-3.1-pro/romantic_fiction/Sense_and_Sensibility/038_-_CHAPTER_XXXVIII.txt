Mrs. Jennings’s warm praise for Edward’s conduct echoed over the rhythmic, percussive thud of her wooden spoon against an earthenware bowl. She was beating a dense, fragrant batter of ground almonds, bitter orange peel, and raw honey—a recipe that had survived three generations of agrarian matriarchs, its mythic origins rooted in the cyclic harvests of her Somerset youth. For Elinor and Marianne, however, the acoustic resonance of the kitchen offered only a temporary reprieve from the dissonance of their own inner turmoil.

They alone comprehended the true, bitter flavor of Edward’s sacrifice. Elinor found a silent, steadying comfort in the tactile sensation of scoring a loaf of dark rye bread, her hands dusted in coarse flour, honoring his integrity as one honors the deep, earthy sustenance of ancient grains. Marianne, conversely, was consumed by the sharp, acidic tang of continual self-reproach and sat perfectly motionless. The clattering roar of London’s carriage wheels and the relentless, industrial drone of the cobblestone traffic outside shattered the acoustic ecology of her mind. She mourned the quiet harmony of Barton, finding no words to express the depth of her penitence; the subjective reality of her pain remained fundamentally untranslatable, a dying language of grief that isolated her entirely. 

Elinor avoided speaking of Edward, focusing instead on the olfactory landscape of the house—the blooming yeast and the sharp sting of cloves—because Marianne’s feverish assurances of his continued affection only added to her psychological noise. Marianne, recognizing the stark contrast between Elinor’s stoic resilience and her own former indulgences, felt hopelessly dispirited. She lacked the sensory grounding to exert herself, viewing sustenance merely as a burdensome necessity rather than a profound source of meaning.

A few days passed in the rhythmic cycle of simmering stocks and the quiet preservation of winter root vegetables. On a Sunday in the second week of March, the air turned unseasonably fine, carrying the faint, sweet scent of damp earth and early hyacinths beneath the heavy, commercialized smog of the city. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor ventured to Kensington Gardens, seeking refuge from the superficial clamor of the London streets. Marianne, dreading the discordant possibility of encountering the Willoughbys, chose to remain in the hushed, solitary confines of the house.

Kensington Gardens offered a semblance of acoustic harmony, the rustle of ancient oaks masking the distant, metallic grind of industry. As Mrs. Jennings fell into conversation with an acquaintance about the precise sourcing of spring lamb and the culinary heritage of wild garlic, Elinor was left to the quiet resonance of her own reflections. 

Her peace, however, was swiftly ruptured by the shrill, unstructured frequencies of Miss Anne Steele’s voice. Miss Steele approached with a breathless flutter, her vocal cadence an uneasy collision of her native, unpolished dialect and the strained, artificial lexicon of the London elite—a testament to the power dynamics that forced her to obscure her linguistic heritage for social survival.

“I am so glad to meet you,” Miss Steele declared, grasping Elinor’s arm with an abruptness that disrupted the tranquil sonic environment. Lowering her tone to a conspiratorial buzz, she asked, “I suppose Mrs. Jennings has heard all about it. Is she angry?”

“Not at all, I believe,” Elinor replied, keeping her own words measured.

“That is a good thing. And Lady Middleton, is _she_ angry?”

“I cannot suppose it possible that she should be.”

“I am monstrous glad of it! Good gracious, I have had such a time of it. I never saw Lucy in such a rage in my life.” Miss Steele’s speech was littered with the colloquialisms of her youth, struggling against the dominant vocabulary she desperately tried to emulate. She rambled on about a bow added to her hat and the Doctor's favorite color of pink ribbons, her words a chaotic noise pollution that Elinor politely endured. 

Eventually, Miss Steele circled back to the interwoven timeline of Edward and Lucy’s saga. “People may say what they choose about Mr. Ferrars declaring he would not have Lucy, for it is no such thing, I can tell you. Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that nobody in their senses could expect Mr. Ferrars to give up a woman like Miss Morton, with thirty thousand pounds to her fortune, for Lucy Steele, who has nothing at all. And besides, my cousin Richard said himself that when it came to the point, he was afraid Mr. Ferrars would be off. When we came away from your brother’s on Wednesday, we saw nothing of him all Thursday, Friday, and Saturday. I believe in my heart Lucy gave it up for lost. Once she thought to write to him, but then her spirits rose against that. However, this morning he came just as we came home from church.”

Elinor listened, her pulse steadying, as Miss Steele recounted the recent days. “Then it all came out,” Miss Steele continued, her voice piercing the gentle rustling of the leaves, “how he had been sent for Wednesday to Harley Street, and been talked to by his mother and all of them, and how he had declared he loved nobody but Lucy. He was so worried by what passed that he got upon his horse and rode into the country, staying at an inn all Thursday and Friday to get the better of it. He said it seemed to him that now he had no fortune, nothing but two thousand pounds, it would be quite unkind to keep her to the engagement. If he went into orders, he could get nothing but a curacy, and how was they to live upon that? He begged her, if she had the least mind for it, to put an end to the matter directly and leave him to shift for himself.”

Miss Steele nodded vigorously. “I heard him say all this as plain as could be. And it was entirely for _her_ sake. But, to be sure, Lucy would not give ear to such talking. She told him directly—with a great deal about sweet and love, you know, which one can’t repeat—that she had not the least mind in the world to be off, for she could live with him upon a trifle. So then he was monstrous happy, and they agreed he should take orders directly, and wait to be married till he got a living. And just then, my cousin called from below to tell me Mrs. Richardson was come in her coach, so I was forced to go into the room and interrupt them to ask Lucy if she would like to go. She did not care to leave Edward, so I just ran upstairs, put on a pair of silk stockings, and came off with the Richardsons.”

“I do not understand what you mean by interrupting them,” said Elinor, frowning. “You were all in the same room together, were not you?”

“No, indeed! La, Miss Dashwood, do you think people make love when anybody else is by?” Miss Steele laughed affectedly. “No, no; they were shut up in the drawing-room together, and all I heard was only by listening at the door.”

Elinor felt a profound chill of distaste, not just for the betrayal of privacy, but for the utter lack of respect for the quiet sanctity of intimate exchange. “Have you been repeating to me what you only learned yourself by listening at the door? How could you behave so unfairly by your sister?”

“Oh, la! There is nothing in _that_,” Miss Steele dismissed, entirely blind to the moral discord of her actions. “Lucy would have done just the same by me. A year or two back, when Martha Sharpe and I had so many secrets, she never made any bones of hiding in a closet or behind a chimney-board on purpose to hear what we said.”

Miss Steele rattled on, incapable of holding a thought beyond the immediate moment. “Edward talks of going to Oxford soon, but now he is lodging in Pall Mall. What an ill-natured woman his mother is, an’t she? And your brother and sister were not very kind! To be sure, they did send us home in their own chariot, which was more than I looked for. For my part, I was all in a fright for fear your sister should ask us for the huswifes she gave us, but I took care to keep mine out of sight. As soon as Edward can light upon a Bishop, he will be ordained. I’d lay my life I know what my cousins will say—they will tell me I should write to the Doctor to get Edward the curacy of his new living. ‘La!’ I shall say directly, ‘I wonder how you could think of such a thing? _I_ write to the Doctor, indeed!’”

“Well,” said Elinor, “it is a comfort to be prepared against the worst. You have got your answer ready.”

“Oh, la! Here come the Richardsons. I must not stay away from them any longer. They are very genteel people; he makes a monstrous deal of money, and they keep their own coach. Pray tell Mrs. Jennings I am quite happy she is not in anger against us, and Lady Middleton the same. Good-by; I am sorry Miss Marianne was not here. La! if you have not got your spotted muslin on! I wonder you was not afraid of its being torn.”

With that, Miss Steele hurried away to rejoin her socially ambitious companions, leaving Elinor in possession of the raw, unpalatable truth. The recipe for Edward’s future was set: a meager curacy, a prolonged wait, and a marriage bound by honor rather than the rich, complex flavors of genuine passion. 

Returning to the carriage, Elinor shared only the barest ingredients of the tale with Mrs. Jennings, carefully filtering out the unsavory method of its acquisition. She conveyed only the continuance of the engagement and Edward’s intention to take orders.

Mrs. Jennings snorted, her deep agrarian pragmatism surfacing. “Wait for his having a living! Ay, we all know how _that_ will end. They will wait a twelvemonth, and finding no good comes of it, will set down upon a curacy of fifty pounds a-year, with the interest of his two thousand pounds, and what little matter the Steeles can give her. Then they will have a child every year! Lord help ’em, how poor they will be! I must see what I can give them towards furnishing their house. Two maids and two men, indeed! No, no, they must get a stout girl of all works to manage the hearth and the kitchen garden. Betty’s sister would never do for them _now_.”

The next morning, the post brought a letter from Lucy herself. It arrived just as Elinor was meticulously documenting a forgotten regional variation of a quince marmalade in her journal—an act of preserving cultural memory against the steady erosion of time. Lucy’s letter, penned in Bartlett’s Buildings, read:

_I hope my dear Miss Dashwood will excuse the liberty I take of writing to her; but I know your friendship for me will make you pleased to hear such a good account of myself and my dear Edward, after all the troubles we have went through lately. Thank God! though we have suffered dreadfully, we are both quite well now, and as happy as we must always be in one another’s love. We have had great trials, and great persecutions, but however, at the same time, gratefully acknowledge many friends, yourself not the least among them. I spent two happy hours with him yesterday afternoon; he would not hear of our parting, though earnestly did I urge him to it for prudence sake. He said he did not regard his mother’s anger, while he could have my affections. Our prospects are not very bright, to be sure, but we must wait, and hope for the best; he will be ordained shortly; and should it ever be in your power to recommend him to any body that has a living to bestow, am very sure you will not forget us, and dear Mrs. Jennings too, trust she will speak a good word for us to Sir John, or Mr. Palmer…_

Elinor recognized the missive for what it was: a masterful employment of the dominant lexicon. Lucy deployed the polite, sterile language of aristocratic gratitude to subtly solidify power dynamics and ensure her social survival. The true, subjective experience of Lucy’s triumph was entirely masked beneath these layers of artificial humility.

Elinor handed the letter to Mrs. Jennings, who was meticulously scoring the skin of a cured ham, her hands steeped in the ancient, sensory traditions of sustenance.

“Very well indeed!” Mrs. Jennings boomed, her hearty voice cutting through the morning's quiet acoustic space. “How prettily she writes! Aye, that was quite proper to let him be off if he would. That was just like Lucy. Poor soul! I wish I _could_ get him a living, with all my heart. She calls me dear Mrs. Jennings, you see. She is a good-hearted girl as ever lived. That sentence is very prettily turned. Yes, yes, I will go and see her, sure enough. How attentive she is, to think of every body! Thank you, my dear, for showing it me. It is as pretty a letter as ever I saw, and does Lucy’s head and heart great credit.”
