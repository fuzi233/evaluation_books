After departing Vienna, and long before you reach Budapest, the Danube slips into a realm of absolute solitude and desolation. Here, its waters abandon any semblance of a main channel, spreading outward in a chaotic fan that transforms the countryside into a swamp stretching for miles. Vast seas of low willow-bushes blanket the ground, and on the great maps, this forsaken expanse is rendered in a fluffy, fading blue, marked only by the large, straggling word *Sümpfe*—marshes.

In times of high flood, this great acreage of sand and shingle beds is nearly submerged, but in the ordinary season, the bushes bow and rustle in the free winds, displaying their silver leaves to the sun in an ever-shifting plain of bewildering beauty. These willows never achieve the dignity of trees; they possess no rigid trunks. They remain humble bushes with rounded tops and soft outlines, swaying on slender stems that tremble at the slightest breeze. Supple as grasses and constantly shifting, they create the illusion that the entire plain is alive and moving. The wind sends waves rising and falling over the whole surface—not waves of water, but waves of leaves, green swells like the sea, until the branches turn and lift, revealing undersides silvery-white against the sun.

Joyful in slipping beyond the control of the stern banks, the Danube wanders at will among an intricate network of channels intersecting the islands with broad avenues where waters pour with a shouting sound. It creates whirlpools, eddies, and foaming rapids; it tears at the sandy banks, carries away masses of shore and willow-clumps, and forms new islands innumerable, which shift daily in size and shape, possessing at best an impermanent life, for the floods eventually obliterate them completely.

Properly speaking, this fascinating phase of the river's life begins shortly after leaving Pressburg. We, in our Canadian canoe with a gypsy tent and frying-pan aboard, reached it on the crest of a rising flood about mid-July. That same morning, when the sky was reddening before dawn, we had slipped swiftly through the sleeping city of Vienna, leaving it merely a patch of smoke against the blue hills of the Wienerwald an hour or two later. We had breakfasted below Fischeramend under a grove of birch trees roaring in the wind, then swept on the tearing current past Orth, Hainburg, Petronell (the ancient Roman Carnuntum of Marcus Aurelius), and so under the frowning heights of Thelsen on a spur of the Carpathians, where the March steals in quietly from the left and the frontier between Austria and Hungary is crossed.

Racing along at twelve kilometers an hour soon brought us well into Hungary. The muddy waters—a sure sign of flood—drove us aground on many a shingle-bed and twisted us like a cork in sudden belching whirlpools before the towers of Pressburg showed against the sky. Then the canoe, leaping like a spirited horse, flew at top speed under the grey walls, navigated the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brucke ferry safely, turned sharply to the left, and plunged on yellow foam into the wilderness of islands, sandbanks, and swamp-land beyond—the Land of the Willows.

The change came suddenly, as when a series of biograph slides snaps down on a street and shifts without warning into the scenery of a lake and forest. We entered the land of desolation on wings, and in less than half an hour there was neither boat nor fishing-hut nor red roof, nor any sign of human habitation within sight. The sense of remoteness from the world of mankind, the utter isolation, the fascination of this singular world of willows, winds, and waters, instantly laid its spell upon us both. We allowed ourselves to joke that we ought by rights to have held some special passport to admit us, and that we had audaciously come without asking leave into a separate little kingdom of wonder and magic—a kingdom reserved for those who had a right to it, with unwritten warnings to trespassers scattered everywhere for those with the imagination to discover them.

Though it was still early afternoon, the ceaseless buffetings of a tempestuous wind made us weary. We began casting about for a suitable camping-ground. But the bewildering character of the islands made landing difficult; the swirling flood carried us ashore and then swept us out again. The willow branches tore at our hands as we seized them to stop the canoe, and we pulled many yards of sandy bank into the water before the wind, with a great sideways blow, shot us into a backwater where we managed to beach the bows in a cloud of spray. Then we lay panting and laughing on the hot yellow sand, sheltered from the wind, under a scorching sun and a cloudless blue sky, surrounded by an immense army of dancing, shouting willow bushes closing in from all sides, shining with spray and clapping their thousand little hands as if to applaud our success.

"What a river!" I said to my companion, thinking of all the way we had traveled from the source in the Black Forest, and how he had often been obliged to wade and push in the upper shallows at the beginning of June.

"Won't stand much nonsense now, will it?" he replied, pulling the canoe a little farther into safety up the sand, and then composing himself for a nap.

I lay by his side, happy and peaceful in the bath of the elements—water, wind, sand, and the great fire of the sun—thinking of the long journey behind us and the great stretch before us to the Black Sea, and how fortunate I was to have such a delightful and charming traveling companion as my friend, the Swede.

We had made many similar journeys together, but the Danube, more than any other river I knew, impressed us from the very beginning with its *aliveness*. From its tiny bubbling entry into the world among the pinewood gardens of Donaueschingen until this moment when it began to play the great river-game of losing itself among deserted swamps, unobserved and unrestrained, it had seemed to us like following the growth of some living creature. Sleepy at first, but later developing violent desires as it became conscious of its deep soul, it rolled, like some huge fluid being, through all the countries we had passed, holding our little craft on its mighty shoulders, playing roughly with us sometimes, yet always friendly and well-meaning, till at length we had come inevitably to regard it as a Great Personage.

How, indeed, could it be otherwise, since it told us so much of its secret life? At night we heard it singing to the moon as we lay in our tent, uttering that odd sibilant note peculiar to itself, said to be caused by the rapid tearing of pebbles along its bed. We knew the voice of its gurgling whirlpools, suddenly bubbling up on surfaces previously calm; the roar of its shallows and swift rapids; its constant steady thundering below all mere surface sounds; and that ceaseless tearing of its icy waters at the banks. How it stood up and shouted when the rains fell flat upon its face! And how its laughter roared out when the wind blew upstream and tried to stop its growing speed! We knew all its sounds and voices, its tumblings and foamings, its unnecessary splashing against bridges, that self-conscious chatter when there were hills to look on, the affected dignity of its speech when it passed through little towns far too important to laugh, and all those faint, sweet whisperings when the sun caught it fairly in some slow curve.

It was full of tricks, too, in its early life before the great world knew it. There were places in the upper reaches among the Swabian forests where it elected to disappear through holes in the ground, to appear again on the other side of porous limestone hills and start a new river with another name; leaving so little water in its own bed that we had to climb out and wade and push the canoe through miles of shallows.

A chief pleasure, in those early days of its irresponsible youth, was to lie low, like Brer Fox, just before the little turbulent tributaries came to join it from the Alps, and to refuse to acknowledge them when they arrived, running for miles side by side with the dividing line well marked, the levels different, the Danube utterly declining to recognize the newcomers. Below Passau, however, it gave up this particular trick, for there the Inn comes in with a thundering power impossible to ignore, pushing and incommoding the parent river so severely that there is hardly room for them in the long twisting gorge that follows, forcing the Danube to hurry itself with great waves and much dashing to and fro to get through in time. During that fight, our canoe slipped down from its shoulder to its breast, having the time of its life among the struggling waves. But the Inn taught the old river a lesson, and after Passau it no longer pretended to ignore new arrivals.

This was many days back, of course, and since then we had come to know other aspects of the great creature. Across the Bavarian wheat plain of Straubing, she wandered so slowly under the blazing June sun that we could well imagine only the surface inches were water, while below there moved, concealed as by a silken mantle, a whole army of Undines, passing silently and unseen down to the sea, very leisurely too, lest they be discovered.

Much, too, we forgave her because of her friendliness to the birds and animals that haunted the shores. Cormorants lined the banks in lonely places in rows like short black palings; grey crows crowded the shingle

Suddenly, I jolted awake, my eyes straining through the flap of my tent onto the desolate expanse outside. My wristwatch, pinned haphazardly against the canvas, glowed under the pale light of the full moon. It was well past midnight—the hour where sleep surrenders to waking terror—and yet, I realized with a sinking stomach, I had barely closed my eyes for two hours. Beside me, the Swede lay in a fitful slumber, his breathing heavy and ragged like the gale that howled relentlessly around us. But the wind alone could not explain the chill crawling up my spine, nor the frantic hammering of my own heart. Something was wrong here, in this immediate circle of darkness, a disturbance that vibrated in the air like a plucked string.

I threw off the covers and scrambled to the edge of the tent, peering out into the chaos. The trees were thrashing, their branches whipping back and forth as the gusts battered them with brutal force. Yet, miraculously, our small patch of green canvas remained anchored, nestled safely in the hollow, unscathed by the fury above. The wind roared over it, offering no resistance strong enough to tear it away. Still, the unease gnawed at me, a cold knot in my gut that refused to loosen. I crawled out of the tent, moving with deliberate silence so as not to rouse my sleeping companion, driven by a strange, electric excitement that made my skin prickle.

Halfway out, dropping to my knees on the coarse sand, I froze. My gaze caught the tops of the bushes directly opposite, their leafy tracery dancing wildly against the black sky. I sat back on my heels, staring, unable to breathe. It was impossible, yet there, standing amidst the willows and slightly elevated above them, were shapes. As the branches swayed, these forms seemed to coalesce, arranging themselves into grotesque, shifting silhouettes that twisted rapidly beneath the lunar glare. They were close, perhaps fifty feet away, looming out of the gloom.

My first, reflexive urge was to wake the Swede, to share this vision, but a sudden, paralyzing hesitation stopped me. Some primal voice whispered that I would not welcome confirmation from anyone else—that the truth needed to remain mine alone. So, I crouched there, staring in a daze, my eyes stinging with the strain of seeing what I dared not fully believe. *Not a dream,* I told myself, the words echoing in the hollow space of my mind. *I am wide awake.*

Then, slowly, the impossible became clear. These were not mere shadows cast by the swaying boughs. Emerging from behind the brush, towering immense and colossal, were figures of a bronze, metallic hue. They moved with a jerky, fluid independence, entirely separate from the chaotic dance of the trees. As I watched, mesmerized, I noted with growing horror that they were far larger than any man, and their proportions defied nature. They were not human. They shifted and flowed, rising in a continuous, spiraling stream from the earth straight up into the starless sky, vanishing completely the moment they pierced the upper darkness. They were interlaced, a single, writhing column of flesh and spirit, their limbs and massive bodies melting into one another, twisting in a serpentine helix that mirrored the convulsions of the wind-lashed forest. They were naked, fluid entities pouring upward, weaving *within* the leaves themselves, ascending in a living pillar toward the heavens. I could never see their faces; they were smooth, featureless masks of terror.

They poured upward unceasingly, bending in great, sweeping curves, their skin a dull, sickly bronze. I stared, forcing every ounce of focus from my weary eyes, trying to find a trick, a shadow play. For a long time, I convinced myself they must dissolve instantly into the movement of the branches, proving nothing but an optical delusion. I searched desperately for evidence of reality, yet deep down, I knew the scale had shifted. The longer I looked, the more certain I became: these figures were alive, breathing, and undeniably present, even if they existed outside the laws of biology or physics.

Far from trembling, I was seized by a profound, terrifying awe, a wonder I had never known before. It felt as though I was witnessing the elemental spirits of this cursed, primordial land given form. Our intrusion had stirred the ancient slumber of the place into violent activity. We were the cause of this awakening, and my mind raced, filling to the brim with myths and legends of gods and demons worshipped by men in ages past. But before I could construct a rational explanation, an impulsion drove me forward. I crept further out on the sand, forcing myself to stand upright. The ground beneath my bare feet still hummed with a residual warmth; the wind lashed my hair and stung my face; and the roar of the river crashed against my ears with a sudden, deafening violence. These sensory inputs screamed that I was sane, that my senses were functioning correctly. And yet, there they stood—or rather, they floated—in a great spiral of terrible grace and strength, silent and majestic, overwhelming me with a deep, primal emotion of worship. I felt compelled to kneel, to bow absolutely before these beings.

Perhaps I would have fallen to my knees in reverence in another minute, had not a sudden, crushing gust of wind swept against me, knocking me sideways and nearly sending me sprawling. It shook the trance violently from me, if only for a second. It offered me a new angle, a fractured perspective. The figures still hovered there, ascending from the heart of the night, but my reason, stubborn and cruel, began to claw its way back to the surface. *It must be subjective,* I argued, fighting the paralysis. *Real, yes, but merely a projection of my own broken mind.* The moonlight and the swaying branches were working together to paint these horrors onto the mirror of my imagination, which I had foolishly projected outward. I knew I had to believe this. I gathered what courage I could and began to step forward across the open patches of sand. *By Jove,* I thought, the doubt creeping in like ice water, *is it all a hallucination? Is it merely a nightmare?* Did not my reason argue in the old, futile way, clinging to the tiny, fragile standards of the "known"?

All I know for certain is that that great column of bronze figures ascended darkly into the sky for what seemed an eternity, existing with a completeness of reality that most men take for granted. And then, without warning, they vanished. Gone. Instantly erased from the landscape.

And the moment they disappeared, the spell broke. The immediate, blinding wonder of their presence faded, and fear rushed down upon me like a freezing flood. The esoteric, bloody meaning of this lonely, haunted region flamed up within me, burning away my sanity. I began to tremble with a dread that shook my bones to the marrow. I cast a quick, frantic glance around—a look of pure horror bordering on panic—as I calculated vain, desperate ways to escape. Realizing how utterly helpless I was to effect anything meaningful, I turned and crept back silently into the tent. I lay down again upon my sandy mattress, pulling the door-curtain down low to blot out the sight of the willows in the moonlight, and buried my face as deep as possible beneath the thin blankets, praying to deaden the sound of the terrifying wind that continued to scream outside.

The silence following the nightmare was heavy, a suffocating blanket that pressed down on my chest until I could barely draw breath. For a long time, I did not return to sleep. Even when the exhaustion finally dragged me back into a fitful slumber, it was only the surface of my soul that surrendered; deep within, a core of terrifying vigilance remained awake, scanning the darkness.

Then, the world shattered. I jolted upward with a scream of pure, primal terror that tore from my throat. It was not the wind, nor the river's roar that roused me. Something else approached slowly, a presence so dense it seemed to shrink the sleeping part of me until it vanished entirely. I sat bolt upright, paralyzed, listening.

Outside, the air was filled with a multitude of tiny, frantic patters. They had been creeping toward us for hours, becoming audible only in the depths of my dreams. Now, they were real. I sat there, wide-eyed and nerve-shredded, as if I had never closed my eyes at all. My lungs burned, struggling against a weight that seemed to press down on every square inch of my skin. Despite the stifling heat of the night, I was drenched in a clammy, icy sweat, shivering violently. Something heavy, relentless, was hammering against the sides of the tent, crushing it from above. Was it the wind itself, given form? Was it the rain, the dripping leaves, the spray whipped from the river and coalescing into deadly droplets? A dozen horrors raced through my mind.

And then, the explanation struck me like lightning: a branch from the poplar, the island's solitary giant, had broken under the gale. Still tangled with other limbs, it hung suspended, waiting for the next gust to tear it free and crush us. Meanwhile, its fronds beat rhythmically against the tight canvas. I yanked aside a flap and lunged into the dark, shouting for the Swede to rise.

But the moment I stepped outside, the illusion collapsed. The tent stood alone, unburdened. There was no hanging limb, no falling rain, no approaching monster. Nothing touched me.

A pale, grey light seeped through the thicket, illuminating the faintly shimmering sand. Above, the stars still crowded the black vault, and the wind howled with magnificent ferocity. Yet the fire was dead, offering no warmth, and already the eastern sky bled crimson through the trees. Hours must have passed since I last watched those ascending figures, and the memory of it returned with the force of a fresh trauma. Oh, how weary I felt, drained by that endless, raging storm! Yet, beneath the deep lethargy of a sleepless night, my nerves hummed with the electric activity of a tireless, gnawing dread. Rest was impossible.

The river had swollen further, its thunder filling the air, sending a fine, chilling mist through my thin shirt. Yet, looking around, I found no evidence to justify my alarm. This profound disturbance in my heart remained unexplained, a mystery wrapped in silence.

My companion had not moved when I called, and there was no need to wake him now. I scanned the camp carefully: the overturned canoe, the two yellow paddles leaning against the bank, the provision sack and extra lantern dangling from a low branch. Everywhere, crowding me in, enveloping me in a suffocating embrace, were the willows—those endless, trembling sentinels. A bird cried out in the growing morning, and a string of ducks wheeled overhead in the dimming twilight. Dry, stinging sand whirled around my bare feet, driven by the gale.

I circled the tent, venturing slightly into the brush to get a view across the water to the far shore. The same profound, indefinable horror seized me anew as I beheld the interminable sea of bushes stretching to the horizon, looking ghostly and unreal in the wan, sickly light of dawn. I paced softly, trying to make sense of that infinite, rhythmic pattering and the crushing pressure on the tent that had woken me. *It* had to be the wind, I told myself, reasoning desperately. The wind driving dry, sharp particles against the taut canvas, battering our fragile roof.

Yet, with every step, my nervousness and malaise grew deeper, more insistent.

Crossing to the opposite shore, I marveled at how the coastline had shifted overnight, how massive chunks of earth the river had ripped away. I plunged my hands and feet into the cool current, washing the sweat from my forehead. Already, a golden glow of sunrise warmed the sky, promising exquisite freshness. Returning, I deliberately walked beneath the exact cluster of bushes where I had seen the column of figures rise. Midway through the clumps, a sudden, paralyzing wave of terror overtook me. From the shadows, a large figure strode past. Someone had passed me, just as surely as any man walks...

A tremendous gust of wind shoved me forward, and once I emerged into the more open space, the sense of terror receded, strangely muted. "The winds are about and walking," I whispered to the empty air, recalling how often spirits move like great presences beneath the trees. The fear hovering around me was unknown, immense, alien—a terror unlike anything I had ever known. It sparked a strange, wild awe in me, a counterweight that nearly held back the worst of the panic. When I climbed to a high point in the middle of the island, overlooking the wide stretch of river glowing red in the sunrise, the scene's magical beauty became overwhelming. A wild yearning rose in my chest, threatening to break into a cry.

But the cry died in my throat. As my gaze drifted from the plain beyond back to the island surrounding me, fixing on our little tent half-hidden among the willows, a dreadful realization leaped into my mind, rendering the fear of the walking winds trivial.

Something had changed in the arrangement of the landscape. It was not merely a shift in perspective; the relationship between the tent and the willows, and the willows and the tent, had altered. The bushes now crowded in unnervingly close, unpleasantly intimate. *They had moved.*

With silent, creeping steps, sliding imperceptibly nearer on the shifting sands, the willows had advanced during the night. Had the wind pushed them, or had they moved of their own accord? I remembered the sound of infinite, tiny patterings, the pressure upon the tent, the crushing weight on my own heart that had woken me in terror. For a moment, swayed by the wind like a sapling, I struggled to stand straight on the sandy hillock. There was a suggestion here of personal agency, of deliberate intent, of aggressive hostility. It terrified me into a rigid stupor.

Then, the reaction followed swiftly. The idea was so bizarre, so absurd, that I felt compelled to laugh. But the laughter would not come. Why? Because the knowledge that my mind was so susceptible to such dangerous imaginings brought a fresh, secondary terror: that the attack was not coming through our physical bodies, but through our minds. And it was coming now.

The wind battered me about, and very quickly, the sun crested the horizon. It was well past four o'clock, and I must have stood on that lonely pinnacle of sand longer than I realized, too afraid to descend to the shadow of the willows. I returned quietly, creepily, to the tent. First, I took another exhaustive survey of the surroundings—and yes, I confess it—I made a few hasty measurements. Pacing along the warm sand, I marked the distances between the trees and the canvas, noting especially the shortest gap.

I crawled stealthily back into my blankets. My companion, seemingly, still slept soundly, and I was grateful for this. If his experiences did not confirm mine, I might find some strength to deny it all, to pretend it was nothing. By day, I could convince myself it was a subjective hallucination, a fantasy born of the night, a projection of an excited, fractured imagination.

Nothing further disturbed me, and I fell asleep almost immediately, utterly exhausted. Yet, even in dreams, I lived in dread of hearing again that weird, multitudinous pattering, or feeling that terrible pressure upon my heart that made breathing impossible.

High noon, the sun bleaching the world to a stark, white glare, was when my companion jolted me from a heavy, dreamless stupor. "Porridge's done," he announced, his voice cutting through the haze like a knife. "Just time to bathe before the river claims us." Before I could fully register the words, the savory scent of frying bacon drifted through the flap of the canvas tent, a cruel, delicious promise of survival.

"The river rises faster by the minute," he said, pacing the narrow floorboards. "Islands are vanishing mid-stream. Our own piece of land is shrinking by the hour."

"Wood?" I croaked, grogginess clinging to my bones.

"The wood and the island will vanish together in a dead heat," he laughed, a brittle, nervous sound. "But we'll have enough to last till then."

I dove into the water at the island's point, where the shoreline had shifted grotesquely overnight. The current swept me downstream with terrifying speed, dumping me onto the landing opposite the tent. The water was ice-cold, a shock that sent a jolt of vitality through my frozen limbs. As the banks blurred past like a nightmare on an express train, I bathed, and in that icy immersion, the terrors of the night seemed to evaporate from my brain, leaving me clear-headed and alert.

Above, the sun blazed with a ferocity that suggested it sought to burn away all sin, yet not a single cloud marred the azure expanse. However, the wind had not diminished a jot; it howled with a persistent, mechanical rhythm.

Suddenly, the implication of my companion's words crashed over me. "Enough to last till tomorrow"—he meant we were staying another night. It was a bizarre reversal. Yesterday, he had spoken of fleeing immediately, of urgency. Now, he lingered. *How* had his mind shifted?

Breakfast was a chaotic affair, marked by great crumbling of the banks and heavy splashings that drove clouds of mist into our pan. While he ranted about the impossibility of navigating the Vienna-Pesth steamers through the deluge, I observed him closely. Something had changed. He was a different man than the evening before. A shadow of excitement, a flicker of shyness, a suspicion that crept into his voice and gestures. I cannot articulate it now, but at the time, I was certain: he was frightened.

He ate little, omitting his pipe for the first time. Beside him lay the map, spread wide, his eyes tracing lines and symbols with a feverish intensity.

"We ought to leave sharp in an hour," I said, sensing an opening for a confession. "Unless they forbid it."

"Rather!" he replied, his gaze fixed on the parchment. "If they'll let us."

"Who'll let us?" I pressed, feigning indifference. "The elements?"

"The powers of this awful place," he murmured, "whatever they are. The gods are here, if gods exist anywhere."

"The elements are the true immortals," I countered, laughing as naturally as I could, though I knew my face betrayed my unease. He looked up, grave and solemn, speaking across the smoke.

"We shall be fortunate if we escape without further disaster."

Exactly what I had feared. The words tightened in my chest. I forced myself to the edge of the precipice, ready to ask the direct question. It was like consenting to a tooth extraction; it had to be done.

"Further disaster?" I demanded. "What has happened?"

"For one thing—the steering paddle is gone," he said quietly.

"The paddle!" I repeated, the blood rushing to my head. Without a rudder, the flooded Danube was a death trap. "But what—"

"And there is a tear in the bottom of the canoe," he added, a tremor in his voice.

I stared at him, unable to speak past the words hanging in the air. On this burning sand, under a scorching sun, a freezing chill descended upon us. He nodded gravely and led the way toward the tent, a few yards away. The canoe lay there as I had left her, ribs uppermost, the single paddle resting on the sand nearby.

"There is only one," he said, stooping to lift it. "And here is the rent in the base-board."

It was on the tip of my tongue to accuse him of seeing what I had clearly noticed hours prior—two paddles—but a sudden impulse silenced me. I stepped forward.

A long, fine tear marred the bottom of the canoe, where a strip of wood had been cleanly sliced away. It looked as though a jagged rock or snag had gnawed through her hull. Investigation confirmed the hole went all the way through. If we had launched, the water would have swollen the wood momentarily, closing the gap. But once in the swift current, the water would pour in, and the light craft would sink instantly.

"There," he muttered, more to himself than to me, "an attempt to prepare a victim for sacrifice. Two victims, rather."

I began to whistle, a habit born of utter confusion, deliberately ignoring his words. I resolved to dismiss them as madness.

"It wasn't there last night," he said, straightening up and looking away.

"We must have scratched her landing," I interjected, stopping my whistle. "Stones are sharp."

I halted abruptly. He turned, meeting my gaze square. I knew the lie was transparent. There were no stones.

"And then there is this," he added softly, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A new, curdling emotion spread over me as I examined it. The blade was scraped down, smoothed to a razor-thin edge, as if sanded with infinite care. The first vigorous stroke would snap it off at the elbow.

"One of us walked in his sleep and did this," I stammered weakly. "Or... perhaps the sand particles blew against it constantly."

"Oh," the Swede said, turning away with a slight, mocking laugh. "You can explain everything."

"The same wind that caught the paddle and tossed it near the bank," I called after him, desperate for a rational anchor. "Flung it into the next crumbling lump."

"I see," he shouted back, disappearing into the willow shadows.

Alone with these baffling proofs of personal agency, my first thought was, *One of us did this, and it certainly wasn't me.* But my second thought, colder and sharper, concluded that it was impossible. Could my companion, the trusted friend of dozens of expeditions, knowingly sabotage us? Absurd. Was this practical, dense-minded man suddenly insane? Also absurd.

Yet the truth remained: some curious alteration had taken root in his mind. He was nervous, timid, suspicious, aware of events he refused to speak of. He was waiting, I sensed, for a climax he expected imminently.

I hurriedly inspected the tent and grounds, finding the measurements unchanged. Deep, bowl-shaped hollows dotted the sand, ranging from teacups to large basins. The wind, undoubtedly, created these miniature craters, just as it had lifted the paddle. The rent in the canoe was the inexplicable anomaly. I clung to the theory of the sharp stone, a rationalization necessary for sanity, a working model for the universe required to perform one's duty.

I melted fresh pitch, and shortly, the Swede joined me. Under ideal conditions, the canoe could not travel safely until the following day. I casually pointed to the sand-funnels.

"Yes," he said, "I know. They're everywhere. But *you* surely can explain them!"

"Wind, of course," I answered instantly. "Street whirlwinds twist leaves into circles. This sand yields."

He offered no reply. We worked in silence. I watched him surreptitiously, convinced he was watching me. He seemed perpetually listening to something I couldn't hear, or anticipating a sound, turning to stare into the bushes, up at the sky, out across the water. Sometimes he pressed a hand to his ear, holding it there for minutes. He said nothing, and I asked no questions. Meanwhile, as he mended the torn canoe with the skill of a Red Indian, I felt a vague dread that he would speak of the willows' changed aspect. If he had noticed *that*, my imagination would fail.

At length, he began to speak.

"A queer thing," he said hurriedly, as if rushing to shed a burden. "About that otter last night."

I had expected something else entirely, and his surprise caught me off guard. I looked up sharply.

"Otters are shy," he started, then interrupted himself. "I mean—do you think... did you think it really was an otter?"

"What else? What else could it be?"

"You saw it before me. It seemed so *huge*. Much bigger than an otter."

"The sunset magnified it," I replied.

He looked at me absently, his mind elsewhere.

"It had extraordinary yellow eyes," he murmured.

"That was the sun," I laughed, a boisterous, defensive sound. "Perhaps next you'll say the man in the boat..."

It was my iron-clad resolve to remain wide-eyed until the sun crested the eastern ridge, guarding against whatever shadows lurked in the dark. Yet, the sheer fatigue of both spirit and limb conspired against me. Before long, a heavy, suffocating blanket of oblivion wrapped around me. My companion's rapid descent into slumber acted as a catalyst, accelerating my own surrender to dreams. His breathing smoothed into a steady rhythm, culminating in the distinct, rhythmic roar of snoring—a sound so profound and soothing that it marked the first and only instance in my life where such a cacophony brought genuine comfort.

This was the final thought lingering in my mind as consciousness slipped away entirely.

A sudden constriction in my throat jolted me awake. The blanket had been pulled over my face, but beneath it, something else pressed heavily against me. My immediate, instinctive fear was that my companion had rolled off his mat onto mine during his rest. I cried out his name and sat upright, and in that same instant, a chilling realization struck me: the tent was *encircled*. Outside, a deafening, multitudinous soft pattering filled the air, a sound that transformed the night into a nightmare.

I shouted again, my voice cracking with terror. He gave no answer. Worse still, the comforting cadence of his snores had vanished, replaced by silence. Furthermore, the tent flap hung open—an unforgivable breach in our sanctuary. Driven by a primal dread, I crawled out into the suffocating darkness to secure the flap. It was then, with a sinking heart, that I realized with absolute certainty: the Swede was gone. He had fled.

Seized by a madness that bordered on hysteria, I dashed out, plunging instantly into a swirling torrent of a humming sound. It surrounded me from every direction, a chaotic, frenzied vibration that echoed the familiar hum of the insects but twisted it into something monstrous. It sounded as though a swarm of giant, invisible bees had descended upon me, thickening the air itself until breathing became a labored, desperate struggle.

Yet, my friend was in peril, and hesitation was not an option.

Dawn was breaking, casting a faint, milky pallor over the horizon above a thin strip of clear sky. The air was utterly still. Through the gloom, I could barely distinguish the dense willow thicket and the river beyond, along with the pale patches of sand. In a frenzy, I ran back and forth across the island, screaming his name, shouting the first words that sprang to my lips. But the willows swallowed my cries, and the oppressive hum muffled them further, confining my voice to a sphere of mere feet around me. I threw myself into the bushes, stumbling over gnarled roots, scraping my face raw as I tore blindly through the tangled, restraining branches.

Then, quite unexpectedly, I emerged onto the point of the island. There, silhouetted against the grey line where water met sky, stood a dark figure. It was the Swede. Already, one foot hovered over the river's edge. One moment longer, and he would have jumped.

I lunged at him, wrapping my arms tightly around his waist and hauling him toward the shore with every ounce of strength I possessed. Naturally, he fought back with furious resistance, emitting a guttural noise identical to that cursed, driving hum. As he thrashed, he blurted out bizarre, incoherent phrases about going "inside" to Them, taking "the path of water and wind," and other horrors I could not grasp even as I dragged him back. They left me sick with a mixture of horror and bewilderment, though I would try in vain to recall their exact meaning later. Finally, I managed to shove him into the relative safety of the tent, throwing him onto the mattress where I pinned him down until the fit subsided.

What struck me most profoundly was the suddenness of the entire episode. Just as abruptly as the humming and the pattering ceased outside, the Swede fell silent and grew calm. I believe this simultaneity was the strangest aspect of the ordeal. He had just opened his eyes, lifting his exhausted face to meet mine, illuminated by the pale light of the rising sun streaming through the doorway. With the innocent, trembling demeanor of a frightened child, he whispered, "My life, old man... it's my life I owe you. But it's all over now anyway. They've found a victim in our place!"

Then he collapsed backward onto his blankets, falling asleep literally within inches of me. He simply succumbed, beginning to snore again with a healthy vigor, as if nothing had transpired and as if he had never attempted to offer his soul to the waters in sacrifice. When the sunlight roused him three hours later—three hours of unceasing vigil for me—it became crystal clear that he retained absolutely no memory of his near-suicide. I judged it prudent to remain silent, sparing neither him nor myself any dangerous inquiries.

He awakened naturally and easily, as I had described, when the sun burned high in a windless, scorching sky. Immediately, he rose to prepare the fire for breakfast. I followed him anxiously as he bathed, noting that he did not dare plunge fully into the water, merely dipping his head and commenting on the peculiar extra chill of the stream.

"The river's falling at last," he remarked, "and I'm glad of it."

"The humming has stopped too," I replied.

He looked up at me with a quiet, composed expression. Evidently, he remembered everything save his own act of self-destruction.

"Everything has stopped," he said, pausing significantly, "because—"

He faltered. However, I sensed that a reference to his earlier ramblings lingered in his mind, and I was determined to draw it out.

"Because 'They've found another victim'?" I asked, forcing a brittle laugh.

"Exactly," he answered, his voice steady. "Exactly! I feel as positive of it as though—as though—I feel quite safe again, I mean," he concluded, though the relief in his tone was tinged with something else.

He began to scan the surroundings curiously. Hot patches of sunlight lay on the sand; there was no breeze. The willows stood motionless. Slowly, he rose to his feet.

"Come," he said, "I think if we look, we shall find it."

He broke into a run, and I hastened to follow. He stayed close to the banks, probing the sandy bays, caves, and shallow backwaters with a stick, while I trailed closely behind.

"Aha!" he exclaimed presently, "Aha!"

The tone of his voice transported me instantly back to the visceral horror of the last twenty-four hours, and I rushed to join him. He pointed his stick at a large, dark object lying half-submerged in the water, half-resting on the sand. Twisted willow roots seemed to entangle it, preventing the river from sweeping it away. Only a few hours prior, this spot must have been underwater.

"Look," he said quietly, "the victim that made our escape possible!"

As I peered over his shoulder, I saw that his stick rested upon the form of a man. He flipped it over. It was the corpse of a peasant, the face buried deep in the sand. Clearly, the man had been drowned, but only a few hours ago. His body must have drifted down upon our island sometime around dawn—at the precise moment the fit had ended.

"We must give it a decent burial, you know," I suggested.

"I suppose so," I replied, shuddering involuntarily. Something about the appearance of that drowned wretch sent a chill through me that I could not suppress.

The Swede glanced up sharply, an unreadable mask on his face, and began to scramble down the bank. I followed at a more leisurely pace. I noted that the current had stripped much of the clothing from the body, leaving the neck and part of the chest starkly exposed.

Halfway down the slope, my companion halted abruptly, holding up a hand in warning. Either my footing betrayed me, or I had gathered too much momentum to stop quickly, and I collided with him, sending him forward with a bounding leap to avoid a fall. Together, we tumbled onto the hard sand, our feet splashing violently into the water. And before we could react, we had crashed somewhat heavily against the corpse.

The Swede let out a sharp cry. I recoiled as if I had been fired upon.

In that split second, as we brushed against the body, a loud, resonant humming erupted from its surface—a chorus of many hums—that swelled with a vast commotion, like countless winged creatures beating the air around us. Then, with a disorienting rush, they ascended upward into the sky, fading gradually until they finally died away in the distance. It was as if we had inadvertently disturbed some living, invisible workers at their labor.
