Years later, while sorting through my mother's papers, I discovered a document neatly filed and labeled in her familiar, precise hand: "From London, April, 1760. My son's dreadful letter." When it finally became my possession, I burned it. I chose not to allow that tale of domestic sorrow and estrangement to remain among our family records, lest future Warringtons glance upon it with curiosity, or disobedient sons cite it as a warning of past familial rebellion. For similar reasons, I have also destroyed the reply my mother sent me during this season of tyranny, revolt, and irritation.

Blinded by the agony of separation from my mistress, and justifiably believing that Mrs. Esmond was the architect of the deepest grief and misery I had ever known, I wrote home to Virginia. My letter, though perhaps lacking in temperance, strove to maintain the utmost respect and reserve. I stated that I knew not her motives, yet I held her accountable for the misery of my future life, which she had wilfully marred. She had caused a rift between me and a virtuous, innocent young woman, whose hopes, health, and happiness were forever dimmed by her interference. The deed was done; I offered no judgment on the perpetrator, who answered only to God, but I did not hide from my mother that the injury inflicted upon me was mortal—that neither her life nor mine could repair it. The bond of my allegiance was severed; I could never again be the dutiful, respectful son she expected.

Lady Esmond replied with extraordinary dignity. Her correspondence was always elegant and refined. She uttered no reproach, merely referring the matter coldly to the "awful tribunal of God" for counsel. She humbly submitted her own conduct to divine scrutiny and asked if I, as a son, could equally answer for myself—could I demonstrate before the Great Judge that I had fulfilled my duty and honored my parents? *O popoi!* My grandfather often quoted Homer regarding how, in our troubles, we inevitably call the gods to witness. When pride, avarice, ambition, or the desire to dominate stir within us, do we not constantly plague Heaven to rule in our favor? During our great American quarrel, did we not both sides appeal to the skies for justice, sing Te Deums for victory, and confidently declare that righteousness would prevail? Was America right solely because she won? Then surely Poland was wrong because she lost? How easily I drift into these digressions about nations and history, while truly thinking of a little woman now gone, who appealed to Heaven with a thousand scriptural texts simply because her son desired a marriage she disliked. We plead, we curse, we kneel, we demand sentences according to law; yet the great course of the world moves on. We pant, strive, struggle, hate, rage, weep passionately, reconcile, race toward victory or defeat, pass away, and are succeeded by other little strugglers. Our days end, nights fall, and mornings rise that shine upon us no more.

Having composed my letter of revolt—a document of which I was perhaps slightly proud—I showed duplicates to Mr. Lambert. I wished him to understand the nature of my relationship with my mother and my determination that, regardless of future threats, I would never view my separation from Theo as voluntary. Whenever I could see her again, I would. My promise to her was *in secula seculorum*, binding at least as long as my life endured. I implied that Theo was similarly bound to me, and her poor father understood this fully. He might separate us; he might even administer poison, and the gentle, obedient creature might drink it and die—but the death or separation would be his doing. Let him answer for it. Though tender-hearted to a fault, unable to bear the thought of torturing any child—especially this one—he could not force the issue. We had tried to part; we could not. He had attempted to separate us, but it was beyond his power. The bars were raised, yet the young couple—the maid within and the knight without—continued to love each other. The wall stood high, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered on either side. Amidst his grief and perplexity, Uncle Lambert possessed ample humor and recognized the sorry nature of his role. Light began to filter through the lime and rough plaster of the wall; the lovers were passing their hands through, then their heads. Indeed, it was the wall's finest business to retreat.

I forget the details of the stages and days that followed; nor does it greatly matter for the instruction of future ages. When my descendants encounter their own romantic entanglements, they will find their own solutions. I believe I ceased visiting Dean Street, as open-air riding was deemed most healthful for Miss Lambert. I acquired a fine horse and rode alongside her carriage. The elderly woman at Tottenham Court soon knew us both intimately, nodding and winking in the most friendly fashion as we passed. I suspect the old good woman was accustomed to taking an interest in young couples and had likely offered the hospitality of her roadside cottage to more than one pair.

The doctor and the country air wrought a prodigious cure upon Miss Lambert. Hetty always served as duenna, and occasionally, during my holidays, Master Charley rode my horse while I sat in the carriage. How much love-making Miss Hetty must have heard! With what exemplary patience she listened! I do not claim she resumed attending Methodist sermons, but certainly, whenever we traveled in a closed carriage, she would kindly and considerately peer out the window. Then, what a flood of letters! What constant running to and fro! Gumbo's bandy legs were perpetually on the move between my quarters and Dean Street; and, for my sake or hers, Mrs. Molly, the girl's maid, was endlessly returning answers from Bloomsbury. By the time autumn leaves began to pale, Miss Theo's roses were in full bloom again, and Doctor Heberden pronounced his cure complete. What else occurred during this blessed period? Mr. Warrington completed his great tragedy, *Pocahontas*. This time it was accepted by Mr. Garrick (having received favorable remarks from his friend Dr. Johnson), and my friend and cousin, Hagan, was engaged by the manager to play the hero, Captain Smith. Hagan's engagement was secured precisely when needed. I had assisted him and his family with means disproportionate to my power, particularly considering my feud with Madam Esmond. Her reply to my angry missive of April arrived towards autumn, written from Virginia with war for war and controlment for controlment. Yet these menaces frightened me little. My poor mother's thunder could not reach me; my conscience—or casuistry—supplied alternative interpretations for her scriptural texts, rendering her oracles weightless in scaring me from my purpose. I informed neither my new love nor any member of my maternal or paternal families—who had bitterly opposed my marriage—of how swiftly my new affections flourished. Why wrangle with them? It was better to seize the day (*carpere diem*) and its sweet loves and pleasures, leaving the railers to grumble and the elders to advise at their leisure.

Besides Madam Esmond, I must admit, in the frantic rage of my temporary separation, I sent notes of wondrous sarcasm to my Uncle Warrington, my Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and my Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget which individual), thanking them for the trouble they had taken in preventing the dearest happiness of my life, and promising corresponding gratitude from my obliged relative. Business brought the jovial Baronet and his family to London somewhat earlier than usual, and Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to return to Clarges Street and her social cards. I saw them. They found me perfectly well. Concluding the match was broken off, I did not choose to undeceive them. The Baroness took heart at my cheerfulness, making many sly jokes about my philosophy and my prudent behavior as a man of the world. As ever, she was bent on securing a rich match for me, and I fear I paid many compliments at her house to a wealthy young soap-boiler's daughter from Mile End, whom the worthy Baroness wished to place in my arms.

"You court her with infinite wit and esprit, my dear," my pleased kinswoman remarked, "but she does not understand half you say, and the other half, I think, frightens her. This *ton de persiflage* is very well in our society, but you must be sparing of it, my dear nephew, amongst these roturiers."

Miss Badge eventually married a young gentleman of royal dignity, though with shattered fortunes, from a neighboring island; and I trust Mrs. Mackshane has since pardoned my levity. There was another person at my aunt's house who did not comprehend my mockery nearly as well as Miss Badge herself: a lady who had witnessed the reign of James II and remained as worldly as ever in the time of King George. I loved to be in her company. If my little readers had access to this volume, I could recount a hundred stories of the great old figures she had known in the great old days—George I and his ladies, St. John and Marlborough, the reigning monarch and the late Prince of Wales, and the causes of their quarrel—but my modest muse sings only for boys and virgins. My dear son Miles cares nothing for court tales, or if he does, he possesses a fresh budget from Carlton House quite as scandalous as the worst of our old Baroness.
