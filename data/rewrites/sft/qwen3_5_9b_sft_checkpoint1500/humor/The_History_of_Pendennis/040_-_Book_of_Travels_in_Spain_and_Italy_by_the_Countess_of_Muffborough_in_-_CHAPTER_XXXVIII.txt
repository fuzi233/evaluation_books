Few individuals in London possessed a sharper eye for financial nuance than Morgan, the valet, yet even he was somewhat outmatched by the sheer magnitude of rumors surrounding Lady Clavering's estate. Before her actual descent upon the capital, the metropolis buzzed with a legend of unimaginable wealth. Her fortune was whispered to be anchored in indigo factories, opium clippers, and banks swollen with rupees, adorned with diamonds and jewels plucked from the coffers of native princes. These treasures, allegedly secured through loans contracted by the princes' ancestors to Lady Clavering's father, formed a tapestry of debt so vast that her London banker's account reportedly contained so many zeros as to induce a collective gasp of awe from any listener. The air also crackled with the presence of Colonel Altamont, an envoy from the Nawab of Lucknow—a man described as having embraced Mahometanism after surviving a thousand wild adventures—who was currently negotiating the sale of the Nawab's legendary nose-ring diamond, 'the Light of the Dewan.'

This fame preceded the Begum herself, a phenomenon that seemed to validate the boasts of Delolme and Blackstone regarding the British Constitution's admission of merit. If one could not buy a seat among the peers, surely one could buy a seat in polite society. Haughtily exclusive circles, usually guarded by invisible barriers, proved remarkably porous to the influx of gold. The upper orders, far from being the stuffy aristocrats the ignorant imagined, were ready to extend hands, serve dinners, and dance at balls for anyone sufficiently wealthy.

While the Claverings prepared for their arrival, our friend the Chevalier Strong was busy orchestrating the transformation of their town house. His soul rejoiced with a fervor akin to ownership as he directed fashionable upholsterers, rearranging pictures and positioning sofas with the precision of a conductor. Simultaneously, he stocked his own modest chambers in Shepherd's Inn, where a former Spanish legionary, whom he had rescued from a breach at a fort, now served as his body-servant. This loyal man happened to be none other than the favorite of the Nawab, the valiant Colonel Altamont.

Ned Strong, however, was a man of profound discretion. Though he suspected a mysterious connection between Sir Francis Clavering and the Colonel—a link forged perhaps during Clavering's irregular days in India—he refrained from prying. Strong viewed his patron as a man of weak purpose and shifting principles, a poltroon who had likely fallen under the Colonel's sway. Indeed, the Colonel held Clavering in his grip. On one occasion, Altamont, reduced to a state of ill humor, snatched a letter from Clavering and crushed it, declaring, "A hundred, be hanged! I'll have no more shilly-shally. Tell Clavering I'll have a thousand, or by Jove I'll split, and burst him all to atoms... If the money ain't here next Friday at twelve o'clock, I'll have a paragraph in the newspaper on Saturday, and next week I'll blow up the whole concern."

Strong conveyed this ultimatum, and true to his word, Clavering scrambled together the sum on the appointed day. Altamont, behaving with the decorum of a gentleman, settled his bill at the inn and departed for a foreign tour, leaving Strong to suspect that forgery lay at the root of Clavering's predicament. Yet, fate had a cruel sense of timing. Within the year, the Colonel returned, ruined by a disastrous streak at Baden-Baden where the color rose fourteen times in succession. Obligated to draw upon Sir Francis for his return, Clavering complied, though with evident reluctance and private curses wishing the Colonel imprisoned in a German debtor's goal forever.

To manage these subsidies, Clavering relied heavily on Strong, who procured funds and negotiated debts in the shadows of his Shepherd's Inn lodgings. For a man accustomed to trading promises for cash, prosperity often brought the money-lender back sooner than expected. Clavering found it expedient to conduct these financial gymnastics at Strong's address, where bills of exchange were drawn and discounted. Strong, despite his own lack of coin, stood as the drawer for nearly all of Clavering's obligations, parleying with holders to secure extensions or installments. It was a life of gallantry amidst the looming threat of the bailiff and the rope, a existence Strong endured with the ease of a seasoned soldier.

Meanwhile, the stage was set within the Claverings' newly acquired Grosvenor Place. The family toured the "gorgeous" dining room, a space furnished in a "middle-aged style"—a choice Lady Clavering attributed to her own maturity. Despite offering only the remnants of lunch, the room was a study in ostentatious "chastity." High-backed Dutch chairs, a sculptured buffet, a sideboard looted from a Low Country church, and a brass cathedral lamp dominated the space, surrounded by portraits, tapestries, and cardboard battle-axes. Adjacent lay a library filled with busts and solemn bronzes, a sanctuary where Sir Francis smoked cigars and read *Bell's Life* before drifting off to sleep or venturing into the gambling halls of Saint James's.

But the drawing rooms were the true marvels. Carpets so fluffy they silenced footsteps, carpets blooming with giant roses and tulips, and an array of seating ranging from heavy bandy-legs to delicate frames so thin only a sylph could occupy them. The room was cluttered with marvellous gimcracks, porcelain poodles and ducks, and paintings of nymphs and shepherdesses. Gilded cages held squawking cockatoos, while clocks sang tunes and boomed the hours. It was a display of elegance that rivaled the finest tastes of the Lower Roman Empire or the Court of Louis XV.

However, Lady Clavering, for all her wealth, possessed a singular lack of reverence for the splendor surrounding her. To Major Pendennis, she noted the exorbitant cost of the furnishings and warned him against attempting to sit in a gossamer gilt chair, recounting how she had nearly broken one the previous evening. Blanche, the sharp-tongued Sylphide, seized the moment to mock Pen for chatting with his mother, accusing him of enjoying the chaos. Pen, flustered by the mention of Laura Amory and the public knowledge of his past rejection, retreated into a feigned adoration of the furniture, praising Lady Clavering's taste with exaggerated deference.

When the topic of social invitations arose, Lady Clavering revealed that Lady Rockminster had "taken them up." While initially delighted by the prospect of having her balls planned by a figure of such high standing, Lady Clavering quickly asserted her independence, refusing to allow Lady Rockminster to dictate her guest list or sit silently at her own head of the table. She proposed a "snug little party" for the 14th, a compromise that elicited a sigh of relief from Blanche and a diplomatic acceptance from the Major, who preferred quiet gatherings to grand displays.

Later, strolling through the park, the Major questioned Pen about his recent entanglement with Miss Amory. With a dandified air, Pen admitted that "used to" and "is" were vastly different terms regarding a woman's heart. The Major, a veteran of the Cape, advised caution, noting how quickly fortunes and affections changed. He urged Pen to avoid the forward and affected Miss Amory, whose character was, in his view, questionable, and instead focus on the pragmatic path of marriage. "Marry a woman with money," the Major counseled, dismissing the romantic notion of a cottage love in favor of the comfort of a well-cooked dinner. He suggested Pen aim for a "coup," perhaps targeting the daughters of Lady Catherine Martingale, whose mother had once been renowned for her beauty.

Pen, quick to adapt, playfully quoted the ballad of the Devil's Walk about a cottage of gentility, a jest that flew over the Major's head. Warrington, the grumbler, scoffed at Pen's growing puppyhood, but the truth was that the Major found genuine pleasure in watching Pen bloom under the influence of better fortune. The young man's former sulkiness had vanished, replaced by a spirit that glowed like the morning sun, a delightful spectacle for an older man who had long ago tired of clowns and harlequins.
