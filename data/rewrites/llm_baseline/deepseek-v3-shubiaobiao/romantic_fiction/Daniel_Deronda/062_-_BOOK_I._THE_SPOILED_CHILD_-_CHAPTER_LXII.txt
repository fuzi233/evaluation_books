*"Fortune is a fickle lover, ever fleeting, never staying; she brushes the hair from your brow, steals a kiss, then flutters away. But Lady Sorrow—ah, she clings close, presses you to her heart, and whispers, 'I have all the time in the world,' as she sits by your bedside and knits."*

—Heine

Mirah had long dreaded this moment, sensing its inevitability like the slow return of an old, unwelcome guest. That afternoon, after singing at a charitable concert in a grand Knightsbridge home—an engagement secured by Klesmer—she stepped into the street, her simple black concert gown hidden beneath a cloak. The murmurs of the dispersing crowd faded behind her, but footsteps, steady and deliberate, fell into rhythm with her own.

Her pulse quickened. She did not need to turn to know who followed her. The specter of her father, long feared, now walked just behind her, and the weight of his presence pressed upon her like a hand at her back. To face him would be to invite a storm of emotions—shame, grief, the crushing weight of memory—and so she walked on, her breath shallow, her mind racing.

She had promised Mrs. Meyrick not to hide from the truth, yet now she regretted that vow. If only she could spare Ezra the shock of their father’s sudden reappearance. Resolved, she slowed her steps as she neared the quiet square where she lived. She would meet this confrontation head-on, not flee from it.

Just as she gathered her courage to turn, the presence at her side solidified—a hand closed around her wrist, and a voice, smooth and coaxing, spoke her name.

She stopped. No startled gasp, no flinch—only a slow, steadying breath as she faced him. His eyes, once bright with charm, now held the practiced warmth of a man who had spent a lifetime bargaining for favor. His face, though lined and sallow, still carried the ghost of handsomeness, his movements light despite the years. His clothes were shabby, his smile too eager.

The sight of him—this man who had been both her protector and her betrayer—twisted something deep inside her. Shame and pity warred in her chest.

*"It is you, father,"* she said, her voice quiet but firm.

*"Why did you run from me, child?"* His words came fast, laced with wounded affection, his hands gesturing as if to pluck excuses from the air. *"Have I ever forced you? Did I not break your engagement in the Vorstadt because it displeased you? And how did you repay me? By vanishing when I needed you most. I had plans—grand ones—for us in Dresden, but you left me to face ruin alone. What father ever sacrificed more for his daughter? And yet you abandoned me when I had no one."*

His voice cracked on a sob—a calculated tremor—as he pressed a yellowed handkerchief to his eyes. Mirah watched, unmoved by his theatrics. She knew this performance too well.

*"You know why I left,"* she said, her tone steady despite the tremor beneath. *"I could not trust you. I believed you deceived my mother. Had I faith in you, I would have stayed."*

*"Deceived her?"* He drew himself up, wounded dignity in every line of his face. *"Never. I meant to return to her, but fate intervened. When word came of her death, I stayed abroad for your sake. I sent money—arranged everything—only to be betrayed by false friends. Lies, all of it!"*

Mirah said nothing. She would not feed his inventions with denial. Instead, she motioned for them to walk on, unwilling to linger where curious eyes might find them.

*"You’ve done well for yourself,"* he observed, his gaze sweeping over her fine, plain dress. *"Good friends, I take it?"*

*"Yes,"* she replied, her mind already turning toward the inevitable meeting with Ezra. *"I teach. I sing. I have friends who know all that I am."*

*"And would they scorn you for a beggar father?"* He laughed, a brittle sound. *"No matter. I came to England with nothing but the hope of finding you. A fool’s errand, perhaps—but a father’s heart is stubborn. I might have thrived abroad, but loneliness wears a man down. I thought perhaps my Mirah might regret leaving me."* He sighed dramatically. *"But the world is cruel to a man whose luck has run out. No one wants a scholar in rags."*

Her chest tightened. She could not let him sink further. *"Where I live is close,"* she said. *"Will you come in?"*

She met his gaze squarely. *"My brother."*

A flicker of unease crossed his face—a barely perceptible twitch of the eyelids. *"Ezra?"* he echoed. *"How did you—"*

*"That is a long story,"* she interrupted. *"But he would not wish me to shut the door on you."*

She stepped onto the threshold, her heart pounding. For the first time, she saw her father hesitate—not in repentance, but in calculation.

*"Wait, Liebchen,"* he said, lowering his voice. *"What manner of man is Ezra now?"*

*"A good man,"* she answered, her voice thick with emotion. *"A noble one. He was once strong, full of hope—but he gave it all up for our mother. He cared for her until her death, and it broke him. Yet even in poverty, he is revered. To stand before him is to stand before truth itself."*

She could not bear to watch the shift in his expression—the quick dismissal of a conscience long buried.

*"Mirah,"* he said, suddenly earnest, *"let me not shame you before him. A little money—just enough to make myself presentable—and I will return as a father should. A coat, a decent shirt—then I might find respectable work. A courier, perhaps. Let me have this dignity."*

She wavered, torn, but then steadied. *"I cannot. I made a promise—no secrets, no hidden aid. We will bear this awhile longer. Then, when you come to us openly, you shall have what you need."*

His face darkened. *"Hard-hearted girl,"* he sneered. *"You dress in silk while your father starves."*

*"It feels cruel,"* she whispered, her lips pale. *"But broken promises are crueler still. They killed our mother. They ruined Ezra. We must endure the consequences now."*

*"Tomorrow, then,"* he snapped, already turning away—but then he paused, his hand outstretched. *"A trifle, at least. For a cigar. Surely that is no betrayal?"*

Her hands trembled as she reached into her pocket and gave him her purse. He clutched it, flashed a smile, and was gone.

Inside, she found Ezra among his manuscripts, his calm presence a balm. The dam within her broke. She fell at his feet, sobbing.

He waited, patient, until she could speak. Then, haltingly, she told him everything.

*"He will not come tomorrow,"* Ezra said softly.

Neither spoke of the fear that he might lurk in shadows, waiting to beg from her again.

*"Our lot is the lot of Israel,"* Ezra murmured at last. *"Grief and glory woven together, as smoke and flame. We inherit the good, and so we feel the evil. These things are bound to us, as our father was bound to our mother."*

His voice carried the weight of ages, as though he spoke not in a London parlor, but in the halls of ancient sages. For in all things—even this—there was a design beyond their understanding.
