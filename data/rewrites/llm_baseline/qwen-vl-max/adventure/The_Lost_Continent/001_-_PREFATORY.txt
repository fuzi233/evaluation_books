We were both stiff from sleeping under the open sky all night, even in the warm climate of Grand Canary. The dew and the coolness of the darkness had left us aching by morning. I usually start the day with a brisk run, but here on this rugged stretch of island terrain, there wasn’t a single level yard to be found. So when Coppinger began doing exercises with two jagged chunks of lava rock—his own brand of dumbbells—I followed suit.

Coppinger had seen his fair share of rough living, but he was also a doctor of medicine—though it seemed he earned a new degree every other year—and he treated health like a religion. He believed in routine, in balance, in preparation. Two days earlier, it had rained, and now a narrow stream trickled along the bottom of the barranca. We descended to it, washed our faces, brushed our teeth—something of a luxury on such an expedition—and felt marginally human again.

“Now,” Coppinger said after emptying our pockets, “we’ve got precious little food left, and what’s left is no better for being wrapped in Spanish newspaper.”

“Yours is mostly tobacco ash,” I replied.

“It’ll get worse if we leave it. And we’ve got more hard climbing ahead.”

That much was clear. So we sat beside the stream, ate everything we had, and prepared ourselves for the ten-mile hike back to the fonda at Santa Brigida, where we’d left our supplies. Coppinger wanted to take more photographs and measurements before we left this cluster of caves, so we’d likely go hungry until then—and miss out on another glass of that rich, old country wine from the PATRON’s cellar.

I can only imagine how astonished English hoteliers in Las Palmas would be if they knew what treasures one could find in the mountain villages up north—if you knew where to look and how to ask.

To be honest, the two mummies I’d already seen satisfied my curiosity. Wrapped in brittle goatskins, they crumbled into dust at the slightest touch. But Coppinger? He was convinced he’d stumbled upon something grand—an ancient Guanche university, or perhaps a sacred college like the one on the opposite side of the island. He wouldn’t rest until he’d searched every cave in sight.

He still had plenty of flash powder and twenty-eight fresh rolls of film. “Let’s finish this now,” he insisted. “No need to come back.”

So I took the rope, he grabbed the crowbar, and we climbed back toward the ridge of the cliff—where we’d been roasted by the sun the day before.

These caves weren’t easy to reach. Otherwise, they’d have been looted long ago. Coppinger claimed that in ancient times, the Guanches used goatskin ropes as ladders, which they could pull up behind them to keep intruders out. It sounded plausible. No better idea occurred to me.

The cave mouths lined up roughly thirty feet below the ridge and fifty above the canyon floor. Spanish curiosity doesn’t extend far beyond walking distance.

Climbing from below would have been awkward. But descending from above with a knotted rope was manageable. Our plan: drop down into each cave, then rappel to the base, climb back up, and repeat. Coppinger was brave, and steady on heights—but he was portly and closer to fifty than forty-five. Still, he was determined.

I went first each time, getting into the cave mouth and helping him follow. But when you’re hanging off a vertical cliff face, clinging to a thin rope like a bootlace, there’s not much help the man below can offer—except good wishes.

I wanted to spare him. The first three caves were small, empty, clearly storage pits. I suggested he skip them. But he refused. He declared one was a prison, another a pottery workshop, and the third a seminary for young priests. Naturally, he didn’t trust my judgment. That’s the way archaeologists are—full of imagination.

As the sun climbed higher, Coppinger began to show signs of exhaustion. He pushed on longer than was safe. I wasn’t happy about it. The drop from the top of the cliffs was rarely less than eighty feet.

Eventually, he admitted defeat. I urged him to head back to Santa Brigida, but he refused. Three more caves remained. If I wouldn’t check them, he’d try himself.

He tried to make it sound like a favor—taking my report instead of inspecting them himself. But I refused. I was tired, drenched in sweat, my head pounding from the sun, my hands raw from the rope. Coppinger, though worn, was still eager.

“Look,” he said, “you never know what you might find. And if you do, it’s yours. I won of claim.”

“Very generous,” I said. “But I don’t want any more goat-skin mummies.”

“Bah! This isn’t a burial cave. See the difference in the opening? Now, be a sport. Just because the others were empty doesn’t mean this one won’t yield something.”

“Oh, very well,” I said, and trudged over the rocks, along the ledge, and up the fissure that saved us a two-mile detour. I jammed the crowbar into a new hold, and lowered myself down.

Swinging into the cave was tricky—the rock overhung above, or had broken away below. I managed it, though I landed hard on my back. But I held onto the rope. Losing it would have stranded Coppinger.

At first glance, this cave was different. The others were crude dens, rounded out haphazardly. This one had been shaped with tools—clean angles, smooth walls. The sides sloped inward toward the ceiling, reminding me of an architecture I’d seen somewhere but couldn’t recall. And there were multiple rooms connected by narrow passages.

I checked for inscriptions—none. Only bats. I lit a cigarette (Coppinger always thought haste meant carelessness) and smoked it through. Then I leaned out and shouted down.

“What’s the news?” he called.

“I’ve searched it thoroughly.”

“Don’t come down yet. Wait. I’m securing the camera and flashlight to the end of the rope. Pull them up. Take half a dozen shots for me.”

“Fine,” I said, and hauled the gear inside.

The photos would be dull—dark, featureless—but Coppinger liked them that way. To avoid halation, I placed the camera on a stone ledge near each doorway, steadied it, and triggered the flash from behind. I photographed four chambers.

Then came one with a wider ledge. I set the camera down, braced it with stones, and sat to recharge the flash unit.

The moment I shifted my weight, the ledge cracked. I dropped half a foot, scrambled back up, and snatched the camera just as it slipped toward the ground.

I smiled. This was no ordinary cave. It was sealed—hermetically—with cement. The odds were good it contained something worth hiding.

At first, all I saw was dust and rubble. I lit a candle and began clearing it away. Soon, I started uncovering something unusual—layers of material that chipped away cleanly. When I brought it into daylight, I saw each layer had two parts: one side was shiny, like talc; the other was coated in a dark, toffee-colored substance that looked like wax. The wax bore patterns.

I knew nothing of ancient scripts. Coppinger had told me repeatedly that the Guanches couldn’t write. So I didn’t think the markings were letters. But I did recognize value. I began scraping it out with the heel of my boot and my pocketknife. The sheets were stuck together—I didn’t separate them. They fit perfectly into the cavity. By breaking the front seal, I reached the base and hacked through the lower layers until I freed a solid block.

It measured twenty inches by fifteen by fifteen, lighter than it looked. After taking final photos, I lowered it down on the rope.

There was nothing more to do. I descended next.

The block lay on the ground. Coppinger was on his hands and knees beside it—wild-eyed with excitement.

“What is it?” I asked.

“I don’t know yet. But it’s the most valuable discovery ever made in the Canary Islands—and it’s yours, you unappreciative fool. At least what’s left of it. Oh, man, you’ve destroyed the beginning and the end of something priceless. My fault. I shouldn’t have let an amateur handle such work.”

“I should say it’s your fault. You said these people couldn’t write. I took your word. For all I knew, it might have been edible.”

“It’s not Guanche,” he snapped. “You should have known that from the talc. Great heavens, do you see nothing? Look at the island’s geology—there’s no talc here!”

“I’m not a geologist. Is it imported?”

“Of course. It’s Egyptian. Obvious. Though how it got here, I don’t know. It’s not readable like a newspaper. The script is a variant of anything discovered so far. And this waxy coating—unique. Some mineral, perhaps asphalt. Not animal wax. I’ll analyze it later. Why they invented it and then abandoned it is a mystery. I could gaze at this all day.”

“Well,” I said, “if it’s all the same to you, I’d rather gaze at a meal. Ten miles to the fonda, and I’m starving. Do you know it’s already four o’clock? Climbing down and back up takes longer than you think.”

Coppinger spread his coat and carefully wrapped the block, refusing to tie it with rope—fear of damaging the edges. He carried it himself most of the way, only letting me take over when he nearly collapsed from exhaustion.

“Suppose you carry it,” he grumbled. “After all, it’s yours.”

When we reached the fonda, I had a hearty dinner and a bottle of old Canary wine, then retired to bed with a pipe. Coppinger ate too, but I suspect he didn’t sleep. Next morning, I found him still poring over the find—eyes bloodshot, but glowing with excitement.

“You’ve stumbled upon the most important historical manuscript ever discovered,” he said. “With your clumsy method, you’ve ruined it. Those top sheets you scraped away—probably a unique account of Yucatan’s ancient civilization.”

“Where’s Yucatan?”

“In the Gulf of Mexico. Ruins now, but once a thriving colony of Atlanteans.”

“Never heard of them.”

“They were real. Atlantis was a continent just north of the Canaries.”

“What’s that winged crocodile in the margin?”

“A beast from those days. A cave-tiger. And that—a colossal bat. Thank goodness the writer illustrated everything. Otherwise, we’d never understand. Whole species have vanished since then—just as a whole continent drowned and three civilizations ended. Worst of all, it was written by a highly educated man who writes badly. I’ve worked all night and only deciphered fragments.”

He rubbed his hands. “It’ll take a year to translate properly.”

“Every man to his taste. Mine wouldn’t last that long. But how did it get here? Did some ancient Egyptian come for the air and write it in a cave?”

“I misjudged. The author wasn’t Egyptian. The script misled me. It was written by Deucalion—priest, general, or both—an Atlantean. How it arrived here? Probably explained in the last pages—smashed by your knife.”

“Abuse me all you like. Deucalion? The Greek mythological figure? He survived the Flood—like Noah.”

“The sinking of Atlantis may be the Flood.”

“Is there a Pyrrha? His wife?”

“I haven’t found her. But there’s a Phorenice—perhaps the same. She seems to be the reigning Empress.”

I studied the illustrations. The perspective was odd, but the creatures were unmistakable—gigantic beasts. One drawing showed a cave-tiger attacking a mammoth.

“By Jove. I wouldn’t have wanted to live then.”

“They probably had ways to fight them. Time will tell.”

He glanced at his watch. “I suppose I ought to feel ashamed. I haven’t slept. Are you leaving?”

“I’m driving back to Las Palmas. Promised a golf game.”

“See you at dinner. Hope my shirts are back. O, lord—I’m sleepy.”

I left him heading to bed, ordered a carriage, and departed. A cable awaited me in Las Palmas—business called. I caught the Liverpool boat just as it steamed out. Close call. The boatmen made a fortune off my rush.

Coppinger was just a passing acquaintance. Back in England, I was buried in work. I didn’t think of him again—until a year later, when a newspaper mentioned he’d donated the manuscript to the British Museum, valued at ten thousand pounds.

I wrote him a sharp note, reminding him I’d discovered it—and that he’d taken liberties with my property.

His reply was formal: “Doctor Coppinger regrets…” and enclosed the English translation. He “admitted” my claim and hoped publication profits would compensate for damage.

I hadn’t expected such coldness. I responded firmly. The only reply was from solicitors: all future communication must go through them.

Publicly, I regret his stance. But since the matter has progressed, I accept his terms. The original text is printed here. Dr. Coppinger bears credit—and responsibility—for the translation. All revenue from readers goes to me, the finder of the talc-upon-wax sheets.

Should the arrangement change, it will be announced publicly. But for now, this seems unlikely.
