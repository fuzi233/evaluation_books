"Through cross to crown!—And though thy spirit's life / Trials untold assail with giant strength, / Good cheer! good cheer! Soon ends the bitter strife, / And thou shalt reign in peace with Christ at length." —KOSEGARTEN.

"Ay sooth, we feel too strong in weal, to need / Thee on that road; But woe being come, the soul is dumb, that crieth not on 'God.'" —MRS. BROWNING.

That afternoon, Margaret walked swiftly toward the Higgins' home. Mary was waiting for her, her face etched with half-distrust. Margaret smiled warmly into her eyes, offering reassurance. Together, they moved quickly through the house, up the stairs, and into the quiet presence of the dead. Immediately, Margaret felt glad she had come. The face, so often weary with pain and restless with troubling thoughts, now bore the faint, soft smile of eternal rest. Slow tears gathered in Margaret's eyes, yet a profound calm settled within her soul. This was death. It appeared more peaceful than life itself. Sacred scriptures flooded her mind: *"They rest from their labours." "The weary are at rest." "He giveth his beloved sleep."*

Slowly, Margaret turned away from the bed. Behind her, Mary humbly sobbed. They descended the stairs without exchanging a word.

Nicholas Higgins stood in the center of the floor, his hand resting on the house-table. His great eyes, startled wide by the news he had heard from many busy tongues as he approached the courtyard, remained dry and fierce. He studied the reality of her passing, forcing himself to accept that her place would know her no more. She had been so sickly, dying for so long, that he had convinced himself she would not actually die; that she would "pull through."

Margaret felt as though she had no business to be there, familiarizing herself with the surroundings of death, which the father had only just begun to learn. There had been a brief pause on the steep, crooked stair when she first saw him, but now she tried to slip past his abstracted gaze, leaving him to the solemn circle of his household misery.

Mary sat down on the nearest chair, threw her apron over her head, and began to weep loudly.

The noise seemed to rouse Nicholas. He seized Margaret's arm suddenly, holding her until he could find words to speak. His throat felt dry; the words came up thick, choking, and hoarse.

"Were yo' with her? Did yo' see her die?"

"No!" Margaret replied, standing still with the utmost patience, now that she realized she was being observed. It took some time before he spoke again, though he kept his grip on her arm.

"All men must die," he said finally, with a strange gravity that first suggested to Margaret he had been drinking—not enough to intoxicate himself, but enough to bewilder his thoughts. "But she were younger than me." Still, he pondered the event, not looking at Margaret, though he held her tightly. Suddenly, he looked up at her with a wild, searching inquiry in his glance. "Yo're sure and certain she's dead—not in a dream, a faint? she's been so before, often."

"She is dead," Margaret answered. She felt no fear in speaking to him, despite the pain his grip caused her arm, as wild gleams crossed the stupidity of his eyes.

"She is dead!" she repeated.

He continued to stare at her with that searching look, which seemed to fade from his eyes as he gazed. Then, abruptly, he released his hold on Margaret. Throwing his body half across the table, he shook it violently, rattling every piece of furniture in the room with his sobs. Mary trembled forward toward him.

"Get thee gone!—get thee gone!" he cried, striking wildly and blindly at her. "What do I care for thee?" Margaret took her hand and held it softly in hers. He tore at his hair, beat his head against the hard wood, and then lay exhausted and stupefied. Neither his daughter nor Margaret moved. Mary trembled from head to foot.

Finally—it might have been a quarter of an hour, perhaps an hour—he lifted himself up. His eyes were swollen and bloodshot, and he seemed to have forgotten anyone was present; he scowled at the watchers when he noticed them. He shook himself heavily, gave them one final sullen look, said not a word, and made for the door.

"Oh, father, father!" Mary cried, throwing herself onto his arm—"not tonight! Any night but tonight. Oh, help me! he's going out to drink again! Father, I'll not leave you. You may strike, but I'll not leave you. She told me last of all to keep you from drink!"

But Margaret stood silently in the doorway, commanding nonetheless. He looked up at her defiantly.

"It's my own house. Stand out of the way, wench, or I'll make ye!" He had shaken Mary off with violence; he looked ready to strike Margaret. Yet she never moved a muscle, never taking her deep, serious eyes off him. He stared back at her with gloomy fierceness. Had she stirred a finger or a toe, he would have thrust her aside with even more violence than he had used against his own daughter, whose face was bleeding from her fall against a chair.

"What are yo' looking at me in that way for?" he asked eventually, daunted and awed by her severe calm. "If yo' think for to keep me from going what gait I choose, because she loved ye—and in my own house, too, where I never asked ye to come, ye're mistaken. It's very hard upon a man that he can't go to the only comfort left."

Margaret sensed he acknowledged her power. What could she do next? He had seated himself on a chair close to the door; half-conquered, half-resentful; intending to leave as soon as she vacated her position, but unwilling to use the violence he had threatened mere moments before. Margaret laid her hand on his arm.

"Come with me," she said. "Come and see her!"

Her voice was very low and solemn, yet there was no fear or doubt expressed in it, regarding either him or his compliance. He sullenly rose. He stood uncertain, with a dogged irresolution on his face. She waited for him there; quietly and patiently waiting for his moment to move. He derived a strange pleasure in making her wait, but eventually, he moved toward the stairs.

They stood beside the corpse.

"Her last words to Mary were, 'Keep my father from drink.'"

"It canna hurt her now," he muttered. "Nought can hurt her now." Then, raising his voice to a wailing cry, he continued: "We may quarrel and fall out—we may make peace and be friends—we may come to skin and bone—and nought of all our griefs will ever touch her more. She's had her portion on 'em. What with hard work first, and sickness at last, she led the life of a dog. And to die without knowing one good piece of rejoicing in all her days! Nay, wench, whatever she said, she can know nought about it now, and I mun ha' a sup of drink just to steady me again sorrow."

"No," said Margaret, softening with his softened manner. "You shall not. If her life has been what you say, at any rate she did not fear death as some do. Oh, you should have heard her speak of the life to come—the life hidden with God, that she is now gone to."

He shook his head, glancing sideways up at Margaret as he did so. His pale, haggard face struck her painfully.

"You are sorely tried. Where have you been all day—not at work?"

"Not at work, sure enough," he said, with a short, grim laugh. "Not at what you call work. I were at the Committee, till I were sickened out with trying to make fools hear reason. I were fetched to Boucher's wife before seven this morning. She's bed-fast, but she were raving and raging to know where her dunner-headed brute of a chap was, as if I'd to keep him—as if he were fit to be ruled by me. The d--d fool, who has put his foot in all our plans! And I've walked my feet sore with going about to see men who wouldn't be seen, now the law is raised against us. And I were sore-hearted, too, which is worse than sore-footed; and if I did see a friend who offered to treat me, I never knew who lay dying here. Bess, lass, thou'd believe me, thou wouldst—wouldstn't thou?" turning to the poor, dumb form with a wild appeal.

"I am sure," said Margaret, "I am sure you did not know; it was quite sudden. But, now, you see, it would be different; you do know; you do see her lying there; you hear what she said with her last breath. You will not go?"

There was no answer. Indeed, where was he to look for comfort?

"Come home with me," she said at last, with a bold venture, half-trembling at her own proposal as she made it. "At least you shall have some comfortable food, which I'm sure you need."
