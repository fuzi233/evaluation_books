As it had been in the cyclical harvests of his Virginian forebears, so it became in the waning summer of Tunbridge Wells: Harry Warrington found himself the axis around which a new season turned. In this merry little watering-place, the acoustic ecology was a jagged cacophony of clattering carriage wheels, the relentless, hollow clinking of guineas at the faro tables, and the sharp, predatory trills of polite gossip. It was a sonic environment that drowned out the quiet rhythms of the natural world, substituting the superficial commercialization of leisure for genuine communion. Yet, within this industrial noise of society, no nobleman inspired so much fascination as the young Virginian. 

Everywhere Harry walked, the market-folks stared, and matrons engineered quiet corners in the assembly rooms to leave him alone with their daughters. At every tea-table, over the delicate aromas of imported Darjeeling and the buttery crumb of seed-cakes, his winnings at play were calculated to the final shilling. So potent was the mythos of his colonial wealth that this hunger for proximity extended even to his servant. Drawn into the subterranean kitchens and servants’ halls, Mr. Gumbo was plied with cellar-cool ale, its bitter malt foaming over the rims of pewter tankards, and thick slabs of cold roasted mutton, the rich, congealed fat melting on the tongue. Gumbo fattened visibly under this rich dietary regimen, taking his place as a leading elder among the Society of Valets, and weaving vast, mythic tales of Virginia’s agrarian bounty—of sugar-cured hams smoked for days over hickory wood, and oyster beds stretching to the horizon—lying more enormously than ever to feed their ravenous curiosity.

Harry bore this sudden prosperity with an agrarian steadiness. Bred to the sensory appreciation of his homeland’s soil and the profound, sustaining rhythms of its seasons, his self-satisfaction was tempered by a quiet modesty. He did not brag of his victories, nor did he puff himself up with the empty air of the metropole. When challenged to play, he yielded to his own inherited code of honor. If summoned to a horse-race, a drinking-bout smelling of sharp juniper gin, or a match at cards, he felt bound to represent Old Virginia. He brought to these contests a firm seat, an unflinching nerve, and a robust physical constitution. He shot with Jack Morris and beat him; he rode a match with Mr. Scamper and won; he played tennis with Captain Batts, the percussive *thwack* of the ball against string echoing his rapid mastery of the sport. At the card tables, surrounded by the stale, suffocating scent of snuff and burning beeswax, he engaged the celebrated gamesters Lord Chesterfield and Lord March, who both praised his cool gallantry. 

Madame de Bernstein, a meticulous curator of her own opulent indulgences, delighted in her nephew's triumphs. Though she could converse brilliantly on Molière or Mr. Fielding, she vastly preferred the immediate, tactile thrills of the world to the silent abstraction of the library. She supervised Harry’s toilette with the eye of a master chef assessing raw ingredients, rummaging through cascades of fine lace for his ruffles and pinning a brilliant diamond-brooch to his frill. Under her wing, he drank deeply from the sweet, sparkling pleasure-cup of Tunbridge. 

But within this cup lay the bitter dregs of a dissolute world. The thinly-peopled wilderness of Harry’s youth possessed a purity of sound and action entirely alien to the vices of this great English city. He blushed like a girl at the daring, commercialized libertinism of his new associates. Yet, the dominant lexicon of this society refused to grant him his innocence. How was Harry to know the world would deliberately mistranslate his kindness toward Madame Cattarina, the saucy French ballet-dancer? 

Harry had seen Lord March parading the dancer about. When Harry encountered her, he recognized the profound alienation of her subjective experience. She was a woman isolated by language death, stranded in a watering-place where the doctors ordered her to drink the sulfuric, metallic waters for her health, yet where barely half a dozen souls could speak her native tongue. Harry’s decision to offer her his arm was rooted in a desire to share that marginalized dialect, to carve out a fragile acoustic space of spoken French amidst the oppressive roar of English society. He indulged her fancies at the toy-shop, enjoying the tactile exchange of purchasing a coveted locket or a length of delicate lace, but his generosity had its limits. He refused to buy her diamond drops, turning off her request with a laugh, demonstrating a shrewd resistance to the utter commercialization of their linguistic bond. 

Yet, the gossips wielded their dominant vocabulary like a weapon, insisting on rendering his innocent translation of friendship into the crude dialect of conquest. Aunt Bernstein herself championed the narrative, eager to stab the tender heart of Lady Maria by proclaiming Harry a conquering Don Juan. The bread which Maria ate at her aunt's expense choked her. How hard, how physically indigestible, the daily sustenance of dependent women could be made by the cruel orchestration of polite society!

It was a dissolute age, poisoned by the mistresses of an old king and a nobility reckless in their pursuit of sensory extremes without spiritual gratitude. The stories of Harry’s supposed wickedness—that he was an abandoned profligate, a hardened spendthrift, a frequenter of painted women—flowed outward like a contaminated stream, finally reaching the honest, agrarian household of Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst. 

The arrival of these rumors shattered the domestic ecology of the Lambert home. The good Colonel was filled with such silent grief that the heavy atmosphere of the house shifted. At first, the sorrow was stifled into the conjugal pillows, a muffled sonic distress, before spilling downstairs to taint the family’s daily meals. Theo and Hester ingested the bitter news, their kind hearts wounded. Hester initially clenched her fists, refusing to consume the wicked stories, but the sheer volume of the world’s scandal eventually forces itself down the throats of the innocent. They wept over the thought of the boy they had nurtured by their hearth fire parading about with a painted Frenchwoman, showering her with diamonds. In vain did the local parson preach against the appetite for scandal; the human palate always hungers for the next ruinous morsel of gossip.

Harry, entirely deaf to the hubbub he was creating, continued his pleasant, idle routines. Emerging one afternoon from a tennis match, his blood humming with exertion, he overtook Colonel Wolfe. Harry held out his hand, but the Colonel’s salutation was so freezing it seemed to drop the very temperature of the air. Wolfe offered a mere tap of his forefinger to the departing Captain Batts, then walked beside Harry in a rigid, deafening silence.

“I have not seen you these many days,” Harry said, trying to bridge the acoustic void.

“You have had other companions,” Mr. Wolfe remarked curtly.

“But I had rather be with you than any of them,” the young man cried.

“Indeed I might be better company for you than some of them. Is it Captain Batts you mean?” Wolfe’s voice was grim. “He bore a rascally reputation in the army. You certainly might find a better friend.”

“Friend! He is no friend,” Harry protested, bewildered by the shifting power dynamics of the conversation. “He only teaches me to play tennis.”

“I will be free with you, Mr. Warrington,” Wolfe said gravely. “I don't like some of your friends. They are men of too high rank and too great fashion for a hard-working poor soldier like me. I met you yesterday openly walking with a French ballet-dancer, and you took off your hat. I must frankly tell you, that I had rather you would not take off your hat when you go out in such company.”

“Sir,” Harry said, the blood rushing hot to his face, “do you mean that I am to forgo the honor of Colonel Wolfe's acquaintance altogether?”

“I certainly shall request you to do so when you are in company with that person,” Wolfe snapped, using a word too foul to transcribe, a crude categorization that violently flattened the dancer’s humanity.

“Great heavens! What a shame it is to speak so of any woman!” Harry cried, deeply attuned to the violence of the label. “How dare any man say that that poor creature is not honest?”

Wolfe looked at him with surprise. “You ought to know best, sir, or the world belies you very much.”

“What ought I to know best? I see a poor little French dancer ordered to drink the waters. Are you so squeamish? Why should I not give this little unprotected woman my arm? There are scarce half a dozen people here who can speak a word of her language. I can talk a little French, and she is welcome to it; and if Colonel Wolfe does not choose to touch his hat to me, by George he may leave it alone!”

“You don't mean to say,” Wolfe said, his eyes narrowing as he probed the depths of Harry’s subjective truth, “that you did not win her at dice, from Lord March?”

“At what?” 

“At dice. Everybody knows the story. I heard it but now in the company of good old Mr. Richardson.”

Harry demanded to know what else the world’s dominant lexicon had written about him. Wolfe laid out the grotesque banquet of rumors: that Harry was a corrupter of virtue, an habitual drunkard, a notorious blasphemer. 

As Harry listened, Wolfe saw the genuine, unfeigned horror in the young man's eyes. “I tell you these things,” Wolfe softened, the rigid military cadence dropping from his voice, “because it is fair that you should know what is said of you, and because I do heartily believe, from your manner of meeting the charge, that you are innocent. I feel I have done you a wrong, and sincerely ask you to pardon me.”

Harry eagerly accepted the apology, shaking hands with restored cordiality. He easily rebutted the worst of the charges, though he owned to the play, believing that a gentleman should not refuse a fair challenge if his means allowed, and noting that even his aunt engaged in the cards. 

Wolfe warned him that while this life might be pleasant to the senses, it lacked the profound meaning of true sustenance. “Sure, every man was made to do some work: and a gentleman must make some. Have you travelled over the country, and made yourself acquainted with its trades and manufactures? These are fit things for a gentleman to study. Honor is the aim of life, and every man can serve his country. Be sure, sir, that idle bread is the most dangerous of all that is eaten; that cards and pleasure may be taken by way of pastime after work, but not instead of work. I think you are rather Warrington the Unlucky, for you are followed by daily idleness, daily flattery, daily temptation, and the Lord send you a good deliverance out of your good fortune.”

The concept of *idle bread*—sustenance consumed without the spiritual grounding of honest labor—struck a resonant chord within Harry’s agrarian soul. He walked away grave, determined to fast from the night’s superficial indulgences. He meant to refuse the sharp bite of the evening’s wine, but passing the ordinary, the rich, savory aromas of roasting meats and the jovial, rhythmic clatter of his fellows broke his resolve. A fourth was needed at his aunt’s table; how could he resist the cyclical pull of the world? He partnered with the old lady, possessed of an almost mythic luck, and once more saw the dawn break, feasting on tender capon and the crisp, golden effervescence of champagne as the sun rose over the hills.
