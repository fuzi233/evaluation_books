Our kin at Castlewood kept us in their company until the dawn of the new year. After a fortnight’s absence—what seemed an eternity to the young man, so utterly captivated by love—he returned to the side of his beloved. Madame de Bernstein, for her part, was not displeased to leave the home of her father. As we distanced ourselves from that place, she began to speak more freely.

What transpired during the tense encounter between her and her niece—when the two women stood locked in a battle of wills—she never revealed. But from that moment forward, the old lady spoke no more of shaping *cette petite* into a proper noblewoman. Indeed, when she mentioned the young Countess at all, it was with a nervous laugh, devoid of malice. Her nephew Eugene, she declared, would be henpecked for the rest of his days—something she saw clearly enough. A little order in the household, she insisted, would do him more good than any grand schemes. The little American gentleman, though vulgar in manner, appeared shrewd enough to serve as steward—a role he might well fill with advantage.

As for the Countess’s mother—well, she had heard rumors that the woman had been a convict transported from England, likely having spent years in prison beating hemp. But such gossip need not be shouted from rooftops. And truly, when it came to certain kinds of people, what did birth matter? The girl herself would now act with honesty for her own sake. She was clever, and would learn English soon enough. And the name she carried—one of great standing—was sufficient to open doors in any society. Was Mr. Van den Bosch a grocer? A smuggler? A slave-dealer? What did it matter? The Countess of Castlewood could afford to be anyone’s daughter. Once my nephew presented her, said the old lady, it would be our duty to stand by her.

Bound by ties of blood, Madame de Bernstein was expected to extend the same affection to her niece as she did to her nephew. Mr. Warrington hoped she would be generous toward Lady Maria, and spoke of his visit to Mr. Hagan and his wife, begging her aunt’s favor for them. But the old lady remained firm in her refusal. She begged that Lady Maria’s name never again be spoken, and then proceeded for two hours recounting anecdotes about her niece—tales so intimate and pointed that, since this book lies open before the innocent eyes of youth, I shall refrain from recording them. Yet let me say this: if the young woman erred, she was not alone in sin within her family. And if she repented, others would do well to follow her example.

Hagan, it is known, led an exemplary life after leaving the stage. He was renowned for both elegance and eloquence in the pulpit. His wife, though she embraced extreme views, was deeply respected in her religious circle. When I last saw her, she spoke of possessing a peculiar spiritual illumination—an idea I suspected at the time stemmed from overindulgence in drink. But I remember when she and her husband were kind to me and mine, during a time when sympathy was rare and many a self-righteous soul turned away.

I have told how easily one rose and fell in my fickle aunt’s favor, how each of us brothers, in turn, was embraced and then cast aside. My moment of glory came after the success of my play. I was introduced to the wit of the town, held my place among them with modest success, praised as “pretty well bred” by the macaronis and men of fashion. Had my purse lasted longer, or had I chosen that path, I might have carved out a respectable career among them. But I loved, at that time, a pair of kind eyes more than the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs, or all the painted beauties of Ranelagh.

Because I loved your mother, children, can you believe it was said that my tastes were low? That my genteel family deplored my way of life? It was so. Both my godly Lady Warrington and worldly Madame Bernstein laid their heads together and lamented my choices.

“Why,” said Religion, ever watchful of Heaven and the main chance, “with his name, he might marry anyone!”

“I meddle with no man’s affairs,” said Uncle Warrington, “and I admire genius. But it is a pity you consort with poets and authors, and that sort of people. When you might have had a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, you let her slip and courted a country girl without a penny.”

“But if I had promised her, uncle?” I asked.

“Promise, promise!” he replied, turning sharply on his heel. “These things are matters of arrangement and prudence. You committed yourself to little Miss Lambert before you’d seen the lovely American lady your mother wished you to marry—as any good mother would. Your duty to her, nephew, your duty to the Fifth Commandment, would have justified breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your mother’s intentions regarding Miss—what was the Countess’s Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing. But a plumb, Master George, is something to look at! I have my dear little Miley at dancing school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell’s daughter. I don’t disguise my hope that the children may form an attachment that endures through life. I tell the Nabob so. We went from the House of Commons one dancing day and saw them. It was beautiful to see the young ones walking a minuet together—it brought tears to my eyes. For I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!”

“But if I prefer Miss Lambert, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess with her hundred thousand pounds?”

“Then, sir,” said the old gentleman, “you have a singular taste—that’s all.” And with that, he left me.

I understood perfectly his vexation at my inability to see the world as he did. Nor did Aunt Bernstein approve of my engagement or the family with whom I spent so much time. Their simplicity wearied her, perhaps even annoyed her, just as certain persons (not so black as they’re painted) dislike holy water. She chafed at my constant presence beside my sweetheart. Having risen high in her favor, I began to fall again. Harry once more became the favorite—and his brother, Heaven knows, was not jealous. He was now our family hero.

He wrote brief letters from the seat of war where he served. At first, Madame Bernstein paid little attention to them—their tone plain, their content unexciting. But we had already learned in London of the battle fought on the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe’s old regiment was among the six British units that helped secure victory on that famed day. At the very same hour, the young General lay sick in his bed near Quebec, stricken by fever and perhaps rage and disappointment at the setback his troops had just endured.

Arriving in the St. Lawrence in June, the fleet carrying Wolfe and his army landed them on the last day of the month on the Island of Orleans, opposite the towering cliffs of Quebec. After the great action in which his General fell, the dear brother who had accompanied him wrote home one of his simple letters, describing his modest role in that glorious day. He added nothing new to the many accounts already written of the battle of September 13th, except one detail: from the testimony of a fellow aide-de-camp who stood beside him, the General never spoke a word after receiving his mortal wound. Thus, the famous phrase attributed to the dying hero must be considered no more authentic than a speech by Livy or Thucydides.

From his position on the island, which lies in the river’s main channel north of the city, the General watched constantly for an opportunity to attack. Above and below the city, he landed forces—here and there—ever seeking an opening. It was surely a grave error on the part of the Marquis of Montcalm to accept battle on equal terms, for the British General had no artillery. When we scaled the heights and reached the Plains of Abraham, we were closer to the city, certainly—but still as far off as ever from entering it.

The game played between these two brave commanders, lasting from July until Mr. Wolfe won the final stake in September, must have been one of the most compelling matches ever fought. On the very night after landing, as my brother recounted, the contest began. At midnight, the French sent a squadron of fire-ships downriver toward the British vessels discharging stores at Orleans. Our sailors thought it sport to tow them clear of the fleet and ground them ashore, where they burned out.

As soon as the French commander learned our ships had entered the river, he marched to Beauport, ahead of the city, and took up a strong position. Once our supplies and hospitals were established, our General crossed from the island to the left bank, drawing nearer to his enemy. He had the fleet behind him in the river, but the entire countryside lay hostile before him. Indians in the forest seized our advance parties and tortured them horribly. The French were as cruel as their Indian allies.

The Montmorenci River flowed between Wolfe and his foe. He could neither attack them nor the city beyond. Determined to find another point of assault, he circled around Quebec and skirted the left shore. Everywhere, defenses were strong—both in front and behind. After enduring the batteries’ fire up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci.

On the right flank of the French position, across the fordable Montmorenci River, stood an enemy redoubt. He wanted that. Perhaps its defense would force the French commander to abandon his lines and bring on a battle. Wolfe decided to risk it. He would move his army from the Island of Orleans and attack from the St. Lawrence. He timed the assault so that at low tide, his lieutenants Murray and Townsend could cross the Montmorenci.

On the last day of July, he launched this desperate gamble. First, he and General Monckton—setting out from Point Levi—crossed the St. Lawrence from their respective stations, met with a storm of shot and artillery as they rowed to shore. No sooner were the troops landed than they rushed toward the redoubt without order, were cut down in great numbers, and forced to retreat.

At the prearranged signal, the troops on the other side of the Montmorenci advanced across the river in perfect order. The enemy evacuated the redoubt and fell back to their lines—but from there they met the attackers with such fierce fire that any impression was impossible. The General had no choice but to withdraw.

The Battle of Montmorenci—fought over and over again by my brother Harry and me over our wine—formed the grim burden of the first dispatch from Mr. Wolfe to reach England, plunging us all into gloom. What could one expect from such a rash commander? What disasters might not follow? Was ever a plan so reckless as bringing three large bodies of men across broad rivers, under murderous batteries, merely to provoke an entrenched enemy into battle?

It was the talk of the town. No wonder sober men shook their heads and prophesied further disaster. The General, weakened by fever, would live barely six weeks longer—and die immortal.

How does greatness come to pass? Is it merit—or madness? Frolic or fortune? Fate? Does the just cause always prevail? How did the French take Canada from the savages, and we from the French? And after which conquest should we sing Te Deum?

We always invoke Heaven in our quarrels, summoning the gods to intervene, no matter the knot. Does Broughton, after pummeling Slack, raise his bruised eye to Jove and thank him for the victory? And if ten thousand boxers do so, why not one? If Broughton is grateful, what must Slack feel?

“By the list of disabled officers—many of rank—you may see, sir, that the army is greatly weakened. The nature of this river deprives the most formidable part of our armament of its power. Yet we face nearly the entire force of Canada. In this situation, I am at a loss. Great Britain demands vigorous action; yet courage should be used only where there is hope. The admiral and I have examined the town for a general assault—he would join in any measure for public service—but I cannot propose such a dangerous undertaking, promising so little success... I found myself so ill, and remain so weak, that I begged the generals to consult for the public good. They agree we should send a corps of four or five thousand—nearly our whole strength—to draw the enemy from their position and force a battle. I have agreed, and preparations are underway.”

So wrote the General—whose noble letters clearly were not penned by our dear scribe—from his headquarters at Montmorenci Falls on September 2nd. Then, on October 14th, the Rodney cutter arrived with the sad news in England: the attack had failed, the chief was ill, the army dwindling, the city too strong for assault. “The only chance,” he wrote, “is to fight the Marquis of Montcalm on less disadvantageous terms, and, if possible, draw him from his position.”

Would the French commander—whose military genius was known across Europe—fall into such a trap? No wonder faces grew pale in the City, doubt and gloom spreading wherever the news reached.

Three days later came the famous letters announcing the astonishing turn of fortune that ended Mr. Wolfe’s remarkable life. If no man is truly happy until death, what shall we say of this one? His end was so glorious that I protest not even his mother nor his mistress should mourn it—or wish him alive again.

I know we speak of a hero. Yet I confess I scarcely know whether to admire the triumph of genius, invention, and daring—or the boldness of a gambler winning against impossible odds. Suppose his ascent had been discovered half an hour earlier? Suppose his men had been beaten back? Suppose the Marquis of Montcalm had refused to leave his entrenchments? Suppose these points—none of which depend on Mr. Wolfe—and what becomes of the glory of the young hero, the minister who discovered him, or the nation drunk with self-congratulation?

What fate shapes our ends—or those of nations? In the many risky games Lord Chatham played, he won this greatest one. And as the greedy British hand seized Canada, it dropped the United States from its grasp. Of course, wisdom after the fact is easy. Now, we marvel at Wolfe’s rashness, and blame others for their mistakes. What generals are we on paper! What sharp retorts come to mind after the deed is done! And once the game is over, how well we see how it should have been played!

Writing thirty years later, it is easy to criticize. But when we first heard of Wolfe’s deeds on the Plains of Abraham—of the army marching in darkness, carried silently up the midnight river—of the rocks scaled by the intrepid leader and his men—of the enemy’s miraculous unawareness, their acceptance of our challenge, their defeat on the open field by sheer valor—we were all intoxicated in England by the news.

The whole nation rose up, feeling stronger for Wolfe’s victory. Not only those who fought, but those at home who had condemned the plan, felt themselves heroes. Our spirit soared as theirs faltered. Friends embraced. Coffee-houses and public places teemed with people eager to talk of the news. Courtiers rushed to the King and the great Minister whose wisdom had guided the campaign. When he appeared, the people followed him with shouts and blessings.

No one mourned the dead warrior—only admired his peaceful passing. Should James Wolfe’s friends grieve and wear mourning because a chariot from heaven had come to carry him away? Let them watch in awe, and see him departing radiant, rising above us, superior.

To have known someone near him was to be honored. Every soldier who fought with him became a hero. In our small circle, it was a distinction to be Harry’s brother. Even Uncle Warrington wrote letters praising Heaven and congratulating himself and me on Harry’s share in the glorious achievement. Aunt Beatrix opened her house and welcomed guests in celebration of the victory. I, too, became a hero—by virtue of my resemblance to my brother.

And Parson Sampson preached a sermon so fiery that his listeners—some warned in advance—could barely be stopped from huzzaing him. They were mobbed as they left the chapel.

“Don’t talk to me about grief,” General Lambert said to his wife, who, dear soul, wanted to allow herself a small indulgence on the day Wolfe’s remains were buried at Greenwich. “If our boys could die like James, you wouldn’t stop them from being shot. You’d scale the Abraham heights to see it done! Wouldst thou mind dying in the arms of victory, Charley?” he asked the little hero from the Chartreux.

“That I wouldn’t,” said the boy. “And the doctor gave us a holiday, too.”

Harry’s promotion was assured after his role in the battle. Our aunt announced her intention to purchase a company for him.
