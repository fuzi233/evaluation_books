The agony of anticipation had finally yielded to the sharp, clear sting of reality. Elinor understood now the chasm between expecting a blow and receiving it. While Edward remained unmarried, her heart had clung to a fragile hope—a secret prayer that fate might intervene, that some timely resolution or better opportunity would spare her sister-in-law and preserve her own peace. But that hope was extinguished. He was married. The news struck her with a violence that made her condemn the lingering flattery in her soul, the foolish belief that she had ever truly wished for his happiness if it meant losing him.

It surprised her, initially, that the wedding had occurred so swiftly, before he could even be ordained or possess the living. Yet, logic quickly followed the shock. Lucy, driven by her own frantic need to secure him, had surely overlooked every obstacle save the fear of delay. They were wed in London and were now hastening toward her uncle's estate. Elinor tried to imagine the scene: Edward, standing within four miles of Barton, hearing Lucy's message, seeing her mother's servant. The image was unbearable.

She pictured their new home at Delaford—a place that simultaneously fascinated and repelled her. In her mind's eye, she saw Lucy as the bustling architect of their domestic life, balancing a desire for smart appearances with ruthless frugality, eager to court the favor of Colonel Brandon, Mrs. Jennings, and every wealthy acquaintance. As for Edward, she saw nothing she wished to see. Whether he was happy or miserable, it mattered not; she turned her gaze away from every sketch of him.

Days passed without a letter. Elinor blamed the silence on the thoughtlessness or indolence of their London connections. Her mother, sensing her daughter's restlessness, asked why she hadn't written to Colonel Brandon. "I did last week," her mother replied, hoping to see him arrive soon. This offered a sliver of comfort; Brandon *must* have news.

Scarcely had Elinor resolved this when a figure on horseback appeared at the window. A gentleman stopped at the gate. Was it Brandon? No, the air and height were wrong. Could it be Edward? She looked again. He had dismounted. There was no mistake. It was Edward. She moved away, sitting down, steeling herself. "He comes from Mr. Pratt's to see us," she told herself. "I will be calm. I will be mistress of myself."

Moments later, she realized her mother and Marianne had seen the error. They exchanged anxious glances, their faces flushing with distress. Elinor wanted to speak, to assure them that no coldness would be shown, but her throat was closed. She was forced to leave their behavior to chance.

Silence filled the room as Edward's footsteps approached. When he entered, his countenance was pale with agitation, his eyes avoiding hers, as if he feared rejection. Mrs. Dashwood, striving to follow Elinor's unspoken lead, offered a forced smile, took his hand, and wished him joy. Edward stammered an unintelligible reply. Elinor's lips had moved in unison with her mother's, and afterward, she regretted not shaking his hand herself. Instead, she sat down and spoke of the weather, her voice a hollow shield against the storm inside.

Marianne hid herself in the shadows, while Margaret, confused by the partial truth, sat rigidly distant. Then, an awful pause descended upon them, broken only by Mrs. Dashwood's desperate attempt to inquire about Mrs. Ferrars. Edward answered hurriedly that she was well.

Elinor, summoning every ounce of courage, asked, "Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?"

"At Longstaple!" Edward exclaimed, surprised. "No, my mother is in town."

"I meant," Elinor said, picking up her work, her voice trembling slightly, "to enquire for Mrs. *Edward* Ferrars."

She dared not look up, but her mother and Marianne turned their eyes sharply to him. Edward flushed, looking perplexed and doubtful. After a hesitation, he said, "Perhaps you mean—my brother—you mean Mrs.—Mrs. *Robert* Ferrars."

"Mrs. Robert Ferrars!" the cry rang out from Marianne and her mother in tones of sheer amazement. Even Elinor, unable to speak, stared at him with impatient wonder. Rising abruptly, Edward walked to the window, fumbling with a pair of scissors, snipping the sheath to pieces as he spoke in a hurried rush.

"Perhaps you do not know... you may not have heard that my brother is lately married to—to the youngest—to Miss Lucy Steele."

The news echoed through the room with unspeakable astonishment, leaving everyone breathless except Elinor. She sat with her head bowed over her work, her heart hammering so violently she felt detached from her body.

"Yes," he continued, "they were married last week, and are now at Dawlish."

Elinor could endure it no longer. She rushed from the room. The moment the door closed behind her, she collapsed into tears of pure, overwhelming joy, a release she feared would never cease. Edward, who had been staring anywhere but at her, saw her flee. Perhaps he saw her emotion, or perhaps he heard it; for immediately after, he fell into a deep reverie, impervious to his mother's inquiries or affectionate words. Finally, without a word, he left the room and walked out toward the village, leaving the others in a state of bewildered confusion, trying to piece together the sudden and wonderful shift in their fortunes.
