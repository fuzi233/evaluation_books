Yes, reader, we must now settle our accounts. I have only briefly to narrate the final fates of the characters whose paths we have crossed in this narrative, and then you and I must shake hands and part for the present.

Let us turn first to the curates—to the much-loved, though long-neglected figures. Come forward, modest merit! Malone, I see, promptly answers the summons. He recognizes his own description immediately.

No, Peter Augustus; we have nothing to say to you. It simply won't do. It is impossible to entrust ourselves with the touching tale of your deeds and destinies. Are you not aware, Peter, that a discriminating public has its peculiar whims? That the unvarnished truth fails to satisfy? That plain facts refuse to digest? Do you not know that the squeak of the real pig is no more relished today than it was in days of yore? Were I to recount the catastrophe of your life and conversation, the public would swoon in hysterics, crying wildly for sal-volatile and burnt feathers. "Impossible!" would be shouted here; "Untrue!" responded there; "Inartistic!" would be solemnly decreed elsewhere. Note this well: whenever you present the actual, simple truth, it is somehow denounced as a lie—it is disowned, cast aside, and thrown onto the parish. Conversely, the product of your own imagination, the mere figment, the sheer fiction, is adopted, petted, and termed pretty, proper, and sweetly natural—the little, spurious wretch receives all the sweets, while the honest, lawful bantling receives all the cuffs. Such is the way of the world, Peter; and as you are the legitimate urchin—rude, unwashed, and naughty—you must stand down.

Make way for Mr. Sweeting.

Here he comes, with his lady on his arm—the most splendid and weighty woman in Yorkshire—Mrs. Sweeting, formerly Miss Dora Sykes. They were married under the happiest auspices; Mr. Sweeting had just been inducted into a comfortable living, and Mr. Sykes was in circumstances to provide Dora with a handsome portion. They lived long and happily together, beloved by their parishioners and a numerous circle of friends.

There! I think the varnish has been applied very nicely.

Advance, Mr. Donne.

This gentleman turned out admirably—far better than either you or I could possibly have expected, reader. He, too, married a most sensible, quiet, lady-like little woman. The match was the making of him. He became an exemplary domestic character and a truly active parish priest (though, to his dying day, he conscientiously refused to act as a pastor). He burnished the outside of the cup and platter with the finest polishing powder; he tended the furniture of the altar and temple with the zeal of an upholsterer and the care of a cabinet-maker. His little school, his little church, and his little parsonage all owed their erection to him, and they did him credit. Each was a model in its way. If uniformity and taste in architecture had been synonymous with consistency and earnestness in religion, what a shepherd of a Christian flock Mr. Donne would have made! There was one art in the mastery of which nothing mortal ever surpassed Mr. Donne: it was that of begging. By his own unassisted efforts, he secured all the funds for all his erections. In this matter, he possessed a grasp of plan and a scope of action quite unique. He begged of high and low—from the shoeless cottage child to the coroneted duke. He dispatched begging letters far and wide—to old Queen Charlotte, to the princesses her daughters, to her sons the royal dukes, to the Prince Regent, to Lord Castlereagh, and to every member of the ministry then in office. Remarkably, he extracted something from every one of these personages. It is on record that he obtained five pounds from the close-fisted old lady Queen Charlotte and two guineas from the royal profligate, her eldest son. When Mr. Donne set out on begging expeditions, he armed himself in a complete suit of brazen mail. Whether you had given a hundred pounds yesterday was no reason why you should not give two hundred today. He would tell you so to your face, and ten to one, he would get the money out of you. People gave to get rid of him. After all, he did some good with the cash. He was useful in his day and generation.

Perhaps I ought to remark that following the premature and sudden disappearance of Mr. Malone from the stage of Briarfield parish (you cannot know how it happened, reader; your curiosity must be sacrificed to your elegant love of the pretty and pleasing), there arrived as his successor another Irish curate, Mr. Macarthey. I am happy to inform you, *with truth*, that this gentleman did as much credit to his country as Malone had done it discredit. He proved himself as decent, decorous, and conscientious as Peter was rampant, boisterous, and... [I choose to suppress the last epithet, lest I let the cat out of the bag]. He labored faithfully in the parish. The schools, both Sunday and day schools, flourished under his sway like green bay trees. Being human, of course, he had his faults. However, these were proper, steady-going, clerical faults—what many would call virtues. The circumstance of finding himself invited to tea with a Dissenter would unhinge him for a week. The spectacle of a Quaker wearing his hat in church, or the thought of an unbaptized fellow-creature being interred with Christian rites, could wreak strange havoc on Mr. Macarthey's physical and mental economy. Otherwise, he was sane and rational, diligent and charitable.

I doubt not a justice-loving public has already remarked that I have thus far shown a criminal remissness in pursuing, catching, and bringing to condign punishment the would-be assassin of Mr. Robert Moore. Here was a fine opening to lead my willing readers in a dance, at once decorous and exciting—a dance of law and gospel, of the dungeon, the dock, and the "dead-thraw." You might have enjoyed it, reader, but *I* should not. I and my subject would presently have quarreled, and then I should have broken down. I was happy to discover that facts perfectly exonerated me from the attempt. The murderer was never punished, for the good reason that he was never caught—the result of the further circumstance that he was never pursued. The magistrates made a shuffling movement, as if intending to rise and perform valiant deeds; but since Moore himself, instead of urging and leading them as heretofore, lay still on his little cottage-couch, laughing in his sleeve and sneering with every feature of his pale, foreign face, they reconsidered and, after fulfilling certain indispensable formalities, prudently resolved to let the matter quietly drop, which they did.

Mr. Moore knew who had shot him, and all Briarfield knew. It was no other than Michael Hartley, the half-crazed weaver once before alluded to, a frantic Antinomian in religion and a mad leveller in politics. The poor soul died of delirium tremens a year after the attempt on Moore, and Robert gave his wretched widow a guinea to bury him.

The winter is over and gone; spring has followed with beamy and shadowy, flowery and showery flight. We are now in the heart of summer—in mid-June—the June of 1812.

It is burning weather. The air is deep azure and red gold. It fits the time; it fits the age; it fits the present spirit of the nations. The nineteenth century wanders in its giant adolescence; the Titan boy uproots mountains in his play and hurls rocks in his wild sport. This summer, Bonaparte is in the saddle; he and his host scour the Russian deserts. With him are Frenchmen, Poles, Italians, and children of the Rhine, numbering six hundred thousand strong. He marches on old Moscow. Under the walls of old Moscow, the rude Cossack awaits him. Barbarian stoic! He waits without fear of the boundless ruin rolling on. He puts his trust in a snow-cloud; the wilderness, the wind, and the hailstorm are his refuge; his allies are the elements—air, fire, water. And what are these? Three terrible archangels ever stationed before the throne of Jehovah. They stand clothed in white, girdled with golden girdles; they uplift vials brimming with the wrath of God. Their time is the day of vengeance; their signal is the word of the Lord of Hosts, "thundering with the voice of His excellency."

"Hast thou entered into the treasures of the snow? Or hast thou seen the treasures of the hail, which I have reserved against the time of trouble, against the day of battle and war? Go your ways. Pour out the vials of the wrath of God upon the earth."

It is done. The earth is scorched with fire; the sea becomes "as the blood of a dead man"; the islands flee away; the mountains are not found.

In this year, Lord Wellington assumed the reins in Spain. They made him generalissimo for their own salvation's sake. In this year he took Badajos, fought the field of Vittoria, captured Pampeluna, stormed San Sebastian; in this year he won Salamanca.
