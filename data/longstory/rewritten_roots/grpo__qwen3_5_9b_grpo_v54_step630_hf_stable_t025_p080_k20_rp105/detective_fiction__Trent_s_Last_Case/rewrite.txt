How does the world distinguish between what truly matters and what merely seems to? When the calculating, indomitable mind of Sigsbee Manderson was shattered by a bullet from an unseen hand, the world lost nothing of consequence. It gained only a stark reminder of the hollowness of the fortune this dead man had amassed—a pile of gold that secured him no loyal friend to mourn him and no deed to honor his memory. Yet, to those swirling within the great vortices of commerce, the news of his end felt as though the very earth had shuddered under a blow.

In the lurid commercial history of his nation, no figure had ever so dominated the collective consciousness of the trading world. He occupied a niche apart in its temples. While financial giants had existed before, directing the forces of capital and charging millions for their labor, Manderson possessed a singularity: a pale halo of piratical romance clung incongruously to his head. Through the years, he stood in every eye as the unquestioned guardian of stability, the extinguisher of manipulated crises, and the foe of the raiding chieftains who infested the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune, inherited from a grandfather who had been a minor buccaneer of his day, had grown through his father, who spent a long life quietly lending money without ever risking a margin. Manderson, who never knew the sensation of lacking large sums, should have belonged entirely to that newer American plutocracy steadied by the traditions of great wealth. It was not so. His upbringing and education instilled in him European notions of a rich man's proper exterior—the quiet magnificence that does not shout for attention. Yet, handed down to him was much of the Forty-Niner spirit and the financial daring of his forbear. During his early "bad manner" period, he was little more than a genius gambler, a prodigy whose hand was against every man's, bringing to the intoxicating pursuit of speculation a brain superior to any opposition. At St. Helena, war was deemed *une belle occupation*; thus, the young Manderson found his match in the multitudinous, complicated dogfight of the New York Stock Exchange.

Then came his transformation. Upon his father's death, when Manderson was thirty, a new revelation of the power and glory of the god he served seemed to descend upon him. With the sudden, elastic adaptability of his nation, he turned to steady labor in his father's banking business, closing his ears to the sound of the Street's battles. In a few years, he controlled the activity of the great firm whose unimpeached conservatism and financial weight lifted it like a cliff above the angry sea of the markets. All mistrust founded on his youthful performances vanished. He was quite plainly a different man. How the change occurred, no one could say with authority, save for a story of certain last words spoken by his father, the only man Manderson had truly respected and perhaps loved.

He began to tower above the financial situation. Soon, his name was current in the bourses of the world. To speak Manderson's name was to conjure a vision of all that was broad-based and firm in the vast wealth of the United States. He planned great combinations of capital, centralized industries of continental scope, and financed the large designs of state or private enterprise with unerring judgment. Many times, when he "took hold" to smash a strike or federate the ownership of great fields of labor, he sent ruin upon a multitude of tiny homes. If miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and invoked disorder, he could be more lawless and ruthless than they. Yet, this was done in the pursuit of legitimate business ends. Tens of thousands of the poor might curse his name, but the financier and the speculator execrated him no more. He stretched a hand to protect or manipulate the power of wealth in every corner of the country. Forcible, cold, and unerring, in all he did he ministered to the national lust for magnitude; and a grateful country surnamed him the Colossus.

There was, however, an aspect of Manderson in this later period that lay long unknown and unsuspected save by a few: his secretaries, lieutenants, and certain associates of his bygone hurling time. This small circle knew that Manderson, the pillar of sound business and stability, had hours of nostalgia for the lively times when the Street trembled at his name. It was, as one of them said, as if Blackbeard had settled down as a decent merchant in Bristol on the spoils of the Main. Now and then, the pirate would glare suddenly out, the knife in his teeth and the sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband. During such spasms of reversion to type, a score of tempestuous raids upon the market were planned on paper in the inner room of the offices of Manderson, Colefax and Company. But they were never carried out. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old self within him and go soberly down to his counting-house—perhaps humming a stave or two of "Spanish Ladies" under his breath. Manderson would allow himself the harmless satisfaction, once the time for action had passed, of pointing out to some Rupert of the markets a coup worth a million to the depredator might have been made. "Seems to me," he would say almost wistfully, "the Street is getting to be a mighty dull place since I quit." By slow degrees, this amiable weakness of the Colossus became known to the business world, which exulted greatly in the knowledge.

At the news of his death, panic swept through the markets like a hurricane, for it came at a luckless time. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two days, Wall Street was a clamorous inferno of pale despair. All over the United States, wherever speculation had its devotees, went a waft of ruin, a plague of suicide. In Europe also, not a few took with their own hands lives that had become pitiably linked to the destiny of a financier whom most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked quietly out of the Bourse and fell dead upon the broad steps among the raving crowd of Jews, a phial crushed in his hand. In Frankfort, one leapt from the Cathedral top, leaving a redder stain where he struck the red tower. Men stabbed, shot, and strangled themselves, drank death, or breathed it as the air, because in a lonely corner of England, the life had departed from one cold heart vowed to the service of greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous moment. It came when Wall Street was in a condition of suppressed "scare"—suppressed, because for a week past the great interests known to act with or be actually controlled by the Colossus had been desperately combating the effects of the sudden arrest of Lucas Hahn and the exposure of his plundering of the Hahn banks. This bombshell, in its turn, had fallen at a time when the market had been "boosted" beyond its real strength. In the language of the place, a slump was due. Reports from the corn-lands were not good, and there had been two or three railway statements expected to be much better than they were. But at whatever point in the vast area of speculation the shudder of the threatened break had been felt, "the Manderson crowd" had stepped in and held the market up. All through the week, the speculator's mind, as shallow as it is quick-witted, as sentimental as greedy, had seen in this the hand of the giant stretched out in protection from afar. Manderson, said the newspapers in chorus, was in hourly communication with his lieutenants in the Street. One journal was able to give in round figures the sum spent on cabling between New York and Marlstone in the past twenty-four hours; it told how a small staff of expert operators had been sent down by the Post Office authorities to Marlstone to deal with the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson, on the first news of the Hahn crash, had arranged to abandon his holiday and return home by the *Lusitania*, but that he soon had the situation so well in hand that he had determined to remain where he was.

All this was falsehood, more or less consciously elaborated by the "finance editors," consciously initiated and encouraged by the shrewd businessmen of the Manderson group, who knew that nothing could better help their plans than this illusion of hero-worship—they knew also that no word had come from Manderson in answer to their messages, and that Howard B. Jeffrey, of Steel and Iron fame, was the true organizer of victory. So they fought down apprehension through four feverish days, and minds grew calmer. On Saturday, though the ground beneath the feet of Mr. Jeffrey yet rumbled now and then with Etna-mutterings of disquiet, he deemed his task almost done. The market was firm, and slowly advancing. Wall Street turned to its sleep of Sunday, worn out but thankfully at peace.

In the first trading hour of Monday, a hideous rumor flew round the sixty acres of the financial district. It came into being as lightning comes—a blink that seems to begin nowhere; though it is to be suspected that it was first whispered over the telephone, together with an urgent selling order by some employee in the cable service. A sharp spasm convulsed the convalescent share-list. In five minutes, the dull noise of the kerbstone market in Broad Street had leapt to a high note of frantic interrogation. From within the hive of the Exchange itself could be heard a droning hubbub of fear, and men rushed hatless in and out. Was it true? asked every man; and every man replied, with trembling lips, that it was a lie put out by some unscrupulous "short" interest seeking to cover itself. In another quarter of an hour, news came of a sudden and ruinous collapse of "Yankees" in London at the close of the Stock Exchange day. It was enough. New York had still four hours' trading in front of her. The strategy of pointing to Manderson as the savior and warden of the markets had recoiled upon its authors with annihilating force, and Jeffrey, his ear at his private telephone, listened to the tale of disaster with a set jaw. The new Napoleon had lost his Marengo. He saw the whole financial landscape sliding and falling into chaos before him. In half an hour, the news of the finding of Manderson's body, with the inevitable rumor that it was suicide, was printing in a dozen newspaper offices; but before a copy reached Wall Street, the tornado of the panic was in full fury, and Howard B. Jeffrey and his collaborators were whirled away like leaves before its breath.

All this sprang out of nothing.

Nothing in the texture of the general life had changed. The corn had not ceased to ripen in the sun. The rivers bore their barges and gave power to a myriad engines. The flocks fattened on the pastures, the herds were unnumbered. Men labored everywhere in the various servitudes to which they were born, and chafed not more than usual in their bonds. Bellona tossed and murmured as ever, yet still slept her uneasy sleep. To all mankind save a million or two of half-crazed gamblers, blind to all reality, the death of Manderson meant nothing; the life and work of the world went on. Weeks before he died, strong hands had been in control of every wire in the huge network of commerce and industry that he had supervised. Before his corpse was buried, his countrymen had made a strange discovery—that the existence of the potent engine of monopoly that went by the name of Sigsbee Manderson had not been a condition of even material prosperity. The panic blew itself out in two days, the pieces were picked up, the bankrupts withdrew out of sight; the market "recovered a normal tone".

While the brief delirium was yet subsiding, there broke out a domestic scandal in England that suddenly fixed the attention of two continents. Next morning, the Chicago Limited was wrecked, and the same day a notable politician was shot down in cold blood by his wife's brother in the streets of New Orleans. Within a week of its rising, "the Manderson story," to the trained sense of editors throughout the Union, was "cold". The tide of American visitors pouring through Europe made eddies round the memorial or statue of many a man who had died in poverty; and never thought of their most famous plutocrat. Like the poet who died in Rome, so young and poor, a hundred years ago, he was buried far away from his own land; but for all the men and women of Manderson's people who flock round the tomb of Keats in the cemetery under the Monte Testaccio, there is not one, nor ever will be, to stand in reverence by the rich man's grave beside the little church of Marlstone.

Knocking the Town Endways

The telephone on Sir James Molloy's desk buzzed, a sharp, mechanical intrusion into the quiet of the *Record*'s offices. Sir James, a tall, swart Irishman of fifty with a black moustache and eyes that held the weight of a man who understood the world too well, made a dismissive gesture with his pen. His secretary, Mr. Silver, abandoned his work and moved swiftly to the instrument.

"Who is that?" Silver asked, his voice tight. "Who?... I can't hear you.... Oh, it's Mr. Bunner, is it?... Yes, but... I know, but he's fearfully busy this afternoon. Can't you... Oh, really? Well, in that case—just hold on, will you?"

He placed the receiver before Sir James. "It's Calvin Bunner, Sigsbee Manderson's right-hand man," he reported, his tone grave. "He insists on speaking to you personally. Says it is the gravest piece of news. He is talking from the house down by Bishopsbridge, so it will be necessary to speak clearly."

Sir James looked at the phone with a mixture of weariness and anticipation, then took the receiver. "Well?" he commanded in his strong, resonant voice. He listened. "Yes," he said.

Moments later, Mr. Silver watched in silence as a look of sheer amazement, bordering on horror, crossed Sir James's face. "Good God!" Sir James murmured. Clutching the instrument, he rose slowly, his ear bent intently to the speaker. At intervals, he repeated a single, sharp word: "Yes." Glancing at the clock, he spoke quickly over the transmitter. "Go and hunt up Figgis and young Williams. Hurry." Mr. Silver darted from the room.

Sir James was a man of untiring business energy, a journalist who played upon the world with a half-cynical competence yet possessed no touch of the charlatan. He saw instantly through pretense in others. In his handsome, well-bred appearance, there was something slightly sinister when anger or intense occupation tightened the lines around his eyes, but beneath that was a generous nature. As managing director and editor-in-chief of the *Record*, and owner of the evening *Sun* across the street, he commanded respect through solid merit rather than empty gifts.

"You're sure that's all?" Sir James asked after a few minutes of earnest listening. "And how long has this been known?... Yes, of course, the police are; but the servants? Surely it's all over the place down there by now.... Well, we'll have a try.... Look here, Bunner, I'm infinitely obliged to you about this. I owe you a good turn. You know I mean what I say. Come and see me the first day you get to town.... All right, that's understood. Now I must act on your news. Goodbye."

He hung up the receiver and seized a railway timetable from the rack. After a rapid consultation of this oracle, he flung it down with a forcible word as Mr. Silver hurried back into the room, followed by a hard-featured man with spectacles, Figgis, and a youth with an alert eye, Williams.

"I want you to jot down some facts, Figgis," Sir James said, banishing all signs of agitation to speak with rapid calmness. "When you have them, put them into shape just as quick as you can for a special edition of the *Sun*." Figgis nodded, glanced at the clock pointing to a few minutes past three, and pulled out his notebook. "Silver," Sir James continued, "go and tell Jones to wire our local correspondent very urgently, to drop everything and get down to Marlstone at once. He is not to say why in the telegram. There must not be an unnecessary word about this news until the *Sun* is on the streets with it—you all understand. Williams, cut across the way and tell Mr. Anthony to hold himself ready for a two-column opening that will knock the town endways. Just tell him that he must take all measures and precautions for a scoop. Say that Figgis will be over in five minutes with the facts, and that he had better let him write up the story in his private room. As you go, ask Miss Morgan to see me here at once, and tell the telephone people to see if they can get Mr. Trent on the wire for me. After seeing Mr. Anthony, return here and stand by." The alert-eyed young man vanished like a spirit.

Sir James turned instantly to Mr. Figgis. "Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered," he began quickly and clearly, pacing the floor with his hands behind him. Mr. Figgis scratched down a line of shorthand with as much emotion as if he had been told the day was fine—the pose of his craft. "He and his wife and two secretaries have been for the past fortnight at the house called White Gables, at Marlstone, near Bishopsbridge. He bought it four years ago. He and Mrs. Manderson have since spent a part of each summer there. Last night he went to bed about half-past eleven, just as usual. No one knows when he got up and left the house. He was not missed until this morning. About ten o'clock his body was found by a gardener. It was lying by a shed in the grounds. He was shot in the head, through the left eye. Death must have been instantaneous. The body was not robbed, but there were marks on the wrists which pointed to a struggle having taken place. Dr Stock, of Marlstone, was at once sent for, and will conduct the post-mortem examination. The police from Bishopsbridge, who were soon on the spot, are reticent, but it is believed that they are quite without a clue to the identity of the murderer. There you are, Figgis. Mr. Anthony is expecting you. Now I must telephone him and arrange things."

Mr. Figgis looked up. "One of the ablest detectives at Scotland Yard," he suggested, "has been put in charge of the case. It's a safe statement."

"If you like," said Sir James.

"And Mrs. Manderson? Was she there?"

"Yes. What about her?"

"Prostrated by the shock," hinted the reporter, "and sees nobody. Human interest."

"I wouldn't put that in, Mr. Figgis," said a quiet voice. It belonged to Miss Morgan, a pale, graceful woman who had silently appeared while the dictation was going on. "I have seen Mrs. Manderson," she proceeded, turning to Sir James. "She looks quite healthy and intelligent. Has her husband been murdered? I don't think the shock would prostrate her. She is more likely to be doing all she can to help the police."

"Something in your own style, then, Miss Morgan," he said with a momentary smile. Her imperturbable efficiency was an office proverb. "Cut it out, Figgis. Off you go! Now, madam, I expect you know what I want."

"Our Manderson biography happens to be well up to date," replied Miss Morgan, drooping her dark eyelashes as she considered the position. "I was looking over it only a few months ago. It is practically ready for tomorrow's paper. I should think the *Sun* had better use the sketch of his life they had about two years ago, when he went to Berlin and settled the potash difficulty. I remember it was a very good sketch, and they won't be able to carry much more than that. As for our paper, of course we have a great quantity of cuttings, mostly rubbish. The sub-editors shall have them as soon as they come in. Then we have two very good portraits that are our own property; the best is a drawing Mr. Trent made when they were both on the same ship somewhere. It is better than any of the photographs; but you say the public prefers a bad photograph to a good drawing. I will send them down to you at once, and you can choose. As far as I can see, the *Record* is well ahead of the situation, except that you will not be able to get a special man down there in time to be of any use for tomorrow's paper."

Sir James sighed deeply. "What are we good for, anyhow?" he enquired dejectedly of Mr. Silver, who had returned to his desk. "She even knows Bradshaw by heart."

Miss Morgan adjusted her cuffs with an air of patience. "Is there anything else?" she asked, as the telephone bell rang.

"Yes, one thing," replied Sir James, as he took up the receiver. "I want you to make a bad mistake some time, Miss Morgan—an everlasting bloomer—just to put us in countenance." She permitted herself the fraction of what would have been a charming smile as she went out.

"Anthony?" asked Sir James, and was at once deep in consultation with the editor on the other side of the road. He seldom entered the *Sun* building in person; the atmosphere of an evening paper, he would say, was all very well if you liked that kind of thing. Mr. Anthony, the Murat of Fleet Street, who delighted in riding the whirlwind and fighting a tumultuous battle against time, would say the same of a morning paper.

It was some five minutes later that a uniformed boy came in to say that Mr. Trent was on the wire. Sir James abruptly closed his talk with Mr. Anthony.

"They can put him through at once," he said to the boy.

"Hullo!" he cried into the telephone after a few moments.

A voice in the instrument replied, "Hullo be blowed! What do you want?"

"This is Molloy," said Sir James.

"I know it is," the voice said. "This is Trent. He is in the middle of painting a picture, and he has been interrupted at a critical moment. Well, I hope it's something important, that's all!"

"Trent," said Sir James impressively, "it is important. I want you to do some work for us."

"Some play, you mean," replied the voice. "Believe me, I don't want a holiday. The working fit is very strong. I am doing some really decent things. Why can't you leave a man alone?"

"Something very serious has happened."

"What?"

"Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered—shot through the brain—and they don't know who has done it. They found the body this morning. It happened at his place near Bishopsbridge." Sir James proceeded to tell his hearer, briefly and clearly, the facts that he had communicated to Mr. Figgis. "What do you think of it?" he ended. A considering grunt was the only answer. "Come now," urged Sir James.

"Tempter!"

"You will go down?"

There was a brief pause.

"Are you there?" said Sir James.

"Look here, Molloy," the voice broke out querulously, "the thing may be a case for me, or it may not. We can't possibly tell. It may be a mystery; it may be as simple as bread and cheese. The body not being robbed looks interesting, but he may have been outed by some wretched tramp whom he found sleeping in the grounds and tried to kick out. It's the sort of thing he would do. Such a murderer might easily have sense enough to know that to leave the money and valuables was the safest thing. I tell you frankly, I wouldn't have a hand in hanging a poor devil who had let daylight into a man like Sig Manderson as a measure of social protest."

Sir James smiled at the telephone—a smile of success. "Come, my boy, you're getting feeble. Admit you want to go and have a look at the case. You know you do. If it's anything you don't want to handle, you're free to drop it. By the by, where are you?"

"I am blown along a wandering wind," replied the voice irresolutely, "and hollow, hollow, hollow all delight."

"Can you get here within an hour?" persisted Sir James.

"I suppose I can," the voice grumbled. "How much time have I?"

"Good man! Well, there's time enough—that's just the worst of it. I've got to depend on our local correspondent for tonight. The only good train of the day went half an hour ago. The next is a slow one, leaving Paddington at midnight. You could have the Buster, if you like"—Sir James referred to a very fast motor car of his—"but you wouldn't get down in time to do anything tonight."

"And I'd miss my sleep. No, thanks. The train for me. I am quite fond of railway travelling, you know; I have a gift for it. I am the stoker and the stoked. I am the song the porter sings."

"What's that you say?"

"It doesn't matter," said the voice sadly. "I say," it continued, "will your people look out a hotel near the scene of action, and telegraph for a room?"

"At once," said Sir James. "Come here as soon as you can."

He replaced the receiver. As he turned to his papers again a shrill outcry burst forth in the street below. He walked to the open window. A band of excited boys was rushing down the steps of the *Sun* building and up the narrow thoroughfare toward Fleet Street. The morning sun cast a pale, watery light over the veranda of the Marlstone Hotel, illuminating the dust motes dancing above the empty plates of the previous night. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood there, his gaze fixed not on the horizon, but on the plate before him, contemplating the mechanics of sustenance. To Cupples, the concept of "breakfast" was not merely a culinary event but a necessary biological imperative, a conscious act requiring the same deliberation as any other function of his orderly existence. He calculated the deficit of the prior day, where the shock of the discovery had left him starved, and resolved with mathematical precision to consume a third slice of toast and an extra egg this morning. The remainder of the caloric debt would be addressed at luncheon; for now, the immediate task was to survey the landscape.

With the detached appreciation of a connoisseur, Cupples scanned the rugged coastline. A massive, perforated rock jutted from the glassy sea, standing as a silent sentinel against the vast, tilted plains of pasture and woodland that sloped upward toward the distant, hazy moors. Cupples possessed a deep, almost religious devotion to such scenery. He was a man of middle height and spare frame, nearing sixty, with a constitution that was delicate yet wiry. A sparse, straggling beard framed a thin but kindly mouth, while his keen, pleasant eyes and sharp nose lent him an air of clerical severity, reinforced by his dark, commonplace attire and soft black hat. The overall effect was undeniably priestly. His mind was a fortress of conscientious industry, devoid of imagination, shaped by a childhood spent in the gloomy domestic establishment of his father's household—a home famously recruited via advertisements describing it as a "serious family." From that fortress, Cupples had escaped with two rare gifts: an inexhaustible kindness of heart and a capacity for innocent gaiety that required no humor to fuel it. Once, with his clerical training, he might have worn the scarlet hat; instead, he found his true vocation among books and museums, a respected member of the London Positivist Society and a retired banker. His life was austere but not unhappy, populated by the quiet company of professors and curators, where he felt most at home. His favorite author was Montaigne.

As Cupples was concluding his solitary meal at a small table on the veranda, the rhythmic rumble of a heavy motor car broke the morning stillness. It turned onto the hotel drive, kicking up a cloud of dust.

"Who is this?" Cupples asked the waiter, who was wiping a tray.

"The manager," the young man replied listlessly, his eyes fixed on the floor. "He's been to meet a gentleman by the train."

The car halted, and the porter hurried forward. Cupples let out a sound of genuine delight as a tall, loosely built man emerged from the vehicle and ascended the steps, tossing his hat onto a nearby chair. The newcomer was significantly younger, his face high-boned and quixotic, wearing a pleasant, slightly manic smile. His rough tweed suit, untidy hair, and short moustache suggested a lack of concern for convention.

"Cupples, by all that's miraculous!" the man cried, lunging forward before Cupples could rise. He seized Cupples' hand in a grip that was surprisingly hard. "My luck is serving me today," the newcomer continued, his speech spastic with excitement. "This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou its former pride recall, or ponder how it passed away? I am glad to see you!"

"I was half expecting you, Trent," Cupples replied, his face lighting up with warm smiles. "You are looking splendid, my dear fellow. I will tell you all about it. But you cannot have had your own breakfast yet. Will you join me at my table?"

"Rather!" Trent exclaimed. "An enormous great breakfast, too—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I wash? I shan't be three minutes."

Trent vanished into the hotel. Cupples, after a brief pause, retreated to the porter's office to make a telephone call, returning moments later to find his friend already seated, pouring tea with an unaffected interest in the menu.

"I expect this to be a hard day for me," Trent said, his voice exhibiting the curious, jerky cadence that seemed habitual to him. "I shan't eat again till the evening, very likely. You guess why I'm here, don't you?"

"Undoubtedly," Cupples replied. "You have come down to write about the murder."

"That is rather a colourless way of stating it," Trent corrected, dissecting a piece of sole with surgical precision. "I should prefer to say I have come down in the character of avenger of blood, to hunt down the guilty and vindicate the honour of society. That is my line of business. Families wait on at their private residences. I say, Cupples, I have made a good beginning already. Wait a bit, and I'll tell you."

A silence fell between them as Trent ate swiftly and abstractedly, while Cupples watched him with happy anticipation.

"Your manager here," Trent finally said, "is a fellow of remarkable judgment. He is an admirer of mine. He knows more about my best cases than I do myself. The *Record* wired last night to say I was coming, and when I stepped off the train at seven this morning, there he was waiting with a motor car the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at having me here. It is fame." He took a sip of tea. "Almost his first words were to ask if I would like to see the body of the murdered man—he thought he could manage it for me. He is as keen as a razor. The body lies in Dr. Stock's surgery, down in the village, exactly as it was found. It's to be post-mortemmed this morning, so I was only just in time. Well, he ran me down to the doctor's, giving me full particulars all the way. I was pretty well *au fait* by the time we arrived. I suppose the manager of a place like this has some pull with the doctor. Anyhow, he made no difficulties, nor did the constable on duty, though he was careful to insist on my not giving him away in the paper."

"I saw the body before it was removed," Cupples remarked. "I should not have said there was anything remarkable about it, except that the shot in the eye had scarcely disfigured the face and caused little effusion of blood. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I expect that, with your trained faculties, you were able to remark other details of a suggestive nature."

"Other details, certainly; but I don't know that they suggest anything. They are merely odd. Take the wrists, for instance. How was it you could see bruises and scratches on them? I dare say you saw something of Manderson down here before the murder."

"Certainly," Cupples said.

"Well, did you ever see his wrists?"

Cupples reflected. "No. Now you raise the point, I am reminded that when I interviewed Manderson here he was wearing stiff cuffs, coming well down over his hands."

"He always did," Trent said. "My friend the manager says so. I pointed out to him the fact you didn't observe, that there were no cuffs visible, and that they had, indeed, been dragged up inside the coat-sleeves, as yours would be if you hurried into a coat without pulling your cuffs down. That was why you saw his wrists."

"Well, I call that suggestive," Cupples observed mildly. "You might infer, perhaps, that when he got up he hurried over his dressing."

"Yes, but did he? The manager said just what you say. 'He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,' he told me, and he drew the inference that when Manderson got up in that mysterious way, before the house was stirring, and went out into the grounds, he was in a great hurry. 'Look at his shoes,' he said to me: 'Mr. Manderson was always specially neat about his footwear. But those shoe-laces were tied in a hurry.' I agreed. 'And he left his false teeth in his room,' said the manager. 'Doesn't that prove he was flustered and hurried?' I allowed that it looked like it. But I said, 'Look here: if he was so very much pressed, why did he part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why did he put on so much? for he had on a complete outfit of underclothing, studs in his shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money and keys and things in his pockets. That's what I said to the manager. He couldn't find an explanation. Can you?"

Philip Trent, a painter and the son of a painter, had secured a reputation within the English art world before reaching his twenties. His works sold, driven by a raw, forcible talent and a working rhythm of leisurely diligence punctuated by bursts of intense creative fervor. While his father's name and a substantial patrimony removed the stigma of struggle, Trent's true engine of success was an unconscious magnetism; a genial spirit and a lively, humorous fancy that commanded genuine affection rather than mere popularity. Even when applying himself vigorously to a task, his face retained an expression of contained vivacity. At thirty-two, he had not yet passed the age of laughter and adventure.

His ascent to a celebrity a hundred times greater than his artistic merit stemmed from a momentary impulse. One day, reading a newspaper dominated by a rare crime—a murder aboard a railway train—Trent, newly fascinated by such affairs, delved into the accounts. The Colossus observed him for some time before offering him the position of private secretary. Bunner was the epitome of corporate efficiency: trustworthy, far-sighted, methodical, and precise. While Manderson could have secured many men with such virtues, he chose Bunner specifically for his speed, discretion, and a singular, almost supernatural instinct for the movements of the stock market.

The sea roared against the base of the cliff, driven by a brisk wind, while the sun poured a dappled, life-giving light across the landscape. Trent, having slept poorly, descended before eight to a secluded pool among the rocks. Following directions he had received, he plunged into the clear water, swimming out between massive grey boulders into the churning surf. He fought briefly against the coastal current before returning to his rocky refuge, battered but revitalized. Ten minutes later, he was scaling the cliff once more. His mind, momentarily cleared of the heavy disgust he felt for the case, began to organize his plans for the day.

It was the day of the inquest, the day after his arrival. After his encounter with the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, he had made little progress. In the afternoon, accompanied by Mr. Cupples, he had walked into town to purchase chemicals, confer privately with a photographer, send a reply-paid telegram, and make inquiries at the telephone exchange. He had spoken little of the case to Cupples, who seemed incurious, revealing nothing of his investigation's results or his intended next steps. After returning from Bishopsbridge, Trent had drafted a lengthy dispatch for the *Record*, sending it via the newspaper's local representative. He had dined with Cupples and spent the remainder of the evening in meditative solitude on the veranda.

As he climbed the cliff that morning, Trent reflected that he had never undertaken a case he disliked so little, yet one that consumed him so completely. The more he contemplated it in the golden sunlight, the more malevolent and challenging it appeared. Hours of sleepless contemplation had filled his mind with suspicion and near-knowledge. Even washed in the fierce purity of the sea, the glorious light only sharpened his vision of the darkness of the guilt he believed existed, repelling him bitterly from the motive he suspected. Yet, his zeal had awakened; the hunt quickened. He would slacken for nothing. By day's end, he hoped, his net would be complete. He had work to do in the morning and awaited the answer to the telegram he had dispatched the previous day with vivid expectancy, though little serious hope.

The path back to the hotel wound along the cliff's edge. Near a spot he had marked from the sea level, where the face had long since crumbled away, he approached the precipice. He looked down, hoping to witness the delicate dance of water washing over broken rock. But there was no rock below. Instead, a broad ledge stood out—a rough platform the size of a large room, thick with wiry grass and walled in steeply on three sides. Close to the sheer drop, a woman sat with her arms around her drawn-up knees, her eyes fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, her face lost in a dream.

To Trent, whose training demanded he live through his eyes, she was the most beautiful picture he had ever seen. Her face possessed a southern pallor, touched by the wind's kiss with color on her cheeks, presenting a profile of delicate regularity devoid of hardness. Yet, her black brows bent downward toward a point of near-meeting, giving her a look of severity in repose, strangely redeemed by the open curve of her mouth. Trent mused that the absurdity of a lover writing sonnets to a mistress's eyebrow depended entirely on the quality of that eyebrow. Her nose was straight and fine, exquisitely avoiding the perdition of excessive length. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, while the lively breeze played with her thick dark hair, blowing backward the two broad bandeaux that should have covered her forehead and agitating a hundred tiny curls from the mass gathered at her nape. Everything about this lady was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat; lustreless black covered her to her bare throat. All she wore was fine and impeccably arranged. Dreamy and delicate as her looks suggested, it was plain she was long-practiced in the oldest of arts: dressing well, possessing a primal joy in the excellence of a body admirably curved in her attitude of embraced knees. With a suggestion of French taste in her attire, she made a very modern figure seated there, until one looked at her face and saw the glow and triumph of all vigorous beings facing sun, wind, and sea together in the prime of the year. One saw a womanhood so unmixed and vigorous, so unconsciously sure of itself, as scarcely to be English, still less American.

Trent, who had halted only for a moment in surprise at seeing the woman in black, passed by on the cliff above her, perceiving and feeling the details set down. At all times, his keen vision and active brain took in and tasted details with a swiftness marvelous to men of slower chemistry; he held that the need to stare was evidence of blindness. Now, the feeling of beauty was awakened and exultant, doubling the power of his senses. In these instants, a picture was printed on his memory that would never fade.

As he went by unheard on the turf, the woman, still alone with her thoughts, suddenly moved. She unclasped her long hands from her knees, stretching her limbs and body with feline grace, then slowly raised her head and extended her arms with open, curving fingers, as if to gather to her all the glory and overwhelming sanity of the morning. This was a gesture not to be mistaken: a gesture of freedom, the movement of a soul's resolution to be, to possess, to go forward, perhaps to enjoy.

So he saw her for an instant as he passed, and he did not turn. He knew suddenly who the woman must be, and it was as if a curtain of gloom were drawn between him and the splendour of the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent little inclined to talk, excusing himself on the plea of a restless night. Cupples, conversely, was in a state of bird-like alertness. The prospect of the inquest seemed to enliven him. He entertained Trent with a disquisition on the history of the coroner's court, remarking on its enviable freedom from the shackles of rule and precedent. From this, he passed to the case awaiting the tribunal that morning.

"Young Bunner mentioned to me last night," he said, "the hypothesis he puts forward regarding the crime. A very remarkable young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my opinion, he possesses a clear-headed knowledge of the world quite unusual for one of his apparent age. He seems to have assumed with perfect confidence the control, as he expresses it, of the complicated business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised very wisely regarding the steps I should take on Mabel's behalf and the best course for her to pursue until the will is executed. I was accordingly less disposed than I might otherwise have been to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. This is a terrible time in which we live, my dear boy. There is none recorded in history, I think, in which the disproportion between the material and the moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the fabric. "I thought," said Trent listlessly, "that Puritanism was about as strong there as the money-getting craze."

"Your remark," answered Mr. Cupples, with as near an approach to humor as was possible for him, "is not in the nature of a testimonial to what you call Puritanism—a convenient rather than an accurate term; for I need not remind you that it was invented to describe an Anglican party which aimed at purging the services and ritual of their Church from certain elements repugnant to them. The sense of your observation, however, is none the less sound, and its truth is extremely well illustrated by the case of Manderson himself, who had, I believe, the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest form. No, Trent, there are other and more worthy things among the moral constituents of which I spoke; and in our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigor have we left for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us. Agricultural machinery has abolished the festival of the Harvest Home. Mechanical travel has abolished the inn, or all that was best in it. I need not multiply instances. The view I am expressing to you," pursued Mr. Cupples, placidly buttering a piece of toast, "is regarded as fundamentally erroneous by many of those who think generally as I do about the deeper concerns of life, but I am nevertheless firmly persuaded of its truth."

"It needs epigrammatic expression," said Trent, rising from the table. "If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula, like 'No Popery', or 'Tax the Foreigner', you would find multitudes to go to the stake for it. But you were planning to go to White Gables before the inquest, I think. You ought to be off if you are to get back to the court in time. I have something to attend to there myself, so we might walk up together. I will just go and get my camera."

"By all means," Mr. Cupples answered, and they set off at once in the ever-growing warmth of the morning. The roof of White Gables, a surly patch of dull red against the dark trees, seemed to harmonize with Trent's mood; he felt heavy, sinister, and troubled. If a blow must fall that might strike down that creature radiant of beauty and life whom he had seen that morning, he did not wish it to come from his hand. An exaggerated chivalry had lived in Trent since the first teachings of his mother; but at this moment, the horror of bruising anything so lovely was almost as much the artist's revulsion as the gentleman's. On the other hand, was the hunt to end in nothing? The quality of the affair was such that the thought of forbearance was an agony. There never was such a case; and he alone, he was confident, held the truth of it under his hand. At least, he determined, that day should show whether what he believed was a delusion. He would trample his compunction underfoot until he was quite sure there was any call for it. That same morning he would know.

As they entered the gate of the drive, they saw Marlowe and the American standing in talk before the front door. In the shadow of the porch was the lady in black.

She saw them and came gravely forward over the lawn, moving as Trent had known she would move, erect and balanced, stepping lightly. When she welcomed him on Mr. Cupples's presentation, her eyes of golden-flecked brown observed him kindly. In her pale composure, worn as the mask of distress, there was no trace of the emotion that had seemed a halo about her head on the ledge of the cliff. She spoke the appropriate commonplace in a low and even voice. After a few words to Mr. Cupples, she turned her eyes on Trent again.

"I hope you will succeed," she said earnestly. "Do you think you will succeed?"

He made his mind up as the words left her lips. He said, "I believe I shall do so, Mrs. Manderson. When I have the case sufficiently complete, I shall ask you to let me see you and tell you about it. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published."

She looked puzzled, and distress showed for an instant in her eyes. "If it is necessary, of course you shall do so," she said.

On the brink of his next speech, Trent hesitated. He remembered that the lady had not wished to repeat the story already given to the inspector—or to be questioned at all. He was not unconscious that he desired to hear her voice and watch her face a little longer, if it might be; but the matter he had to mention really troubled his mind. It was a queer thing that fitted nowhere into the pattern within whose corners he had by this time brought the other queer things in the case. It was very possible that she could explain it away in a breath; it was unlikely that anyone else could. He summoned his resolution.

"You have been so kind," he said, "in allowing me access to the house and every opportunity of studying the case, that I am going to ask leave to put a question or two to yourself—nothing that you would rather not answer, I think. May I?"

She glanced at him wearily. "It would be stupid of me to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent."

"It's only this," said Trent hurriedly. "We know that your husband lately drew an unusually large sum of ready money from his London bankers and was keeping it here. It is here now, in fact. Have you any idea why he should have done that?"

She opened her eyes in astonishment. "I cannot imagine," she said. "I did not know he had done so. I am very much surprised to hear it."

"Why is it surprising?"

"I thought my husband had very little money in the house. On Sunday night, just before he went out in the motor, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed to be irritated about something and asked me at once if I had any notes or gold I could let him have until next day. I was surprised at that, because he was never without money; he made it a rule to carry a hundred pounds or so about him always in a note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him all I had by me. The coroner, acutely aware that for a single day he stepped out of the shadows of provincial solicitor life into the blinding glare of public scrutiny, resolved to meet this fleeting eminence with dignity. He was a man of substantial build and jovial disposition, possessing a genuine fascination for the theatrical elements of his office. The news of Manderson's mysterious demise within his jurisdiction had elevated him to the status of the happiest coroner in England. His respectable aptitude for organizing facts was bolstered by a copiousness of impressive rhetoric that treated juries as malleable clay, occasionally obscuring dubious interpretations of evidentiary rules with a flourish of oratory.

The proceedings took place in a long, unfurnished chamber recently annexed to the hotel, originally designed as a ballroom or concert hall. A regiment of reporters was entrenched in the front rows, while witnesses awaited their turn in chairs set to one side of the table where the coroner presided. Flanking him on the other side sat the jury, a double row of men with plastered hair and a feigned air of casual confidence. The remainder of the space was filled by an undistinguished public, listening in awed silence to the opening solemnities. Among the press, seasoned veterans muttered amongst themselves; those who recognized Trent by sight quietly assured the others that the detective was not present in the court.

The identity of the deceased was established by his wife, the first witness called. After probing into the health and circumstances of the dead man, the coroner guided her through an account of the last occasion she had seen her husband alive. Mrs. Manderson was led through her testimony with the sympathy every man felt for that dark figure of grief. She lifted her thick veil before speaking, and the extreme pallor combined with her unbroken composure produced a singular impression. This was not an impression of hardness. The first thing one sensed in her presence was an interesting femininity. She was not enigmatic; rather, it was evident that the force of a powerful character was at work, mastering the emotions of her situation. Once or twice, as she spoke, she touched her eyes with her handkerchief, yet her voice remained low and clear to the very end.

Her husband, she stated, had ascended to his bedroom around his usual hour for retiring on Sunday night. His room was effectively a dressing-room attached to hers, connected by a door usually kept open during the night. Both rooms had separate entrances onto the passage. Manderson had always preferred the greatest simplicity in his bedroom arrangements and liked to sleep in a small room. She had not been awake when he arrived but had been half-aroused, as was usual, when the light was switched on in his room. She had spoken to him. She had no clear recollection of her words, as she had been very drowsy at the time; however, she remembered that he had been out for a moonlight drive in the car. She believed she had asked if he had had a good run and what time it was. She had asked for the time because she felt as though she had only slept for a very short duration and had expected her husband to be out very late. In answer to her question, he told her it was half-past eleven and added that he had changed his mind about going for a run.

"Did he say why?" the coroner asked.

"Yes," replied the lady, "he did explain why. I remember very well what he said, because—" she paused with a slight appearance of confusion.

"Because—" the coroner insisted gently.

"Because my husband was not as a rule communicative about his business affairs," answered the witness, raising her chin with a faint touch of defiance. "He did not—did not think they would interest me, and as a rule referred to them as little as possible. That was why I was rather surprised when he told me that he had sent Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to retrieve some important information from a man departing for Paris by the next day's boat. He said that Mr. Marlowe could accomplish it quite easily provided he had no accident. He mentioned that he had started in the car, then walked back home a mile or so, and felt all the better for it."

"Did he say any more?"

"Nothing, as well as I remember," the witness said. "I was very sleepy and dropped off again in a few moments. I just remember my husband turning his light out, and that is all. I never saw him alive again."

"And you heard nothing in the night?"

"No: I never woke until my maid brought my tea in the morning at seven o'clock. She closed the door leading to my husband's room, as she always did, and I supposed him to be still there. He always required a great deal of sleep. He sometimes slept until quite late in the morning. I had breakfast in my sitting-room. It was about ten when I heard that my husband's body had been found." The witness dropped her head and waited silently for her dismissal.

But it was not to be yet.

"Mrs. Manderson." The coroner's voice was sympathetic but carried a hint of firmness now. Is it the fact that your relations with your late husband had not, for some time past, been relations of mutual affection and confidence? Is it the fact that there was an estrangement between you?"

The lady drew herself up and faced her questioner, color rising in her cheeks. "If that question is necessary," she said with cold distinctness, "I will answer it so that there shall be no misunderstanding. During the last few months of my husband's life, his attitude toward me had given me great anxiety and sorrow. He had changed toward me; he had become very reserved and seemed mistrustful. I saw much less of him than before; he seemed to prefer to be alone. I can give no explanation at all of the change. I tried to work against it; I did all I could with justice to my own dignity, as I thought. Something was between us, I did not know what, and he never told me. My own obstinate pride prevented me from asking what it was in so many words; I only made a point of being to him exactly as I had always been, so far as he would allow me. I suppose I shall never know now what it was." The witness, whose voice had trembled in spite of her self-control over the last few sentences, drew down her veil when she had finished and stood erect and quiet.

One of the jury members asked a question, not without obvious hesitation. "Then was there never anything of the nature of what they call 'words' between you and your husband, ma'am?"

"Never." The word was spoken colorlessly; yet everyone felt that a crass misunderstanding of the possibilities of conduct in the case of a woman like Mrs. Manderson had been met with some severity.

Did she know, the coroner asked, of any other matter that might have been preying upon her husband's mind recently?

Mrs. Manderson knew of none whatever. The coroner intimated that her ordeal was at an end, and the veiled lady made her way to the door. The general attention, which followed her for a few moments, was now eagerly directed upon Martin, whom the coroner had proceeded to call.

It was at this precise moment that Trent appeared at the doorway and edged his way into the great room. But he did not look at Martin. He was observing the well-balanced figure moving quickly toward him along an opening path in the crowd, and his eye was gloomy. He started, as he stood aside from the door with a slight bow, to hear Mrs. Manderson address him by name in a low voice. He followed her a pace or two into the hall.

"I wanted to ask you," she said in a voice now weak and oddly broken, "if you would give me your arm a part of the way to the house. I could not see my uncle near the door, and I suddenly felt rather faint.... I shall be better in the air.... No, no; I cannot stay here—please, Mr. Trent!" she said as he began to make an obvious suggestion. "I must go to the house." Her hand tightened momentarily on his arm as if, for all her weakness, she could drag him from the place; then again she leaned heavily upon it, and with that support, and with bent head, she walked slowly from the hotel and along the oak-shaded path toward White Gables.

Trent went in silence, his thoughts whirling, dancing insanely to a chorus of "Fool! fool!" All that he alone knew, all that he guessed and suspected of this affair, rushed through his brain in a rout; but the touch of her unnerved hand upon his arm never for an instant left his consciousness, filling him with an exaltation that enraged and bewildered him. He was still cursing himself furiously behind the mask of conventional solicitude that he turned to the lady when he had attended her to the house and seen her sink upon a couch in the morning-room. Raising her veil, she thanked him gravely and frankly, with a look of sincere gratitude in her eyes. She was much better now, she said, and a cup of tea would work a miracle upon her. She hoped she had not taken him away from anything important. She was ashamed of herself; she thought she could go through with it, but she had not expected those last questions. "I am glad you did not hear me," she said when he explained. "But of course you will read it all in the reports. It shook me so to have to speak of that," she added simply; "and to keep from making an exhibition of myself took it out of me. And all those staring men by the door! Thank you again for helping me when I asked you.... I thought I might," she ended queerly, with a little tired smile; and Trent took himself away, his hand still quivering from the cool touch of her fingers.

The testimony of the servants and of the finder of the body brought nothing new to the reporters' net. That of the police was as colorless and cryptic as is usual at the inquest stage of affairs of this kind. Greatly to the satisfaction of Mr. Bunner, his evidence afforded the sensation of the day and threw far into the background the interesting revelation of domestic difficulty made by the dead man's wife. He told the court in substance what he had already told Trent. The flying pencils did not miss a word of the young American's story, and it appeared with scarcely the omission of a sentence in every journal of importance in Great Britain and the United States.

Public opinion the next day took no note of the faint suggestion of the possibility of suicide which the coroner, in his final address to the jury, had thought it right to make in connection with the lady's evidence. The weight of evidence, as the official had indeed pointed out, was against such a theory. He had referred with emphasis to the fact that no weapon had been found near the body.

"This question, of course, is all-important, gentlemen," he had said to the jury. "It is, in fact, the main issue before you. You have seen the body for yourselves. You have just heard the medical evidence; but I think it would be well for me to read you my notes of it in so far as they bear on this point, in order to refresh your memories. Dr. Stock told you—I am going to omit all technical medical language and repeat to you merely the plain English of his testimony—that in his opinion death had taken place six or eight hours previous to the finding of the body. He said that the cause of death was a bullet wound, the bullet having entered the left eye, which was destroyed, and made its way to the base of the brain, which was quite shattered. The external appearance of the wound, he said, did not support the hypothesis of its being self-inflicted, inasmuch as there were no signs of the firearm having been pressed against the eye, or even put very close to it; at the same time it was not physically impossible that the weapon should have been discharged by the deceased with his own hand, at some small distance from the eye. Dr. Stock also told us that it was impossible to say with certainty, from the state of the body, whether any struggle had taken place at the time of death; that when seen by him, at which time he understood that it had not been moved since it was found, the body was lying in a collapsed position such as might very well result from the shot alone; but that the scratches and bruises upon the wrists and the lower part of the arms had been very recently inflicted, and were, in his opinion, marks of violence.

"In connection with this same point, the remarkable evidence given by Mr. Bunner cannot be regarded, I think, as without significance. It may have come as a surprise to some of you to hear that risks of the character described by this witness are, in his own country, commonly run by persons in the position of the deceased. On the other hand, it may have been within the knowledge of some of you that in the industrial world of America the discontent of labour often proceeds to lengths of which we in England happily know nothing. I have interrogated the witness somewhat fully upon this. At the same time, gentlemen, I am by no means suggesting that Mr. Bunner's personal conjecture as to the cause of death can fitly be adopted by you. That is emphatically not the case. What his evidence does is to raise two questions for your consideration. First, can it be said that the deceased was to any extent in the position of a threatened man—of a man more exposed to the danger of murderous attack than an ordinary person? Second, does the recent alteration in his demeanour, as described by this witness, justify the belief that his last days were overshadowed by a great anxiety? These points may legitimately be considered by you in arriving at a conclusion upon the rest of the evidence."

Thereupon the coroner, having indicated thus clearly his opinion that Mr. Bunner had hit the right nail on the head, desired the jury to consider their verdict.

"Enter!" Trent's voice cut through the quiet of the hotel sitting-room.

Mr. Cupples stepped inside, finding the room bathed in the dim, amber light of early evening. Outside, the coroner's jury had already delivered their verdict—a swift, damning condemnation of unknown parties—without ever leaving the box. Inside, however, the air was thick with the chemical tang of development fluid and the sharp scent of wet paper. Trent stood before a window, his face pale as parchment, his movements jerky and nervous. He held a photographic dish of enamelled metal, rotating it slowly as he scrutinized the image within.

"Take the sofa," Trent instructed, his eyes never leaving the dish. "Those chairs are a bargain bin purchase from the aftermath of the Holy Inquisition's suppression in Spain. Not worth the dust." He held the negative up to the fading daylight, his head tilted in a pose of critical assessment. "The wash is adequate, I believe. We'll let it dry and clear the clutter."

As Trent busied himself clearing the table of basins, racks, and bottles, Mr. Cupples picked up each item with innocent fascination.

"That is hypo-eliminator," Trent explained, watching Cupples uncork and sniff a bottle. "Essential when speed is required. Though, I wouldn't recommend drinking it. It strips sodium hypophosphite, but I wouldn't be surprised if it stripped a human being just as effectively." He stacked the remaining debris onto the crowded mantelpiece and climbed onto the table to face his companion. "There is a unique advantage to a hotel sitting-room: its lack of beauty prevents distraction. It is a sanctuary for a mind at work, not a place for idle contemplation. Have you ever occupied this room, Cupples? I have, hundreds of times. It has chased me across England for years. I would feel adrift if, in some distant hotel, they furnished me with a different space. Notice this tablecloth; I spilled ink on it during my stay in Halifax. I scorched a hole in the carpet in Ipswich. But look—the glass over 'Silent Sympathy' has been repaired, whereas I once kicked it in Banbury. My finest work always emerges from here. Since the inquest this afternoon, I have developed several exceptional negatives. There is a proper darkroom downstairs."

"The inquest," Cupples interjected, sensing the sudden shift in Trent's demeanor. "That brings to mind my thanks for looking after Mabel this morning. I had no notion she would fall ill upon leaving the box; she seemed remarkably composed. She possesses extraordinary self-command, which led me to believe she could manage alone while I heard the evidence. Fortunately, she found a friend to assist, and she is deeply grateful. She is herself again."

Trent, hands buried in his pockets, frowned slightly but offered no immediate response. "Tell you what," he said after a pause, "I was just reaching the crux of the matter when you arrived. Care to witness a glimpse of high-class police work? It is the very caliber of duty that old Murch should be performing right now. Perhaps he is; but I pray to heaven he isn't."

With a spring, Trent vanished into his bedroom. Moments later, he emerged carrying a large drawing board laden with a chaotic array of objects.

"First, acquaint yourself with these," Trent announced, arranging them on the table. "A substantial ivory paperknife, two leaves torn from my own diary, a bottle of dentifrice, and a small case of polished walnut. Several of these must be returned to their rightful places in someone's bedroom at White Gables before nightfall. That is my nature—nothing halts me. I borrowed them this morning while everyone was at the inquest. I suspect some might find the procedure peculiar if they knew."

His gaze settled on the final object on the board. "And this? Can you identify it without touching?"

"Certainly," Cupples replied, leaning in with intense curiosity. "It appears to be an ordinary glass bowl, perhaps a finger-bowl. Nothing unusual about it," he added after a moment of scrutiny.

"I see little either," Trent admitted, a spark of excitement igniting his eyes. "And that is precisely where the intrigue lies. Take this stout bottle, Cupples, and extract the cork. Do you recognize the powder within? You've likely swallowed pounds of it in your lifetime; it is given to infants. It is known as 'grey powder'—mercury and chalk. Remarkable stuff."

Holding the basin sideways over a sheet of paper, Trent directed Cupples. "Pour a small amount of powder here, onto this section of the bowl. Perfect. Even Sir Edward Henry could not have handled the dust with greater precision. You have done this before, Cupples; you are an old hand."

"I assure you, I have not," Cupples stated gravely. "It is a complete mystery to me. What did I do?"

"Treat the powdered area lightly with this camel-hair brush," Trent commanded. "Examine it again. Did you notice anything before?"

Cupples peered closely. "Curious," he murmured. "Yes, there are two large grey finger-marks on the bowl. They were absent previously."

"I am Hawkshaw the detective," Trent observed, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Shall I deliver a brief lecture on the mechanics of glass finger-bowls? When one lifts such an object, invisible traces remain, persisting for days or months. These are the marks of your fingers. The human hand, even when seemingly clean, is rarely entirely dry. In moments of profound anxiety, Cupples, it becomes notably moist, leaving an imprint on any cold, smooth surface it touches. That bowl was handled recently by someone with a rather damp hand."

He sprinkled more powder. "Here on the opposite side is the thumb-mark; the impressions are excellent." He spoke quietly, yet Cupples could see Trent burning with intensity as he stared at the faint grey patterns. "This one corresponds to the index finger. Need I remind a man of your worldly knowledge that the pattern is a single-spiral whorl, with deltas symmetrically arranged? This, the print of the second finger, is a simple loop, featuring a staple core and fifteen counts. I know the count precisely because I possess the exact same two prints on this negative, which I have examined in minute detail. Look!"

Trent held a negative up to the waning sunlight, pointing with a pencil tip. "Observe the bifurcation of that ridge. It is present here. It is present there. Note the tiny scar near the center; it matches perfectly. There are dozens of ridge characteristics upon which an expert would testify in a witness box that the marks on this bowl and the photographs on this plate were created by the identical hand."

"And where did you capture these? What does it signify?" Cupples asked, his eyes wide.

"I discovered them on the interior of the left-hand pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson's bedroom. Unable to remove the window itself, I photographed them by affixing black paper to the rear of the glass. The bowl originates from Manderson's room; it is the vessel used to store his false teeth nightly. I secured it."

"But those cannot be Mabel's finger-marks," Cupples protested.

"I should think not!" Trent declared decisively. "They are twice the size of any print Mrs. Manderson could produce."

"Then they must belong to her husband."

"Perhaps they do. Shall we verify this once more? I believe we can."

Whistling softly, though his face remained deathly pale, Trent produced another small, squat bottle containing a dense black powder. "Lamp-black," he explained. "Hold a piece of paper in your hand for a moment, and this little demonstration will reveal the pattern of your fingers."

Using tweezers, Trent selected one of the diary leaves and held it out for Cupples to inspect. No marks appeared. He then tilted some powder onto one surface of the paper, flipped it, and dusted the other side. After gently shaking off the excess, he presented the leaf to Cupples in silence. On one side, clearly etched in black, appeared the same two finger-prints Cupples had seen on the bowl and the photographic plate. Trent picked up the bowl for comparison, then turned the paper over. On the reverse side lay a bold, black replica of the thumb-mark, matching the grey impression on the glass.

"Same individual, you see," Trent said, a short, sharp laugh escaping him. "I suspected it, and now I know."

He walked to the window and gazed outward. "Now I know," he repeated in a low, resonant tone, as if speaking to himself. His voice was laced with bitterness. Cupples, bewildered, stared at Trent's motionless back for a few moments.

"I remain entirely in the dark," Cupples ventured. "I have often heard of this fingerprint business and wondered how the police apply it. It is of extraordinary interest to me, but upon my life, I cannot see how Manderson's fingerprints are going—"

"I am deeply sorry, Cupples," Trent interrupted, snapping back to the table with swift energy. "When I initiated this investigation, I intended to guide you through every step. Do not mistake my silence for doubt regarding your discretion. I must hold my tongue about the entirety of this, at least for the present. Let me tell you this: I have uncovered a fact that bears the terrifying weight of painful consequences should it be discovered by anyone else."

He fixed Cupples with a hard, darkened gaze and struck the table with his fist. "It is dreadful for me, right now. Until this moment, I had hoped against hope that I was mistaken about this fact. I may still be wrong in the surmise I base upon it. There is only one path forward, and I must steel myself to take it."

He smiled suddenly at Cupples's face of consternation. "All right—I shall not be tragic any longer. I will explain everything when the time is right. Look here, I am not half finished with my game involving the powder-bottles yet."

He dragged one of the disreputable chairs to the table and sat down to test the broad ivory blade of the paperknife. Cupples, swallowing his amazement, leaned forward in an attitude of deep interest and handed Trent the bottle of lamp-black.

The Wife of Dives

Rain lashed against the windowpane of Mrs. Manderson's sitting room at White Gables, turning the garden outside into a blurred wash of grey and green. It was a rare break in the June gloom, yet the atmosphere inside was heavier than the storm. The landscape beyond was a sullen expanse of mist drifting from the sea, the sky a dead, unbroken slate shedding moisture that tapped against the glass like a persistent, despairing insect. Mrs. Manderson stood before the glass, her face a mask of sorrow, staring at a world that offered no comfort to a woman bereaved, isolated, and stripped of purpose.

A knock shattered the silence. She straightened, a reflex born of the sudden realization that the crushing weight of the world had momentarily overwhelmed her spirit. "Come in," she called.

Her maid announced that Mr. Trent had arrived, apologizing for the early hour but insisting on a matter of urgent importance. Mrs. Manderson nodded, her resolve hardening. She walked to the mirror, studying the olive-toned face reflected back at her, shaking her head at the grimace that threatened to form, before turning to greet her visitor.

Trent entered, and she noted immediately that the man she knew was gone. In his place stood a figure drained of sleep, his usual fixed good humor replaced by a reserved, unreadable tension that sent a chill through her.

"May I come to the point at once?" he asked, taking her hand. "I have a train to catch at Bishopsbridge at noon, but I cannot leave until I settle this. It concerns you alone, Mrs. Manderson. I have worked half the night and thought the rest, and I know what I must do."

"You look wretchedly tired," she said, her kindness a shield against the storm in his eyes. "Won't you sit? This chair is restful. Of course, it is about this terrible business and your work as a correspondent. Ask me anything you think I can properly answer. I trust you won't make my situation worse than duty requires. If you say you must see me, I know it is because you ought to."

"Mrs. Manderson," Trent said, measuring his words with deliberate care, "I promise I won't make it worse than I can help. But I am bound to make it bad for you—though, between ourselves, I hope only in private. Whether you can tell me what I ask is up to you, but on my word of honor, I will ask only enough to decide whether to publish or suppress certain grave discoveries regarding your husband's death. Things unknown to anyone else, and unlikely to be suspected. What I have found—what I believe I have practically proven—will shock you regardless. But it could be worse. If you give me reason to believe publication would destroy you further, I will burn this manuscript," he laid a long envelope on the small table beside him, "and nothing of it will ever see print. It contains a private note to my editor and a dispatch for the *Record*."

He paused, his gaze steady. "You may refuse to speak. If you do, my duty compels me to take this to London today and leave it to my editor's discretion. I am not entitled to suppress it merely on a hunch. But if I gather from you—and no one else—that there is substance to the danger I fear, then as a gentleman and as one who wishes you well, I will not publish. I decline to assist the police in some directions. Do you follow?"

"I understand perfectly," Mrs. Manderson whispered. She drew a deep breath. "I don't know what dreadful thing you've found, but it was honorable of you to come to me. Will you please tell me?"

"I cannot," Trent replied firmly. "The secret belongs to my newspaper unless it is yours. If it is yours, you may read and destroy my manuscript. Believe me, I detest such mystery-making from the bottom of my soul; but I did not create this enigma. This is the most painful hour of my life, and you make it harder by refusing to treat me like a hound. The first thing I ask is this: Is it true, as you stated at the inquest, that you had no idea why your late husband changed his attitude toward you, becoming mistrustful and reserved in his final months?"

Mrs. Manderson's dark brows lifted, her eyes flaring with sudden fire. She rose quickly. Trent stood up simultaneously, retrieving his envelope, signaling the end of the interview. But she raised a hand, color rushing to her cheeks, her voice quickening. "Do you know what you ask, Mr. Trent? You ask if I perjured myself."

"I do," he answered, unmoved. "You knew already I had not come to preserve polite fictions, Mrs. Manderson. The notion that no reputable person could withhold the truth under oath is a fiction itself."

He waited, but she was silent. She walked to the window, and he watched miserably as her shoulders moved slightly before settling. Turning her face away to the dismal weather, she finally spoke.

"Mr. Trent," she said, "people trust you. I feel that things I wish to keep hidden are safe with you. I know you have a serious reason for this, though I do not know what it is. Perhaps telling you the truth would assist justice. To understand it, you must know about my marriage. Many could tell you as well as I that it was not... a successful union. I was only twenty. I admired his force, his courage, his certainty; he was the only strong man I had ever known. But I soon learned he cared more for his business than for me. I think I realized even sooner that I had deceived myself, blinding myself with impossible promises because I was dazzled by the prospect of spending money beyond the dreams of any English girl. I have despised myself for that for five years."

She paused, her voice trembling slightly. "My husband's feelings for me... I cannot speak of that. But alongside that, he believed I was destined for a great place in society. He thought I would throw myself into it with enjoyment, become a personage, and bring him credit. That was his idea, and it remained even as other delusions faded. I was a part of his ambition. His bitter disappointment was that I failed him socially. I think he was too shrewd not to know the risk of marrying a girl brought up for music and books, unpractical and self-indulgent, yet he reckoned on me to do the honors of his position. I found I could not."

Mrs. Manderson had spoken herself into a fervor unseen before. Her words flowed freely, her voice ringing with a natural expressiveness dulled by recent shock and restraint. She turned swiftly from the window, facing him now, her beautiful face flushed, her eyes gleaming, her hands gesturing emphatically as she surrendered to the impulse to release pent-up truths.

"The people!" she exclaimed. "Can you imagine what it is to step from a world of creative work, of dignity and ideals, into a world where one must be shamefully rich to exist? Where money is the only currency, where the millionaires are jaded and sport is their only refuge, and the idle are duller and vicerous than the workers? And the women? They live for display, silly amusements, and immorality. Do you know how awful that life is? There are clever people, certainly, but they are swamped and spoiled. It is all empty, empty! Oh, I suppose I exaggerate, and I did make friends and have happy times; but that is how I feel now. The seasons in New York and London, the house-parties, the yacht cruises—all the same people, all the same emptiness."

"And you see," she continued, her voice rising, "my husband could not have an idea of all this. His life was never empty. He lived in business plans and difficulties. He had no suspicion of my inner turmoil, and I never let him know; it would not have been fair. I felt I must do something to justify myself as his wife, sharing his fortune and position. The only way was to try, and try, to live up to his idea of my social qualities. I acted my best. It became harder year by year. I was never a popular hostess; how could I be? I was a failure, yet I persisted. I stole holidays now and then. I felt I was breaking a bargain when I took an old school friend, who couldn't afford to travel, to Italy for a month, or when I stayed in London with quiet people who knew me from childhood. We lived as in the old days, worrying about theater seats and cheap dressmakers. Those were my best times, my escapes. But I felt my husband would hate to know how much I enjoyed returning to that old life."

"In the end, despite everything, he came to know. He could see through anything once his attention was turned to it. He had always seen that I was not fulfilling his vision of a social figure, and perhaps he thought it my misfortune rather than my fault. But the moment he realized I wasn't playing my part with spirit, he knew the whole story. He divined how I loathed the luxury and the brilliance simply because of the people who surrounded them. It happened last year. I don't know how or when. Perhaps some woman suggested it to him—they all understood, of course. He said nothing to me, and I think he tried not to change his manner at first, but such things hurt. It worked in both of us. I knew he knew. Eventually, we were merely polite and considerate. Before he found me out, we had been on a footing of intelligent companionship; we talked freely about things we agreed or disagreed upon without it going deep. Then that ended. I felt the foundation of our coexistence slipping away. At last, it was gone."

"It had been like that," she concluded simply, "for months before he died."

She sank into the corner of a sofa by the window, relaxing her body as if after a great exertion. Silence fell between them. Trent hastily sorted through a tangle of impressions, amazed by the frankness of her story and the vigorous expressiveness of her delivery. In this vivid being, carried away by an impulse to speak, he saw the real woman in a state of activity, unlike the pale, self-disciplined creature of majesty she presented to the world. With this amazement came a terror of her dark beauty, kindled by excitement into an appearance scarcely mortal. Incongruously, a knot of ideas rushed into his mind: she was unique not because of her beauty, but because it was united with an intensity of nature. In England, the very beautiful were placid, and the fiery women seemed to have burned up their beauty. No beautiful woman had ever cast this spell on him before. When it came to wit, he had preferred the brighter flame to the duller, without much regard for the lamp. "All this is very disputable," his reason argued, but instinct answered, "Yes, except that I am under a spell"; and a deeper instinct cried out, "Away with it!" He forced his mind back to her story, finding growing within him an irrepressible conviction. It was all very fine, but it would not do.

"I feel as if I have led you into saying more than you meant, or than I wanted to learn," he said slowly. "But there is one brutal question which is the whole point of my inquiry." He braced his frame as if preparing for a plunge into cold water. "Mrs. Manderson, will you assure me that your husband's change toward you had nothing to do with John Marlowe?"

And what he dreaded came. "Oh!" she cried, a sound of pure anguish, her face thrown up, open hands stretched out as if for pity. Then the hands covered her burning face, and she flung herself aside among the cushions at her elbow. He saw nothing but her heavy crown of black hair, her body moving with sobs that stabbed his heart, and a foot turned inward gracelessly in an abandonment of misery. Like a tall tower suddenly breaking apart, she had fallen in ruins, weeping helplessly.

Trent stood up, his face white and calm. With a senseless particularity, he placed his envelope exactly in the center of the little polished table. He walked to the door, closing it noiselessly as he went out. Within minutes, he was tramping through the rain, out of sight of White Gables, going nowhere, seeing nothing, his soul shaken by the fierce effort to kill and trample the raving impulse that had seized him in the presence of her shame. That impulse clamored to drag himself before her feet, to pray for pardon, to pour out words he knew not what, but knew were straining at his lips—to wreck his self-respect forever, and hopelessly defeat even the crazy purpose that had almost possessed him, by drowning her wretchedness in disgust, by babbling with the tongue of infatuation to a woman with a husband not yet buried, to a woman who loved another man.

Such was the magic of her tears, quickening in a moment the thing which, as his heart had known, he must not let come to life. For Philip Trent was a young man, younger in nature even than his years, and a way of life that kept his edge keen and his spirit volcanic had prepared him very ill for the meeting that comes once in the early manhood of most of us, usually—as in his case, he told himself harshly—to no purpose but the testing of virtue and the power of the will.

Hitherto Unpublished

My Dear Molloy:—Should you not be at your office when this reaches you, know that I have solved the Manderson mystery. The enclosed dispatch details the solution. My role ends here; yours begins with deciding how to wield this information. It implicates an unsuspected individual, practically accusing him of murder. Prudence dictates withholding publication until his arrest, and legality forbids it thereafter until a guilty verdict is secured. You may choose to release it then, or perhaps utilize the facts sooner. That is your prerogative. For now, contact Scotland Yard. I am done with this case; I pray I never touched it. Follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I commence this third, and likely final, dispatch to the *Record* regarding the Manderson murder with a conflicted heart. Relief washes over me, for in my previous reports, justice demanded I withhold crucial facts. To reveal them then would have alerted a suspect of uncommon boldness and resourcefulness, potentially allowing him to flee. Now, I set forth those withheld truths. Yet, I confess, the tale of treachery and perverted intellect leaves a bitter taste. It reveals a revolting depth of motive beneath the crime's surface, a puzzle I believe I have finally unraveled.

Recall my first dispatch, describing the scene upon my arrival at Marlstone on Tuesday morning. I recounted the discovery of the body, the surrounding mystery, local theories, and Manderson's movements the prior evening. I noted a peculiar detail: a quantity of whisky, far exceeding his nightly habit, had vanished from his private decanter. The following day, the inquest, I reported only an abstract of the proceedings. As I write, that day is not yet over, and my investigation has concluded, pointing directly to the man who must clear himself of Manderson's death.

Beyond the central enigma of Manderson's early departure, two minor oddities stood out from the start. First, the body was found mere yards from the house, yet no one claimed to hear a cry or struggle. Manderson was not gagged; wrist marks indicated a fight; and at least one pistol shot had been fired. This silence was baffling, especially given that Martin, the butler, was a poor sleeper with keen hearing, and his bedroom window faced the shed where the body lay.

Second, Manderson had left his dental plate by the bedside. He had dressed fully—necktie, watch, chain—yet forgotten the plate he wore daily. It was not a frantic flight; anyone familiar with removable dentures knows inserting it is second nature. These anomalies hinted at a shadow lurking in the mystery.

My breakthrough came in Manderson's bedroom. Amidst the rigid simplicity of the room, I examined the shoes. On the upper shelf, where Manderson's patent leather dress shoes should have been, I found them. They were old, well-polished, and expertly made. However, a connoisseur would notice a slight split in the leather of the vamp, where the laces rise. The stitching holding the upper to the sole was strained and visible at the toe and outer side. These were not signs of Manderson's careful, narrow feet. They were the marks of someone forcing their larger feet into shoes too small.

The tears were fresh. Manderson wore his own shoes with care; none of the others bore such damage. Someone else had worn them recently. The possibility of post-mortem tampering was negligible; the body had been found only twenty-six hours prior. Nor would Manderson have allowed others to borrow and ruin his prized footwear. The only men in the house were the butler and two secretaries. Yet, I let my thoughts wander, allowing the daemon of deduction to range.

Psychologically, once a key fact is grasped, the mind rearranges itself around it. The thought "Someone other than Manderson wore these shoes" triggered a cascade of realizations. It was unlike Manderson to drink heavily, to be untidily dressed, to skip washing, or to leave his bedroom without his false teeth. It was strange he spoke so freely to his wife at bedtime, or left his dental plate behind. All these inconsistencies converged on one terrifying certainty: Manderson was not in the house that night.

If a man was impersonating Manderson, why force his feet into Manderson's narrow shoes? To create the illusion that Manderson was present. The maid had found the shoes outside the bedroom door, polished and replaced on the shelf, ensuring the house appeared occupied. Similarly, the missing dental plate was a deliberate prop. The impersonator had brought it, leaving it in the bedroom to cement the belief that Manderson had retired there.

This implied Manderson was dead before the impostor arrived. The clothing confirmed this. The trousers, waistcoat, and shooting jacket were in the bedroom. Martin had seen the jacket on the man at the telephone. The impersonator knew Martin would recognize the coat, so it was essential.

A critical oversight had blinded everyone: Martin and Mrs. Manderson had never seen the impostor's face. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep, merely resumed a conversation with her "husband." Martin saw only the man's back as he crouched at the telephone, wearing Manderson's broad-brimmed hat. The back of a head and neck holds too much character; the impostor, matching Manderson's build, needed no disguise beyond the hat, jacket, and mimicry.

The entry via the garden window, avoiding the front door where Martin might intercept him, was logical. The missing whisky was likely a comfort for a man fresh from a bloody crime, seeking solace before executing his final act. He knew when to stop, realizing the true danger lay ahead: shutting himself in Manderson's room and staging the scene, risking detection by the woman in the adjoining room. From her bed, she could only see a cupboard; the estrangement between the couple suggested she would assume the presence of her husband was normal.

Following this hypothesis, I visualized the sequence: The impostor entered, rang for Martin, and sat at the telephone with his back to the door. While Martin was occupied, the impostor ascended to the bedroom floor, entered Marlowe's room, and placed the murder weapon—a revolver taken from Marlowe's case—onto the mantelpiece. He then moved to Manderson's room, placed the shoes outside, scattered the clothes, set the dental plate in the bowl, and selected a fresh suit.

Who was this false Manderson? Five conclusions emerged:
1. He knew Manderson intimately.
2. He matched Manderson's height and shoulder breadth but had larger feet.
3. He possessed exceptional mimicry skills.
4. He knew the household routines intimately.
5. He desperately needed to convince everyone Manderson was alive until after midnight.

Mr. John Marlowe fit this profile perfectly. He had been Manderson's private secretary for four years, sharing Manderson's height and build. Though slightly slimmer, Marlowe's shoes were notably larger. I telegraphed an Oxford friend regarding Marlowe's acting history. The reply revealed Marlowe was a former Oxford University Dramatic Society member, playing roles like Bardolph and Mercutio, renowned for character acting and imitations. A photograph in Marlowe's bedroom confirmed his theatrical past. He had lived as part of the family, knowing every domestic detail. Furthermore, Marlowe had a verified alibi: he arrived in Southampton at 6:30 AM on Monday morning to execute a commission Manderson supposedly gave him.

Yet, contradictions remained. Only Marlowe knew Manderson intended to go to Southampton. Manderson, upon returning from the drive, openly mentioned Southampton to two people, even calling a hotel there to verify Marlowe's story. This call was the one Martin was busy with. If Manderson was alive until after midnight, Marlowe could not have committed the murder. But if Manderson died by eleven, how did Marlowe appear in Southampton by 6:30 AM? He had to exit the house unseen and unheard before midnight, while Martin, instructed to stay awake until 12:30 AM, stood sentinel near the staircase.

This was the crux. I spent the day before the inquest testing my theory, finding only one weakness: Martin's vigilance. I needed proof that the impostor could have fled before Martin fell asleep. I decided to wait until the inquest proceedings at the hotel began, securing White Gables for my investigation.

Armed with a camera, I searched for evidence. I photographed two fresh, large fingerprints on the polished front of the drawer in Manderson's bedroom. I found five prints on the French window in Mrs. Manderson's room, which stood open at night, and three on the glass bowl holding the dental plate. I collected samples from Marlowe's bedroom and compared them with prints Marlowe had unknowingly made on my diary leaves earlier.

By six PM, two hours after the jury's verdict, I had matched the prints. Two of the five window prints and the three on the bowl belonged to Marlowe's left hand; the remaining three on the window and two on the drawer were his right hand. By eight PM, enlarged prints from a professional photographer confirmed the identity. Marlowe had recently been in Manderson's bedroom and Mrs. Manderson's room, places he had no business visiting.

At nine PM, back in my hotel room, I finalized the narrative. The impostor, having staged the room and spoken to Mrs. Manderson about Marlowe's trip to Southampton, switched off the light and lay in bed. Once assured she was asleep, he slipped out in stocking feet, carrying the bundle of clothes and shoes. He pushed the French window open, stepped onto the balcony railing, and dropped to the lawn below.

This escape could have taken half an hour after his entry at 11:30 PM. The rest is conjecture for the authorities. The corpse was found next morning, dressed untidily. Marlowe appeared in the car in Southampton by 6:30 AM.

I conclude this manuscript in my hotel room at Marlstone. It is four o'clock in the morning. I depart for London by the noon train from Bishopsbridge, delivering these pages immediately to you. Please convey the substance of this report to the Criminal Investigation Department.

From Munich, Trent dispatched a letter to Sir James Molloy, his pen moving with the weary precision of a man shedding a heavy burden. "I am returning the cheque you sent for my work on the Manderson case," he wrote. "What I submitted was not worth one-tenth of the sum; yet I could not bring myself to pocket it. I have developed a peculiar aversion to taking money for this specific business. If you have no objection, please charge the cost of the material to your standard space rate and remit the balance to a charity that does not engage in bullying. I have come here to visit old friends and organize my thoughts. The conclusion that dominates my mind is that for the present, I require employment filled with activity. I have discovered I cannot paint—not even a fence. Would you consider employing me as your Own Correspondent? If you can secure me a genuine adventure, I promise dispatches of consequence. Once that is concluded, I shall settle down to steady work."

Sir James's reply arrived via telegram, ordering Trent immediately to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning was once again abroad amidst a blaze of civil revolt. It was a roving commission, and for two months Trent followed the trail. Fortune favored him as often as usual. He was the sole correspondent to witness General Dragilew's assassination in the streets of Volmar by an eighteen-year-old girl. He witnessed burnings, lynchings, fusillades, and hangings; every day his soul recoiled anew at the imbecilities spawned by misrule. Many nights he slept in the shadow of danger; many days he fasted. Yet, there was not a single evening or morning when the face of the woman he loved with hopeless devotion did not haunt his vision.

He harbored a bitter pride in the enduring power of this infatuation. It fascinated him as a phenomenon, astounding and enlightening him. Nothing of the sort had ever visited him before. It validated much of what he had previously found dubious in the recorded experiences of men.

At thirty-two, Trent could not feign ignorance of the world of emotion. What he had learned had come unpursued and unpurchased, devoid of intolerable memories. Broken to the realities of sex, he remained troubled by its inscrutable history. He navigated life with a strange respect for certain feminine weaknesses and a profound terror of certain feminine strengths. He had maintained a lukewarm faith that something remained within him to be awakened, and that the voice calling it forth would arrive in its own time, if ever, and not through any active seeking.

He had not anticipated that, if this truth were to manifest, it might take a sinister form. The two aspects of his feelings toward Mabel Manderson that had utterly surprised him were the insane suddenness of their eruption and their extravagant hopelessness. Prior to their arrival, he had been inclined to laugh at the permanence of unrequited passion, dismissing it as a generous boyish delusion. He now knew he had been wrong, and he lived bitterly under that knowledge.

In his imagination, the woman always appeared exactly as he had first seen her, performing that great gesture of passionate joy as he had walked past unseen on the cliff's edge. That gesture, signaling her new liberty as a release from torment, had confirmed with terrible force his prior suspicion that widowhood was her passport to happiness with a man she loved. He could not pinpoint the exact moment he first suspected this, but he believed the seed was sown during his first meeting with Marlowe. His mind would have automatically noted that such evident strength and grace, coupled with the tall young man's mannerisms, might captivate any woman of unfixed affections. The connection with what Mr. Cupples had revealed about the Mandersons' married life must have formed in the unconscious depths of his mind. By the time he began searching for the motive of the crime, after verifying the murderer's identity, the thought presented itself as an established fact. *Motive, motive!* How desperately he had sought another explanation, turning his back on the grim notion that Marlowe—obsessed by passion like himself, and perhaps privy to maddening truths about the wife's unhappiness—had taken the guiltiest leaf from the book of Bothwell. Yet, in all his investigations and subsequent broodings, he had found nothing to prompt Marlowe to such a deed—nothing but that temptation, whose full strength he could not know, yet which, if it existed, must have pressed urgently upon a bold spirit in which scruple had been paralyzed. If he could trust his senses, the young man was neither insane nor inherently evil. But that could not exonerate him. Murder for a woman's sake was not a rare crime; Heaven knew! While the modern feebleness of impulse in comfortable classes and their respect for the apparatus of detection had made it rare among them, it was far from impossible. It only required a man of equal daring and intelligence, his soul drugged by the vapors of an intoxicating intrigue, to plan and execute such a deed.

A thousand times, with a heart full of anguish, he had tried to reason away the dread that Mabel Manderson had known too much about the intended destruction of her husband's life. That she knew the full truth after the event was undeniable; her unforgettable collapse when the question of Marlowe was suddenly and bluntly posed had swept away his last hope that there was no love between them, and seemed to speak of a dread of discovery. In any case, she knew the truth after reading his manuscript, and it was certain that no public suspicion had been cast upon Marlowe since. She had destroyed his manuscript, then, and taken his word to keep the secret that threatened her lover's life.

But it was the monstrous thought that she might have known murder was brewing, and guiltily kept silence, that haunted Trent's mind. She might have suspected, guessed something; was it conceivable that she was aware of the whole plot, that she connived? He could never forget that his first suspicion of Marlowe's motive had been roused by the fact that his escape was made through the lady's room. At that time, before seeing her, he had been ready to entertain the idea of her equal guilt and cooperation. He had pictured to himself some passionate *hystérique*, merciless as a cat in her hate and her love, a zealous abettor, perhaps even the ruling spirit of the crime.

Then he had seen her, spoken with her, helped her in her weakness; and such suspicions, since their first meeting, had seemed the vilest of infamy. He had seen her eyes and her mouth; he had breathed the woman's atmosphere. Trent was one of those who fancy they can scent true wickedness in the air. In her presence, he had felt an inward certainty of her ultimate goodness of heart; and it was nothing against this that she had abandoned herself for a moment, that day on the cliff, to the sentiment of relief at the ending of her bondage, of her years of starved sympathy and unquickened motherhood. That she had turned to Marlowe in her destitution he believed; that she had any knowledge of his deadly purpose he did not believe.

And yet, morning and evening, the sickening doubts returned, and he recalled again that it was almost in her presence that Marlowe had made his preparations in the bedroom of the murdered man, that it was by the window of her own chamber that he had escaped from the house. Had he forgotten his cunning and taken the risk of telling her then? Or had he, as Trent thought more likely, still played his part with her then, and stolen off while she slept? He did not think she had known of the masquerade when she gave evidence at the inquest; it read like honest evidence. Or—the question would never be silenced, though he scorned it—had she lain expecting the footsteps in the room and the whisper that should tell her it was done? Among the foul possibilities of human nature, was it possible that black ruthlessness and black deceit were hidden behind that good, straight, and gentle seeming?

These thoughts would scarcely leave him when he was alone.

Trent served Sir James well, earning his pay for six months, before returning to Paris, where he resumed work with a better heart. His powers had returned, and he began to live more happily than he had expected among a tribe of strangely assorted friends—French, English, and American artists, poets, journalists, policemen, hotel-keepers, soldiers, lawyers, businessmen, and others. His old faculty of sympathetic interest in his fellows won for him, just as in his student days, privileges seldom extended to the Briton. He enjoyed again the rare experience of being taken into the bosom of a Frenchman's family. He was admitted to the momentous confidence of *les jeunes*, and found them as sure that they had surprised the secrets of art and life as the departed *jeunes* of ten years before had been.

The bosom of the Frenchman's family was the same as those he had known in the past, even to the patterns of the wallpaper and the movables. But the *jeunes*, he perceived with regret, were totally different from their forerunners. They were much more shallow and puerile, much less really clever. The secrets they wrested from the Universe were not such important and interesting secrets as had been wrested by the old *jeunes*. He believed and deplored this until one day he found himself seated at a restaurant next to a too well-fed man whom, in spite of the ravages of comfortable living, he recognized as one of the *jeunes* of his own period. This one had been wont to describe himself and three or four others as the Hermits of the New Parnassus. He and his school had talked outside cafes and elsewhere more than solitaries do as a rule; but, then, rules were what they had vowed themselves to destroy. They proclaimed that verse, in particular, was free. The Hermit of the New Parnassus was now in the Ministry of the Interior and already decorated; he expressed to Trent the opinion that what France needed most was a hand of iron. He was able to quote the exact price paid for certain betrayals of the country, of which Trent had not previously heard.

Thus he was brought to make the old discovery that it was he who had changed, like his friend of the Administration, and that *les jeunes* were still the same. Yet he found it hard to say what precisely he had lost that so greatly mattered; unless indeed it were so simple a thing as his high spirits.

One morning in June, as he descended the slope of the Rue des Martyrs, he saw approaching a figure he remembered. He glanced quickly around, for the thought of meeting Mr. Bunner again was unacceptable. For some time he had recognized that his wound was healing under the spell of creative work; he thought less often of the woman he loved, and with less pain. He would not have the memory of those three days reopened.

But the straight and narrow thoroughfare offered no refuge, and the American saw him almost at once.

His unforced geniality made Trent ashamed, for he had liked the man. They sat long over a meal, and Mr. Bunner talked. Trent listened to him, now that he was in for it, with genuine pleasure, now and then contributing a question or remark. Besides liking his companion, he enjoyed his conversation, with its unending verbal surprises, for its own sake.

Bunner was, it appeared, resident in Paris as the chief Continental agent of the Manderson firm, and fully satisfied with his position and prospects. He discoursed on these for some twenty minutes. This subject at length exhausted, he went on to tell Trent, who confessed that he had been away from England for a year, that Marlowe had shortly after the death of Manderson entered his father's business, which was now again in a flourishing state, and had already come to be practically in control of it. They had kept up their intimacy, and were even now planning a holiday for the summer. Mr. Bunner spoke with generous admiration of his friend's talent for affairs. "Jack Marlowe has a natural big head," he declared, "and if he had more experience, I wouldn't want to have him up against me. He would put a crimp in me every time."

As the American's talk flowed on, Trent listened with a slowly growing perplexity. It became more and more plain that something was very wrong in his theory of the situation; there was no mention of its central figure. Presently Mr. Bunner mentioned that Marlowe was engaged to be married to an Irish girl, whose charms he celebrated with native enthusiasm.

Trent clasped his hands savagely together beneath the table. What could have happened? His ideas were sliding and shifting. At last he forced himself to put a direct question.

Mr. Bunner was not very fully informed. He knew that Mrs. Manderson had left England immediately after the settlement of her husband's affairs, and had lived for some time in Italy. She had returned not long ago to London, where she had decided not to live in the house in Mayfair, and had bought a smaller one in the Hampstead neighborhood; also, he understood, one somewhere in the country. She was said to go but little into society. "And all the good hard dollars just waiting for some one to spraddle them around," said Mr. Bunner, with a note of pathos in his voice. "Why, she has money to burn—money to feed to the birds—and nothing doing. The old man left her more than half his wad. And think of the figure she might make in the world. She is beautiful, and she is the best woman I ever met, too. But she couldn't ever seem to get the habit of spending money the way it ought to be spent."

His words now became a soliloquy: Trent's thoughts were occupying all his attention. He pleaded business soon, and the two men parted with cordiality.

Half an hour later Trent was in his studio, swiftly and mechanically "cleaning up". He wanted to know what had happened; somehow he must find out. He could never approach her, he knew; he would never bring back to her the shame of that last encounter with him; it was scarcely likely that he would even set eyes on her. But he must get to know!... Cupples was in London, Marlowe was there.... And, anyhow, he was sick of Paris.

The two months that followed were a period in Trent's life he would never recall without a shudder. He encountered Mrs. Manderson roughly six times, and each encounter left him baffled and infuriated. Her cool friendliness, a carefully calibrated distance between mere acquaintance and the first stage of intimacy, seemed designed to drive him mad. At the opera, to his further astonishment, he found her seated with Mrs. Wallace, a frisky matron he had known since childhood. It appeared that upon her return from Italy, Mrs. Manderson had somehow drifted into social circles to which he belonged by nurture and disposition. She claimed it stemmed from pitching her tent in their shared hunting grounds; several of his friends were neighbors. Trent retained a dim, horrid memory of that occasion: he had been unlike himself, ill at ease, burning with shame, and engaging in idiotic loquacity about his adventures in the Baltic provinces, only to discover he was addressing Mrs. Wallace exclusively. The other lady, when he joined them, had completely lost the slight agitation with which she had stopped him in the vestibule. During the latter half of the opera, sitting behind them in the box, Trent had been conscious of nothing but the angle of her cheek, the mass of her hair, the lines of her shoulder and arm, her hand resting on the cushion. The next time he saw her, at a country house where both were guests, and the subsequent encounters, he had managed to keep himself in hand. He matched her manner and acquitted himself, he thought, decently enough.

Considering that he lived in an agony of bewilderment, remorse, and longing. He could make absolutely nothing of her attitude. It was beyond doubt that she had read his manuscript and understood the suspicion indicated in his final question to her at White Gables. Yet how could she treat him thus, so frankly, as she treated every man who had done her no injury?

His intuitive sense, despite the absence of any differentiation in her outward manner, had become clear: an injury had been done, and she felt it. Several times, on rare and brief occasions when they spoke apart, he received a warning from that same sense that she was approaching the subject; each time he turned the conversation with the ingenuity born of fear. He made two resolutions. The first was that upon completing a commissioned work tying him to London, he would leave and stay away. The strain was too great. He no longer burned to know the truth; he wanted nothing to confirm his fixed internal conviction by faith—that he had blundered, that he had misread the situation, misinterpreted her tears, and written himself down a slanderous fool. He speculated no more on Marlowe's motive in the killing of Manderson. Mr. Cupples returned to London, and Trent asked him nothing. He knew now that he had been right in those words, remembered for the emphasis with which they were spoken: "So long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her." He met Mrs. Manderson at dinner at her uncle's large, tomb-like house in Bloomsbury, and spent most of the evening conversing with a professor of archaeology from Berlin.

His second resolution was that he would not be with her alone.

While she celebrated the rites of tea, and for some time thereafter, she joined with such natural ease in his slightly fevered conversation on current affairs that he began to hope she had changed her resolve to corner him and speak gravely. She appeared careless now, smiling in a way that recalled, not for the first time since that night at the opera, what was written long ago of a Princess of Brunswick: "Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul." She played with a perfection of execution and feeling that moved him now as it had moved him before. "You are a musician born," he said quietly when she finished, and the last tremor of the music had passed. "I knew that before I first heard you."

Yet, when I meet these individuals face-to-face, they speak plainly. It strikes me as absurd."

"It is no absurdity to them," Trent declared, setting down his pen with a sigh of relief and rising to his full height. "Allow me to elucidate. A nation like ours, possessing a profound aversion to mental exertion, advances through life with a vocabulary that is both small and rudimentary. Consequently, long words become anomalies. Like any anomaly, they are either comical or terrifyingly solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European nation, it would pass unnoticed. Here, however, it has become a proverb; we grin upon hearing it in a speech or reading it in a headline, regarding it as a masterpiece of wit. Why? Simply because it comprises two lengthy words. The concept expressed is as mundane as boiled mutton. Then there is 'terminological inexactitude.' We still roar with laughter at that phrase! The entire jest rests solely on the length of the words. It is identical when we seek to appear profoundly serious; we signal our gravity by turning to verbose obscurity. When a solicitor opens a sentence with 'pursuant to the instructions previously communicated to our representative,' he feels he has earned his six-and-eightpence. Do not laugh! It is the absolute truth. Now, the Continental mind operates differently. They are perpetually preoccupied with ideas themselves, resulting in a lexicon where every shopkeeper or peasant possesses a vocabulary that remains utterly alien to the vast majority of Britons. I recall dining recently with a friend, a Parisian cab driver. We ate at a grimy establishment opposite the central post office, surrounded by porters and drivers. The conversation was universal, yet I felt a London cabman would have felt profoundly out of his element. Terms like 'functionary,' 'unforgettable,' 'exterminate,' and 'independence' flew across the table with alarming frequency. And these were merely ordinary, jovial, red-faced men. Mind you," he hastened to add as she moved to retrieve his pen, "I mention this solely to illustrate my point. I am not suggesting cabmen should aspire to intellectualism. I agree with Keats: happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me. But when one considers the collective industrial intellect of the nation..."

"Oh, no, no, no!" Mrs. Manderson interrupted sharply. "I know nothing at this moment save that your discourse must cease if we are to progress with this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You shall not evade this. Come!" She placed the pen firmly into his palm.

Trent regarded the instrument with distaste. "I warn you not to dampen my spirits," he said, looking dejected. "Believe me, men who remain silent are far more difficult to endure than those who chatter. Beware of mute natures. I admit, I am avoiding this task. It borders on indecency. To compose such a letter while seated in the same room as you creates a discordant mixture of moods."

She guided him back to his chair before the desk and gently pressed him down. "Very well, but please attempt it. I wish to witness your handiwork, and I desire it sent forthwith. You see, I could be content to let matters rest; yet you insist on uncovering the truth. If you must, I want it done immediately. Do it now—you possess the ability if you choose—and I shall dispatch it the moment it is finished. Have you never felt that urge? The desperate longing to thrust the troubling letter into the post, to cast it off your hands so that it cannot be recalled, rendering further fretting pointless?"

"I shall comply with your wishes," he said, turning to the paper, which he dated from his hotel. Mrs. Manderson watched him, her dark eyes holding a gentle light, as she prepared to smooth his unruly hair. But she restrained herself. Moving silently to the piano, she began to play with soft, tentative fingers. Ten minutes passed in silence before Trent spoke.

"If he chooses to reply that he will say nothing?"

Mrs. Manderson glanced over her shoulder. "Of course he dares not take that path. He will speak to prevent you from denouncing him."

"But I have no intention of doing so," Trent insisted. "You would not permit it—you said so; moreover, I shall not if you forbid it. The evidence is too tenuous now."

"But," she laughed softly, "poor Mr. Marlowe does not know you refuse, does he?"

Trent sighed deeply. "What extraordinary things codes of honor are!" he murmured, lost in abstraction. "I know there are actions I should perform without hesitation, yet which would disgrace you if I did—such as striking a man who grossly insults me, or swearing violently when I injure my shin in the dark. And now, you calmly advise me to bluff Marlowe with a tacit threat I do not intend to carry out—a maneuver that even hell's most abandoned fiend would never employ in the drunkenness of guilt. Well, I shall not do it." He resumed his writing, and the lady, wearing an indulgent smile, returned to her quiet music.

A few moments later, Trent announced, "At last, 'Yours faithfully.' Would you care to review it?" She crossed the dim room, switching on a reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning against his shoulder, she read the following:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—_You will perhaps recall that we met, under unfortunate circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone._
_On that occasion, it was my duty, as a representative of the press, to conduct an independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I did so, and I arrived at specific conclusions. You may learn from the enclosed manuscript, originally drafted as a dispatch for my newspaper, what those conclusions were. For reasons requiring no further elaboration, I decided at the last moment not to make them public, nor to communicate them to you; they are known to only two persons besides myself._

At this juncture, Mrs. Manderson raised her eyes quickly from the letter. Her dark brows knit together in concern. "Two persons?" she asked, her voice tinged with inquiry.

"Your uncle is the other," Trent explained. "I sought him out last night and revealed the entire narrative. Do you harbor any objection? I always felt uneasy withholding this from him, having led him to expect a full disclosure; my silence appeared as mystery-making. Now, it is to be resolved finally, and there is no question of shielding you. I wished him to know everything. He is a shrewd adviser in his own right, and I should prefer his presence when I confront Marlowe. I have a strong feeling that two minds will serve us better than one in this interview."

She sighed. "Yes, naturally, your uncle ought to know the truth. I hope there is no one else involved." She pressed his hand. "I so desperately wish all that horror buried—buried deep. I am very happy now, dear, but I shall be happier still once you have sated that curious mind of yours, uncovered everything, and stamped the earth down upon it all." She continued reading.

_Quite recently, however, facts have come to my knowledge which have altered my decision. I do not mean that I shall publish what I discovered, but that I have determined to approach you and request a private statement. If you possess anything to say that would cast the matter in a different light, I can imagine no reason why you should withhold it._
_I therefore expect to hear from you regarding when and where I may call upon you; unless you prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case, I desire that Mr. Cupples, whom you will remember, and who has reviewed the enclosed document, should also be present.—Faithfully yours,_

_Philip Trent._

"How very stiff!" she remarked. "Now I am certain you could not have made it any stiffer within your own chambers."

Trent slid the letter and its enclosure into a long envelope. "Indeed," he said, "I believe it will cause him to sit up abruptly. Now, this matter must not risk failure. It would be best to send a special messenger with orders to deliver it directly into his hands. If he is away, it must not be left unattended."

She nodded. "I can arrange that. Wait here for a moment."

Upon her return, he was rummaging through the music cabinet. She sank onto the carpet beside him, her dark brown skirts pooling around her. "Tell me something, Philip," she said.

"If it is among the few things I know."

"When you saw your uncle last night, did you tell him about—about us?"

"I did not," he answered. "I remembered you had said nothing of telling anyone. It is for you—is it not?—to decide whether we reveal our confidence to the world immediately or later."

"Then will you tell him?" She looked down at her clasped hands. "I wish you to tell him. Perhaps if you think you can guess why.... There! That is settled." She lifted her eyes again to his, and for a time, silence hung between them.

He leaned back in the deep chair. "What a world!" he exclaimed. "Mabel, will you play something on the piano that expresses mere joy, the genuine article, nothing feverish or like thorns under a pot, but joy that has decided in favor of the universe? It is a mood that cannot last forever, so we had better extract all we can from it."

She moved to the instrument and struck a few chords while she pondered. Then, with all her soul, she began to work the theme from the final movement of the Ninth Symphony, a melody akin to the sound of the opening of the gates of Paradise.

The room was vast, dominated by an old oaken desk with a deep body, positioned before a window that overlooked the green expanse of St. James's Park. The furnishings bore the mark of refined taste, yet the atmosphere was heavy with the solitude of a bachelor. John Marlowe unlocked the desk and withdrew a long, stout envelope from its depths.

"I understand," he addressed Mr. Cupples, "that you have read this."

"I read it for the first time two days ago," Mr. Cupples replied, seated comfortably on a sofa, his face benevolent as he surveyed the room. "We have discussed it fully."

Marlowe turned to Trent. "There is your manuscript," he said, placing the envelope on the table. "I have scrutinized it three times. I do not believe there is another man who could have extracted as much of the truth as you have set down there."

Trent ignored the compliment. He sat by the table, gazing stonily at the fire, his long legs twisted beneath his chair. "You mean, of course," he said, drawing the envelope toward him, "that there is more of the truth to be disclosed now. We are ready to hear you as soon as you like. I expect it will be a long story, and the longer the better, so far as I am concerned; I want to understand thoroughly. What we should both like, I think, is some preliminary account of Manderson and your relations with him. It seemed to me from the first that the character of the dead man must be somehow an element in the business."

"You were right," Marlowe answered grimly. He crossed the room and seated himself on a corner of the tall cushion-topped fender. "I will begin as you suggest."

"I ought to tell you beforehand," said Trent, locking eyes with him, "that although I am here to listen to you, I have not as yet any reason to doubt the conclusions I have stated here." He tapped the envelope. "It is a defense that you will be putting forward—you understand that?"

"Perfectly." Marlowe was cool and in complete possession of himself, a man transformed from the worn-out, nervous being Trent had remembered at Marlstone a year and a half ago. His tall, lithe figure was held with the perfection of muscular tone. His brow was candid, his blue eyes clear, though they still retained, as he paused to collect his thoughts, the look that had troubled Trent at their first meeting. Only the lines of his mouth revealed that he knew himself in a position of difficulty and meant to face it.

"Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind," Marlowe began in his quiet voice. "Most of the very rich men I met in America had become so by virtue of abnormal greed, or abnormal industry, or abnormal personal force, or abnormal luck. None possessed remarkable intellects. Manderson delighted in heaping up wealth; he worked incessantly at it; he was a man of dominant will; he had quite his share of luck; but what made him singular was his brainpower. In his own country, they would perhaps tell you that it was his ruthlessness in pursuit of his aims that was his most striking characteristic; but there are hundreds of them who would have carried out his plans with just as little consideration for others if they could have formed the plans.

"I'm not saying Americans aren't clever; they are ten times cleverer than we are, as a nation; but I never met another who showed such a degree of sagacity and foresight, such gifts of memory and mental tenacity, such sheer force of intelligence, as there was behind everything Manderson did in his money-making career. They called him the 'Napoleon of Wall Street' often enough in the papers; but few people knew so well as I did how much truth there was in the phrase. He seemed never to forget a fact that might be of use to him, in the first place; and he did systematically with the business facts that concerned him what Napoleon did, as I have read, with military facts. He studied them in special digests which were prepared for him at short intervals, and which he always had at hand, so that he could take up his report on coal or wheat or railways, or whatever it might be, in any unoccupied moment. Then he could make a bolder and cleverer plan than any man of them all. People got to know that Manderson would never do the obvious thing, but they got no further; the thing he did do was almost always a surprise, and much of his success flowed from that. The Street got rattled, as they used to put it, when it was known that the old man was out with his gun, and often his opponents seemed to surrender as easily as Colonel Crockett's coon in the story. The scheme I am going to describe to you would have occupied most men long enough. Manderson could have plotted the thing, down to the last detail, while he shaved himself.

"I used to think that his strain of Indian blood, remote as it was, might have something to do with the cunning and ruthlessness of the man. Strangely enough, its existence was unknown to any one but himself and me. It was when he asked me to apply my taste for genealogical work to his own obscure family history that I made the discovery that he had in him a share of the blood of the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife, a terrible woman who ruled the savage politics of the tribes of the Wilderness two hundred years ago. The Mandersons were active in the fur trade on the Pennsylvanian border in those days, and more than one of them married Indian women. Other Indian blood than Montour's may have descended to Manderson, for all I can say, through previous and subsequent unions; some of the wives' antecedents were quite untraceable, and there were so many generations of pioneering before the whole country was brought under civilization. My researches left me with the idea that there is a very great deal of the aboriginal blood present in the genealogical make-up of the people of America, and that it is very widely spread. The newer families have constantly intermarried with the older, and so many of them had a strain of the native in them—and were often rather proud of it, too, in those days. But Manderson had the idea about the disgracefulness of mixed blood, which grew much stronger, I fancy, with the rise of the negro question after the war. He was thunderstruck at what I told him, and was anxious to conceal it from every soul. Of course I never gave it away while he lived, and I don't think he supposed I would; but I have thought since that his mind took a turn against me from that time onward. It happened about a year before his death."

"Did Manderson," asked Mr. Cupples, so unexpectedly that the others started, "have any definable religious attitude?"

Marlowe considered a moment. "None that ever I heard of," he said. "Worship and prayer were quite unknown to him, so far as I could see, and I never heard him mention religion. I should doubt if he had any real sense of God at all, or if he was capable of knowing God through the emotions. But I understood that as a child he had had a religious upbringing with a strong moral side to it. His private life was, in the usual limited sense, blameless. He was almost ascetic in his habits, except as to smoking. I lived with him four years without ever knowing him to tell a direct verbal falsehood, constantly as he used to practise deceit in other forms. Can you understand the soul of a man who never hesitated to take steps that would have the effect of hoodwinking people, who would use every trick of the markets to mislead, and who was at the same time scrupulous never to utter a direct lie on the most insignificant matter? Manderson was like that, and he was not the only one. I suppose you might compare the state of mind to that of a soldier who is personally a truthful man, but who will stick at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of the game allow it; and the same may be said of business as many business men regard it. Only with them it is always wartime."

"It is a sad world," observed Mr. Cupples.

"As you say," Marlowe agreed. "Now I was saying that one could always take Manderson's word if he gave it in a definite form. The first time I ever heard him utter a downright lie was on the night he died; and hearing it, I believe, saved me from being hanged as his murderer."

Marlowe stared at the light above his head and Trent moved impatiently in his chair. "Before we come to that," he said, "will you tell us exactly on what footing you were with Manderson during the years you were with him?"

"We were on very good terms from beginning to end," answered Marlowe. "Nothing like friendship—he was not a man for making friends—but the best of terms as between a trusted employee and his chief. I went to him as private secretary just after getting my degree at Oxford. I was to have gone into my father's business, where I am now, but my father suggested that I should see the world for a year or two. So I took this secretaryship, which seemed to promise a good deal of varied experience, and I had let the year or two run on to four years before the end came. The offer came to me through the last thing in the world I should have put forward as a qualification for a salaried post, and that was chess."

At the word Trent struck his hands together with a muttered exclamation. The others looked at him in surprise.

"Chess!" repeated Trent. "Do you know," he said, rising and approaching Marlowe, "what was the first thing I noted about you at our first meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. I couldn't place it then, but I know now where I had seen your eyes before. They were in the head of no less a man than the great Nikolay Korchagin, with whom I once sat in the same railway carriage for two days. I thought I should never forget the chess eye after that, but I could not put a name to it when I saw it in you. I beg your pardon," he ended suddenly, resuming his marmoreal attitude in his chair.

"I have played the game from my childhood, and with good players," said Marlowe simply. "It is an hereditary gift, if you can call it a gift. At the University I was nearly as good as anybody there, and I gave most of my brains to that and the O.U.D.S. and playing about generally. At Oxford, as I dare say you know, inducements to amuse oneself at the expense of one's education are endless, and encouraged by the authorities. Well, one day toward the end of my last term, Dr. Munro of Queen's, whom I had never defeated, sent for me. He told me that I played a fairish game of chess. I said it was very good of him to say so. Then he said, 'They tell me you hunt, too.' I said, 'Now and then.' He asked, 'Is there anything else you can do?' 'No,' I said, not much liking the tone of the conversation—the old man generally succeeded in putting people's backs up. He grunted fiercely, and then told me that enquiries were being made on behalf of a wealthy American man of business who wanted an English secretary. Manderson was the name, he said. He seemed never to have heard it before, which was quite possible, as he never opened a newspaper and had not slept a night outside the college for thirty years. If I could rub up my spelling—as the old gentleman put it—I might have a good chance for the post, as chess and riding and an Oxford education were the only indispensable points.

"Well, I became Manderson's secretary. For a long time I liked the position greatly. When one is attached to an active American plutocrat in the prime of life one need not have many dull moments. Besides, it made me independent. My father had some serious business reverses about that time, and I was glad to be able to do without an allowance from him. At the end of the first year Manderson doubled my salary. 'It's big money,' he said, 'but I guess I don't lose.' You see, by that time I was doing a great deal more than accompany him on horseback in the morning and play chess in the evening, which was mainly what he had required. I was attending to his houses, his farm in Ohio, his shooting in Maine, his horses, his cars, and his yacht. I had become a walking railway-guide and an expert cigar-buyer. I was always learning something.

"Well, now you understand what my position was in regard to Manderson during the last two or three years of my connection with him. It was a happy life for me on the whole. I was busy, my work was varied and interesting; I had time to amuse myself too, and money to spend. At one time I made a fool of myself about a girl, and that was not a happy time; but it taught me to understand the great goodness of Mrs. Manderson." Marlowe inclined his head to Mr. Cupples as he said this. "She may choose to tell you about it. As for her husband, he had never varied in his attitude towards me, in spite of the change that came over him in the last months of his life, as you know. He treated me well and generously in his unsympathetic way, and I never had a feeling that he was less than satisfied with his bargain—that was the sort of footing we lived upon. And it was that continuance of his attitude right up to the end that made the revelation so shocking when I was suddenly shown, on the night on which he met his end, the depth of crazy hatred of myself that was in Manderson's soul."

The eyes of Trent and Mr. Cupples met for an instant.

"You never suspected that he hated you before that time?" asked Trent; and Mr. Cupples asked at the same moment, "To what did you attribute it?"

"Speak lower!" Trent urged. "The head-waiter has a weak heart. Milk and soda! Cupples, you may think you have a strong constitution, and I grant you that, but I warn you this habit has killed many a robust man. Be wise. Trent, a respected customer, ordered wine he knew well. "If you have taken a vow, drink the water at your elbow in the name of all teetotal saints, but do not seek cheap notoriety by demanding milk and soda."

"I have never taken a pledge," Cupples said, examining his mutton favorably. "I simply don't care for wine. I bought a bottle once to see what it was like, and it made me ill. But it was likely bad wine. I will taste yours, as it is your dinner. I assure you, Trent, I should like to do something unusual to show how strongly I feel. I haven't been this delighted in years. To think—the Manderson mystery resolved, the innocent exculpated, and your happiness with Mabel crowned—all at once! I drink to you, my dear friend." He took a tiny sip.

"You have a great nature," Trent said, deeply moved. "Your outward semblance belies your soul's immensity. I expected to see an elephant conducting the opera before you drank my health. Dear Cupples! May his beak retain that delicate rose-stain forever!—No, curse it all!" He broke out, surprising a flicker of discomfort on Cupples' face as he tasted the wine again. "I have no business meddling with your tastes. I apologize. You shall have what you want, even if it causes the head-waiter to perish in his pride."

Once Cupples was supplied with his monastic drink and the waiter had retreated, Trent looked across the table with significance. "In this babble of many conversations, we can speak as freely as on a bare hillside. The waiter is whispering to the young woman at the pay-desk. We are alone. What do you think of that interview this afternoon?"

Without pausing to cut his mutton, Cupples replied, "The most curious feature was the irony. We both held the clue to that mad hatred of Manderson's which Marlowe found so mysterious. We knew of his jealous obsession; we withheld that knowledge, properly, for Mabel's sake. Marlowe will never know what he was suspected of. Strange! Nearly all of us move unconsciously through a network of opinions others hold about us. I recall discovering years ago that many believed I had secretly joined the Roman Catholic Church. This absurd fiction stemmed from my advocacy of weekly meat abstinence. Manderson's belief regarding his secretary probably rested on much slighter ground. It was Mr. Bunner, I think, who told you of his hereditary temper of suspicious jealousy. Regarding Marlowe's story, it appeared straightforward to me, not especially remarkable once we admit we are dealing with a disordered mind."

Trent laughed loudly. "I confess the affair struck me as unusual."

"Only in the development of details," Cupples argued. "What is abnormal in the essential facts? A madman conceives a crazy suspicion; he hatches a plot against his fancied injurer involving his own destruction. Put thus, what is remarkable to anyone who knows lunatics? Turn to Marlowe. He finds himself in peril where truth won't save him. He escapes via bold deception. That seems to happen daily." He attacked his mutton, now unrecognizable.

"I should like to know," Trent said after a pause, "if there is anything on earth you couldn't represent as ordinary by such an argument."

A gentle smile touched Cupples' face. "Do not suspect me of empty paradox. My meaning will be clearer if I mention things I find essentially remarkable. Let me see... the life history of the liver-fluke, thanks to Poulton's researches."

"I am unable to argue the point," Trent replied. "Science may have smiled on the liver-fluke's birth, but I never heard it mentioned."

"It is not an appetizing subject," Cupples thoughtfully conceded. "All I mean is that remarkable things happen all around us if we see them; we do our perceptions no credit by regarding only those surrounded by sensational detail as remarkable."

Trent applauded with his knife handle. "I haven't heard you like this for years. You must be almost as above yourself as I am. It is a bad case of the unrest men miscall delight. But I won't sit still and hear the Manderson affair dismissed as commonplace. You may say what you like, but impersonating Manderson was an extraordinarily ingenious idea."

"Ingenious—certainly!" Cupples replied. "Extraordinarily so—no! In those circumstances, it was not strange that a clever man should conceive it. It lay almost on the surface. Marlowe was famous for imitating Manderson's voice; he had a talent for acting, a chess-player's mind, and intimate knowledge of the establishment. I grant the execution was brilliant, but everything favored it. As for the essential idea, I do not place it in the same class of ingenuity as utilizing recoil to eject and reload a firearm. I admit, however, that the details possessed unusual features. It developed a high degree of complexity."

"Did it really strike you that way?" Trent asked with desperate sarcasm.

"The affair became complicated," Cupples continued unmoved, "because after Marlowe's suspicions were awakened, a second subtle mind interfered with the first. Such duels happen in business and politics, but less frequently in crime."

"I should say never," Trent replied. "The reason is that even clever criminals seldom run to strategic subtlety. When they do, they don't get caught, since clever policemen have less strategic subtlety than the ordinary clever criminal. But that deep quality seems rarely to accompany the criminal make-up. Look at Crippen. He was a very clever criminal; he solved the central problem of clandestine murder, the disposal of the body, with extreme neatness. But how far did he see through the game? Criminals and policemen are often swift, bold tacticians, but neither is good for more than a simple plan. It's a rare faculty in any walk of life."

"One disturbing reflection remains," Cupples said, seemingly tired of abstractions. "If Marlowe had suspected nothing and walked into the trap, he would almost certainly have been hanged. How often may not a plan to fix murder on an innocent person have succeeded? There are surely cases where the accused, found guilty on circumstantial evidence, died protesting innocence. I shall never again approve of a death sentence based solely on such evidence."

"I never have," Trent agreed. "To hang in such cases seems to fly in the face of the obvious principle: 'you never can tell.' I agree with the American jurist who says we should not hang a yellow dog for stealing jam on circumstantial evidence, not even if he has jam on his nose. Attempts to fix crimes on innocent men happen constantly. It's a marked feature of coercive rule, whether in Ireland, Russia, India, or Korea; if police cannot catch a man by fair means, they do it by foul. But there is a peculiar case in the State Trials: the Campden Case. The plotter gave up his own life to secure the death of his victims. Probably you've heard of it."

Cupples confessed his ignorance and took another potato.

"John Masefield wrote a remarkable play about it," Trent said. "If it comes to London, you should see it. Women weep undemonstratively at oleo-margarine sentiment in the theatre; imagine their hysterics at that play! The facts were that John Perry accused his mother and brother of murdering a man, swearing he helped them. He told a detailed story with answers to everything, except the curious fact that the body couldn't be found. The judge, probably drunk in Restoration days, made nothing of that. The mother and brother denied it. All three were found guilty and hanged on John's evidence. Two years later, the man they were hanged for murdering returned from the sea, kidnapped by pirates. His disappearance gave John his idea. The point is that John's inclusion of himself in the accusation, amounting to suicide, convinced everyone of its truth. It was so obvious that no man would die to get someone else hanged. That is exactly the answer the prosecution would have made if Marlowe had told the truth. Not one juryman in a million would have believed in the Manderson plot."

Cupples mused. "I have no acquaintance with that branch of history. But childhood recollections return. We know the spiritual truth underlying this: the insane depth of Manderson's jealous hatred. We can understand he was capable of such a scheme. But usually, justice breaks down in penetrating spiritual truth. Sometimes it is deliberately concealed, as in Manderson's case.
