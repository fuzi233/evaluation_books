When Romola reached the entrance of San Marco, a friar waited there, expecting her arrival. Monna Brigida withdrew into the adjacent church, and Romola was escorted to the door of the chapter-house in the outer cloister, the limit beyond which no woman was permitted to enter.

As the door swung open, the soft external light merged with the glow of two tapers set behind a wheeled bed. This illumination revealed the emaciated face of Fra Luca. Above it sat the tonsured crown of golden hair, and deep-sunken hazel eyes were fixed upon a small crucifix held before him. He was propped up in a near-seating position. As Romola threw aside her veil, she became acutely aware of another monk standing beside the bed, his black cowl pulled tight over his head; he moved toward the door as she entered. In the background, a crucified form rose high and pale against the frescoed wall, its lower half framed by pale, sorrowful faces.

In the next instant, her eyes met Fra Luca's as they lifted from the crucifix. She was seized by a pang of recognition that fused this monastic, wasted frame with the memory of her fair young brother.

"Dino!" she cried, her voice sounding like a low wail of pain.

Yet she did not lean toward him. She remained upright, pausing at a distance of two yards. An unconquerable repulsion gripped her regarding his monkish appearance. To her, it seemed the mark of cowardly ingratitude that had left her father desolate—a sign of groveling superstition capable of branding such disloyalty as "piety." Her father, a man of proud sincerity and simple living, had been one of the few frank pagans of his age. He had raised her with a silent disregard for any claim the Church might have on regulating the beliefs and actions of those endowed with cultivated reason. In her mind, the Church belonged to the chaotic mass of people from whom they had always separated themselves. Consequently, she possessed no framework of thought that could view her brother's path anything other than with incurious, indignant contempt.

Still, the loving nature of Romola's soul had clung to that image of her brother in the past. While she stood rigidly aloof, a yearning search burned in her eyes for something too faint to discern clearly. But there was no corresponding emotion in the face of the monk. He gazed at the younger sister, returned to him in her full womanly beauty, with the distant look of a spirit revisiting a former life.

"My sister!" he said, his voice feeble, interrupted, yet distinct. "It is well that you have not delayed your coming further, for I have a message to deliver to you, and my time is short."

Romola took a step closer. She assumed the message would be one of affectionate penitence addressed to her father, and her heart began to soften. Nothing could erase the long years of abandonment; yet, seeing the culprit reflect on those years with a sense of irreparable wrong, would surely evoke pity. At this final hour, there would be understanding and forgiveness. Dino would pour out natural filial feelings; he would inquire about his father's blindness—how swiftly it had descended, how the long dark days had been endured, what life existed now in the home where he himself had been nurtured. The final words from the dying lips would surely be ones of tenderness and regret.

"Romola," Fra Luca began, "I have had a vision concerning you. Three times in the last two months I have experienced it, and each time it grew clearer. Thus, I journeyed from Fiesole, convinced it was a mandate from heaven that I must deliver. And I draw a promise of mercy from this fact, that my breath is preserved specifically to—"

The difficult breathing that constantly broke his speech prevented him from finishing. Romola felt her heart chill anew. It was a vision, then—this message. One of those visions her father had often alluded to with bitter resentment. Indignation surged to her lips.

"Dino, I thought you had some words to send to my father! You deserted him when his sight failed; you made his life utterly desolate. Did you never care? Never repent? What is this religion of yours that places visions ahead of natural duties?"

The deep-sunken hazel eyes turned slowly toward her, resting upon her in silence for several moments, as if weighing whether to answer.

"No," he said finally, speaking in the same low, passionless tone, as though some non-human spirit were using dying human organs. "No; I have never repented for fleeing from the stifling, poisonous air of sin that was hot and thick around me, threatening to overwhelm my senses like intoxicating wine. My father could not hear the voice calling me night and day; he knew nothing of the demonic tempters trying to drag me back from following it. My father lived amidst human sin and misery without believing in them. He was like one busily picking shining stones in a mine, unaware that a world was dying of plague above him. I spoke, but he listened with scorn. I told him the studies he wished me to pursue were either childish trifles—dead toys—or else they must be enlivened by pulses beating to worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts, for these worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts constituted the entire substance of the poetry and history he demanded I contemplate continually."

"Has not my father led a pure and noble life, then?" Romola burst out, unable to remain silent while hearing this implied accusation against her father.

"He has sought no worldly honors; he has been truthful; he has denied himself all luxuries; he has lived like one of the ancient sages. He never wished you to live for worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts. He wished you to live as he himself has done, according to the purest maxims of philosophy, in which he raised you."

Romola spoke partly from rote, as ardent and sympathetic young creatures often do, but she spoke with intense conviction. A pink flush colored her face, and she quivered from head to toe. Her brother was slow to reply, regarding her passionate countenance with strangely impassive eyes.

"What were the maxims of philosophy to me? They commanded me to be strong when I felt weak; when I was ready, like the blessed Saint Benedict, to roll myself among thorns and court smarting wounds as a deliverance from temptation. For the Divine Love had sought me, penetrated me, and created a great void within me, like a seed needing room to grow. I had been raised in careless indifference to the true faith; I had not studied the doctrines of our religion. Yet it seemed to seize me like a rising flood. I felt there existed a life of perfect love and purity for the soul—a existence free from uneasy hunger for pleasure, free from tormenting questions, free from fear of suffering. Even before knowing the history of the saints, I had a foretaste of their ecstasy. For the same truth permeated pagan philosophy: that it is a bliss within man's reach to die to mortal needs and live in the life of God as the Unseen Perfection. But to attain this, I must forsake the world. I must have no affection, no hope binding me to that which passes away. I must relate to my fellow-beings only as human souls connected to the eternal, unseen life.

"That need urged me continually. It overwhelmed me in visions when my mind grew weary from the vain words recording the passions of dead men. It seized me after I had been tempted into sin and turned away with loathing from the scent of the emptied cup. And in visions, I perceived the meaning of the Crucifix."

He paused, breathing heavily for a minute or two, but Romola felt no impulse to speak again. It was futile for her mind to attempt contact with the mind of this unearthly brother; as useless as trying to grasp a shadow. When he spoke again, his heaving chest was calmer.

"I felt whom I must follow. But I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who professed to have renounced the world, my soul would be stifled by the fumes of hypocrisy, lust, and pride. God had not chosen me, as He chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil within the Church and the world. He called upon me to flee. I took the sacred vows, and I fled—I fled to lands where danger, scorn, and want bore me continually, like angels, to repose on the bosom of God. I have lived the life of a hermit, ministering to pilgrims. But my task has been brief: the veil dividing me from my everlasting rest has grown exceedingly thin. I returned to Florence that—"

"Dino, you *did* want to know if my father was alive," Romola interrupted, the picture of that suffering life stirring in her again the desire for union and forgiveness.

"—That before I die, I might urge other brothers of our order to study Eastern tongues, as I failed to do, and to pursue greater ends than I did. And I find them already bent on this work. Since I came, Romola, I have felt I was sent partly to you—not to renew the bonds of earthly affection, but to deliver the heavenly warning conveyed in a vision. For I have had that vision thrice. Through all the years since first the Divine Voice called me while I was still in the world, I have been taught and guided by visions.
