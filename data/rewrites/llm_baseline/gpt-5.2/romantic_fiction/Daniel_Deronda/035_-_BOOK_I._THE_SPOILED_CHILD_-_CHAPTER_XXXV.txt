If conscience were weighed out in due proportion to wrongdoing, the chronicles of our kind would read like a different music altogether: the great architects of hunger and ruin would be the ones kept awake by their own inward knives, and the petty thief would sleep as soundly as a well-fed child. Yet wickedness has its wages; and the terrible secret is that the human creature is rarely made of a single metal. Most men and women are compounded—capable of harm, yet still shadowed by a fear that resembles justice, and a pity that resembles love. Those faint resemblances are enough to spoil the pure serenity of cruelty. They are enough to make even the well-dressed wrong-doer start at his own footsteps in a corridor.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Daniel Deronda knew the Grandcourts had reached the Abbey; but up to the moment he went upstairs to dress for dinner he had not once seen them. The day had brought a splendid fall of snow, deep and unbroken in the park, whitening even the cedar boughs so that they drooped like heavy-laden platters. The children had made a festival of it—snow-balls that burst in soft floury clouds, a fort rising with earnest little hands packing and smoothing as if they were shaping dough, and “cousin” Deronda pressed into service because no game felt complete unless he planned it, judged it, or was pelted by it. Afterward there had been billiards; the click of balls, the muffled swish of the cue, and the subdued laughter—those civilized sounds that never quite become noise in an old house—carried the afternoon away without his dwelling on the evening’s encounter.

But in his room, with the hush that comes before the half-hour bell, the prospect of meeting Gwendolen returned to him and began to work like a spice warmed in the palm—subtle, inevitable. He found himself asking what marriage to Grandcourt would do to her; whether there would be any new shade upon her manner since Diplow, as there had been since that first vision of her at Leubronn, bright as a flame above the roulette-table.

“I fancy there are natures one could see growing—or degenerating—every day, if one watched them,” he thought, fastening a cuff with a patience that did not quiet his mind. “Some of us go on faster than others. And she keeps strong traces of anything that has once impressed her. That business of the necklace—and merely the knowledge that somebody disapproved her play—had bitten in. But an impressible creature may be driven either way: to desperation as readily as to anything better. And whatever fascinations Grandcourt may have for caprice—good heavens! who can believe he would call out tender affection in daily companionship? One might want to strike him only to see if he could show a sign of feeling. I’m afraid she married him from ambition—to escape poverty. Yet she fled him at first… Poor thing! she may have been pressed into it. How can one feel anything else than pity for a young creature like that—full of unused life—hanging blind expectation on that remnant of a human being?”

The harsher his inner phrases for the bridegroom, the more they seemed to wrap and soften the bride in excuse. He had no intimate knowledge of Grandcourt—only that ordinary polite contact which had left the impression of a man whose natural interest in the world had been worn away, as if by long handling, until only the smooth outer surface remained.

And it is a common arrangement in society’s secret ledger: when a marriage of note is made, the men are apt to pity the bride and the women the bridegroom. Each is thought to have done worse than might have been; and if the bride is young and charming, young men on the spot commonly conclude she cannot really love a husband so uninteresting to themselves, but has chosen him for reasons that taste like money or position. Who pities the husband? Even his female friends often take his situation as a kind of reckoning—he might have chosen otherwise.

Deronda, perhaps, was excusable for saving no pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck anyone as likely to take more pain from life than he gave it; but for Gwendolen—young, eager, used to the flattery that makes a lovely girl believe the world owes her obedience—how swiftly might life turn from bright expectancy into the bitter knowledge of what cannot be undone? Deronda would have had to be dull indeed not to feel some interest in her entrance. Yet the honeymoon already lay three weeks behind; she had been enthroned at Ryelands and at Diplow; she was not one to expose helplessly whatever went on within her. She would have learned how to wear her face—what to show, what to hide.

A various party had been invited to meet the new couple. The old aristocracy was there in Lord and Lady Pentreath; the old gentry in young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam from the Worcestershire branch; politics and “the public good” as expressed in cider, in Mr. Fenn, member for West Orchards, with his two daughters; Lady Mallinger’s family in her brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife; and the useful bachelor element in Mr. Sinker, the eminent counsel, and Mr. Vandernoodt—pleasant enough at Leubronn to be adopted by Sir Hugo in England.

They were all assembled in the drawing-room before the Grandcourts appeared. The time was chiefly spent in noticing the children—little Raymonds among Lady Mallinger’s three girls—who were always allowed to make part of this hour. The scene itself had a rich domestic splendour: cedar paneling pierced with full-length portraits set in deep backgrounds; a ceiling glowing with coats of arms ranged between the sockets; pale wax-lights softened and warmed by the great red fire of oak-boughs; the deep-piled carpet swallowing sharp footfalls so that conversation seemed to float without edge or clatter. There was that peculiar acoustic of old rooms well-draped—sound held in gentleness, as if the house itself insisted that voices behave. It was an environment that nourished the nerves: no metallic ringing, no thin modern echo; only the low crackle of wood and the occasional childish murmur checked by breeding.

Lady Mallinger, fair, matronly, in black velvet, moved about with a tiny white dog on her arm as if it were the last flourish of her costume. The children were scattered among the ladies; most of the gentlemen stood a little aloof, making those moderate remarks that bridge the long minutes before dinner.

Deronda was drawn aside by Mr. Vandernoodt, a close-clipped, pale-eyed man, nonchalant and commodious, of good Dutch blood imported at the Revolution—one of those who are nothing in particular, but are understood to have access to everything in particular. He made an excellent foil to Deronda’s vivid gravity, and he was now talking of the bride and bridegroom with the industrious air of a gatherer of personal tidbits.

“Men who have seen a good deal of life don’t always end by choosing their wives so well,” Vandernoodt murmured. “He has had rather an anecdotic history—gone rather deep into pleasures, I fancy, lazy as he is. But, of course, you know all about him.”

“No, really,” Deronda answered, with an indifference that was honest. “I know little more than that he is Sir Hugo’s nephew.”

At that moment the door opened, cutting off further confidences. The room, with its firelight and portraits and hushed voices, was made to set off any figure of distinction; and when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt entered, no one could deny they had it.

Grandcourt was exactly as before marriage: neither more nor less perfect in costume, neither more nor less cut and finished in impassibility. A man like that would accept nothing under the best in outward equipment, wife included; and the bride was precisely what he might be expected to choose. “By George, I think she’s handsomer, if anything!” Vandernoodt whispered. Deronda thought so too, though he said nothing.

She wore white silk and diamonds—diamonds at her throat, in her ears, in her hair—sparkling coldly, like frost that has caught the firelight. The new splendour seemed to make her beauty flash into a more unquestionable authority, even if it did not make it more satisfying. Some faces, in their peculiar beauty, are like original works of art: the first encounter brings a question; the second, perhaps, a startled assent. At Diplow he had seen more than he expected of that tender, appealing charm called womanly. Was there a change now? He distrusted his own impressions; yet as he watched her receive greetings with proud, quiet coldness and a superficial smile, it seemed to him that within she carried the same demonic force he had felt at Leubronn—the resolute glance, the turning away as a loser, as if wounded pride could be transmuted into scorn.

There was no time to conclude more. Before Deronda could even approach for a proper greeting, the summons to dinner went round them like a bell of command.

He sat not far from opposite her at table. Sometimes he caught what she said in answer to Sir Hugo, who was at his liveliest with her, glad to show off hospitality as if it were a well-roasted joint offered with a flourish. But though Deronda turned his eyes toward her with the intention of bowing, she gave him no opening for some time—her face always angled away, her attention so precisely managed that recognition seemed postponed by design.

At last Sir Hugo, who might have imagined they had spoken already, said, “Deronda, you will like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favourite Klesmer.”

Gwendolen’s eyelids had been lowered; Deronda, looking toward her, thought he saw a quivering reluctance as she was obliged to raise them and return his unembarrassed bow and smile. Her own smile moved only her lips, as if the rest of her face refused it. It lasted but an instant; Sir Hugo went on without pause.

“The Arrowpoints have condoned the marriage, and he is spending Christmas with his bride at Quetcham.”

“I suppose he will be glad of it for the sake of his wife,” said Deronda; “else I dare say he would not have minded keeping at a distance.”

“It’s a sort of troubadour story,” said Lady Pentreath, deep-voiced and easy. “I’m glad to find a little romance left among us. Our young people now are getting too worldly wise.”

“It shows the Arrowpoints’ good sense, however, to have adopted the affair after the fuss in the paper,” Sir Hugo added. “Disowning your own child for a _mésalliance_ is something like disowning your one eye: everybody knows it’s yours, and you’ve no other to make an appearance with.”

“As to _mésalliance_, there’s no blood on any side,” said Lady Pentreath. “Old Admiral Arrowpoint was one of Nelson’s men—a doctor’s son. And we all know how the mother’s money came.”

“If there were any _mésalliance_, I should say it was on Klesmer’s side,” said Deronda.

“Ah, you think it’s the immortal marrying the mortal. What is your opinion?” Sir Hugo asked, turning to Gwendolen.

“I have no doubt Herr Klesmer thinks himself immortal,” she said coolly. “But I dare say his wife will burn as much incense before him as he requires.”

“Don’t you approve of a wife burning incense before her husband?” Sir Hugo returned, jocose.

“Oh, yes—if it were only to make others believe in him,” Gwendolen said. Then, with a little lift of gaiety, “When Herr Klesmer admires his own genius, it will take off some of the absurdity if his wife says Amen.”

“Klesmer is no favourite of yours, I see,” Sir Hugo said.

“I think very highly of him, I assure you,” she answered, with sudden seriousness, as if correcting herself. “His genius is quite above my judgment, and I know him to be exceedingly generous.”

Deronda wondered, as he listened, what he should have thought of her had he known nothing of her before—perhaps that she wore hardness and defiance as a shield over painful consciousness. Yet why did she manage her recognition of him with such deliberate chill?

Sir Hugo, changing the subject, turned to the room itself. “Isn’t this a beautiful room? It was part of the refectory of the Abbey. There was a division made by those pillars and the three arches, and afterward they were built up. Else it was half as large again originally. There used to be rows of Benedictines sitting where we are sitting. Suppose we were suddenly to see the lights burning low, and the ghosts of the old monks rising behind all our chairs!”

“Please don’t!” Gwendolen cried, with a playful shudder that had a sharpness in it. “It is very nice to come after ancestors and monks—but they should know their places and keep underground. I should be rather frightened to go about this house all alone. I suppose the old generations must be angry with us because we have altered things so much.”

“Oh, the ghosts must be of all political parties,” Sir Hugo said. “Those fellows who wanted to change things while they lived and couldn’t do it must be on our side. But if you would not like to go over the house alone, you will like to go in company, I hope. You and Grandcourt ought to see it all. And we will ask Deronda to go round with us. He is more learned about it than I am.”

Gwendolen stole a glance toward Deronda. He must have heard; his face was turned in their direction as he helped himself to an entrée, but his look might have been painted—so still, so unreadable. At the thought of Deronda showing her and Grandcourt the place that would be theirs—and that she remembered, with a painful emphasis, might have been his—certain old thoughts rushed in upon her, not new in themselves, but newly placed, newly embarrassing. She felt something furtive in her own glance, something that might betray too much. With her quick resource against betrayal, she said playfully, “You don’t know how much I am afraid of Mr. Deronda.”

“How’s that? Because you think him too learned?” Sir Hugo asked, missing nothing.

“No. Ever since I first saw him at Leubronn. When he came to look on at the roulette-table I began to lose. He cast an evil eye on my play. He didn’t approve it. He has told me so. And now, whatever I do before him, I am afraid he will cast an evil eye on it.”

“Gad! I’m rather afraid of him myself when he doesn’t approve,” Sir Hugo said, and glanced at Deronda. Then, lowering his voice with his chronic facetiousness, he added to Gwendolen, “I don’t think ladies generally object to have his eyes upon them.”

“I object to any eyes that are critical,” she said in a cool, high voice, turning her neck as if to throw off a touch. “Are there many of these old rooms left in the Abbey?”

“Not many. There is a fine cloistered court with a long gallery above it. But the finest bit of all is turned into stables—part of the old church. When I improved the place I made the most of every other bit; but it was out of my reach to change the stables. So the horses have the benefit of the fine old choir. You must go and see it.”

“I shall like to see the horses as well as the building,” said Gwendolen.

“Oh, I have no stud to speak of,” Sir Hugo answered. “Grandcourt will look with contempt at my horses. I’ve given up hunting, and go on in a jog-trot way, as becomes an old gentleman with daughters. The fact is, I went in for doing too much at this place. We all lived at Diplow two years while the alterations were going on. Do you like Diplow?”

“Not particularly,” said Gwendolen, with an indifference that sounded as if she had always had more houses than she could digest.

“Ah! it will not do after Ryelands,” Sir Hugo returned, well pleased. “Grandcourt took it for the hunting. But he found something so much better there,” he added, lowering his voice with a pleased smile, “that he might well prefer it to any other place in the world.”

“It has one attraction for me,” said Gwendolen, passing over the compliment with a chill smile, “that it is within reach of Offendene.”

“I understand that,” Sir Hugo said, and let the subject fall.

An amiable baronet, for all his good humour, is not made of stone; a strong desire for a particular possession will press on his spirits like a thumb on a bruised fruit. Sir Hugo would have been glad that Grandcourt, with or without reason, should prefer any other place to Diplow; and because wishing is so deft at dressing itself as benevolence, he even wished Grandcourt’s disgust for Diplow might not be associated, in his own mind, with this marriage to so charming a bride. Gwendolen was much to his taste; later he observed to Lady Mallinger that he should never have taken her for a young girl who had married beyond her expectations.

Deronda had not caught much of this talk, his attention being elsewhere; but what he did see of Gwendolen—the careful angles of her face, the managed coolness, the sudden sharpenings—deepened his impression that there was something newly artificial in her, as if she had taken up a part and was determined not to let it slip.

Later, in the drawing-room, someone asked Deronda to sing. He sat at the piano and did so. The instrument gave out its sound with that mellow restraint which old rooms breed—no harsh brightness, no jangling reverberation, only a rounded tone that seemed to settle into the velvet and wood as naturally as warmth into bread. Afterward Mrs. Raymond took his place; and when Deronda rose, he observed that Gwendolen had left her seat and come to the far end of the room, as if to listen more fully. She stood with her back to everyone, apparently contemplating a fine cowled head carved in ivory that hung above a small table.

He longed to go to her and speak. Why should he not obey the impulse as he would toward any other woman present? Yet he hesitated, watching the graceful line of her back, the stillness with which she held herself—still as if she were listening not to the music but to the house’s own stored whispers. The fire made a faint, steady undertone; voices came and went like softened birds in a winter thicket.

If you have any reason for not indulging a wish to speak to a fair woman, it is a bad plan to look long at her back. The wish to see what it screens grows stronger. There may be, on the other side, a smile that would undo your scruples.

Deronda went at last to the end of the little table, standing at right angles to her. Before he could begin, she turned—no smile at all, but an appealing look of sadness, so different from the chill effort she had made at table, that his prepared words fell away. For a space of time that felt large to both, though it would have measured as nothing to the room, they looked at each other: she as if taking a deep rest in confession, he answering with a sympathy that, for the moment, neutralized everything else.

“Will you not join in the music?” he said at last, taking refuge in the obvious.

The look of confession had been involuntary; a just perceptible shake and change passed over her, as if she roused herself from a dangerous sleep.

“I join in it by listening,” she replied calmly. “I am fond of music.”

“Are you not a musician?”

“I have given a great deal of time to music. But I have not talent enough to make it worth while. I shall never sing again.”

“But if you are fond of music, it will always be worth while in private—for your own delight. I make it a virtue to be content with my middlingness,” Deronda said, smiling. “It is always pardonable, so long as one does not ask others to take it for superiority.”

“I cannot imitate you,” she said, recovering that tone of artificial vivacity which sat on her like a bright, tight glove. “To be middling with me is another phrase for being dull. And the worst fault I have to find with the world is that it is dull. Do you know, I am going to justify gambling in spite of you. It is a refuge from dullness.”

“I don’t admit the justification,” Deronda answered. “I think what we call the dullness of things is a disease in ourselves. Else how can anyone find an intense interest in life? And many do.”

“Ah, I see! The fault I find in the world is my own fault,” she said, smiling at him as if it were a clever retort and nothing more. Then, after a moment, lifting her eyes again toward the ivory head, she asked, “Do you never find fault with the world—or with others?”

“Oh, yes. When I am in a grumbling mood.”

“And hate people?” she insisted, her voice tightening. “Confess you hate them when they stand in your way—when their gain is your loss. That is your own phrase, you know.”

“We are often standing in each other’s way when we can’t help it,” Deronda said. “I think it is stupid to hate people on that ground.”

“But if they injure you—and could have helped it?” she said, and there was a hard intensity in her words, unaccountable in talk meant to be casual.

A painful impression held back his answer for an instant; then he said, with a graver, deeper intonation, “Why, then, after all, I prefer my place to theirs.”

“There I believe you are right,” she said, with a sudden little laugh that rang too quickly and ended too soon, as if it had been summoned to cover something. And she turned away to rejoin the group at the piano.
