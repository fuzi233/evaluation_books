Carmilla refused to allow any attendant to sleep inside her room, so my father arranged for a servant to keep watch outside her door—ensuring she could not slip away unnoticed again. The night passed without incident, but the next morning brought an unexpected visitor.

Early, before I had even stirred from bed, the doctor arrived. I had not been told of his summoning, and when Madame led me to the library, I found the elderly physician waiting—the same solemn little man with his white hair and spectacles. His expression darkened as I recounted my recent experiences, the nightmares, the spectral visitations, the inexplicable weakness. He listened intently, his gaze locked onto mine with a mixture of fascination and dread.

When I finished, he leaned against the window recess, silent for a long moment. Then, without a word to me, he requested an audience with my father.

Father entered with his usual cheer, jesting that the doctor would surely call him a fool for dragging him out on a trivial matter. But his smile died when he saw the physician’s grave face. They withdrew into the same recess where I had stood moments before, their voices hushed, their postures tense. Madame and I lingered at the far end of the room, straining uselessly to catch a whisper of their exchange. The thick stone walls swallowed their words whole.

When my father finally turned back to us, his face was ashen. He called me forward, his voice carefully controlled.

"Laura, come here. Madame, you needn’t stay—the doctor has instructions for now."

I approached, my pulse quickening. I did not feel ill, only drained, as though something had been leeching the life from me in slow, insidious drafts.

The doctor’s questions were precise, clinical. He asked about the needle-like pain in my neck, the choking sensation, the cold currents that had shuddered through me. Then, with unsettling focus, he directed my father to examine a spot just below my throat.

My collar was adjusted slightly. A breath of cold air brushed my skin.

"God above," my father whispered.

I twisted, trying to see. "What is it?"

"A mark," the doctor said, his voice low. "A small blue spot. No larger than the tip of your finger."

Fear coiled in my stomach. "Is it dangerous?"

The doctor hesitated. "Not if treated. But precautions must be taken."

Precautions. The word hung in the air, heavy with unspoken meaning. Madame was instructed never to leave me alone—not for a moment. The insistence of it prickled the back of my neck.

Father then mentioned another patient—Carmilla. Her symptoms, he said, were milder but similar. The doctor agreed to return that evening to examine her.

They left together, pacing the grounds in deep discussion. I watched from the window as the doctor rode away, his figure swallowed by the dark fringe of the forest.

Madame and I exchanged uneasy glances. She suspected a sudden fit might strike me, some violent seizure requiring immediate aid. I hoped it was merely an overabundance of caution—a fear of fainting spells or some girlish ailment.

Father returned later with a letter in hand, his mood altered. General Spielsdorf was coming, though the news brought no joy to my father’s face. If anything, he seemed to resent the timing.

"Papa," I pressed, "what did the doctor say is wrong with me?"

"Nothing you need fret over," he replied, sharper than I’d ever heard him. Then, softening, he added, "You’ll know soon enough. For now, trust me."

Before I could press further, he announced we would ride to Karnstein at noon—Madame and I accompanying him, while Carmilla would follow later with Mademoiselle for a picnic amidst the ruins.

The journey was beautiful, the road winding through untamed woods and rolling hills. But beauty could not distract me from the unease gnawing at my thoughts.

Then, rounding a bend, we met the General himself on horseback. After courteous greetings, he joined us in the carriage, his servant leading his horse ahead.

And though the sun shone bright above us, I could not shake the chill that had settled deep in my bones.
