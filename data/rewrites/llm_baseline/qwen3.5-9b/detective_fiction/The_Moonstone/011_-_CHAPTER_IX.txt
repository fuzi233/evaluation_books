June twenty-first, the day of the birthday, dawned cloudy and unsettled, but by noon the sky had cleared with remarkable bravery. In the servants' hall, we commenced this annual celebration as custom dictates: presenting our modest gifts to Miss Rachel, accompanied by the traditional speech delivered yearly by me as the senior steward. I adhere to the strategy employed by Her Majesty when addressing Parliament—a method of delivering essentially the same sentiments annually. Prior to delivery, my address is awaited with the fervor reserved for groundbreaking revelations; yet once spoken, and found wanting in novelty despite expectations, the household grumbles briefly before turning hopeful eyes toward next year's potential innovation. An easily governed populace, whether in the House of Commons or the kitchen—is the lesson learned.

Following breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I held a private consultation regarding the Moonstone. The time had arrived to transfer the gem from the bank at Frizinghall into Miss Rachel's personal custody. Whether Mr. Franklin had attempted renewed courtship of his cousin and met with rejection, or whether his chronic sleeplessness was exacerbating the erratic fluctuations in his temperament, remains unclear. Certain, however, is that on the morning of the birthday, Mr. Franklin displayed none of his usual composure. Within moments, he oscillated through twenty distinct opinions concerning the diamond. I, conversely, remained steadfast upon the established facts: no incident warranted alarming my lady regarding the jewel, and the legal mandate compelling Mr. Franklin to deliver it to his cousin remained unaltered. Though he wrestled with the decision, logic eventually compelled him to adopt my perspective. We agreed that after lunch, he would ride to Frizinghall to retrieve the Diamond, likely returning with Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies to accompany him home.

With arrangements finalized, Mr. Franklin returned to Miss Rachel. They occupied the entire morning and part of the afternoon engaged in the perpetual task of adorning the front door, with Penelope mixing colors under strict direction. As luncheon approached, my lady paced the room, handkerchief pressed to her nose due to the pungent fumes rising from Mr. Franklin's vehicle, vainly attempting to dismiss the artists. It was not until three o'clock that they removed their smocks, liberating Penelope—who suffered greatly from the fumes—and cleaning the paint from their attire. Yet, their labor was complete; the door bore the decorations of the birthday. The griffins, cupids, and myriad other figures were undeniably magnificent, though so numerous, so entwined in floral motifs, and so contorted in posture that they lingered uncomfortably in the mind long after viewing ceased. To note that Penelope concluded her morning duties by vomiting in the scullery is not done in malice toward the vehicle; indeed, the smell vanished once dry. If art demands such sacrifices—even from my own daughter—I maintain that art must prevail.

Mr. Franklin seized a bite from the luncheon table and departed for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though privately understood by himself and me to be fetching the Moonstone. Taking my station at the sideboard to oversee the dining preparations, I possessed ample mental space while Mr. Franklin was absent. Having attended to the wines and reviewed the staff, I retreated to compose myself before the guests arrived. A scent of tobacco and a perusal of a particular volume previously mentioned in these pages steadied both body and mind.

My reverie was shattered by the clatter of horsehooves outside. Stepping to the door, I welcomed a procession consisting of Mr. Franklin and his three cousins, led by one of the late Mr. Ablewhite's grooms. Strangely, Mr. Godfrey appeared devoid of his customary cheerfulness. He offered a polite handshake and expressed delight at finding me in good health, yet a palpable gloom hung over him, inexplicable to me. When I inquired about his father's condition, he replied curtly, "Much as usual." Conversely, the two Miss Ablewhites radiated vitality sufficient to lift the spirits of twenty men. Nearly as tall as their brother, these robust, golden-haired, ruddy-cheeked girls overflowed with health and energy; the horses beneath them trembled under their weight. Dismounting without assistance, they bounced upon the ground as if crafted of India rubber. Every utterance began with a loud "Oh!" every action executed with a resounding "bang," and they giggled and shrieked at the slightest provocation. "Bouncers" is the apt designation.

Exploiting the din created by the young ladies, I managed a private exchange with Mr. Franklin in the hall. "Have you secured the Diamond safely, sir?" I asked. He nodded, tapping the breast pocket of his coat. "Have you seen any sign of the Indians?" "Not a glimpse," he replied, immediately requesting my lady. Upon learning she was in the small drawing-room, he proceeded directly there.

Before a full minute elapsed, the bell rang, summoning Penelope to inform Miss Rachel that Mr. Franklin Blake desired her presence. Half an hour later, crossing the hall, I was abruptly halted by a cacophony of screams emanating from the drawing-room. While I was not alarmed—recognizing the distinctive "Oh!" of the Miss Ablewhites—I entered, ostensibly seeking dinner instructions, to ascertain the nature of the disturbance.

There stood Miss Rachel at the table, transfixed, holding the Colonel's ill-fated Diamond. Kneeling on either side of her were the two "Bouncers," devouring the gem with their eyes and screaming with ecstatic delight whenever it caught the light. Opposite them stood Mr. Godfrey, clapping his hands like a child and murmuring, "Exquisite! Exquisite!" Seated nearby, Mr. Franklin pulled nervously at his beard, casting anxious glances toward the window. There, framed by the glass, stood the figure he observed: my lady, clutching the extract from the Colonel's Will, her back turned to the assembled group.

When I requested my orders, she faced me. I noted the furrow of concern between her brows and the tightening of her jaw. "Come to my room in half an hour," she commanded. "I shall have something to say to you then." Departing, it was evident she grappled with the same dilemma that had troubled Mr. Franklin and me during our earlier meeting at the Shivering Sands. Did the legacy of the Moonstone prove she had wronged her brother cruelly, or did it confirm he was worse than her darkest fears? These were profound questions for my lady to resolve, especially while her daughter, ignorant of the Colonel's character, stood there holding the birthday gift.

Before I could withdraw, Miss Rachel, ever mindful of the elderly servant present since her birth, called out, "Look, Gabriel!" She flashed the jewel before my eyes, illuminated by a shaft of sunlight piercing the window. Good heavens! It *was* a diamond! Of size comparable to a plover's egg! The radiance streaming from it resembled the glow of the harvest moon. Gazing downward into the stone, one looked into a yellow abyss that swallowed all else; it seemed unfathomable, this tiny object held between thumb and forefinger, appearing as vast as the heavens themselves. We positioned it in the sun, then extinguished the lights; it glowed fiercely from within, casting a lunar luminescence in the darkness. No wonder Miss Rachel was captivated; no wonder her cousins shrieked. The Diamond exerted such a powerful hold on me that I erupted in a roar of "Oh!" matching the volume of the Bouncers. The sole individual maintaining composure was Mr. Godfrey. Placing an arm around each sister's waist, he glanced sympathetically between the gem and me, remarking, "Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!" Presumably intending to instruct me, he merely reminded me of the impending dinner. I shuffled downstairs to my regiment of waiters. As I exited, Mr. Godfrey declared, "Dear old Betteredge, I hold the truest regard for him!" while embracing his sisters and admiring Miss Rachel, bestowing upon me this token of affection. Such a reservoir of goodwill to draw upon! Compared to him, Mr. Franklin seemed a mere savage.

Half an hour later, I presented myself in my lady's chamber as instructed. Our conversation largely mirrored our previous discussion at the Shivering Sands, save that I carefully withheld information regarding the "jugglers," deeming it unjustified to alarm my lady given the lack of immediate danger. Upon receiving my dismissal, I perceived she viewed the Colonel's motives in the darkest light possible and intended to remove the Moonstone from her daughter's possession at the earliest opportunity.

Returning to my quarters, I encountered Mr. Franklin, who inquired if I had seen his cousin Rachel. I reported seeing nothing of her and, unable to locate Mr. Godfrey, suspected he was near his sister. Mr. Franklin's suspicions aligned with mine; he tugged vigorously at his beard and slammed the library door with significant intent.

Uninterrupted further in my preparations for the birthday dinner, I soon donned my white waistcoat. Penelope then appeared at my dressing table, ostensibly to brush my remaining locks and adjust my cravat. In high spirits, she conveyed news: "Miss Rachel has refused him." "Refused whom?" I asked. "The Ladies' Committee-Man, father," she replied. "A nasty, sly fellow! I detest him for trying to usurp Mr. Franklin!" Had I possessed the breath, I would have protested this indecent characterization of an esteemed philanthropist. However, Penelope was currently adjusting my cravat, channeling her entire emotional intensity into her fingers, nearly strangling me in the process
