It was in the tenth hour of that day that we launched the raft. The paddles moved with ease, balanced by the rests I had rigged, and the craft glided forward with little effort. I took the rear paddle, the Maid the front, and together we pushed steadily, the raft gaining a rhythm just shy of walking pace across the rough terrain of the Land.

By the twelfth hour, we paused to eat and drink, then resumed our journey. Once underway, the motion became almost automatic—no more than a gentle rocking on our feet. Time slipped away in quiet conversation, punctuated by the Maid’s soft glances back at me, her lips parting as if to tempt me closer. But each time I reached for my paddle, she shook her head—tenderly, firmly—refusing to let me leave my post.

At the eighteenth hour, we pulled in the paddles. The Maid laid out the cloak, fashioning it into a bed. We ate, drank, and fell asleep almost instantly—deep, untroubled rest, as though the world had cradled us in silence. Eight hours later, we woke together, blinking at one another, disoriented for a moment, then smiling as recognition returned. We were safe. Nothing had disturbed us. We laughed, kissed, washed our faces in the sea, and set off again.

That day passed in peace. We drifted along the coast, unhurried, content. Our voyage lasted four full days—each twenty-four hours long. We made no haste, moving only as fast as my strength allowed. Nothing notable occurred, save once: a great beast emerged from the sea, lumbering onto the shore to feed upon the sparse growth. It seemed natural, not monstrous. Its presence stirred no fear—only relief that we were distant from it. And so it was with all creatures of this land: they bore no taint of horror, but rather the quiet dignity of ancient life. Perhaps, I mused, the early world had been like this—simple, wild, yet untouched by unnatural dread. It struck me then that extremes do meet: the deep world had birthed beings not unlike those of the beginning. Not proof, merely thought—shaped by circumstance, yes, but perhaps also by something deeper, something beyond reason’s grasp. Still, I held no objection. For even if all form is shaped by environment, I believed there must be an inner force—a spirit peculiar to each living thing. That spirit, I thought, remained constant beneath change. Whether it was the cause or effect of life, its purity defined humanity. Where it was untainted, man stood. Man, I believed, had always been man—though his body might have changed, his spirit evolved slowly, shaped by need. Yet no development could make him cease to be man. His nature had limits, boundaries of being. And so, even if science proved man once walked the seas, I would still see him as man—not fish, but man modified. I could accept such truths without losing faith in his essence. Though I would never claim certainty, neither would I close my mind.

I did not speak here of what comes after death—for who can say? But I held a hope: that beyond this life, we may reach a world of beauty, uplifted, perfected. A vision glimpsed when we stood in holiness beside the Beloved. That hope sustained me.

But now, back to the journey.

Another wonder marked our passage: a fire-hill rising from the sea. The waters boiled around it, not everywhere, but in places where jets of flame shot skyward, roaring across the distance. Strange grunts rose from the depths near its base—gases escaping, I thought. The sight told us of immense power stirring beneath the earth. Long after we passed, we turned to watch it, awestruck.

Beyond that, nothing stirred—save the narrowing of the sea into smaller channels, linked by narrow waterways that carried us onward.

I showed the Maid the two places where I had slept when first arriving in this land. She listened closely, eager to know every detail, every memory.

On the fourth day, at the tenth hour, we reached the shore—a flat stretch leading up to the mouth of the First Gorge. We had begun our voyage at the same hour. The journey had been peaceful, blessed. If only the road ahead held such calm. But I knew better. Danger lay ahead. Yet our hearts were steady. We would go forward, conquer, and return to the Mighty Home. There, we would live in love, forever. That was worth every trial.

We beached the raft as best we could, wondering if anyone would ever see it again. The Maid cut a small piece of wood—a token. Then we gathered our gear. She helped me don my armor. I strapped the scrip and pouch to my back, gripped the Diskos. She wore her bundle, now smaller, and her knife at her side.

Then she knelt and kissed the raft. Memories flooded her—of beginnings, of survival, of rebirth. Her heart stirred with sorrow. I knelt beside her, gently lifting her to her feet. I took her hand, and together we stepped away from the water, toward the dark mouth of the Great Gorge.

To our right, miles away, loomed the mountain—huge, grim—where four fire-hills burned high above the night. Below them, ash-covered hills stretched upward, built over eternity. The Maid stared, transfixed. None of us could ever forget such a sight.

We climbed into the gorge’s high mouth, stepping into shadow. After a short while, we reached a bend—a sharp turn left into utter darkness. We paused. Turned back.

And looked one last time upon the Deep Land—the sea, the fire-mountains, the whispering life below, hidden in eternal night.

In that moment, I wondered: would the Humpt Men ever awaken to the fullness of human spirit? A strange thought, yes—but not unnatural. The land before us felt like the old world renewed. We saw ancient things through new eyes.

We stood silent, listening to the distant mutter of fire and gas, the faint pulse of life across the void. Only a few paces separated us from the silence of the gorge—our gateway to the Night Land. The Maid clung to my arm. Tears traced her cheeks—not only grief, but joy, longing, the ache of leaving behind the island where she had nursed me back to life. She would tell stories to her children—of this place, unseen by them, a wonder forever.

Then I turned. She slipped her hand into mine. We walked past the mighty corner of the gorge, stumbling into the gloom.

For sixteen hours we moved steadily, the darkness our only companion. Twenty-six hours since our last sleep. Foolish, I knew—I needed strength. The Maid warned me. But wisdom came soon enough. We found a safe spot, ate, drank, and counted our progress. From then on, we limited our journey to sixteen hours a day, sleeping eight. We kept this rhythm until we emerged from the gorge—five days in total.

When we entered the lighter parts of the gorge, we felt a lift in spirits. But often, thick gases rose from fissures, choking the air. The smell was rank. Silence reigned—except when a lone gas-fire moaned or whistled, its sound echoing across the waste, making loneliness a weight in the chest. The Maid felt it too.

She remembered how I had come alone, searching for her in the unknown. And I, knowing this, felt a quiet pride—natural, true. When a woman remembers your sacrifice, loves you more for it, the heart swells. Think of your own love-days—do not such thoughts echo with familiar pain?

On the fifth day, near the seventh hour, I began to hear odd sounds—whispers from the rocks, as if the stone itself spoke. I drew the Maid close, Diskos in hand, and we advanced cautiously.

Three times we passed gas-fires, their flames dancing, moaning softly, sometimes whistling. Other sounds came from the rocks—strange, unexpected, yet oddly familiar.

Then, suddenly, I realized: these were echoes. Distant echoes, caught by nearby cliffs, bouncing back to us. A faraway sound, distorted, made to seem near. And then I knew—it was the monstrous piping of the Great Gas Fountain. I told the Maid. She grew eager. This was both wondrous and a sign—we were nearing the end.

We looked ahead. Fires leapt everywhere, blurring the view. The fountain’s dance was still too far to see clearly, dwarfed by nearer flames.

But soon, as we advanced, the gorge flickered—light and dark shifting in the distance, like shudders across the night. That was it—the distant dance of the Great Flame. We watched as the light trembled, growing stronger, merging into a slow rise and fall of blue fire.

The sound grew louder—steady, then swelling into a monstrous piping, rich and changing in tone.

We passed the last small fires. Now we stood in the fireless stretch, where only the Great Fountain lit the way. It loomed huge, casting a trembling glow across the gorge.

We came near, stunned by the roar. Standing at the mouth of the gorge, we gazed at the flame, voiceless. My arm wrapped around the Maid. We stood close, silent. How could we speak? The noise was overwhelming.

After a long pause, we turned to face each8 other. We kissed—solemn, tender—in the light of the monster flame.

Then we looked again. The light spread wide, blue and strange, illuminating the far side of the gorge. There, under the flickering glow, lay a desolate world of mountains—lost, forgotten.

And now, we saw the path ahead. The bottom of the Mighty Slope—the final leg of our journey—appeared only when the flame leapt high, revealing jagged rocks and steep inclines.

We walked on a mile, seeking shelter from the noise. It was past the seventeenth hour. We ate, drank, rested among boulders.

When we woke, we ate again. We sat in silence, watching the flame dance—lonesome, majestic, surrounded by silent giants of rock. Then we gathered our gear and began the climb toward the utter dark of the Mighty Slope.

The ascent began. For sixteen hours, we stumbled upward, blind, hands and knees dragging us forward. I led, Diskos ready. I tied two straps from the scrip and pouch, connecting my belt to hers—so I could feel her close behind.

We traveled sixteen-hour days. We ate and drank at the sixth and twelfth hours, again before sleep, and upon waking. Sleep was always eight hours. I guarded our strength carefully. The Night Land awaited.

Often, I grew sickened—exhausted, lost in endless darkness. Each step was a fumble, each climb a battle against invisible walls. It felt as if we wandered beyond life, trapped in a blackness that never lessened.

Then I would stop. Whisper to the Maid. Call her close. Pull her into my arms, out of the dark. And in that embrace, we found comfort.

And so we climbed—sixteen hours a day. Then, for eight days, we ascended through the most dreadful night. After the first day, we crawled on hands and knees. I led, the Diskos at my hip. The strap between us—my anchor.

We moved in rhythm, eating, drinking, sleeping. Our bodies grew numb. The darkness affected us—making us feel unreal, dreamlike.

Yet still, I stopped often. Called her near. Held her. And in those moments, we found strength—not just in will, but in love.

And so we climbed—upward, forever—into the heart of the Night Land.
