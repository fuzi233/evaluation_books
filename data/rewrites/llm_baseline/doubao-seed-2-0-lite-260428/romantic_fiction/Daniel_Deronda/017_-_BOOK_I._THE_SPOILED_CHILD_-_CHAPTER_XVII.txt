“This is true the poet sings, That a sorrow’s crown of sorrow Is remembering happier things.”
—TENNYSON, *In Memoriam*

The last honey-gold days of July draped soft, fading light over the Thames the evening Daniel Deronda’s quiet, unmoored life shifted on its axis.

It had been more than a year since he’d returned to England, his formal education complete, expected to slip easily into the gilded routine Sir Hugo Mallinger had laid out for him. At the baronet’s gentle urging, he’d begun reading law just to fend off the lazy, aimless idleness that threatened to swallow him whole—but the half-hearted decision only tangled his existing indecision deeper. What was he meant for, exactly? The old, unspoken question hummed under his skin day and night: the secret of his unknown parent, the face he’d spent half his life trying to picture, the vague, heavy sense that he was permanently out of step with every other privileged young man about town.

Rowing was his only true escape. He kept a small, sleek boat moored at Putney, and whenever Sir Hugo didn’t need him, he’d push off at dusk and row until the sun sank low and the first stars pricked the purple sky, the river wrapping around him like a quiet cloak no one else could pierce. That evening he wore a loose dark blue shirt and a close-fitting skull-cap, his curls cropped short, a soft wave of beard framing his mouth—no trace left of the seraphic little boy who’d once trailed clouds of glory, but the rare, gentle gravity of his gaze was still there: the exact look Gwendolen Harleth had once complained was “dreadful” for how softly, unflinchingly it saw right through you. His hands were long, strong, flexible, the kind of hands that could pull an oar for hours without tiring, that could hold a falling person steady, no trace of the foppish, dainty grace affected by other rich young men.

He’d been half-humming under his breath the whole way upriver, the mournful gondolier’s song from *Otello* that set Dante’s lines to music: no greater sorrow than to remember happy times in the depths of misery. He didn’t even realize he was singing out loud when he pulled aside to make way for a heavy barge near Kew Bridge, resting his oars an arm’s length from the riverbank.

The first notes of the final pianissimo line, nella miseria, drifted over the water.

Most of the people meandering on the towing path barely noticed him. But one girl standing a few yards down the bank froze. She couldn’t have been more than eighteen, small and slim as a wren, a delicate little pale face peeking out from under a large black hat, a heavy long wool cloak wrapped around her shoulders even on that warm summer night. Her hands were clasped tight in front of her, her eyes fixed dead on the surface of the river with a look of statue-perfect despair, so still and unblinking she might have been carved from marble.

Deronda stopped singing mid-phrase. The sudden silence jolted her. She turned, their eyes locked, and for a long, stretched beat of time the whole rest of the world fell away. Her gaze was soft, skittish, like a fawn about to bolt into the undergrowth—no flush, no sharp alarm, just a quiet timidity that made her hold his gaze a fraction too long before she turned and walked slowly to a weathered wooden bench tucked under a tree.

His chest ached. He had no right to linger, no right to intrude on a stranger’s private grief, so he picked up his oars and rowed on. But he couldn’t shake the image: the dark folds of her cloak, the pale cameo of her face, the long dark lashes fanned over her cheeks. He knew there were a thousand lost, lonely girls slipping through the cracks of the world, their tragedies unremarked, unwritten, like small wounded birds dragging broken wings to die hidden in the hedgerows. But that evening, that quiet sorrow tangled with all his own old doubts—what right did he have to slip into the easy, unthinking routine of society, to apologize for all its wrongs just to get ahead? He let his oars fall slack, let the tide carry him slowly back downstream as the sun sank lower, painting the sky and the river in double streaks of rose and amber.

He tucked his boat into the shadow of a line of willows on the bank opposite Kew Gardens, propping his head on his hands against the cushions to watch the sun set, to wait for the stars to prick through the dusk, that soft sacred hour he loved more than any other. He’d almost forgotten the girl entirely, half lost in his own soft reverie, when he saw movement on the bank across the water.

It was her.

A cold jolt of dread shot through him. He didn’t move, didn’t dare startle her. She glanced around to make sure no one else was near, pushed her hat deep into the willow bushes, then shrugged off her heavy wool cloak. She lowered it slowly into the river, held it under the surface until it was heavy and saturated, until Deronda understood exactly what she planned to do: wrap that water-logged cloak around herself, sink into the dark, and never come up.

He didn’t hesitate. He grabbed his oar and drove the boat across the stretch of water in two sharp strokes.

She looked up, terrified, and sank down onto the muddy bank, clutching the half-soaked cloak to her chest, covering her face like she could wish him away if she tried hard enough. But he steadied the boat right at the edge, and spoke so softly his voice was barely louder than the lapping of the water.

“Don’t be afraid. You’re unhappy. Please trust me. Tell me what I can do to help you.”

She dropped her hands and looked up. His face was lit by the last of the sunset, and she knew him. The man from the bridge, the man who’d been singing that song that had wormed its way straight into her empty, hurting chest. For a long moment they just looked at each other again.

“I saw you before,” she said, her voice low and sweet, a faint lilt to it that felt almost foreign, but not quite. Then, dreamily, like the words had fallen out of her before she could stop them: “Nella miseria.”

It all clicked. “You heard me singing Nessun maggior dolore,” he said, and the sound of the words on her tongue made the melody hum in his own ears. “You mustn’t stay here, you’ll catch your death. Let me take you somewhere safe. Let me take that cloak.”

She clutched it tighter for a second, her eyes searching his face, wide and wondering. “You look good. Perhaps this is God’s command.”

“Trust me,” he said, leaning forward, his voice low and fierce with earnestness. “I will die before I let any harm come to you.”

She stood then, let the heavy saturated cloak slip from her hands onto the wet grass, her small chilled hands clasped tight over her waist, stepping so close to the boat her toes nearly touched the edge of the hull. The words Great God! slipped out of him unbidden, a quiet prayer, and the old, old thought flashed through his mind—maybe the mother he’d never known, the mother he’d spent years wondering about, had once stood just like this, alone and desolate, no one to reach out a hand to her.

She let him wrap his hand around hers, his big, warm one closing soft around her tiny cold palm. Then she hesitated, drawing back a little. “I have nowhere to go. No one belonging to me in all this land.”

“I’ll take you to a kind lady who has daughters,” Deronda said immediately. He wouldn’t bring her to the grand, gilded formal house in Park Lane, not to face staring footmen and sharp suspicious maids, not to jolt her fragile, frayed nerves. He’d take her to the warm little cottage in Chelsea, to Mrs. Meyrick, the Quaker-soft widow who’d been a second mother to his friend Hans, and Hans’s three sweet, romantic daughters who still thought all the drama in the world was out of history books and old plays, who’d treat this lost girl like Rebecca from *Ivanhoe*, who’d take her in no questions asked.

“Are you part of the theatre?” she asked, timidly.

“Not at all,” he said, smiling, holding out his coat to her. She was shivering now, in the cool dusk, and she wrapped it around her shoulders, the fabric soft and warm from his body. She wouldn’t take his biscuits, said she’d bought bread that morning with the last of her money, and that she couldn’t eat a bite anyway.

He rowed steadily downstream, the twilight deepening all around them, the stars pricking out one by one. She sat quiet on the cushions, watching the dip of his oar in the water, the tension slowly draining out of her small frame.

“If you hadn’t come,” she said, after a long silence, “I’d be dead now.”

“Never speak of that,” he said, his throat tight. “I hope you’ll never be sorry I found you.”

“I don’t know how I’ll ever be glad to live,” she whispered. “The sorrow has lasted far longer than the happy times. I thought death couldn’t be wrong—that before God, life and death are the same, that I could keep my soul pure. But now… I’m commanded to live. I don’t know how.”

“You’ll find friends,” he promised her. “I’ll find them for you.”

She shook her head, tears glistening on her cheeks. “Not my mother and brother. I ran away from far across the sea, to find them. I thought God would guide me. This morning I woke up and all I could hear was never, never. But now…” She paused, looking right at him, straining to see his face in the faint moonlight. “You want to know if I’m English? I was born here. But I’m a Jewess.”

Deronda went still, his chest flooding with a strange, soft, thunderous recognition. All the years he’d spent wondering about his own hidden heritage, all the half-formed questions he’d never dared to ask—suddenly they had a face, a story, a life right in front of him.

“Do you despise me for it?” she whispered, like a small hurt animal.

“Of course not,” he said, gentle and firm. “Only a fool would despise anyone for that. There are good and bad people of every faith.”

The tears burst out of her then, great heaving sobs, and she buried her face in her knees. Deronda said nothing, just rowed on, carrying her away from the river that had almost claimed her, toward the little warm house in Chelsea, toward the rest of their lives. That evening, he felt, he had grown years older in a single hour. For the first time in all his adrift, unmoored life, he had found a purpose: not to fight some grand abstract battle of the world, but to keep this small, fragile lost soul safe. It felt less like a burden, and more like a miracle.
