Major Pendennis and Captain Costigan were two old campaigners who’d dodged everything from Waterloo musket fire to angry landladies demanding back rent, so you’d better believe they kept their cool when the Major made his grand entrance. The rest of the crew crammed into Cos’s sitting room? Not so much.
Slow, dreamy Miss Fotheringay’s usually unflappable heart suddenly thumped so hard you could see her bodice move, her cheeks flushing a bright, healthy cherry red just as Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks glared at her like she’d intentionally lost his favorite prized foxhound. The crooked little old man, Mr. Bows, perched on the window seat had been trying to scribble theater sheet music for the past ten minutes, but the two young lads’ fencing lesson had them stamping and hopping around so hard the floorboards shook like a jelly mold. He was overjoyed to drop his quill and gape at the newcomer, who tipped his perfectly blacked boots and distributed bows so graceful you’d have thought he was greeting the entire royal court.
“Me daughter—me dear friend Mr. Bows—me gallant young pupil, Sir Derby Oaks!” Costigan announced, waving a hand like he was hosting a royal levee rather than a crumby second-floor lodging. “Give me one minute, Meejor, and I’m all yours.”
He bolted for his tiny adjacent bedroom so fast his coat tails flapped, slicked his lank hair with a ancient hairbrush older than the Battle of Trafalgar, tore off his frayed old stock, tied on the brand-new one Emily had stitched for him, and threw on the fancy new coat he’d ordered special for her benefit—all in the space of 60 seconds flat, the man had impressive speed even three sheets to the wind. Sir Derby followed him in, and waddled out a minute later squeezed into his absurdly tight “shell jacket”, stitched by a sadistic tailor who clearly hated large cavalry officers, which he, Milly, and probably even poor smitten Pen all thought was the height of masculine perfection.
The two elders made small talk about the weather before Costigan made his grand reappearance in his “new shoot”, as he insisted on calling the coat. “No need to apologize for greeting you in my shirt sleeves, Meejor,” he purred, oozing Irish charm.
“An old soldier could spend his time worse than teaching a lad how to wield a sword,” the Major replied, ever suave. “Why, I swear I remember hearing you swing a tune pretty well yourself back in the day, Captain Costigan, at Sir Richard Strachan’s table in Walcheren.”
This news made Bows look up so fast his glasses slid down his nose, utterly baffled, because he was fairly sure Costigan had never left Dublin after 1810. The Major didn’t let that little detail trip him up. “But we can hash out old war stories later. I came here today to pay my respects to Miss Fotheringay,” he said, and bowed so low and courtly he could’ve been greeting a duchess, not a provincial theater star. “My nephew Arthur raves about your performances, of course—but he’s a starry-eyed 18-year-old boy, so I had to come judge for myself. I’ve seen every top actress on the London stage, and I swear, you outshine all of them. You’re as majestic as Mrs. Siddons herself.”
“Faith, I’ve been saying that for years!” Costigan winked at his daughter, gesturing for her to pull the half-finished satin gown off the only empty chair. Milly did, and dropped a curtsy so deep you’d have thought he’d handed her a diamond tiara.
“Just as pathetic as Miss O’Neill, too,” the Major went on, “those little snippets of song you sing reminded me of Mrs. Jordan in her prime, and your bearing’s exactly like the great French tragedienne, Mars—have you ever seen her perform, Miss Fotheringay?”
“Oh, there was two Mahers over in Crow Street,” Milly said plainly. “Fanny was decent enough, but Biddy was naught to write home about.”
“Sure, the Major means the god of war, not the two Dublin lasses!” Costigan jumped in, saving everyone from the awkward beat.
“Not that Mars, though I suppose Venus herself can be forgiven for mixing them up,” the Major grinned, directing the joke right at Sir Derby, who’d just stomped back into the room in his skin-tight jacket. Neither of them got the pun, and Sir Derby just scowled harder, looking like he wanted to ask what the devil this snooty old army guy was even doing in his favorite actress’s lodgings.
The Major was delighted by the sulky look on the baronet’s face. *Oh, good*, he thought. A rival with 10 thousand a year and a cavalry commission. That’s way better than my idiot nephew.
“Hope I didn’t interrupt your fencing practice,” he said smoothly. “I’m only in Chatteris a day or two, and I was desperate to meet my old comrade Costigan, and see the lady who enchanted me so much from the stalls last night. I wasn’t the only man smitten! My reverend friend went home raving about your Ophelia, and I saw Sir Derby fling a bouquet that no actress ever deserved more. Aren’t those the very blooms sitting in that glass on the mantelpiece?”
“I’m very fond of flowers,” Milly simpered, batting her lashes hard at Sir Derby. He still looked like he’d swallowed a sour plum.
“‘Sweets to the sweet’, right? That’s the line from Hamlet, isn’t it?” the Major chirped, still being unshakeably friendly.
“Pon my life, I don’t know,” Sir Derby grunted. “Never was much for books.”
“Can that be?” the Major gasped, all mock-surprised. “You don’t take after your father, then! He was one of the finest scholars I ever met, and I knew him very well, y’know. He even saved my life once, back in the day.”
Milly’s eyes went wide, rolling first to the Major then over to Sir Derby all full of soft gratitude. The baronet, though? He looked like he wished his apothecary dad had let the Major die of the fever instead. “My dad was a good doctor, yeah,” he mumbled. “Not my line of work. Got an appointment to keep. Bye Cos, bye Miss Fotheringay.”
He stormed out before she could even beg him to stay, the clatter of his sabre thudding down the creaky stairs, and they all heard him curse little Tom Creed out in the passage and kick the boy’s peg-top so hard it sailed into the middle of the street.
“Monstrous fine looking young man, that,” the Major said to Costigan, not cracking a smile even though he was this close to snorting with laughter.
“A credit to the army, that lad!” Costigan gushed. “Refined manners, princely fortune, rides sixteen stone like he’s a featherweight.”
“Perfect champion, no doubt all the ladies adore him,” the Major nodded.
“He’s alright on a horse,” Milly said, “but he never has anything interesting to say.”
“Best seen, not heard, that one,” Bows muttered, making the Major make a mental note: this woman can bring an audience to tears on stage, but she can’t follow a basic pun about a French actress? Wild.
Costigan, ever the hospitable Irish host, immediately insisted on plying the Major with drinks. The Major, who was no more hungry than a man who’d just gorged himself at a Lord Mayor’s banquet, declared he was fainting from hunger and dying for a biscuit and a glass of wine. He knew the easiest way to soften a man up is to let him play the generous host. “Get out the old Madara, Milly love,” Costigan winked.
Milly snuck downstairs, handed little Tommy Creed sixpence to run to the Grapes pub for a pint of Madara and sixpennyworth of assorted biscuits, promising him two biscuits for himself if he ran fast. While he was gone, she sat with the landlady gossiping about how nice the Major was, how butter wouldn’t melt in his mouth, and how Sir Derby had stormed off in a jealous huff before they could figure out how to keep both him and rich little Pen happy.
Bows, who’d seen enough messy theater romances to last three lifetimes, cleared his throat and pointed out that Milly was late for rehearsal, or the manager would fine her half a day’s pay. She grabbed her bonnet and shawl, smiling pretty as ever, and the Major shook her hand so warmly it could’ve been paternal. Bows hobbled out behind her, thoroughly confused: the old Pendennis isn’t here to support the engagement? That can’t be right.
The second the door clicked shut, Costigan dove for the bottle and chugged the last of the old leftover wine, then tore into the new pint from the pub so fast his hands shook. He was already well oiled when he leaned across the table, eyes glistening. “That lad Pen’s gonna make your family a fine son-in-law, Major! When he moves into Fairoaks Park, he’ll take me with him, eh? Me poor old aching bones won’t have to live in this tiny lodging no more.”
The Major sipped his wine, making a face like it was the finest vintage from Lord Steyne’s cellar, then dropped the bombshell. “Wait a second. Pen’s not an heir to two thousand a year. His dad, my brother, was a country apothecary, and he left all his money—five hundred a year, total—to his wife. Pen doesn’t get a penny until his mother dies, and her family lives to be 90. He’s got no fortune at all.”
Costigan’s face went bright tomato red. He shot up, slamming his fist on the table. “Are you making a fool out of me? Insulting me and me daughter? I’ve got stacks of that boy’s mushy love letters! I’ll drag him to court for breach of promise! I’ll demand satisfaction, sir! I’ll fight you if you try to jilt me girl!”
The Major didn’t even flinch. He stood up, adjusted his gloves, and stared Costigan dead in the eye. “Threaten to publish a teenager’s silly love poems and challenge a man to a duel over a marriage that would leave your daughter living in a garret on 500 a year? Be my guest. I’m not scared of any Costigan in Ireland. I’m here to stop this stupid, poverty-bound match, and I will stop it.”
Costigan drew himself up to his full 5 foot 4 height, trying to look as fierce as a lion. “You said you’re staying at the George Hotel, Major? A friend of mine will call on you before you leave Chatteris. You’ll hear more from me.”
The Major stormed down the stairs, and Costigan bowed after him over the landing, as dramatic as if he’d just won a standoff against Napoleon himself. By the time the Major hit the street, he was fuming—but also grinning. He’d already derailed the whole silly romance, before his starry-eyed nephew even had a clue what was going on.
