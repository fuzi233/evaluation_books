I apologize for holding you captive with my own affairs and this beehive of a chair. I am fully aware that a dozing elderly man in a sun-drenched garden is hardly captivating material. Yet, facts must be recorded exactly as they unfolded, and I implore your patience for a few more moments while I await the arrival of Mr. Franklin Blake later this afternoon.

Before I could succumb to sleep again following my daughter Penelope's departure, the rhythmic clatter of dishes in the servants' hall signaled that dinner was served. As one who prefers to dine in my own sitting room, I had no part in the household meal save for offering my best wishes for their digestion before returning to my chair. Just as I stretched my limbs, another woman collided with me. It was not Penelope, but Nancy, the kitchen maid. I had been blocking her path, and I noted immediately that her expression was sullen—a demeanor I, as head of the staff, never permit to pass unchallenged.

"What are you neglecting your dinner for?" I asked. "What troubles you, Nancy?"

Nancy attempted to push past me without reply. Seizing the moment, I rose and grasped her by the ear. She is a pleasant, plump young woman, and it is my custom to demonstrate my personal approval of a girl in this manner.

"What is the matter now?" I insisted.

"Rosanna is late again for dinner," Nancy replied. "I've been sent to fetch her. All the heavy lifting falls on my shoulders in this house. Leave me be, Mr. Betteredge!"

The Rosanna in question was our second housemaid. Possessing a certain pity for her, and observing that Nancy intended to summon her with unnecessary harshness, I decided I had no urgent business elsewhere and might as well retrieve Rosanna myself. I knew she would receive a gentle reminder regarding punctuality far better from me.

"Where is Rosanna?" I inquired.

"At the sands, of course!" Nancy retorted, tossing her head. "She suffered another fainting fit this morning and requested fresh air. I have no patience with her!"

"Return to your dinner, my girl," I said. "I have patience with her, and I shall fetch her."

Nancy, possessing a formidable appetite, looked gratified. When she is pleased, she appears lovely; when she appears lovely, I affectionately pat her under the chin. It is not immorality—it is merely habit.

Thus, I took my stick and prepared to depart for the sands.

Wait! It would be premature to leave just yet. I must detain you further to recount the tale of the sands and the story of Rosanna, for the matter of the Diamond is intimately connected to both. How desperately I strive to advance my narrative without digression, and how poorly I succeed! But alas, persons and circumstances arise so vexatiously in this life that they demand attention. Let us proceed calmly and briefly; we shall soon be immersed in the mystery, I promise you!

Rosanna, placing the person before the thing as a mark of basic politeness, was the sole new addition to our household. Approximately four months prior to the present time, my lady visited London and toured a Reformatory designed to rescue fallen women from returning to criminal lives after release from prison. The matron, noting my lady's interest, presented a girl named Rosanna Spearman and recounted a most tragic history, one I lack the heart to detail here, for neither do I wish to be made wretched without purpose, nor do I believe you would relish it. The conclusion was that Rosanna Spearman had been a thief; though not of the caliber to organize city-wide robberies, the law seized her, leading inevitably to prison and the reformatory. Despite her actions, the matron believed Rosanna was a rare soul, one in a thousand, who merely required an opportunity to prove her worthiness of Christian compassion. My lady, a woman of genuine faith, declared to the matron, "Rosanna Spearman shall have her chance, in my service."

One week later, Rosanna Spearman joined our establishment as the second housemaid.

No one was informed of her background except Miss Rachel and myself. My lady, consulting me on nearly all matters, sought my counsel regarding Rosanna. Having recently adopted the habit of aligning with my lady's views, I concurred heartily with her decision.

No girl could have received a fairer opportunity than this poor creature. None of the servants were privy to her past, and thus, no one could cast it in her face. She received her wages and privileges equal to the others, occasionally receiving a word of private encouragement from my lady. In return, she proved herself entirely worthy of such kindness. Though physically frail and occasionally plagued by the aforementioned fainting spells, she performed her duties modestly, uncomplainingly, and with care. Yet, strangely, she failed to make friends among the other female servants, save for my daughter Penelope, who treated her kindly, though never intimately.

I struggle to comprehend what offense she committed. She possessed no beauty to incite envy; indeed, she was the plainest woman in the house, compounded by the misfortune of having one shoulder larger than the other. What the servants chiefly resented, I believe, was her silence and solitude. While the others gossiped, she read or worked during her leisure hours. When it was her turn to step outside, nine times out of ten, she quietly donned her bonnet and walked alone. She never quarreled, never took offense; she maintained a stubborn yet civil distance between herself and the rest. Furthermore, despite her plainness, there was a distinct quality about her, something akin to a lady, evident in her voice or her face. Whatever it was, the other women seized upon it instantly, declaring—most unjustly—that Rosanna Spearman gave herself airs.

Having recounted Rosanna's story, I must now address one of the peculiar habits of this strange girl before proceeding to the tale of the sands.

Our residence stands high on the Yorkshire coast, adjacent to the sea. We possess magnificent walking paths in every direction except one. That single route is a dreadful walk. It extends for a quarter of a mile through a gloomy plantation of firs, emerging onto the loneliest and most unsightly little bay on our entire coastline.

Here, the sandhills descend to the sea, terminating in two rocky spits jutting opposite one another until they vanish into the water. One is known as the North Spit, the other the South. Between them lies the most treacherous quicksand on the Yorkshire shores, shifting backward and forward at certain seasons. At the turn of the tide, some unseen movement in the deep causes the entire surface of the quicksand to shiver and tremble in a manner remarkably visible, earning it the local name of the Shivering Sand. A massive bank, half a mile offshore near the bay's mouth, breaks the force of the incoming ocean. Whether winter or summer, when the tide flows over the quicksand, the sea appears to leave its waves behind on the bank, rolling its waters in smoothly with a heave, covering the sand in silence. It is a desolate and horrifying retreat. No boat dares venture into this bay. No children from our fishing village, Cobb's Hole, ever come here to play. Even the birds of the air seem to give the Shivering Sand a wide berth. That a young woman, with dozens of pleasant walks available and companionship at her command, should prefer this place, choosing to sit and read or work alone here during her break, passes belief. Nevertheless, it is true that this was Rosanna Spearman's favorite walk, aside from her occasional visits to Cobb's Hole to see the only friend she possessed in the neighborhood. It is also true that I was now setting out for this very location to retrieve the girl for dinner, bringing us full circle to our starting point and launching us properly toward the sands.

I saw no sign of the girl within the plantation. Upon emerging through the sandhills onto the beach, there she was, in her small straw bonnet and plain grey cloak, which she always wore to conceal her deformed shoulder as much as possible. She stood alone, gazing out at the quicksand and the sea.

She startled when I approached, turning her head away. Refusing to avoid eye contact being another transgression I never tolerate, I turned her toward me and saw she was weeping. My bandanna handkerchief—one of six exquisite pieces gifted by my lady—was conveniently in my pocket. I produced it and said to Rosanna, "Come and sit down, my dear, on the slope of the beach beside me. I will wipe your eyes first, and then I shall venture to ask what has brought you to tears."

At my age, you will find sitting on a beach slope a far more arduous task than it appears now. By the time I was settled, Rosanna had dried her own eyes with a far inferior handkerchief—cheap cambric. She appeared very quiet and deeply wretched, yet she sat beside me like a good girl when I instructed her. When one wishes to comfort a woman most effectively, one should take her upon one's knee. I considered this golden rule. But alas! Rosanna was not Nancy, and that is the truth of it!

"Now, tell me, my dear," I said, "what are you crying about?"

"About the years that are gone, Mr. Betteredge," Rosanna replied quietly. "My past life still returns to me sometimes."

"Come, come, my girl," I said. "Your past life is thoroughly washed away. Why cannot you forget it?"

She took hold of one of the lappets of my coat. I am a somewhat slovenly old man, and much of my food and drink often splashes onto my clothing. Sometimes one woman, sometimes another, cleans the grease from me. The day before, Rosanna had removed a spot from the lappet of my coat using a new composition guaranteed to remove anything. The grease was gone, but a dull patch remained on the fabric's nap where the grease had been. The girl pointed to that spot and shook her head.

"The stain is removed," she said. "But the place shows, Mr. Betteredge—the place shows!"

A remark that catches a man off guard via his own coat is difficult to answer. Moreover, something in the girl herself made me particularly sorrowful for her at that moment. She had lovely brown eyes, plain as she was otherwise, and she looked at me with a sort of reverence for my happy old age and good character, things forever beyond her own reach, which weighed heavily on my heart for our second housemaid. Unable to comfort her further, there was only one course of action left. That was to take her to dinner.

"Help me up," I said. "You are late for dinner, Rosanna—and I have come to fetch you in."

"You, Mr. Betteredge!" she exclaimed.

"They told Nancy to fetch you," I replied. "But I thought you might prefer your scolding if it came from me."

Instead of assisting me, the poor thing slipped her hand into mine and gave it a gentle squeeze. She struggled hard to prevent further tears and succeeded, which I respected. "You are very kind, Mr. Betteredge," she said. "I do not want any dinner today—let me remain here a little longer."

"What draws you to stay here?" I asked. "What brings you everlastingly to this miserable place?"

"Something draws me to it," the girl replied, tracing patterns in the sand with her finger. "I try to keep away from it, and I cannot. Sometimes," she whispered, as if frightened by her own thoughts, "sometimes, Mr. Betteredge, I think that my grave is waiting for me here."

"There is roast mutton and suet pudding waiting for you!" I declared. "Go to dinner immediately. This is what happens, Rosanna, when one thinks on an empty stomach!" I spoke severely, naturally indignant at my age to hear a twenty-five-year-old woman discussing her end!

She seemed not to hear me. She placed her hand on my shoulder and held me there, seated beside her.

"I think the place has laid a spell on me," she said. "I dream of it night after night; I think of it while stitching. You know I am grateful, Mr. Betteredge—you know I strive to deserve your kindness and my lady's confidence. But I wonder sometimes whether this life here is too quiet and too good for a woman like me, after all I have endured, Mr. Betteredge—after all I have endured. It is more lonely to be among the other servants, knowing I am not what they are, than it is to be here. My lady does not know, the matron at the reformatory does not know, what a dreadful reproach honest people feel within themselves toward a woman like me. Do not scold me, there's a dear good man. I do my work, don't I? Please do not tell my lady I am discontented—I am not. My mind is unquiet, sometimes, that is all." She snatched her hand from my shoulder and suddenly pointed downward to the quicksand. "Look!" she said. "Is it not wonderful? Is it not terrible? I have seen it dozens of times, and it is always as new to me as if I had never seen it before!"

I looked where she pointed. The tide was turning, and the dreadful sand began to shiver. The broad brown face of it heaved slowly, then dimpled and quivered all over. "Do you know what it looks like to *me*?" Rosanna asked, seizing my shoulder again. "It looks as if it had hundreds of suffocating people beneath it—all struggling to reach the surface, and all sinking lower and lower into the dreadful deeps! Throw a stone in, Mr. Betteredge! Throw a stone in, and let us see the sand suck it down!"

This was unhealthy talk! This was an empty stomach feeding an unquiet mind! My answer, a rather sharp one in the poor girl's own interest, was at the tip of my tongue when it was abruptly cut short by a voice among the sandhills shouting my name.
