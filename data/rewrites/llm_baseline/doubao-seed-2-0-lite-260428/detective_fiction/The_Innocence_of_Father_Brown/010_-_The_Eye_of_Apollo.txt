That strange, smoky translucence unique to the Thames, a blur that somehow still lets the light cut through, bleared from dull grey to searing glitter as the noon sun climbed high over Westminster. Two men tramped across Westminster Bridge: one so tall he could have been a giant striding past the Houses of Parliament, the other so short and round in his clerical cassock he looked like a hunched little shadow of the abbey huddled just opposite. The tall man was Hercule Flambeau, ex-master thief turned private detective, bound for his brand new office in a half-finished high-rise block facing the abbey gates. The short man was Father Brown, fresh off sitting a deathbed vigil in Camberwell, popping by to see his friend’s new workspace.

The building was a distinctly American import: sky-high, stuffed full of over-oiled telephones and automatic lifts, still understaffed and half-empty. Only three tenants had moved in so far: Flambeau, the office directly above him, and the office directly below. The rest of the floors stood completely bare. But even before they stepped through the front doors, one object caught their eye immediately: slung across the facade outside the office above Flambeau’s, a massive gilded effigy of a human eye, wreathed in golden sun rays, big enough to take up the space of three whole office windows.

“On earth is that?” Father Brown stopped dead, tilting his round face up to stare.
“Some new fad religion,” Flambeau laughed. “The kind that forgives you all your sins by insisting you never had any to begin with. Very similar to Christian Science, from what I’ve heard. A bloke calling himself Kalon — I very much doubt that’s his real name — took the flat above mine. Two lady typists run their agency below me, and this enthusiastic old humbug is camped out on top. Calls himself the New Priest of Apollo. Worships the sun.”
“Better he watch his back,” Father Brown said gravely. “The sun was the cruellest of all the old gods. But what’s that monstrous eye for?”
“Their whole schtick, as far as I can tell,” Flambeau explained. “They say if your mind is perfectly steady, you can endure anything. Their two big symbols are the sun and the open eye — they claim anyone truly healthy can stare straight at the sun without blinking.”
“If a man were really healthy,” Father Brown blinked, “he wouldn’t bother to stare.”
“Supposedly it cures every physical illness, too,” Flambeau added, careless.
“Can it cure the one spiritual disease?” Father Brown asked, sudden and serious.
“What’s that?” Flambeau grinned.
“Thinking you’re perfectly well.”

Flambeau was far more curious about the quiet little office below him than the flamboyant sun temple above. A lucid, pragmatic Southerner, he could not wrap his head around the pale, bright new age religions popping up all over London — but he was never bored by people, especially sharp, striking ones. The two sisters who ran the typing agency below him were far more interesting than they looked. Both slight, dark, and brisk, the elder, Pauline, was tall and arresting, with a sharp aquiline profile that looked like it had been carved by a knife. She cut through life like a blade, her eyes bright as polished steel, not soft as diamonds, her slim straight figure just a touch too stiff to feel casual grace. Her younger sister Joan was her dimmer, quieter shadow: a little greyer, a little paler, trailing after her older sister with a quiet, dog-like devotion that held more quiet tragedy than Pauline’s loud, blazing self-assurance.

What no passer-by would have guessed was that Pauline Stacey was a landed heiress, born to a crest and half a county’s worth of estates, a vast fortune she had kept rather than give away, pouring it all into her model typing business and leagues to help other working women rise. She hated weakness, hated anything she saw as a “crutch”. Once Flambeau walked in to drop off a typing job just in time to see her fling Joan’s spectacles to the floor and stamp them to shards, mid-rant about “sickly medical nonsense” and the cowardice of relying on artificial rubbish instead of willpower. She dared Joan to ever bring such trash into the office again, demanding to know if people thought they all needed wooden legs or glass eyes too, as if they were born crippled slaves.

Flambeau, bemused, asked her point-blank why a lift was a triumph of man’s willpower but a pair of spectacles was a coward’s crutch. Pauline’s eyes blazed.
“Machines, engines, those are marks of human force — man’s force, woman’s force too! They devour distance, defy time. That is true science. But these silly little plasters and pills the doctors peddle? They are badges of poltroonery. I was born free! People only think they need those things because nurses and nannies train them to fear the dark, to fear the sun, tell children not to stare up at it until they can’t look without blinking. Why should there be one star in the sky I am not allowed to see? The sun is no master of mine. I can stare at it whenever I choose.”

Flambeau laughed and complimented her, but the second he got to his own floor he whistled under his breath. So she had fallen right into the clutches of the con man upstairs with his golden eye. He soon saw the bond between the upper and lower flats was growing tighter by the day. Kalon, the self-styled priest of Apollo, was physically magnificent: nearly as tall as Flambeau, with a mane of blonde hair tossed back like a lion’s, a full golden beard, sharp bright blue eyes, the very image of a golden saint-king out of Saxon legend. No amount of tacky office decor — the brass nameplate by his door, the clerk in a starched collar manning his outer office, the gilded sun eye hanging outside like an optician’s ad — could dim the raw, vivid charisma that rolled off him. Even in his casual linen work suit he was an imposing, magnetic figure; when he donned his white ritual vestments and golden circlet to salute the sun at dawn, noon, and sunset, passers-by in the street would stop short, their laughter dying on their lips.

On the day of the death, Father Brown hung back on the sidewalk as Flambeau barreled for the entrance, squinting up at Kalon on his top-floor balcony. The sun priest was already standing straight, hands lifted, his deep, ringing voice carrying all down the crowded street as he chanted his solar litany, staring straight into the flaming midday disc of the sun without the faintest flinch of an eyelid. The little priest below blinked so hard his eyes were crinkled shut.
“O sun!” Kalon’s voice boomed. “O star too great to be numbered among the stars! O white father of all unwearied things, white flames, white flowers, white mountain peaks! Primal purity, into thy peace—”

There was a rush, a crash, a woman’s high, shattering scream that cut through the litany. Half a dozen people charged in through the building gates, half a dozen charged out, all yelling over each other. A wave of raw, unnameable horror flooded the street. Two figures stood completely still through the chaos: Kalon the sun priest on his balcony high above, and Father Brown, small and shabby, in the crowd below.

Then Flambeau’s towering form filled the doorway, his voice roaring like a foghorn, ordering someone to run for a surgeon. Father Brown ducked through the crowd after him, Kalon’s smooth, chanting voice still drifting down from the balcony, praising the happy sun god of fountains and flowers.

They found the body crumpled at the bottom of the open lift shaft. It was Pauline Stacey, dead on impact, her skull cracked, her bright defiant face still. Flambeau had not doubted it for a second. Suicide seemed impossible for a woman who insisted tragedy did not exist, who thought willpower could conquer every last law of nature. But who on earth could have murdered her? The building was half empty, barely occupied.
“Where is that fraud Kalon?” Flambeau snarled, red in the face.
A calm, deep voice answered: “I have been standing on my balcony worshipping the sun for the last full fifteen minutes.”
Flambeau turned to see Father Brown at his elbow. “Then if he was up there the whole time, who killed her?”
“Let’s go upstairs and find out,” Father Brown said. “We have half an hour before the police turn up.”

They searched the typing agency first, and found Joan Stacey gone. Flambeau blanched. “Her sister’s out — vanished.”
Father Brown nodded. “Or she went up to Kalon’s suite. Let’s wait for them in the Staceys’ office downstairs.”
He settled in a big red leather chair by the entrance, where he could watch the stairs and the landings. He did not wait long. Four minutes later, three figures descended the stairs: Joan Stacey, her face drawn, streaks of premature grey in her dark hair, heading straight for her desk; Kalon himself, still in his white ritual robes and golden circlet, sweeping down the stairs like a painting of Christ leaving Pontius Pilate’s praetorium; and Flambeau, following behind, bewildered and frowning. Joan calmly laid out her papers on the desk, her hands steady as anything. If she was a criminal, she was ice cold.

Father Brown smiled a tiny, odd little smile at her, then turned to Kalon.
“Prophet,” he said, “I want you to tell me: do you actually think murder is wrong at all?”
Kalon lifted his crowned head. “Is that an accusation?”
“No,” Father Brown said gently. “It’s the speech for the defence.”

Kalon rose, tall and glowing, seeming to fill the tiny little office with the weight of his presence. He raged, magnificent: “You are Caiaphas! Your church and mine are the only two real things on this earth. I worship the sun of the living; you worship the darkening of the sun, the god of the dying. Your whole church is nothing but a black police force of spies, dragging confessions of sin out of men by treachery and torture. You would prove men guilty. I would prove them innocent. You teach them they are sinners. I teach them they are pure.”
He leaned against the mantelpiece, unflinching. “I will tell you the full case against me, no hiding. The dead woman Pauline was my bride, not by your petty chapel laws, but a law far purer than yours. She was leaving me half a million pounds in a will she wrote at that very table this morning, to fund our church. You think that gives me motive? I do not care if you put me in chains. Prison is nothing but waiting for her at a wayside station. The gallows would only be riding headlong to meet her.”
He waved the thought away. “But I could not have killed her. My alibi is unbreakable. Half Westminster saw me on my balcony from before noon until a quarter past. My clerk will swear I never left my top-floor suite. A hundred witnesses. I know exactly what happened to her, though. She was ambitious, determined to master the laws of matter. She kept saying if your will was strong enough, you could float down through empty air like a feather. She tried to levitate down the lift shaft. Her will failed at the last second. That is no murder. That is a heroic failure.”

Flambeau stared, half convinced. Father Brown sat hunched on his chair, looking at the floor, a pained wrinkle across his forehead like he was ill. “If that is the case,” he said, blinking like he was squinting through tears, “you have no reason to stay here. Just take that will you spoke of and go. I wonder where the poor lady left it.”
