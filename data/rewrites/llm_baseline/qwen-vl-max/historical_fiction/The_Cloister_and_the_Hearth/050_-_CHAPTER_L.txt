Among strangers, Margaret Brandt found a fragile peace. And soon, an unexpected turn of fortune lifted her spirits. A prominent citizen, gravely ill and desperate, had consulted doctor after doctor—each more reckless than the last—until he lay near death. Desperate, he summoned Peter Brandt. Peter arrived to find the man bled dry, purged beyond reason, and reduced to a shadow of himself. Without hesitation, Peter threw out every bottle of medicine in the room, flung open the window, and began anew. He mixed egg yolks with strong Schiedam gin and gave it in small doses. Then came a stew of meat cooked in red wine and water, seasoned with gentle herbs that did no harm. Slowly, the man recovered—though he looked more like a ghost wrapped in linen than a living man. But he was voluble, and word of his recovery spread through the streets. Peter’s name became known, not from skill, but from luck. And so, at long last, the quiet physician who had labored in obscurity for years found fame—and money.

The old man’s pride stirred; his purse filled. Yet he spent much on rare herbs and costly remedies, determined to invest every coin in healing arts. Margaret, however, quietly set aside a portion, tucked away in a white-walled chest. The victory was sweet—but too late. Its joy proved fatal.

One evening, as she kissed him goodnight, his voice seemed thick, unsteady.

The next morning, he was found speechless, barely conscious.

Margaret, who had been her father’s devoted student for years, recognized the signs at once: a stroke. But fearing her own judgment, she sent for a doctor. One of those who had been thwarted by Peter—yet failed to kill the civic dignitary—arrived promptly. He confirmed her diagnosis with cheerfulness and immediately recommended bleeding. She refused. “He always opposed bloodletting,” she said firmly, “especially in the aged.”

Peter lived—but never again as before. His memory faltered. He could no longer prescribe with certainty. Patients waited, some demanding only him, others willing to accept another healer. Misery loomed. Margaret resolved to seek counsel and comfort from her cousin William Johnson—someone she had avoided for years, out of pride and poverty.

She found him seated in a dim room with his servant, both clearly under the influence of drink. Suppressing surprise, she greeted them politely, then explained she had come to speak of family matters. With a quiet glance toward the servant, she hinted at privacy.

But the woman misunderstood.

“Oh, you can speak before me, can’t you, my old man?” she snapped.

Margaret flushed. “I beg your pardon, mistress. I did not know my cousin had fallen into the customs of this town. Well, I shall return another time.” She rose to leave.

“I don’t know what you mean by ‘customs of the town,’” the woman retorted, rising sharply. “But this I do know: it is the duty of a faithful servant to protect her master from being devoured by his beggarly kin.”

Margaret shot back, “You’re too modest, mistress. You are no servant. Your speech betrays you. It is only when the ape climbs the tree that we see her tail plainly. No—there sits the servant, God help him! And while it stands thus, fear not that his kin will ever be so poor in spirit as to come where the likes of you may mock their charity.” With one silent, reproachful look at her cousin—so passive, so powerless—she turned and left, proud and composed. Not until she reached home did she allow herself to weep.

Now two men were dependent upon her—a pregnant girl and a newborn child yet to come.

Yet it was this very child, though helpless, who would become their greatest ally.

Nature was strong in Margaret Brandt—the same nature that drives birds to build nests, beasts to hunt, insects to prepare for winter. Stirred by instinct, she sat and thought, and thought again, how to outpace destitution. Though five gold coins remained, she saw starvation creeping closer, inevitable and cold.

When women think with the intensity of men, they often think just as wisely. Margaret rose, instructed Martin to move Peter to another room, cleaned her chamber, polished the glass globe, hung it from the ceiling, dusted the crocodile, and nailed it to the outside wall. Then, after giving Martin careful directions, she stationed him as sentinel at the door, telling all who approached that a great, ancient, learned alchemist resided within—sometimes amusing himself by curing sickness.

Patients soon arrived. They demanded to see the doctor.

“That cannot be,” Margaret replied. “He is deep in study, seeking the philosopher’s stone. Not even princes may disturb him. Tell me your symptoms, and return in two hours.”

And oh, the mysterious power of belief! When they returned, the remedy was always ready.

Sometimes, if the patient was important, she examined his face, felt his pulse and skin, listened to his story, then carried it carefully to Peter’s room. There, she recalled how her father would have treated such a case, pondered deeply, wrote a cryptic prescription in illegible script, and brought it down with reverence. “Can you read this?” she would ask.

Usually, the reply was, “I am no reader,” or, with awe, “Nay, mistress, I cannot make sense of it.”

“Then let me,” she would say. “It is sovereign. Consider yourself cured.”

If she had the ingredients, she sometimes allowed the patient to watch her mix the medicine—consulting the sacred formula with anxious care, lest science suffer in her hands. So they left, lighter in purse, heavier in faith, deceived to their hearts’ content. *Populus vult decipi.* And when they were gone, she took down two small boxes Gerard had made—labeled *Today* and *Tomorrow*. Into *Today* went the smaller coins; into *Tomorrow*, the larger ones, along with the few remaining gold pieces from Sevenbergen. Thus, she met daily needs and saved for the storm she knew was coming. She blended drugs with simples, virtue with vice. Her conscience pricked her sharply. Each night, after the day’s deception, she knelt and prayed, “Forgive me, Lord, for the sake of my child.”

But then came reports of cures. And slowly, her conscience hardened.

Martin Wittenhaagen, once a burden, now proved useful. Like many who had endured hardship, he had stiffened—not softened. Still strong, he could carry the doctor herself around Rotterdam. Instead, he carried her slops.

In this new role, he displayed soldierly traits: obedience without question, punctuality, accuracy, speed—and drunkenness.

He fell in with “good fellows”—rogues who fed him Schiedam. He rambled, bragged.

Doctor Margaret had risen high in his esteem. All this—the crocodile as banner, the dotard hidden in ambush, the disposal of waste, the receipt of coin—struck him not as science, but as strategy. In his cups, he boasted before a mixed crowd: “Me and my General—we are a biting of the burghers!”

When this tale spread through the city, Doctor Margaret received a summons from the constables. They arrested her, trembling and pleading for mercy, and brought her before the burgomaster. Standing beside him were real physicians—men in long robes and square caps—accusing her of unlawfully treating the Duke’s subjects.

At first, she could not speak. The word *Law* paralyzed her. But when questioned gently, she told the truth: she had been her father’s pupil, followed his methods, and healed many. “And it is not for myself, sirs,” she said, “but I have two helpless, honest men at home. How else can I feed them? Ah, good sirs, let a poor girl earn her bread honestly. You do not hinder men from making theirs idly and shamefully. And oh, sirs, you are husbands, fathers—surely you must see I have cause to work and provide as best I may?”

Before the words left her lips, she wished she could take them back. She placed one hand over her heart, the other across her face, hiding her tears—but not the streams that slipped beneath.

Her plea fell on deaf ears. Perhaps a female bailiff might have yielded. But not these men.

“What concern is it of ours?” the burgomaster said. “Unless you swear never to break the law again, I will remit imprisonment—but exact the fine.”

Doctor Margaret clasped her hands and vowed, with trembling sincerity, never, never, never to cure body or beast again. Released, she turned at the door and curtsied humbly, thanking them for their mercy.

To pay the fine, she must open the *Tomorrow* box—immediately. But in excess caution, she had nailed it shut, afraid even temptation might prevail. Now, she could not remove the nails. The constables grew impatient. “Let us break it for you,” they offered.

“No,” she insisted. “You’ll break it worse than I.” Taking a hammer, she struck weakly, lost strength, and wept hysterically. At last, she broke it—and a tiny cry escaped her. She paid the fine—every penny of her unlawful earnings, plus two gold coins. When they were gone, she gathered the broken fragments and remaining coins on the table. Her arms wrapped around them. Her hair, rich and loose, tumbled down. She bowed her forehead upon the wreckage and sobbed, “My love’s box is broken—and my heart with it.” She stayed there, motionless.

Martin entered. She could not lift her head. “What has happened?” she whispered. “My love’s box is broken—and so is mine.”

Martin was not sad—angry. Some traitor had betrayed her. Who had spoken? Whose stony heart had delivered her to this fate? Whoever it was, he would feel the point of his arrow. The irony of his position—delivering justice in the form of a shaft—did not occur to him.

“Idle talk! idle talk!” moaned Margaret, her brow still pressed to the table. “When you’ve killed every gossip in this town, can we eat them? Tell me how to keep us all, or hold your tongue and let the saints whisper to me.”

Martin fell silent, casting uneasy glances at his defeated General.

That evening, she rose, washed her face, tied up her hair, and ordered Martin to take down the crocodile and put out a basket instead.

“I can launder better than anyone on this street,” she said. “And you must carry it.”

“For your sake, I will,” he replied.

“Good Martin. Forgive me—I spoke harshly.”

As they spoke, a man came for advice. Margaret told him the mayor had forbidden her to sell medicine. “But,” she added, “I will gladly wash, iron, and starch your linen. I will come to your house and fetch it.”

“Are you mad, young woman?” he snapped. “I came for a doctor, not a washerwoman!” And he stormed off.

“There goes a foolish creature,” she sighed.

Soon, a woman came to describe her sick child. Margaret stopped her.

“We are forbidden to sell medicine. But I will wash, iron, and starch your linen—for you. I will come and fetch it from your house.”

“Oh, yes,” said the woman. “I have smocks and ruffs in need of cleaning. Come for them. And when you’re there, you can look at the boy.” She gave the address and the time her husband would be away—though she liked him well enough.

An introduction is an introduction. Two or three patients, denied medicine, became Doctor Margaret’s customers.

“Now, Martin, you must help. I’ll no more cats than can slay mice.”

“Mistress, the stomach is not lacking, but the headpiece—worst luck.”

“Oh, I don’t mean starching and ironing—that takes a woman, and a skilled one. But washing? A man can manage that. Why, a mule has wit enough to do it with his hoofs, if you’d drive him into the tub. Come—off with your doublet, and try.”

“I am your man,” said the brave old soldier, stripping for the task. “I’ll risk my arm in soapsuds—if you’ll risk your glory.”

“My what?”

“Your glory and honor as a—washerwoman.”

“Gramercy! If you’re man enough to bring me half-washed linen to iron, I’m woman enough to throw it back into the suds.”

So the brave girl and the brave soldier worked together, keeping the wolf from the door. More they could not do. Margaret repaired the *Tomorrow* box. As she leaned over the glue, her tears mingled with it. She cemented her exiled lover’s box with her sorrow. A smile may be allowed here—but one of pity, not mockery. Not the empty laughter of the modern fool, ridiculing sacred things. But when mended, the box stood empty.

They paid rent weekly, kept the pot boiling—but no more.

Then came a chain of events. Recommended by one to another, Margaret began washing for the mayor. One day, returning clean linen, she heard in the kitchen that the mayor’s only daughter was gravely ill, likely to die.

Poor Margaret could not resist questioning. A maid told her what symptoms she had seen—but they were vague. Yet each gossip added a detail, another a clue. Margaret collected them all.

Later, she met the mayor himself. He recognized her instantly. “Why, you’re the unlicensed doctor.”

“I was,” she replied. “Now I’m your worship’s washerwoman.”

He reddened. “That’s rather a fall.”

“Nay, I bear no malice. You might have been harder. Rather, I wish to do you a kindness. Sir, you have a sick daughter. Let me see her.”

He shook his head. “I cannot. I enforce the law on others—I may not break it myself.”

Margaret stared. “Alas, sir, I seek no reward now for curing folk. I am a washerwoman. I believe one may heal the world—if only the world will starve her in return.”

“That is just,” said the mayor. “And if you make no profit from it, there is no offense.”

“Then let me see her.”

“What good will it do? The finest doctors in Rotterdam have seen her—and helped nothing. Her illness is inscrutable. One says spleen, another liver, another blood, another stomach. Another says she is possessed. Indeed, she seems haunted—shuns company, pines alone, eats less than a sparrow. Speaks rarely, hears none. Grows thinner, paler, nearer death.”

“Sir,” said Margaret, “if you took your velvet doublet to six shops in Rotterdam and asked if it was fine, worth how much per ell—six traders would agree exactly. Why? Because they know their trade. But your doctors disagree. Why? Because they do not know theirs. Each is enamored of one ailment, sees it in every patient. Had they never seen your daughter, they would have said the same—spleen, blood, stomach, lungs, liver, madness—or possession. Let me see her. We are of the same sex. That matters much.”

Still hesitant, the mayor hesitated.

“Saints of heaven!” she cried, losing patience. “Is this how men love their own flesh and blood? Her mother would have taken me in her arms and brought me to her bedside.”

Two violet eyes flashed fire.

“Come with me,” he said hastily.

“Mistress, I have brought thee a new doctor.”

The young woman, pale and eighteen, gave a contemptuous twist of her shoulder and turned further toward the fire.

Margaret sat beside her softly. “But one who will not torment you.”

“A woman?” exclaimed the girl, surprised and scornful.

“Tell me your symptoms.”

“For what? You’ll learn nothing.”

“You’ll lose nothing.”

“Well, I have no appetite, and no heart for anything. Now cure me and go.”

“Patience. Does your food taste flat in your mouth?”

“Yes. How did you know?”

“I did not—until you told me. I think you would fare better with a little good company.”

“I doubt it. What use is their chatter to me?”

Margaret asked the father to leave. Alone, she asked practical questions. Then reflected.

“When you wake in the morning, do you tremble?”

“No. Yes. How did you know?”

“Shall I give you medicine—or merely tease you with my silly talk?”

“Which you will.”

“Then I shall tell you a story. Of two true lovers.”

“I hate stories of lovers,” said the girl. “But since you insist, it may be less nauseating than your physic.”

Margaret told the tale of Ursel and Conrad—Ursel, daughter of an old physician; Conrad, son of a hosier at Tergou. Their adventures, sorrows, hardships. She told it from a woman’s heart, in a soft, earnest voice. Gradually, it captured the girl’s attention. When she paused, her patient was disappointed.

“Nay, nay—I must hear the end. I will.”

“But I do not know it. Only God does.”

“Oh, your Ursel was a jewel,” the girl said, truly moved. “Would she were here.”

“Instead of you?”

“I do not say that.” She blushed.

“You only think it.”

“Thought is free. But if she were here, I’d give her a kiss—poor thing.”

“Then give it to me. For I am she.”

“No, no—you cannot be.”

“I am. And pray, sweet mistress, do not break your word—kiss me, with a good heart. For oh, my heart lies heavy—just as yours does.”

The young lady rose, embraced Margaret, and kissed her. “I am sorry for you,” she sighed. “You are a good soul. You have done me good—just a little.” (A gulp caught in her throat.) “Come again. Come again.”

Margaret returned. She talked, watched closely, and discovered only one topic stirred her—love. Then she said to the mayor, “I know your daughter’s trouble—and it is curable.”

“The blood?”

“No.”

“The stomach?”

“No.”

“The liver?”

“No.”

“The devil?”

“No.”

“What then?”

“Love.”

“Love? Nonsense! Impossible! She is but a child. She never leaves the house unguarded. Never has.”

“All the better. Then we need not search far.”

“I shall command her to tell me the villain’s name—how he enchanted her with magic.”

“Oh, how foolish are the wise!” said Margaret. “Would you frighten her? No—let us work by art first. If that fails, then violence and folly may follow.”

She persuaded the mayor to pay all his servants in front of his daughter—on a Saturday.

Fifteen people entered, received their wages, and left with kind words. Margaret had sat close to the girl throughout. Then she rose and stepped out. The mayor followed.

“Sir, who is that black-haired lad?”

“That is Ulrich, my clerk.”

“Well then—it is he.”

“Heavens forbid! I took him from the streets.”

“But your worship is wise. You did not take him without reason?”

“Reason? None. I liked his looks.”

“Was that not merit? He pleased the father’s eye. And now—he pleases the daughter’s. This has happened since Adam.”

“How do you know?”

“I held her hand. Lightly touched her wrist. When others came and went, it was as if dogs and cats passed through. But when Ulrich entered, her pulse leapt, her eyes shone. When he left, she sank and sighed—as if the sun had vanished from the room. Nay, burgomateur, do not look at me so. I am no witch. Only a poor girl, taught by a neglected doctor’s wisdom. This has happened thousands of years before us. Wait here. Do not spoil good work with meddling.”

She returned to her patient and asked for a lock of her hair.

“Take it,” said the girl, listlessly.

“You are like a statue of marble. How long do you intend to remain so?”

“Until I am in my grave, sweet Peggy.”

“Who knows? Maybe in ten minutes, you’ll be burning hot.”

She ran to the shop, then returned swiftly.

“Good news! He loves her—more than a little. Now—what shall we do? Marry your daughter, or bury her? There is no third way. Shame and love rend her virgin heart to pieces.”

The mayor hesitated—then chose marriage. With effort, he accompanied Margaret to his daughter. But men are slow to drink wormwood. He stood silent.

Doctor Margaret smiled. “Mistress, your lock is gone. I sold it.”

“And who was mad enough to buy such a thing?” asked the girl scornfully.

“Oh, a black-haired lad with white teeth. They call him Ulrich.”

The pale face flushed—brow and all.

“He said, ‘Oh, sweet mistress, give it to me.’ I told him whose it was. ‘No,’ I said, ‘selling is my livelihood, not giving.’ So he offered this, that—nothing less than his next quarter’s wages.”

“Cruel,” murmured the girl, barely audible.

“You are in one tale with your father. He said to me, ‘If he loves her hair so well, surely he loves the rest of her.’ And he added, ‘An honest lad. He shall have her—provided she leaves her sulks and consents.’ So—what say you, mistress? Will you marry Ulrich—or be buried in the churchyard?”

“Father! Father!”

“It is so, girl—speak your mind.”
