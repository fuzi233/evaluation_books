A woman’s heart bears burdens as heavy as a man’s, though the world seldom sees them. We who write from the male perspective often sketch only the edges of her sorrow—leaving it to the woman reader to fill in the depth, the quiet agony, the silent strength that lies beneath. So too have I done here: not painted Margaret Brandt’s trials in full, but hinted at them, trusting the reader’s understanding to complete what words cannot fully convey.

And now, as the moment of her greatest trial drew near, Gerard was far away—beyond reach, beyond hope. She had little use for Eli’s sudden kindness, though he offered her comfort and shelter. Her reserve was easy enough to explain: she said she preferred solitude, that she needed privacy in this time of change. But the truth was deeper, more instinctive. There were two men in that house whose very presence chilled her blood. In their eyes she saw malice; in their glances, secrets whispered between them. She could not trust herself near them, nor endure their watchful silence. So she chose to remain at home, where she could be alone with her thoughts—and her fears.

Catherine came every day, wrapped in anticipation, her own motherly instincts overflowing. She was one of those women born to nurture, to multiply, to cradle life into being. Once the children came, they never left her side—not even when they grew strong and loud. She clucked over them like a hen, and somehow, through her care, they seemed to thrive. It was in this spirit she urged Eli to return to Tergou while she stayed behind in Rotterdam. “The poor lass has no one,” she said. “No one who knows how to help her. What good are two soldiers? They’d be better off out of doors in such a time.”

And so it was—a great comfort to Margaret. For all her fear, she found solace in Catherine’s presence, in her practical wisdom, in her unshakable faith. Yet still, her heart trembled. She would soon give birth, and Gerard was gone. The thought of dying alone, without him by her side, haunted her dreams. But life, in its strange balance, had prepared her for this moment. Poverty had taught her resilience. Her father’s frailty had forced her to rise each morning, to work, to endure. The rich, she knew, often fell prey to idle despair—but she had never known such luxury. She had always been on her feet, and now, thanks to Catherine’s steady counsel, she kept going until the end.

One evening, just as the sun dipped below the horizon, casting long shadows across the room, she lay weak and spent, but safe. Beside her, nestled close, was a small face—her child. And above them both, the heavens opened wide, not with fire or storm, but with the gentle light of new life. A peace settled over her, deep and undeniable.

“Why do you weep, sweetheart?” Catherine asked softly.

“All of a sudden,” Margaret murmured. “He is not here to see it.”

“Ah, well, lass,” Catherine replied, brushing a tear from her cheek. “He will be here before the babe is weaned. Meantime, God has been kinder to you than to any woman born. Think on it: it might have been a girl. My own Kate threatened me with one, and yet look—we have the bonniest boy in Holland, and a fine heavy one at that. Praise the saints for it!”

“Ay, mother,” Margaret whispered, her voice breaking. “I am a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to see it. It seems strange—I bore him well enough to be apart from me in my pain. But oh, how hard it is that he should not share my joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!” She stretched out her arms, feeble and pleading.

Catherine moved quickly about the room, busying herself with linens and blankets, but avoided looking directly at her daughter. Her own eyes filled with tears—she could not bear to hear her absent son named so fervently, so desperately.

Then, turning back, she found Margaret staring at her with a look neither of pain nor joy, but something deeper—something almost divine.

“Heard you nought?” Margaret asked.

“No, my lamb. What?”

“I did call upon Gerard—but now.”

“Ay, sure I heard that.”

“Well,” Margaret said, her voice hushed, “he answered me.”

“Tush, girl,” Catherine said, trying to laugh. “Say not that.”

“Mother,” Margaret insisted, “as sure as I lie here with his boy beside me—his voice came back to me. ‘Margaret!’ So. Yet methought it was not his happy voice. Perhaps it was the distance. All voices grow sad when they travel far.”

She paused, her gaze fixed on the infant, her eyes softening, glowing with a tenderness that made the room seem brighter.

“Why art not happy, sweetheart?” Catherine asked gently. “And I so happy this night?”

“Mother,” Margaret whispered, “I seem never to have felt a pain or known a care.”

And then, silence.

That very night, Gerard threw himself into the Tiber.

And at that same hour, as she heard his name spoken in her mind, he cried it aloud—into the jaws of death, into the depths of despair.

“Margaret!”

Let those who can, account for it. I cannot.
