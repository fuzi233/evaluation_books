To truly grasp the architecture of Fra Colonna's worldview, one must first confront the absolute poverty of his perception regarding the grandeur of the Eternal City. On those rare occasions when the artist Gerard was permitted to walk the streets alongside the friar, the world unfolded *en beau*—wrapped in the suffocating, golden haze of Roman imperial nostalgia. Yet, the friar, a man of stark realism, consistently drenched these romantic reveries in cold water.

"This place Rome?" Fra Colonna would scoff, gesturing to the cobblestones beneath their boots. "It is merely the tomb of mighty Rome." He dragged Gerard's attention downward, pointing out how twenty or thirty feet of ancient triumphal arches lay buried beneath the modern thoroughfares, and how contemporary palaces sprawled directly atop the severed heads of colossal columns. Coupling the cramped confines of the current city with the gigantic, skeletal vestiges of antiquity that poked through the soil like rotting teeth, the friar delivered a simile that cut deeper than any blade. "I tell thee," he intoned, "this village they call Rome is but as one of those swallow's nests ye shall see built on the eaves of a decayed abbey."

"Old Rome must indeed have been fair then," Gerard murmured, captivated by the ghost of history.

"Judge for yourself, my son," the friar countered, his voice dripping with cynical authority. "You see the great sewer, a work of the Romans in their very childhood, destined to outlast Vesuvius itself. You see the fragments of the Temple of Peace. Imagine, if you could, the Capitol with its twenty-five temples. Note this Monte Savello; what is it, if you please, but the ruins of the ancient Theatre of Marcellus? And as for Testaccio, one of the highest hills in modern Rome, it is but an ancient dustheap—the refuse of old Rome, where women flung their broken pots and pans until they formed a mountain."

*"Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem."* From the foot, Hercules; from the claw, the lion.

Gerard listened with respectful deference, but when the holy friar proceeded by analogy to imply that the moral superiority of the pagan Romans was proportionately grand, he resisted stoutly. "Has then the world lost anything by Christ's coming?" he challenged, though he immediately flushed, sensing the weight of his own ingratitude toward his benefactor.

"Saints forbid!" the friar snapped, recoiling as if struck. "'Twere heresy to say so." Having made this direct concession, he quickly retreated into the labyrinth of subtle circumlocution, evading the theological trap until they reached the forbidden door via a spiral back staircase.

In the midst of their journey, they arrived at a church where a knot of agitated persons gathered in the porch. Inside, a demon was being exorcised. Fra Colonna possessed a peculiar tic: a curious, low moan that escaped him whenever facts contrary to Zeno or Epicurus were presented to him. This sound conveyed to those who knew it not merely strong dissent, but a profound pity for human credulity, ignorance, and error—especially when such blindness prevented men from seeing the merits of Paganism.

The friar moaned softly and declared, "Then come away."

"Nay, father, prithee! Prithree! I ne'er saw a devil cast out."

Reluctantly, the friar accompanied Gerard into the nave, giving a final, exaggerated shrug. There, huddled before the altar, knelt the demoniac, a scarf tied tightly around his neck, used by the officiating priest to hold him fast like a dog on a chain. Few attended; rumor had spread that the last demon expelled in this church fled no further than into the lap of one spectator—"like a coney ferreted out of one burrow running to the next."

When Gerard and the friar approached, the priest seemed satisfied that sufficient witnesses had assembled. He rose, breviary in hand, and began the ritual. First, he sought to identify the entity tormenting the victim.

"Come out, Ashtaroth. Oho! It is not you then. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No; he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul."

"Aha! What, have I found ye? 'Tis thou, thou reptile; at thine old tricks. Let us pray!"

"Oh, Lord, we pray thee to drive the foul fiend Nebul out of this thy creature: out of his hair, and his eyes, out of his nose, out of his mouth, out of his ears, out of his gums, out of his teeth, out of his shoulders, out of his arms, legs, loins, stomach, bowels, thighs, knees, calves, feet, ankles, finger-nails, toe-nails, and soul. Amen."

The priest then rose from his knees, turning to the congregation with quiet geniality. "Gentlemen, we have here as obstinate a devil as you may see in a summer day." Facing the patient, he spoke with rigorous intensity, alternating between addressing the man and the fiend. Through the same trembling mouth, the victim alternated between hatred of the holy names and complaints of internal agony.

It was the priest who first confused the victim with the culprit in the public imagination, launching physical assaults—cuffing the man soundly, kicking him, and spitting repeatedly in his face. He then ignited a candle, deliberately dimmed it, and burned it until the flame seared his own fingers, dropping it hastily. Next, he produced the *custodia*, displaying the *Corpus Domini* within. He lit another candle, this time with caution, and addressed the demoniac in his human guise with civility, dismissing him and accepting the applause of the crowd.

"Good father," Gerard observed, "you have their names by heart. Our northern priests possess no such exquisite knowledge of the hellish squadrons."

"Ay, young man, here we know all their names, and eke their ways, the reptiles. This Nebul is a bitter hard one to hunt out."

With affable ease, the friar recounted several of his experiences, culminating in a tale from the previous day: driving a great hulking fiend out of a woman through her mouth, leaving behind nails, pins, and a tuft of hair, while the creature screamed in anguish, *"Tis not thou that conquerest me. See that stone on the window sill. Know that the angel Gabriel coming down to earth once lighted on that stone: 'tis that has done my business."*

The friar let out a characteristic moan. "And you believed him?"

"Certes! Who but an infidel would discredit a revelation so precise?"

"What, believe the father of lies? That is pushing credulity beyond endurance."

"Oh, a liar does not always lie."

"Ay, doth he whenever he tells an improbable story to begin, and shows you a holy relic, arming you against the Satanic host. Fiends—if any exist—are not so simple. Thou shouldst have answered him out of antiquity: *'Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes.'"*

"Some blackguard chopped his wife's head off on that stone, young man; you take my word for it." With a final flourish of dismissal, the friar hurried Gerard away.

"Alack, father, I fear you abashed the good priest."

"Ay, by Pollux," the friar chuckled, wiping sweat from his brow. "I blistered him with a single touch of 'Socratic interrogation.' What modern can parry the weapons of antiquity?"

Later that afternoon, after Gerard completed his day's work, a fine lackey appeared demanding his attendance at the Palace Cesarini. He was ushered into a noble apartment where a girl sat working on a tapestry. She rose, excused herself, and returned shortly with news that her mistress wished to see him.

An hour passed, the silence stretching taut, until Gerard began to fret. "These nobles think nothing of a poor fellow's time." Just as he resolved to slip out, the door swung open, admitting a superb beauty followed by two maids. It was the young princess of the house of Cesarini. She entered speaking loudly and haughtily to her attendants, but upon spotting Gerard, her voice softened to a feminine whisper. "Are you the writer, messer?"

"I am, Signora."

"'Tis well."

She seated herself on a high chair, almost a throne, while Gerard and the maids remained standing.

"What is your name, good youth?"

"Gerard, signora."

"Gerard? Body of Bacchus! Is that the name of a human creature?"

"It is a Dutch name, signora. I was born at Tergou, in Holland."

"A harsh name, girls, for so well-favoured a youth; what say you?"

The maids assented warmly.

"What did I send for him for?" the lady inquired with lofty languor. "Ah, I remember. Be seated, Ser Gerardo, and write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover; at least he says so."
