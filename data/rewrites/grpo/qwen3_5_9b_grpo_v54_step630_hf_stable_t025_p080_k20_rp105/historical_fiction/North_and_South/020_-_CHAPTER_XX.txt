Margaret returned home weighed down by the heavy burden of what she had witnessed and heard, finding it difficult to summon the energy required for the duties awaiting her. Her mother, confined to the house, relied upon Margaret's brief excursions to bring news and maintain a stream of cheerful conversation.

"And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?"

"She was so ill I never thought of asking her," Margaret replied, her voice heavy with sorrow.

"Dear! It seems everyone is ill these days," Mrs. Hale remarked, a flicker of envy crossing her face as one invalid often feels toward another. "But it must be terribly sad to be sick in one of those narrow back streets." Her natural kindness soon overcame this sentiment, and the old habits of Helstone returned to her thoughts. "It is hard enough here. What could you possibly do for her, Margaret? Mr. Thornton has sent me some of his vintage port since you left. Do you think a bottle would do her any good?"

"No, mother! I do not believe they are destitute; they certainly do not speak as if they were. Besides, Bessy's illness is consumption—she will not require wine. Perhaps I might bring her a jar of preserves made from our beloved Helstone fruit. Oh, mother, mother! How am I to dress in my finery and attend these smart gatherings after the grief I have seen today?" Margaret finally broke the restraint she had imposed upon herself, pouring out the details of her visit to Higgins's cottage.

The news distressed Mrs. Hale profoundly, leaving her restless and irritated until compelled to act. She ordered Margaret to pack a basket in the drawing-room to be sent immediately to the family. She was nearly angry when Margaret suggested it did not matter if it waited until morning, knowing Higgins had arranged for their immediate needs and that she had left money with Bessy. Mrs. Hale chided her daughter for being unfeeling and refused to rest until the basket was dispatched.

"Nevertheless, we may have been acting wrongly," she mused later. "It was only the last time Mr. Thornton visited that he remarked those who prolong the struggle by aiding the strikers are not true friends. Was not that Boucher-man a striker?"

Mr. Hale, fresh from a lesson with Mr. Thornton that had devolved into conversation, was consulted by his wife. In her agitated state, Margaret did not dwell on the broader implications of their charity. Mr. Hale listened with the composure of a judge, recalling the stark clarity of Mr. Thornton's words from moments prior. He reached an uneasy compromise. While he admitted that, as a general rule, refusing aid that propped up folly during a strike—which would inevitably end with masters importing labor or inventing machines—was the kindest course, he could not condemn their specific action regarding Boucher. He resolved to visit the man the following morning to ascertain what could be done.

The next day, Mr. Hale found Boucher absent but spoke at length with his wife. Promising to secure an Infirmary order for her, and observing the ample provisions Mrs. Hale had left behind—lavishly consumed by the children who ruled the house in their father's absence—he returned with a report far more consoling than Margaret had dared to hope for. Indeed, her father's imagination, reacting to her previous alarm, painted the situation as better than it truly was.

"I shall go again to see the man himself," Mr. Hale declared. "I struggle to compare these dwellings with our cottages in Helstone. Here, laborers possess furniture our workers could never dream of buying, and foods they would deem luxuries. Yet, with their wages stopped, the pawnshop appears to be their only recourse. One must learn a different standard here in Milton."

Bessy, too, showed signs of improvement, though her weakness was such that she seemed to have forgotten her feverish desire to see Margaret dressed, or perhaps that desire had been a delirious fantasy.

Margaret could not help but contrast this strange preparation for a visit she dreaded with the joyful, girlish dressing sessions she and Edith had shared less than a year ago. Her only pleasure now in adorning herself was the thought that her mother would delight in seeing her. She blushed when Dixon opened the drawing-room door to offer his admiration.

"Miss Hale looks well, ma'am, doesn't she? Mrs. Shaw's coral could not have been chosen better. It provides the perfect touch of color, ma'am. Otherwise, Miss Margaret, you would have been too pale."

Margaret's thick black hair was too dense for braiding; instead, it was twisted into massive, silky coils that encircled her head like a crown, gathered into a large spiral knot behind, held fast by two large coral pins resembling small arrows. Her white silk sleeves were looped with matching cords, and heavy coral beads rested against her neck, just below the curve of her milk-white throat.

"Oh, Margaret! How I wish I could accompany you to one of the old Barrington assemblies, taking you as Lady Beresford used to take me!"

Margaret kissed her mother in response to this burst of vanity, though she could barely smile, her spirits so low.

"I would rather stay at home with you, much rather, mother."

"Nonsense, darling! Be sure to observe the dinner closely. I shall love to hear how they manage things in Milton. Especially the second course, dear. Look what they serve instead of game."

Mrs. Hale would have been astonished had she seen the sumptuousness of the table and its appointments. Margaret, with her London-bred palate, found the array of delicacies oppressive; half the quantity would have sufficed for a tighter, more elegant effect. Yet, it was one of Mrs. Thornton's rigid laws of hospitality that every separate dainty be provided for all guests who cared to partake. Careless of abstinence in her daily life, it was part of her pride to set a feast before her guests. Her son shared this sentiment. He had never known society that did not depend on the exchange of superb meals; even now, though he denied himself the unnecessary expense of a sixpence and regretted sending the invitations, he was glad to witness the old magnificence of preparation.

Margaret and her father arrived first. Mr. Hale was punctually anxious. The drawing-room was empty save for Mrs. Thornton and Fanny. Every cover had been removed, and the apartment blazed with yellow silk damask and a brilliantly flowered carpet. Every corner was filled with ornament until it became a weariness to the eye, presenting a strange contrast to the bald ugliness of the view into the great mill-yard, where wide folding gates stood open for carriages. The mill loomed high on the left, casting a shadow from its many stories that darkened the summer evening before its time.

"My son was engaged up to the last moment on business. He will be here directly, Mr. Hale. May I beg you to take a seat?"

Mr. Hale stood by a window as Mrs. Thornton spoke. Turning away, he asked, "Do you not find such close proximity to the mill rather unpleasant at times?"

She drew herself up proudly. "Never. I have not become so refined as to desire to forget the source of my son's wealth and power. Besides, there is not another factory in Milton. One room alone is two hundred and twenty square yards."

"I meant that the smoke and the noise—the constant comings and goings of the workpeople—might be annoying!"

"I agree with you, Mr. Hale!" Fanny interjected. "There is a continual smell of steam and oily machinery, and the noise is perfectly deafening."

"I have heard noise called music far more deafening," Mr. Hale replied calmly. "The engine-room is at the street-end; we hardly hear it except in summer when windows are open. As for the murmur of the workpeople, it disturbs me no more than the humming of a hive of bees. If I think of it at all, I connect it with my son and feel how all belongs to him, that his is the head that directs it. Just now, there are no sounds from the mill; the hands have been ungrateful enough to turn out, as perhaps you have heard. But the very business I mentioned refers to the steps he intends to take to teach them their place." The stern expression on her face deepened into dark anger as she spoke, and it did not clear when Mr. Thornton entered. She instantly perceived the weight of care and anxiety he carried, though his greeting to his guests appeared cheerful and cordial. He shook hands with Margaret. She was unaware it was the first time their hands had met. He inquired after Mrs. Hale and heard Mr. Hale's hopeful account; glancing at Margaret to gauge her agreement, he saw no dissenting shadow on her face. As he looked with this intention, he was struck anew by her great beauty. He had never seen her so dressed, yet now the elegance of her attire seemed befitting her noble figure and lofty serenity of countenance, suggesting she ought to always appear thus. She was talking to Fanny about things he could not hear, but he observed his sister's restless manner, her continual adjusting of her gown, her wandering, purposeless eyes. He contrasted this uneasily with Margaret's large, soft eyes that looked steadily at one object, as if from their light beamed a gentle influence of repose; the curving lines of her red lips, parted in listening; the head bent forward to create a long, sweeping line from the light-catching raven hair to the smooth ivory tip of her shoulder; the round white arms and tapering hands, laid lightly across each other in a perfectly motionless, pretty attitude. Mr. Thornton sighed as he took it all in with one of his sudden, comprehensive glances. Then he turned his back on the young ladies and threw himself, with effort but with all his heart and soul, into conversation with Mr. Hale.

More people arrived, increasing the crowd. Fanny left Margaret's side to assist her mother. Mr. Thornton felt that in this influx no one was speaking to Margaret, and he was restless under this apparent neglect. Yet, he never approached her himself; he did not look at her. Only he knew what she was doing—or not doing—better than anyone else in the room. Margaret was so unconscious of herself and so amused by watching others that she never considered whether she was left unnoticed. Someone escorted her to dinner; she did not catch the name, nor did her host seem inclined to talk to her. A very animated conversation proceeded among the gentlemen, while the ladies remained mostly silent, taking notes on the dinner and critiquing each other's dresses. Margaret caught the thread of the general conversation, grew interested, and listened attentively. Mr. Horsfall, the stranger whose visit had sparked the gathering, asked questions regarding the trade and manufactures of the place, while the other Milton men offered answers and explanations. A dispute arose, warmly contested, and was referred to Mr. Thornton, who had hardly spoken before but now gave an opinion so clearly stated that even the opponents yielded. Margaret's attention was drawn to her host; his manner as master of the house and entertainer was so straightforward, simple, and modest that it was thoroughly dignified. Margaret thought she had never seen him to such advantage. When he had come to their house, there had always been something of over-eagerness or a vexed annoyance, as if he felt unjustly judged yet too proud to explain himself. Now, among his peers, there was no uncertainty regarding his position. He was regarded as a man of great force of character and power. There was no need to struggle for their respect; he possessed it and knew it, and the security of this gave a fine, grand quietness to his voice and ways that Margaret had missed before.

He was not in the habit of talking to ladies, and what he did say was somewhat formal. To Margaret herself, he hardly spoke at all. She was surprised to realize how much she enjoyed the dinner. She knew enough now to understand many local interests, even some of the technical terms used by the eager millowners. She silently took a very decided part in the question they were discussing. At least, they spoke in desperate earnest—not in the used-up style that had worn her down at old London parties. She wondered that, with all the dwelling on manufacturers and trade, no allusion was made to the pending strike. She did not yet know how coolly such matters were taken by the masters, viewing them as having only one possible end. To be sure, the men were cutting their own throats, as they had done many times before; but if they were fools enough to put themselves into the hands of a rascally set of paid delegates, they must take the consequence. One or two thought Thornton looked out of spirits, and of course, he must lose by this turn-out. But it was an accident that might happen to anyone; and Thornton was as good at managing a strike as any in Milton. He was as iron-willed a chap as any in Milton. The hands had mistaken their man in trying that dodge on him. They chuckled inwardly at the idea of the workmen's discomfiture and defeat in their attempt to alter one iota of what Thornton had decreed.

It was rather dull for Margaret after dinner. She was glad when the gentlemen came, not merely because she caught her father's eye to brighten her sleepiness, but because she could listen to something larger and grander than the petty interests the ladies had discussed. She liked the exultation in the sense of power these Milton men displayed. It might be rather rampant, savoring of boasting, but still they seemed to defy the old limits of possibility in a kind of fine intoxication caused by the recollection of what had been achieved and what yet should be. If in her cooler moments she might not approve of the spirit in all things, there was much to admire in their forgetfulness of themselves and the present, in their anticipated triumphs over all inanimate matter at some future time none of them would live to see.
