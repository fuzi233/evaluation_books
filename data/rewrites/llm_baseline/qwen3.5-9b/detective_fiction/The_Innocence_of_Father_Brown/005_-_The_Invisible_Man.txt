In the cool, blue twilight of two steep streets in Camden Town, a confectioner's shop at the corner glowed with an intensity that resembled the burning butt of a cigar—or perhaps, more accurately, the spent fuse of a firework. Its light fractured into a kaleidoscope of colors, bouncing off mirrors and illuminating piles of gilt-wrapped cakes and gaily colored sweetmeats. Against this fiery backdrop, the noses of numerous children pressed close to the glass, mesmerized by chocolates wrapped in metallic reds, greens, and golds that seemed superior to the treat within. Dominating the display was a massive white wedding cake, appearing simultaneously distant and delicious, as inviting as if the entire North Pole were edible. While such rainbow temptations drew the neighborhood's youth aged ten or twelve, this corner held a specific allure for older lads as well. Standing before the window was a young man, no younger than twenty-four, captivated by the scene. To him, the shop possessed a fiery charm, though his fascination extended beyond mere confectionery.

He was a tall, burly figure with red hair, a resolute face, and a notably listless demeanor. Tucked under his arm was a flat, grey portfolio filled with black-and-white sketches he had sold to publishers with varying degrees of success. His career had suffered a significant setback when his uncle—an Admiral—disinherited him for his Socialistic views, following a lecture the young man had delivered condemning that economic theory. His name was John Turnbull Angus.

Finally entering the establishment, Angus walked through the shop toward the back room, a makeshift pastry-cook restaurant. He raised his hat respectfully to the young lady serving there. She was a striking figure: dark, elegant, and alert, dressed entirely in black, with a flushed complexion and piercing, rapid dark eyes. After a brief pause, she followed him into the inner room to take his order.

His request was precise and routine. "I want, please," he stated, "one halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee." Before the girl could turn away, he added, "Also, I require you to marry me."

The young lady stiffened instantly. "Those are jokes I do not permit," she declared sharply.

Angus lifted his grey eyes, revealing a gravity that surprised her.

"Truly and sincerely," he replied, "this is as serious—as serious as the halfpenny bun. It is costly, like the bun; one pays for it. It is indigestible, like the bun. It causes pain."

The dark-eyed girl never broke her gaze, scrutinizing him with an almost tragic intensity. By the end of her examination, a faint shadow of a smile touched her lips, and she seated herself.

"Do you not consider," Angus observed absent-mindedly, "that it is somewhat cruel to consume these halfpenny buns? They might mature into full-sized penny buns. I shall abandon these brutal customs once we are wed."

The girl rose and walked to the window, lost in deep, sympathetic contemplation. When she finally turned back with an air of resolve, she was startled to find Angus meticulously arranging various items from the shop window onto the table. There was a pyramid of brightly colored sweets, several plates of sandwiches, and two decanters holding the mysterious port and sherry typically favored by pastry cooks. In the center of this neat assembly, he had carefully lowered the enormous load of sugared white cake that had served as the window's centerpiece.

"What on earth are you doing?" she demanded.

"Duty, my dear Laura," Angus began.

"Oh, for heaven's sake, stop a moment!" she cried, "and cease talking to me in that manner. What is all this?"

"A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope."

"And what constitutes that?" she asked impatiently, gesturing to the mountain of sugar.

"The wedding-cake, Mrs. Angus," he corrected gently.

The girl marched to the artifact, shoved it back into the window with a clatter, and returned to the table, resting her elegant elbows on the surface. She regarded Angus unfavorably, though with considerable irritation.

"You give me no time to think," she said.

"I am not such a fool," he answered calmly; "that is my Christian humility."

She continued to stare at him, her expression growing increasingly grave beneath a lingering smile.

"Mr. Angus," she said steadily, "before we proceed further with this nonsense, I must inform you briefly about myself."

"Delighted," Angus replied gravely. "You might share details about yourself while I await yours."

"Oh, do hold your tongue and listen," she snapped. "It involves nothing I am ashamed of, nor anything I regret specifically. However, imagine my reaction if there existed something that is no concern of mine, yet haunts me as a nightmare?"

"In that event," the man said seriously, "I would suggest you retrieve the cake."

"Well, you must hear the story first," Laura insisted. "First, I must mention that my father owned the 'Red Fish' inn in Ludbury, and I used to serve in the bar."

"I have often wondered," Angus interjected, "why this particular confectioner's shop carries such a distinctly Christian atmosphere."

"Ludbury is a sleepy, grassy village in the Eastern Counties," Laura continued. "The only visitors to the 'Red Fish' were occasional traveling salesmen, and otherwise, the most dreadful types imaginable—though you've likely never encountered them. I mean small, lounging men with barely enough to survive, leaning against bar counters betting on horses, clad in clothes just slightly too fine for their station. Even these wretched youths were rare at our house, but two of them were exceptionally common. Both lived on their own means, were painfully idle, and overdressed. Yet, I felt a pang of pity for them, for I suspected they frequented our empty bar due to slight physical oddities—the sort of quirks locals mock. It wasn't exactly a deformity, more an anomaly. One was a remarkably small man, akin to a dwarf or a jockey. He lacked a jockey's appearance, possessing instead a round black head, a neatly trimmed black beard, and bright, bird-like eyes. He jingled coins in his pockets and wore a heavy gold watch chain. He appeared only dressed extravagantly, yet never quite like a true gentleman. Though a futile idler, he was curiously clever at useless arts: impromptu magic tricks, setting fifteen matches ablaze like fireworks, or carving bananas into dancing dolls. His name was Isidore Smythe. I can still see him approaching the counter, fashioning a jumping kangaroo from five cigars."

"The other fellow was quieter and more ordinary, yet he terrified me far more than poor Smythe. He was very tall and slight, with light hair. His nose had a high bridge, giving him a spectral sort of handsomeness, marred by one of the most appalling squints I have ever witnessed. When he fixed his gaze upon you, you lost your bearings, unsure of your location or what he was seeing. I suspect this disfigurement embittered him; whereas Smythe delighted in showing off his monkey tricks, James Welkin—the squinting man—never performed anything but soaking in our bar parlor and taking solitary walks through the flat, grey countryside surrounding us. Still, I believe Smythe was also sensitive about his stature, though he managed it with greater style. Thus, I was genuinely puzzled and startled when both men proposed marriage within the same week."

"Well, I did what I now deem a foolish thing. Yet, these fellows were my friends in a way, and I feared they would think I rejected them solely because they were impossibly ugly. So, I concocted an excuse regarding my principles: I claimed I would never marry someone who hadn't earned their own fortune, refusing to live on inherited wealth. Two days after delivering this well-intentioned speech, the entire trouble commenced. The first news I received was that both had departed to seek their fortunes, as if stepping into a silly fairy tale."

"I have never seen either since," Laura admitted. "However, I received two letters from the little man, Smythe, which were quite thrilling."

"Have you heard from the other?" Angus asked.

"No," Laura hesitated. "He never wrote. Smythe's first letter merely stated he had started walking to London with Welkin, but Welkin was such a proficient walker that Smythe dropped out to rest by the roadside. He was subsequently picked up by a traveling show. Being nearly a dwarf and genuinely clever, he thrived in entertainment, eventually rising to perform at the Aquarium. That was his first missive. His second was far more alarming, arriving only last week."

Angus drained his coffee cup, watching her with mild patience. A slight twist of amusement played on her lips as she continued, "Presumably, you have seen the hoardings advertising 'Smythe's Silent Service'? Or perhaps you are the sole individual unaware of it. It concerns some clockwork invention designed to automate household chores. You know the concept: 'Press a Button—a Butler Who Never Drinks.' 'Turn a Handle—Ten Housemaids Who Never Flirt.' You must have seen the ads. Whatever these machines are, they are generating immense profits, all for that little imp I knew in Ludbury. I cannot help but feel glad the poor chap has succeeded; yet, frankly, I am terrified he may appear any moment to claim he has 'carved his way in the world,' as he undoubtedly has."

"And the other man?" Angus repeated, his tone obstinately quiet.
