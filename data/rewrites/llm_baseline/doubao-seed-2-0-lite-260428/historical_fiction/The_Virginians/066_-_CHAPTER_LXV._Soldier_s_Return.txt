You may marvel at the endless, vainglorious posturing of great powers, and of the French in particular: would you believe that after the skirmish at St. Cas – little more than a small rearguard action, by all accounts – they wasted whole casks of gunfire in celebration at Paris’s Les Invalides, blustering and bragging over our misfortune? Is there any shred of magnanimity in cheering because five or six hundred brave souls were trapped by ten thousand foes on a tidal shore, and met the fate that ever threatens the hindmost of a retreat?

I had half a mind to paint a merry picture of London’s rejoicings for our earlier glorious success at St. Malo: polished captured guns dragged in procession by grinning tars, stout horse-grenadiers prancing past, the mob waving hats, roaring cheers, and picking pockets all the while, while our dear friends looked down on the triumph from a Fleet Street balcony. But now that the French have copied the Tower of London’s displays and set their trivial St. Cas victory up against our St. Malo win, I scorn to waste words on the stale affair. *Nolo, non Malo* – I want no part of that foolish one-upmanship. All I care for is that Harry returned from the whole sorry business with a whole skin. Have I ever said he was so much as bruised? Have I not, for fear of distressing my tender young readers, insisted he was in finer fettle than he had ever been in his life?

The sea air had tanned his cheek, the musket ball that whistled past his temple had only singed the curl at his side. The ocean soaked his gaiters and his uniform through to the skin, but never swallowed him. It is true he showed his coat lapels to the enemy for a little while – but only for as short a time as possible, slipping out of their line of sight as quick as his legs could carry him. And what are lapels, after all, but reverses? Coats wear them just as men do, and our only duty is to bear small setbacks with courage and good humour.

“I can tell you,” Harry recounted to his gathered friends, “we all ran for it when our line broke. The luckiest man was he who reached the boats first. French horse and foot were hard on our heels all the way down the beach, cutting down stragglers and bayoneting the wounded on the sand. Poor Armytage was shot no more than three paces ahead of me, and fell. I hoisted him up over my shoulder and staggered through the surf to our boat. The sailors in our craft didn’t flinch an inch, even though shot was whistling past their ears, shattering their oar blades, and riddling their ensign through and through. The officer in command was as cool as if he’d been sipping punch in a Portsmouth tavern – and you may be sure we drank a full bowl of that very punch the moment we set foot back on English soil. Poor Sir John was less fortunate than me. He never lived to reach the ship. The service has lost a fine soldier, and Miss Howe a true gentleman for a husband. These casualties are inevitable, you see. His younger brother inherits the baronetcy.”

“It is the poor lady I am thinking of,” Miss Hetty cut in, the story half-told. “And the King! Why did the King encourage Sir John to go? No gentleman could refuse a command from his sovereign. And now the poor man is dead! Oh, what a state his Majesty must be in!”

“I have no doubt his Majesty is in a very deep state of grief,” said Papa Lambert, wagging his head with a dry little smile.

Hetty flushed. “You are laughing! Do you mean to say, sir, that when a gentleman dies almost in his service, the King of England would not feel for him? If I thought that, I vow I would turn for the Pretender this very day!”

“That saucy tongue of yours would make a very pretty ornament for Temple Bar’s spiked heads,” the General teased, for he saw the turmoil under Hetty’s sharp words: the remorse, the consternation, the gush of gratitude that Harry had come home unharmed, sending her small heart beating fast under her stays. “No, my dear. If kings wept for every private soldier lost in battle, what a life of misery we would condemn them to. I think better of his Majesty than to imagine him so weak. And if little Miss Hester Lambert got her Pretender on the throne, I doubt she would be any happier. That Stuart family was never famed for being overburdened with tender feelings.”

“But if the King sent Harry – that is, Sir John Armytage – off to the war where he lost his life, oughtn’t his Majesty to repent very deeply?” the young lady pressed.

“If Harry had fallen, no doubt the court would have gone into full mourning. As it is, the ladies and gentlemen were all in bright coloured gowns and coats only yesterday,” remarked the General.

“Why not light bonfires to celebrate our defeats, and put on sackcloth and ashes to mark our victories?” George put in, dry as ever. “I protest I have no desire to thank Heaven for helping us burn a handful of fishing ships at Cherbourg.”

“Ah, but you do, George!” Harry said, earnest, even though he acknowledged his brother’s wit was far sharper than his own. “When your country wins a fight, you’re glad – I know I am. And when I had to run before the French, I was ashamed. I couldn’t help it, even though I did it. It doesn’t seem right, somehow, that an Englishman should ever have to turn his back to the foe.” He paused, and grew grave. George smiled, but did not ask his brother what the average French private might think of the matter.

“’Twas a bad business,” Harry went on, “but lucky it was no worse. Word from prisoners we exchanged says the French commander, the Duke of Aiguillon, was what you’d call a soft, moistened chicken. Our whole retreat could have been cut off to a man, only we happened to be in a very great hurry to get away ourselves. The French local militia fought famously, I will say that – dozens of their gentry volunteers marched right in the front line, as men of station ought to do. They say the Chevalier of Tour d’Auvergne charged after us despite the Duke’s explicit orders to hold back. The officers who brought over our lists of prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe told us all that. By the by, George – that young Howe is a lord now, since word came home his elder brother was killed fighting in America. He’s a brave fellow, no matter if he’s a commoner or a peer.”

“And his sister, who was to have married poor Sir John Armytage – only think what a state of grief she must be in!” Hetty sighed, who had grown far more tender and sentimental these past weeks.

“And his mother!” added Mrs. Lambert. “Have you seen her advertisement in the newspapers, appealing to the electors of Nottingham? ‘Lord Howe being now absent upon the public service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favour of your votes and interests that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother as your representative in Parliament.’ Is that not a gallant woman?”

“A perfectly Laconic woman,” George agreed. “What son could fail to be brave, raised by a mother like that?” the General added.

The two Warrington brothers glanced at each other, unspoken understanding between them. “If one of us were to fall in defence of his country, we have a Spartan mother back in Virginia who would write just that same notice,” George said.

“If Sparta lies anywhere south of the Potomac, I reckon we do,” Harry put in, grinning. He fell quiet again, pensive. “And to think we two have both faced the enemy now, and both been whipped by him, brother.”

Hetty looked at him, and her heart ached. She saw him in her mind’s eye, staggering through the surf towards the boats, his wounded comrade heavy on his shoulder, the French closing in behind, shot flying all around him. It was her words that had goaded him to volunteer for the expedition, to prove he was no idle fop. And now he was home, and he never once reproached her for it. Unless directly asked, he barely spoke of his adventures at all. He was gentle, grave, and unfailingly courteous to Hetty, and open and tender to every other member of the Lambert household. But those taunts she had flung at him had cut deep. His whole bearing seemed to say to her, Little hand, you never ought to have been lifted against me! It is cruel to scorn any man, much less one who has been so devoted to you and yours. I may not be quick of wit, but where the heart is concerned, I am the equal of the best of men, and the very best of my heart has always belonged to your family.

Harry’s quiet endurance of the wrong she had done him restored his place in Hetty’s esteem, a place he had lost in those months of idle, aimless drifting before the expedition. She gave him the respect that all brave men are owed: she no longer snapped at him with pert answers, no longer made cutting, sarcastic remarks about his conduct. In a word, Hetty was humiliated, and altered, and greatly improved.

The whole world seemed to change its mind about Harry, just as he had changed himself. He was no longer sulky and indolent, no longer moping over his own unworthiness or defying his neighbours to find good in him. The colonel of his regiment wrote an official report praising his conduct as exemplary, and recommended him for one of the vacant ensign’s commissions left open by the men who fell at St. Cas. Unlucky as the expedition’s end had been, it had turned out a stroke of fortune for him. When the rest of the volunteers trickled back to St. James’s Street, they all sang his praises. The volunteers and their exploits were the toast of every drawing room in town. Even if he had been a commanding general struck down mid-victory, Sir John Armytage could scarcely have earned half the public sympathy the nation lavished on the survivors and dead of the failed Brittany raid. The newspapers brimmed with letters eulogising him, wits and men of feeling vying to write the most beautiful epitaph in his honour, and the whole country bewailed the fate of his affianced bride.

Lord Howe himself spoke of Mr. Henry Warrington’s courage in the highest terms, and so Harry received his full share of the favour and admiration the city lavished on all the returning volunteers. No doubt thousands of common soldiers who had fought alongside them were every bit as brave – but the English nation has always loved its gentlemen to distinguish themselves in battle, and the young volunteers were unanimously hailed as modern Paladins and heroes. White’s and Almack’s put on illuminations for their homecoming, and all of St. James’s welcomed the young knights back with open arms. Even Harry’s distant relations wrote to congratulate him. A letter arrived from Castlewood, where Aunt Bernstein had lately taken up residence, full of praise for his valour and tucked with a generous little bank bill as a token of her affectionate approval. It came enclosed under Lord Castlewood’s frank, who sent his kind regards to both the Warrington brothers, and an open invitation to come and stay at his country house whenever they pleased.

Along with that, a private letter arrived for Harry, spelt very badly but in a handwriting that made him smile the moment he saw it. His affectionate cousin Maria Esmond wrote that she had been hearing nothing but his praises everywhere, and shared fully in all his good and bad fortune, and that whatever happened to him, she begged he would keep a small little place for her in his heart. Parson Sampson, she added, had preached a wonderful sermon that very week on the horrors of war and the noble courage of gentlemen who volunteered to face battle for their country. The chaplain soon wrote to Harry himself, a gushing, overheated letter hailing him as his friend, his benefactor, his glorious hero. Even stuffy old Sir Miles Warrington sent a hamper of game all the way from Norfolk: one small bird, shot sitting, had a string and note tied round its leg, sent with Harry’s love, as the very first victim of young Miles’s new fowling-piece.

And before long, Mr. Lambert came trundling over to their Southampton Row lodgings, his face bright with unmistakeable joy, to announce that Mr. Henry Warrington was to be gazetted forthwith as Ensign in the Second Battalion of Kingsley’s, the 20th Regiment of Foot, which had seen heavy fighting in the Brittany campaign and was now being reorganized as the distinct new 67th Regiment. Its colonel had not been with the men on the St. Cas expedition: he was off at Cape Breton, even then leading the capture of those guns at Louisbourg whose triumphant arrival in England had sparked such national celebration just weeks before.
