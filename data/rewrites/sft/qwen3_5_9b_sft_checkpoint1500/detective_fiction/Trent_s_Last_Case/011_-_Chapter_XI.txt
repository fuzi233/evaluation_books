Hitherto Unpublished

My Dear Molloy:—Should I fail to locate you at your desk, this letter serves as proof that I have finally identified the killer of Manderson. The facts contained herein implicate an unsuspected individual, practically accusing him of the murder. I trust you will exercise discretion regarding publication prior to his arrest, as doing so prematurely would be both unethical and potentially illegal. However, once he stands trial and is convicted, the material may become public. Until then, you are free to utilize the information as you see fit, perhaps by contacting Scotland Yard. Consider this inquiry closed; I have exhausted the Manderson mystery, and I pray I never encounter another of its ilk. Attached follows my full dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I commence this, my third and likely final bulletin concerning the Manderson homicide, with a mixture of professional satisfaction and personal revulsion. A profound sense of relief washes over me, for in my previous reports, duty compelled me to withhold critical data. Revealing those truths sooner would have alerted a cunning adversary, granting him time to evade capture. Now, I shall disclose everything. Yet, I confess a distaste for the narrative of treachery and twisted intellect I am about to recount. It leaves a bitter aftertaste, revealing a malice buried beneath the surface of the crime itself, a motive that remains as dark as the puzzle of the killing.

As recounted in my initial dispatch, I arrived at Marlstone on the Tuesday preceding the tragedy. I detailed the grim discovery of the body near the shed, the eerie silence reported by the household despite the evident struggle and gunshot, and the peculiar absence of Manderson's dental plate. I also noted the missing bottle of whisky. By the time of the inquest, I had concluded an investigation that has now pinpointed the single individual who must absolve himself of Manderson's death.

Two anomalies, obvious to any observer from the start, haunted my initial analysis. First, the total silence within the house. The butler, Martin, was notoriously insomniac and possessed ears of exceptional sensitivity; his room, situated with windows agape, overlooked the very shed where Manderson's body lay. Had anyone struggled with the victim, or fired a pistol, Martin surely would have woken. Second, Manderson's forgotten denture. To leave one's false teeth behind after rising, dressing completely, and stepping out into the night was inconceivable for a man of Manderson's habits, where oral hygiene and appearance were paramount.

Initially, these quirks offered no direction. They merely deepened the shadow of the mystery. However, a singular observation shifted the trajectory of my entire investigation.

While cataloguing Manderson's extensive collection of footwear, I focused on the pair he wore on the fatal evening. Though the shoes were high-quality, old, and polished, a close inspection revealed a telling defect. The leather around the vamp, specifically where the lacing meets the upper, had split slightly. Furthermore, the stitching securing the upper to the sole showed signs of severe strain. Such damage occurs only when a foot larger than intended forces itself into tight leather. Manderson, meticulous about his narrow feet, never wore shoes that pinched. Therefore, someone else had worn these specific shoes recently, and the tears were fresh.

This deduction triggered a cascade of realizations. Manderson was rarely drunk, yet the missing whisky suggested otherwise. He was fastidious, yet the body was found disheveled, in unwashed, yesterday's clothes, and devoid of his denture. He was taciturn, yet the "corpse" had been conversational with his wife. Most jarring was the fact that Manderson supposedly left the house without his teeth.

Every thread of evidence pointed to a single, terrifying conclusion: The man who walked out of Manderson's bedroom that night, and the man who allegedly returned later, was not Manderson.

If this were true, the man who wore Manderson's clothes, mimicked his voice, and spoke to his wife and butler must have done so to create an illusion. Why? To establish that Manderson was alive in the house until after midnight. This implied that Manderson was already dead before the intruder arrived.

The pieces fell quickly. The intruder needed to avoid footprints, so he used Manderson's shoes. He needed to prove Manderson was home, so he left the denture and the clothes. He needed to explain his presence, so he fabricated a story about sending Manderson's secretary, Marlowe, to Southampton.

A crucial oversight in my initial review emerged now: Neither Martin nor Mrs. Manderson had ever seen the intruder's face. Martin saw only the back of a man crouching by the telephone, wearing Manderson's hat, imitating a characteristic posture. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep, heard a voice she believed to be her husband. The intruder, possessing a frame similar to Manderson's and skilled in mimicry, required no mask save for the hat and jacket.

I visualized the scene. The intruder, fresh from a violent act elsewhere, slipped into the house via the garden window, avoiding the alert Martin. He consumed the missing whisky, calming his nerves before embarking on his performance. He moved to Manderson's room, placed the body there, stripped it, and prepared the stage for his deception. When Mrs. Manderson murmured from her adjacent room, asking if the drive had been pleasant, the impostor answered in Manderson's voice, volunteering details about Marlowe's trip to Southampton—a lie designed to anchor a timeline that would protect him.

Following this hypothesis, I reconstructed the night's events. Between ten and eleven, Manderson was shot, presumably far from the house. His body was dragged to the shed and stripped. Around eleven, the impostor entered the library, disguised as Manderson, rang for the butler, and feigned a late-night phone call. He then ascended to the bedroom floor, hid Marlowe's revolver in his room, and staged the scene in Manderson's chamber. Finally, clad in Manderson's clothes, he exited through the balcony, dropping silently onto the lawn.

But who was this phantom?

Five criteria defined the suspect. He must have been intimately acquainted with Manderson, capable of perfect mimicry, physically similar in stature and shoulder width, familiar with the household routines, and possess large feet relative to Manderson's.

Mr. John Marlowe, Manderson's former private secretary, fit every bill. They were nearly identical in height and build, though Marlowe was slightly slimmer. As a former student actor at Oxford, renowned for his character roles and imitative skills, he possessed the necessary artifice. He had lived within the Manderson circle, knowing their secrets. And critically, he had a motive to fabricate Manderson's survival until after midnight.

His alibi was robust, yet fragile. He claimed to have traveled to Southampton by 6:30 AM the following morning. If he had departed Marlstone at 10:30 PM, the drive would have been impossible for the vehicle he used, a modest Northumberland, to reach Southampton by 6:30 AM. However, if Manderson died at eleven, and Marlowe assumed his identity shortly thereafter, he would need to exit the house unseen before Martin, who stayed awake until 12:30 AM, could intercept him.

How did he escape?

I spent the remainder of the day before the inquest verifying my theory. I brought a camera to White Gables. Under the guise of searching for clues, I photographed the scene. On the polished front of the dresser in Manderson's room, I found two large, distinct fingerprints. On the glass bowl holding Manderson's lost denture, and on the panes of the French window in Mrs. Manderson's room, I found additional prints.

Back at the hotel, I compared these latent prints with samples I had previously obtained from Marlowe. The match was undeniable. Two of the large prints on the dresser belonged to Marlowe's left hand; the others to his right. The prints on the window and the bowl were unequivocally his.

The evidence was irrefutable. Marlowe had been in Manderson's bedroom and Mrs. Manderson's room. He had handled the denture and the furniture. The timeline held: he entered the house, performed his charade, and fled through the balcony window, crossing the iron railing to the ground below. He had achieved his goal of making the world believe Manderson was alive until the witching hour, thereby shielding himself from suspicion of the murder which, in reality, had occurred hours prior.

By six o'clock, I had completed my analysis. By eight, the photographs confirmed the identity. By nine, I sat in my hotel room, ready to submit this truth to the authorities.

I conclude this account with absolute certainty: On the night of the murder, the man who impersonated Manderson, having disposed of the body and staged the room, lay in wait for Mrs. Manderson to fall asleep. Once assured of her silence, he crossed her threshold in stocking feet, carrying the bundle of stolen clothes, pushed open the balcony doors, and dropped to the grass below.

Within half an hour of entering the room, he was gone. The corpse was found the next morning, dressed in Manderson's attire. Marlowe appeared at Southampton at 6:30 AM, playing the role of the shocked, devoted secretary.

I finish this manuscript in my sitting room at Marlstone. It is four o'clock in the morning. I depart for London by noon, and immediately upon arrival, I shall deliver these pages to the Criminal Investigation Department.
