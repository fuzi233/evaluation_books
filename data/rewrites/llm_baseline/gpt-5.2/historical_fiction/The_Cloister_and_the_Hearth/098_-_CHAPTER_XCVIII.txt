A fortnight after, when the flat land about Gouda lay rinsed clean by a thin rain, a pale, bowed figure came up the causeway toward the Dominican house that stood beyond the town, where fields began again and the air changed its temper. The convent’s bell did not clang like a market bell; it spoke with a softer throat, and the sound went abroad over wet hedges and the breathing ditches, mingling with the plash of oars, the far cry of geese, the low, perpetual chew of wind in reeds. Even the gate—heavy oak, iron-scarred—made its own honest music when the porter drew it, not the brutal shriek of newfangled wheel and forge, but a deep complain that settled the mind instead of fretting it.

The man who entered had the look of one who had eaten little, and slept less. It was Gerard.

He came as men sometimes come home to a first hearth: not because it is grand, but because its smells and sounds are older than their grief. His own manse had become more than he could bear. Every board and ledge there remembered her; the very common noises were turned against him—footsteps that were not hers, a pot-lid that fell too sharply, a gust that rattled the shutters with an empty hand. Here, within these walls, the day was portioned by rule: not by appetite, nor by memory’s cruel caprice. The broth would be broth at noon, the bread would be bread at night; and the bell would tell him when to rise, when to kneel, when to sleep. He desired peace as a starving man desires a bowl held steady.

He asked speech with Brother Ambrose.

Ambrose governed the convent as deputy, the prior having lately died, and the new one, though appointed, not having yet arrived. Ambrose, kind by nature and made kinder by discipline, received Gerard as one receives a worn traveler: he did not begin with questions that bite, but with a chair, and a cup, and quiet.

They gave him the convent’s plain refreshment—dark bread that still held the scent of rye-fields, and a pottage of peas and herbs, not over-salted, warmed through with that slow, patient stirring that keeps the bottom from catching. The kitchen sounds came muffled through stone: the scrape of a ladle on the cauldron’s rim, the thud of a loaf on the board, the steady whisper of knife against onion. Such homely acoustics, ordered and unadvertised, can heal more than sermons; they are a language of care no proud tongue can translate.

Gerard entered as a novice, on probation; yet the truth was, he was a failing man and knew it, and came there to end life as he began it—under a rule, among brethren, near gentle Ambrose, his friend, and the other monks to whom his house and heart had always stood open. It was not that he sought to be remarkable in dying. He wanted the opposite: to slip into the convent’s rhythm as into a familiar tune, where the notes are known and there is no need to improvise pain into speech.

Ambrose, who knew his value and his sorrow, was not without a kindly hope of curing him and restoring him to his parish. With this view he lodged him in a comfortable cell over the gateway, where one could hear the gate-chain and the porter’s step, and, beyond, the low talk of travelers and the more distant hush of fields—a soundscape that keeps a man tethered to the world without dragging him into it. And Ambrose forbade him to fast or to practice any austerities.

“Eat,” Ambrose bade him, with that practical tenderness which understands that the soul rides the body like a rider on a horse. “Take the broth. Take the bread. You shall not scrape yourself to holiness. God is not fed by your hunger.”

So for some days Gerard obeyed. He drank what they set him—thin ale that smelt faintly of grain and smoke, water cool as a stone cup can keep it. He took the convent fare, small, regular, honest. Yet the food did not bloom in him; it only stayed him, as a little lamp stays the dark without conquering it. The past still pressed on him, but here it pressed more softly, as if the walls themselves remembered other sorrows and did not make a spectacle of his.

Then, in a few days, the new prior arrived.

He proved a very Tartar.

At first he was absorbed in curing abuses and tightening the general discipline. He strode through the house as if correction were a virtue in itself: ears pricked for laughter, eyes sharp for comfort, as though a man’s peace must always be purchased with a visible pinch. The convent’s sounds altered under him: the same bell now seemed to command more than to invite; the same footsteps in the cloister rang harder, for men walked as if on eggs for fear of being heard.

One day, hearing that the vicar of Gouda had entered the convent as a novice, the prior’s mouth tightened, and his words struck like a cleaver on a block.

“’Tis well,” said he. “Let him first give up his vicarage then, or go. I’ll no fat parsons in my house.”

So the prior sent for Gerard.

Gerard went.

He crossed the cloister, where the air smelt of damp stone and extinguished tallow, and of the kitchen’s distant steam. A sparrow chattered in the gutter, and the sound, small and innocent, seemed for a moment like a remembered word in a mother tongue—something one cannot quite carry into harsher speech. He mounted the stair, hearing his own breath in the hush, and came before the prior.

The moment they saw one another they both started, as if an old name, long buried, had been spoken aloud in the wrong language.

“Clement!”

“Jerome!”
