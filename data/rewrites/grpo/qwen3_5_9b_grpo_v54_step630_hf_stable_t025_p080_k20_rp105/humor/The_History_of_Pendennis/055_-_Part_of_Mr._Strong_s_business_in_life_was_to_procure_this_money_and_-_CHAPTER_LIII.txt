Fanny stood frozen, her breath hitching in her throat as she took in the scene before her. Two women blocked her path, and the elder one's face was a mask of such terrifying, inscrutable alarm that Fanny's heart hammered against her ribs. She knew instantly: this was Pen's mother. There was a haunting resemblance between the widow's haggard, sunken eyes and the fever-dream gaze Arthur had worn while tossing in his bed. Fanny cast a desperate, wistful glance at Mrs. Pendennis and then at Laura. Laura's face was as unyielding and expressionless as a block of granite. Hard-heartedness and a suffocating gloom clung to both newcomers; neither offered even a flicker of mercy or sympathy for the trembling girl. Fanny's eyes darted wildly to the Major standing behind them. Old Pendennis lowered his eyelids, peering up from beneath them with a stealthy, worried look directed at Arthur's poor little nurse.

"I—I wrote to you yesterday, if you please, ma'am," Fanny stammered, her limbs trembling violently as she spoke. She was as pale as Laura, whose sad, menacing visage loomed over Mrs. Pendennis's shoulder.

"Did you, madam?" Mrs. Pendennis replied, her tone crisp and cold. "I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. I am his mother, you understand."

"Yes, ma'am. I—this is the way to his—" Fanny began, but she was cut short. "Oh, wait a minute!" she cried out, panic rising in her chest. "I must prepare you for his—"

The widow, whose face had been hopelessly cruel and ruthless just moments before, suddenly recoiled with a gasp and a stifled cry.

"He's been so since yesterday," Fanny whispered, her teeth chattering uncontrollably.

From the open door of Pen's room erupted a horrid shriek of laughter. After several raucous shouts, the poor wretch began to sing a college drinking-song, then hurrahed and shouted as if he were in the midst of a wild wine-party, thumping his fist against the wainscot. He was completely delirious.

"He does not know me, ma'am," Fanny said, her voice breaking.

"Indeed. Perhaps he will know his mother; let me pass, if you please, and go in to him." The widow hurriedly shoved past little Fanny and swept through the dark passage leading into Pen's sitting-room. Laura sailed by Fanny without a word, and Major Pendennis followed them. Fanny collapsed onto a bench in the passage, sobbing and praying as best she could. She would have died for him, yet they hated her. They offered not a single word of thanks or kindness, those fine ladies. She sat there in the gloomy corridor, unsure of how long she remained. They never came out to speak to her. She stayed until Doctor Goodenough arrived for his second visit of the day, finding the poor little thing huddled at the door.

"What, nurse? How's your patient?" asked the good-natured Doctor, his voice warm. "Has he had any rest?"

"Go and ask them. They're inside," Fanny answered, pointing vaguely toward the room.

"Who? His mother?"

Fanny nodded silently, unable to speak.

"You must go to bed yourself, my poor little maid," the Doctor said gently. "You will be ill, too, if you don't."

"Oh, mayn't I come and see him? Mayn't I come and see him! I—I love him so," the little girl pleaded. As she spoke, she fell to her knees, clutching the Doctor's hand in such an agony of devotion that it melted the kind physician's heart, causing a mist to cloud his spectacles.

"Pooh, pooh! Nonsense! Nurse, has he taken his draught? Has he had any rest? Of course you must come and see him. So must I."

"They'll let me sit here, won't they, sir? I'll never make no noise. I only ask to stop here," Fanny begged. The Doctor called her a stupid little thing, tapped her pale cheek with his finger, and bustled into the farther room.

Inside, Mrs. Pendennis was ensconced pale and solemn in a great chair by Pen's bedside. Her watch lay on the bed-table beside his medicines. Her bonnet and cloaks were laid out in the window. She held her Bible in her lap, a constant companion she never traveled without. Her first move upon seeing her son had been to take Fanny's shawl and bonnet from his drawers, bring them out, and drop them onto his study-table. She had closed the door on Major Pendennis and Laura, effectively taking possession of her son.

She had harbored a great terror that Arthur would not recognize her, but that pang was spared her in part. Pen knew his mother quite well; he smiled and nodded familiarly. When she entered, he instantly fancied they were back home at Fairoaks. He began to talk, chatter, and laugh in a rambling, wild way. Laura could hear him from outside. His laughter shot shafts of poison into her heart. It was true, then. He had been guilty—an intrigue with a servant-maid, and she had loved him—and he was dying, most likely raving and unrepentant. The Major occasionally hummed out a word of remark or consolation, which Laura barely heard.

It was a dismal sitting for everyone. When Goodenough appeared, he seemed like an angel descending into the room. It is not only for the sick man that the Doctor comes; it is for the sick man's friends. His presence is often as good for them as for the patient. We have all watched for him! What an emotion the thrill of his carriage wheels in the street, and finally at the door, has made us feel! How we hang upon his words, seeking comfort in a smile or two if he can vouchsafe that sunshine to lighten our darkness! Who hasn't seen the mother prying into his face to know if there is hope for the silent infant battling fever? Ah, how she looks into his eyes! What thanks if there is light there; what grief and pain if he casts them down, daring not say "hope!" Or it is the house-father who is stricken. The terrified wife looks on while the Physician feels his patient's wrist, smothering her agonies as the children are called to stay their play. Over the fever-stricken patient, the expectant wife, and the unconscious children, the Doctor stands as if he were Fate, the dispenser of life and death. He must let the patient off this time; the woman prays so for his respite! One can imagine how awful the responsibility must be to a conscientious man: how cruel the feeling that he gave the wrong remedy, or that he might have done better; how harassing the sympathy with survivors if the case is unfortunate—how immense the delight of victory!

After a hasty ceremony of introduction to the new arrivals, of whose arrival he had been made aware by the heartbroken little nurse waiting outside, the Doctor proceeded to examine the patient. The condition of high fever was undeniable, and he deemed it necessary to exercise the strongest antiphlogistic remedies in his power. He consoled the unfortunate mother as best he might, offering the most comfortable assurances he could venture—that there was no reason to despair yet, that everything might still be hoped for from his youth, the strength of his constitution, and so forth. Having done his utmost to allay the horrors of the alarmed matron, he took the elder Pendennis aside into the vacant room (Warrington's bedroom) for a little consultation.

The case was very critical. The fever, if not stopped, might and would carry off the young fellow; he must be bled forthwith; the mother must be informed of this necessity. "Why was that other young lady brought with her?" the Doctor asked. "She was out of place in a sick-room."

"And there was another woman still, be hanged to it!" the Major said. "The—the little person who opened the door." His sister-in-law had brought the poor little devil's bonnet and shawl out, flinging them upon the study-table. "Did Goodenough know anything about the—the little person?" the Major asked. "I just caught a glimpse of her as we passed in," the Major continued, "and begad she was uncommonly nice-looking." The Doctor looked queer; the Doctor smiled. In the very gravest moments, with life and death pending, such strange contrasts and occasions of humor arise, and such smiles pass to satirize the gloom, as it were, and to make it more gloomy!

"I have it," he said at last, re-entering the study. He wrote a couple of notes hastily at the table and sealed one. Then, taking up poor Fanny's shawl and bonnet along with the notes, he went out into the passage to that poor little messenger. "Quick, nurse; you must carry this to the surgeon, and bid him come instantly; and then go to my house, and ask for my servant Harbottle, and tell him to get this prescription prepared, and wait until I—until it is ready. It may take a little in preparation."

So poor Fanny trudged away with her two notes, found the apothecary who lived in the Strand hard by, and who came straightway, his lancet in his pocket, to operate on his patient. Then Fanny made for the Doctor's house in Hanover Square.

The Doctor was at home again before the prescription was made up, which took Harbottle, his servant, such a long time in compounding. During the remainder of Arthur's illness, poor Fanny never made her appearance in the quality of nurse at his chambers any more. But for that day and the next, a little figure might be seen lurking about Pen's staircase—a sad, sad little face looked at and interrogated the apothecary, the apothecary's boy, the laundress, and the kind physician himself as they passed out of the chambers of the sick man. And on the third day, the kind Doctor's chariot stopped at Shepherd's Inn, and the good, honest, and benevolent man went into the porter's lodge and tended a little patient whom he had there. For the best remedy he found was on the day when he was enabled to tell Fanny Bolton that the crisis was over, and that there was at length every hope for Arthur Pendennis.

J. Costigan, Esquire, late of Her Majesty's service, saw the Doctor's carriage and criticized its horses and appointments. "Green liveries, bedad!" the General said. "And as foin a pair of high-stepping bee horses as ever a gentleman need sit behind, let alone a doctor. There's no end to the pride and arrogance of them doctors nowadays—not but that is a good one, and a scientific character, and a right good fellow, bedad; and he's brought the poor little girl well through her fever, Bows, me boy;" and so pleased was Mr. Costigan with the Doctor's behavior and skill that, whenever he met Dr. Goodenough's carriage in future, he made a point of saluting it and the physician inside in as courteous and magnificent a manner as if Dr. Goodenough had been the Lord Lieutenant himself, and Captain Costigan had been in his glory in Phoenix Park.

The widow's gratitude to the physician knew no bounds—or scarcely any bounds, at least. The kind gentleman laughed at the idea of taking a fee from a literary man, or the widow of a brother practitioner; and she determined when she got to Fairoaks that she would send Goodenough the silver-gilt vase, the jewel of the house, and the glory of the late John Pendennis, preserved in green baize, and presented to him at Bath, by the Lady Elizabeth Firebrace, on the recovery of her son, the late Sir Anthony Firebrace, from the scarlet fever. Hippocrates, Hygeia, King Bladud, and a wreath of serpents surmount the cup to this day; which was executed in their finest manner by Messrs. Abednego, of Milsom Street; and the inscription was by Mr. Birch, tutor to the young baronet.

This priceless gem of art the widow determined to devote to Goodenough, the preserver of her son; and there was scarcely any other favor which her gratitude would not have conferred upon him, except one, which he desired most, and which was that she should think a little charitably and kindly of poor Fanny, of whose artless, sad story he had got something during his interviews with her, and of whom he was induced to think very kindly—not being disposed, indeed, to give much credit to Pen for his conduct in the affair, or not knowing what that conduct had been. He knew enough, however, to be aware that the poor infatuated little girl was without stain as yet; that while she had been in Pen's room it was to see the last of him, as she thought, and that Arthur was scarcely aware of her presence; and that she suffered under the deepest and most pitiful grief, at the idea of losing him, dead or living.

But on the one or two occasions when Goodenough alluded to Fanny, the widow's countenance, always soft and gentle, assumed an expression so cruel and inexorable that the Doctor saw it was in vain to ask her for justice or pity, and he broke off all entreaties, and ceased making any further allusions regarding his little client. There is a complaint which neither poppy, nor mandragora, nor all the drowsy syrups of the East could allay, in the men in his time, as we are informed by a popular poet of the days of Elizabeth; and which, when exhibited in women, no medical discoveries or practice subsequent—neither homoeopathy, nor hydropathy, nor mesmerism, nor Dr. Simpson, nor Dr. Locock can cure, and that is—we won't call it jealousy, but rather gently denominate rivalry and emulation in ladies.
