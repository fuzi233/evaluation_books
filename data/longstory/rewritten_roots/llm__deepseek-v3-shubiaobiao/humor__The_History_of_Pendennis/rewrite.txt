My Good Doctor,

Thirteen moons ago, when this tale seemed ready to bow off the stage and I with it, a certain benevolent soul dragged you to my sickbed—where, let’s be honest, I’d have likely stayed forever were it not for your relentless poking, prodding, and general refusal to let me expire.

I do enjoy reminiscing about your saintly patience (and the frankly shocking number of people who suddenly remembered they liked me) during that cheerful period when I was at my most charmingly bedridden. And since you stubbornly refused payment beyond a grunted “thank you,” allow me to engrave my gratitude here in ink, lest you forget:

(There. Now we’re even.)

Yours, with only mild exaggeration,

Ladies, gentlemen, and unfortunate souls who’ve stumbled upon this volume—welcome! What you hold in your hands (or glare at on your screen) is the literary equivalent of a slightly tipsy dinner guest: occasionally eloquent, often rambling, and prone to confessing things better left unsaid.

Yes, this book is flawed. Yes, it meanders. But isn’t that the charm of a good conversation? If you wanted polished artifice, you’d be reading someone far more dignified—someone who *doesn’t* admit to abandoning a perfectly good plot full of murderous rogues and swooning ladies because, frankly, the author realized he knew nothing about criminals. (A shame, really. I had grand visions of rivaling Eugène Sue, but it turns out my expertise ends at mildly improper dinner parties.)

Let’s be clear: this is not a tale of thrilling villainy. Colonel Altamont was *this close* to being shoved out a window for your entertainment, but alas, mercy prevailed. Instead, you get something far more scandalous—*the truth*. Or at least, my version of it.

Some of you, dear readers, have already clutched your pearls over my audacity to suggest that young men might, on occasion, *gasp*, be tempted by things. Shocking! Unthinkable! And yet, here we are. If you demand fiction where every hero is a paragon of virtue, untouched by human frailty, might I suggest a children’s fable? This story operates under the radical notion that people—even educated ones—are complicated.

So, proceed with caution. You’ll find no claptrap, no cheap thrills—just an earnest (if occasionally bumbling) attempt to reflect life as it is, not as your drawing-room etiquette insists it ought to be. If that offends, well… my apologies. But not enough to change a word.

Farewell, and may your reading be at least as entertaining as my writing *wasn’t* at times.

—Yours, with only *mild* regrets,
Pendennis
KENSINGTON, 1850 (or thereabouts)

Major Arthur Pendennis was a man of routine, and his routine was *exquisite*. Every morning at precisely a quarter past ten, he graced his favorite Pall Mall club with his presence, looking every inch the retired military gentleman—if military gentlemen retired with immaculate gloves, suspiciously lush whiskers, and a cane that practically announced his arrival with a fanfare. His boots were polished to a mirror finish, his cravat crisper than a fresh banknote, and his waistcoat buttons bore the royal crown—because, naturally, even his *buttons* had connections.

The club staff treated him like visiting royalty, which suited the Major just fine. His usual table—strategically positioned by the fire but near the window—was his throne, and woe betide any upstart who dared claim it. (Two had tried, once. They did not try again.) His mail was already arranged for his inspection: elegant little notes from marchionesses, solemn dinner invitations from bishops, and one particularly thick envelope from the Marquis of Steyne, requesting his presence at Richmond for an evening of French conversation (which the Major spoke *flawlessly*, thank you very much).

As he sifted through this mountain of social obligation, one letter sat apart—plain, provincial, and decidedly *not* from a duke. Its postmark read *Clavering St. Mary’s*, and its seal was distressingly homemade. The Major, with the air of a man forced to acknowledge a distant and slightly embarrassing cousin, finally broke it open.

Inside, disaster.

His sister Helen’s handwriting trembled with maternal distress: *Arthur—my dearest boy—has lost his mind. He insists on marrying an actress. She is twelve years his senior. Help.*

The Major’s breakfast nearly became his last.

A second letter—this one from Arthur himself—confirmed the horror. The boy had fallen headlong into the clutches of one *Miss Fotheringay* (née Costigan), a thespian of allegedly ancient Irish royalty, if her father’s drunken boasts were to be believed. Arthur’s prose was a masterpiece of youthful melodrama: *"A love like mine is contracted once and for ever!"* he declared, as if he were the first idiot to say such a thing. *"I shall die without ever knowing another passion!"* (The Major made a mental note to send the boy a dictionary, lest he confuse *passion* with *lunacy*.)

Worse still, Arthur had the gall to invoke *Virgil*—*"qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes"*—as if quoting Latin somehow made eloping with a middle-aged tragedienne more respectable. The Major’s eye twitched. His evening at Richmond—*gone*. His plans to dine with bishops—*postponed*. Instead, he would spend the night rattling in a mail coach toward the countryside, where he would have to disentangle his nephew from the clutches of a woman old enough to know better.

As he penned furious cancellations to his illustrious hosts, one thought burned brighter than his indignation: *This is what comes of letting boys read poetry.*

In the bustling days of George the Magnificent’s reign, tucked away in the quaint town of Clavering, lived a gentleman by the name of Pendennis. Those with long memories might recall his name once painted above a humble shopfront in Bath, adorned with a golden pestle and mortar—where Mr. Pendennis practiced the dual arts of apothecary and surgeon. There, he not only dosed feverish gentlemen and ushered ladies through delicate predicaments but also sold hair-powder and toothbrushes to farmers’ wives over the counter.

Yet behind this modest trade lay a pedigree as old as the Druids—or so the Pendennises claimed. Their lineage, tangled with Norman blood and whispered connections to Welsh and Breton nobility, was a point of pride. Young John Pendennis had even tasted university life at Cambridge before his father’s debts yanked him back to the mortar and pestle. He loathed the trade, but necessity (and a pushy uncle in the same business) left him little choice.

Fate intervened when Lady Ribstone, a baronet’s wife, stumbled into his shop after a collision with a drunken sedan chair. Smelling salts and flattery later, Pendennis found himself appointed her personal apothecary. From there, his fortunes rose: the shop grew smarter, the toothbrushes vanished, and soon he traded his apron for a gig, then a carriage emblazoned with the Pendennis crest. His bedridden mother, watching from her window, wept with pride.

By forty, Pendennis had shed his past entirely. He bought an estate in Clavering, framed his dubious pedigree, and styled himself a country squire. He married Helen Thistlewood, a gentlewoman left penniless by the death of her tyrannical patroness, Lady Pontypool. Helen, though decades younger, brought grace and quiet devotion to the union. Their son, Arthur, grew up believing his father had always been a gentleman—oblivious to the pills and plasters of Bath.

At Fairoaks, their ivy-clad manor, Pendennis played the part flawlessly: inspecting crops, hosting dinners, and name-dropping his brother, Major Pendennis, a fixture in London society. The Major, ever the bachelor, regaled them with tales of lords and ladies, though he sniffed at local heiresses. Meanwhile, Helen—radiant, pious, and kind—drew admiration and envy in equal measure.

Young Arthur idolized his mother, her goodness as unchanging as the sunset over Clavering Park. Yet beneath the idyll lurked the family’s open secret: the apothecary’s past, the fabricated pedigree, and the relentless striving to belong among those who never quite forgot where the Pendennises began.

Arthur Pendennis, at the ripe age of sixteen, had officially entered what he considered his golden years—though his mother still referred to him as "her dear boy" and insisted on straightening his collar whenever he was within arm’s reach. In appearance, he was what polite society might call "sturdy," though his mother preferred "athletic." His hair, a respectable shade of brown that turned suspiciously golden in sunlight (a phenomenon he credited to his own radiant charm), crowned a face that was round, perpetually flushed, and freckled in a way that suggested either robust health or an overfondness for sunbathing. His whiskers, when they finally deigned to appear, were of a reddish hue—less "dashing military officer" and more "young man who has been experimenting with his father’s shaving kit." Still, his eyes were blue, his laugh was hearty, and his mother remained convinced he was the handsomest boy in the county.

The question of further schooling had been settled with the ease of a well-placed hint. Pen, with the dramatic flair of a seasoned thespian, had lamented the *terrible* influences at Greyfriars—those *wild* older boys, the *shocking* vices, the *certain moral ruin* that awaited him should he return. His mother, clutching her handkerchief as if warding off an invisible wave of debauchery, promptly declared that home was the safest place for him. His uncle, ever the pragmatist, had suggested the military—specifically, the Foot Guards, where Pen might strut about in a splendid red coat and impress young ladies at Sunday promenades. Pen’s heart swelled at the thought. He had visions of himself in gleaming boots, saber at his side, perhaps even frowning heroically in the direction of France.

His mother, however, reacted as if he had proposed joining a circus. "A *soldier*, Arthur? *My* son?" Her voice trembled with the horror of it. "Your dear father would never have allowed it!" (Mr. Pendennis, deceased, was conveniently summoned as an authority on all matters whenever Helen required unassailable moral backing.) And so, with a sigh and the air of a man sacrificing his dreams on the altar of maternal love, Pen relinquished his military ambitions—though he privately consoled himself by galloping about the countryside on his mare, Rebecca, imagining himself a dashing cavalry officer whenever no one was looking.

When not engaged in these martial fantasies, Pen dabbled in literature—or, more accurately, he devoured every book in the house that wasn’t nailed down. Greek tragedies, French novels, epic poetry—if it had words, he read it, though his retention was questionable. His tutor, the perpetually flustered Mr. Smirke, attempted to guide him through the classics, but Pen preferred to *gallop* through the Iliad rather than *plod*, as Smirke insisted. The result was that Pen could quote Homer impressively—if selectively—while retaining only the vaguest notion of what any of it meant.

Smirke, for his part, was a man of delicate constitution and poetic inclinations, forever sighing over sonnets and casting soulful glances in the direction of Pen’s mother. Pen found this both amusing and vaguely irritating. Still, the curate was a useful audience for his own literary efforts, which at this stage consisted mostly of tragic verses addressed to imaginary women with names like "Zuleika" and "Ianthe." (Real girls, alas, were in short supply in the vicinity of Fairoaks, unless one counted the Admiral’s daughters, who were either too young, too loud, or too fond of giggling at their own jokes.)

Pen’s magnum opus, a blood-soaked tragedy in which no character survived past Act Two, was met with such uproarious laughter from his mother that he promptly tossed it into the fire. Undeterred, he embarked on an epic poem about Cortez conquering Mexico, though it stalled somewhere around the third stanza. Politics also seized his imagination—briefly. He wrote a fiery denunciation of the Jesuits (despite knowing very little about them) and rode about at election time waving a blue ribbon, convinced that he was single-handedly preserving the Protestant faith.

But beneath all this youthful posturing, there stirred the inevitable: *love*. Or, at least, Pen’s idea of it—a grand, poetic, thoroughly impractical emotion that required a muse. Alas, the countryside offered slim pickings. The local girls were either too young, too old, or too preoccupied with turnips to appreciate his soulful glances. He took to peering into passing carriages, hoping to glimpse some enchanting beauty, only to find elderly ladies or farmers’ daughters with complexions like well-scrubbed potatoes.

His mother, blissfully unaware of these romantic yearnings, had already decided that Pen would marry little Laura—once she was old enough, and he was settled as either a brilliant barrister or a charming country vicar. Pen, meanwhile, remained cheerfully oblivious to this maternal scheming, his head too full of poetry, dreams of glory, and the intoxicating notion that the world was vast and waiting for him.

And so, between galloping across fields, scribbling verses, and sighing over mythical women, Arthur Pendennis passed these early years—utterly convinced of his own sophistication, and entirely unaware of how very young he still was.

Upon returning to the George, Mr. Foker and his guest settled into the coffee-room for a hearty meal. Mr. Rincer, the waiter, served them with the solemnity of a man presenting a feast to royalty. Foker attacked the turtle soup and venison with the same vigor he once reserved for smuggled sausages and ginger beer. Pen couldn’t help but admire his friend’s discerning palate—especially when Foker denounced the champagne as "condemned gooseberry" but winked approvingly at the port.

Foker knew every servant by name, inquiring after their families with genuine interest. As the London coaches prepared to depart, he leaned out the window, shouting greetings to the drivers and guards, even mimicking the post-horns with startling accuracy.

"Sherry, sham, port, and a spot of coffee—not half bad, eh, Pen?" Foker declared, patting his stomach once the feast was done. Then, with a grin, he announced it was time to "toddle off" to the theatre.

Pen, flushed and bright-eyed, followed eagerly. They paid their shillings to the drowsy ticket-seller—"Mrs. Dropsicum, Bingley’s mother-in-law, terror of Lady Macbeth," Foker whispered—and found their seats in a half-empty house. A smattering of farmers, a few rowdy gallery boys, and a handful of free-admission loungers filled the space. In a private box, Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers and young Cornet Tidmus of the Dragoons heckled the actors between sips of brandy.

Bingley, the manager, took center stage as the brooding *Stranger*, clad in tight pantaloons, Hessian boots, and a cloak draped dramatically over his shoulders. His face, heavily rouged, peered out from beneath a massive wig, and his fingers glittered with stage jewelry—most notably a diamond ring he twirled with theatrical flair.

But Pen barely noticed Bingley. His attention was fixed on the moment Mrs. Haller—*the* Mrs. Haller—stepped onto the stage.

She was magnificent. Tall, regal, with a cascade of dark hair framing a face of such arresting beauty that Pen’s breath caught in his throat. Her eyes, deep and luminous, seemed to hold entire worlds of sorrow and mystery. When she spoke, her voice—rich, velvety—wrapped around him like a spell.

The audience erupted. Umbrellas thumped; officers cheered; Foker bellowed, "Bravo!" Pen, lost in rapture, barely registered the rest of the play. He only saw *her*—her every gesture, every glance, every tremble of her hand.

When the curtain fell, Pen was jolted back to reality. Foker elbowed him. "She’s a stunner, ain’t she?"

Pen didn’t answer. He couldn’t. His heart was racing, his thoughts a whirlwind.

The after-piece—a raucous farce featuring Bingley in tights—held no interest for him. Excusing himself, he stumbled out into the night, his mind ablaze. He rode home in a daze, the image of Mrs. Haller burning behind his eyelids.

By morning, the fever had only grown. He dressed with unusual care, his reflection in the mirror that of a man transformed. At breakfast, Laura eyed his finery with suspicion.

"Where are you off to?" she asked.

"Baymouth," Pen said airily. "An old schoolfellow is studying there. Thought I’d see how he’s getting on."

Laura frowned. His mother studied him, her expression unreadable.

Pen didn’t care. He mounted his horse and set off at a gallop, his heart pounding not from the ride, but from the wild, intoxicating thought—

Rebecca the mare, who clearly had a better sense of direction than her rider, thundered into Baymouth at a pace that suggested she knew where the best oats were stashed. Pen, clinging on for dear life, skidded to a halt at the inn, tossed her reins to a skeptical stable boy, and bolted toward Mr. Foker’s lodgings—conveniently located above a chemist’s shop that did a brisk trade in cigars and soda water, thanks to its clientele’s questionable life choices.

Upon arrival, Pen found not Foker, but his associate, Mr. Spavin—a man who looked like he’d been pickled in gin and left to dry in the sun. Spavin was engaged in the noble pursuit of teaching a small dog to perform tricks for bits of biscuit, a sight so profoundly absurd that Pen briefly questioned his life choices.

“Foker’s out,” Spavin drawled, eyeing Pen’s alarmingly fresh complexion with the disdain of a man who hadn’t seen sunrise sober in years. “Might be back in an hour. Might be back in a week. Might be dead in a ditch. Who’s to say?”

Disappointed but undeterred, Pen wandered down to the beach, where he took out his frustrations by hurling rocks into the sea like a man personally offended by the tides. When this proved unsatisfying, he decided to hunt Foker down like a bloodhound with a vendetta. Rebecca, who had just begun to enjoy her hay, was swiftly re-saddled and subjected to another gallop along the Chatteris road.

Four miles later, Pen arrived at the George Inn, where the hostler informed him—with the glee of a man who’d witnessed last night’s chaos firsthand—that Mr. Foker was indeed present, though currently “recovering” from a night of singing, brawling, and attempting to fight a postboy (a battle wisely avoided, as the hostler noted, “on account of Foker being built like a twig”).

Upstairs, Pen discovered Foker buried in a mountain of bedding, his face barely visible beneath a red silk nightcap.

“Hullo!” Pen called.

“Who goes there?” Foker croaked, as if auditioning for Hamlet’s ghost. “Pendennis? Good lord, man, are you here to drag me to church? What time is it?”

“One in the afternoon,” Pen replied.

Foker gasped in mock horror. “My mother would faint. She sent me here to ‘cultivate my neglected genius’—by which she meant Latin verbs, not whiskey.” He then proceeded to recount the previous night’s revelry, which involved three officers, a man named Bingley, and a certain Captain Costigan—who, Foker assured Pen, sang a *riveting* rendition of *The Little Pig Under the Bed*.

Speaking of Costigan, the man himself was waiting downstairs, having arrived at the ungodly hour of eleven, patiently sipping his way through the morning while Foker slept off his sins.

Pen, vibrating with nervous energy, was ushered into the sitting room, where he found the Captain—a man whose boots had seen better centuries—perched in the window, squinting at a newspaper as if it were written in hieroglyphics.

“Ah! The young gentleman from the cathedral yard!” Costigan boomed, executing a bow so elaborate it nearly toppled him. “I saw you admiring my daughter’s performance last night. A talent like hers comes but once in an age—nay, in *several* ages!”

Pen, who had been mentally rehearsing this moment since dawn, promptly forgot how to speak. He managed to stammer out something about Miss Fotheringay being the finest actress alive, which delighted Costigan so much that he clasped Pen’s hand like a long-lost son.

“You, sir, have a *soul*!” the Captain declared. “Not like that miser Bingley, who pays her a pittance! Two guineas a week! *Two!* And from that, she must clothe herself like the star she is!”

Pen, whose pockets were considerably lighter after this conversation, soon found himself handing over two sovereigns for tickets to her benefit show—an investment he would’ve doubled if not for fear of offending the Captain’s *delicate* sensibilities.

Breakfast was a spectacle. Costigan, upon spotting food, transformed into a man possessed, regaling them with tales of regimental feasts, duels avoided, and legendary nights of claret consumption. By the time the Curaçao arrived, he was weeping with joy at the privilege of dining with such “noble, generous youths.”

Then came the invitation Pen had been silently praying for:

“Would ye do me the honor, Mr. Pendennis, of calling upon my humble abode? I’d be proud to introduce ye to me daughter, the peerless Miss Fotheringay.”

Pen’s heart attempted to leap out of his chest. He made a noise that was half gasp, half squeak, and nodded so vigorously he nearly gave himself whiplash.

The Captain’s “humble abode” turned out to be a dim little room above a tailor’s shop, where two grubby children immediately assaulted Costigan’s coattails in hopes of sweets. Up the creaking stairs they went, Pen’s knees shaking so violently he was amazed he didn’t collapse into a heap.

And then—*there she was*.

Miss Fotheringay, offstage, was even more devastating. Every movement was a masterpiece. If she leaned against the mantelpiece, she looked like a Grecian statue. If she sat down, she arranged herself like a queen holding court. Her voice, when she spoke, was so rich and melodious that Pen briefly considered swooning for dramatic effect.

“It’s very fine weather,” she observed.

“Very,” Pen agreed, as if this were the wittiest exchange in human history.

The conversation meandered from the weather to theatrical gossip, during which Pen learned that “Ophalia” (as she pronounced it) was her next role. He barely registered the words—he was too busy marveling at the way she said *Pendennis*, rolling it around like a fine wine.

“I never knew my name was so pretty before,” he blurted.

She smiled. “’Tis a very pretty name. Not like Pentweazle.”

And just like that, Arthur Pendennis was lost forever.

Young Mr. Pen’s studies in Cicero and Euripides suffered a severe decline, much to the relief of his beleaguered tutor, Mr. Smirke, who found himself with an uncharacteristically light workload. The true victim of Pen’s distraction, however, was poor Rebecca, his horse, who endured frequent sprints to Chatteris under the pretense of fencing lessons—though more often, the lad was galloping off for entirely different engagements.

Pen’s clandestine excursions to Prior’s Lane were frequent, and when Rebecca went lame, his despair rivaled that of Richard III at Bosworth. The local stablemaster at Chatteris grew fond of Pen’s custom, as the young romantic poured coins into both veterinary bills and hired mounts, all in the name of love.

Once a week, under the thin guise of studying Greek plays with Smirke, Pen would vanish in time to catch the *Competitor* down coach, linger in Chatteris for a few blissful hours, and return on the *Rival* night mail. His secret nearly unraveled when Smirke, ever the guileless clergyman, almost confessed to Mrs. Pendennis that no such scholarly sessions had occurred—until Pen’s boot heel met the curate’s toe under the table with a silencing crunch.

Their conversations, naturally, turned to Pen’s infatuation. Few things are as amusing (or agonizing) as listening to two lovestruck men discuss their affections, especially when one is a starry-eyed youth and the other a nervous bachelor clergyman. When Pen, under solemn vows of secrecy, revealed his condition, Smirke turned pale.

“I—I trust it is not an *unworthy* attachment?” the curate stammered, already envisioning his moral duty to inform Pen’s mother and the ensuing familial explosion.

“Unworthy?” Pen erupted. “She is purity itself! I would stake my heart on no other! I keep this private for reasons too weighty to disclose, but let no man question her honor—or mine—or, by heaven, I’ll call him out!”

Smirke, with a weak chuckle, surrendered. “Spare me the duel, Arthur—you know I’m no swordsman.” Thus, the curate found himself further entangled in his pupil’s schemes, and Greek conjugations languished.

Had Smirke been more observant, he might have noticed the flood of passionate verses in the *County Chronicle*—no longer signed *NEP*, but boldly as *EROS*—dedicated to *Miss Fotheringay of the Theatre Royal*. But as neither tutor nor mother connected the dots, Pen’s poetic outpourings remained a clandestine triumph.

“Who is this lady your rival keeps praising in the paper?” Helen Pendennis inquired one day. “His lines resemble yours, dear, though yours are far superior. Have you seen this Miss Fotheringay?”

Pen admitted he had—on the night of *The Stranger*, where she played Mrs. Haller. “She’s to have a benefit soon,” he added casually. “*Ophelia* in *Hamlet*—Shakespeare, you know. We could take the carriage from the Clavering Arms…”

Little Laura clapped in delight, Helen’s eyes misted over at the thought of her late husband’s love for the Bard, and the matter was settled. Smirke, though raised by a pious Clapham mother who frowned upon theaters, found himself unable to resist the dual temptations of Shakespeare and Mrs. Pendennis’s company. His feeble protestations crumbled under the weight of gratitude—and the unspoken thrill of sitting beside Helen all evening.

The night of the play arrived. The Pendennis party, splendidly turned out, occupied a box—Helen regal in black velvet and diamonds, Laura rosy-cheeked and wide-eyed, Smirke palpitating in his best white tie, and Pen barely containing his agitation. The audience was lively: young blades from Baymouth in their finery, dragoons in the pit, and Colonel Swallowtail himself, Waterloo medal gleaming, presiding over his officers in the boxes.

Then, from the corner of her eye, Helen noticed an odd figure bowing extravagantly at Pen.

“Who is that peculiar person, Arthur?”

Pen flushed. “Captain Costigan, ma’am—a Peninsular veteran.” He omitted the crucial detail: the Captain was father to the evening’s Ophelia.

The play commenced. Miss Fotheringay, in flowing white, was a vision of tragic beauty—her dark hair tumbling as she sighed, wept, and strewed imaginary flowers with practiced grace. She died exquisitely, and when Hamlet and Laertes dueled over her grave, Pen nearly strangled the actor playing the Prince in his fervor.

The curtain fell to thunderous applause. Officers roared, Smirke—transformed into a zealot—shouted *Bravo!* with unclerical vigor, and even Helen waved her handkerchief. Laura, bewildered but enchanted, declared Ophelia *the most beautiful creature in the world*.

Then came the moment: Miss Fotheringay, radiant, was led forward for her curtain call. Pen nearly burst, bellowing her name, hat aloft, while Foker and Spavin whooped from their box. Helen, swept up in the excitement, joined the ovation.

Had Pen been alone with his mother on the ride home, he might have confessed all. Instead, he rode atop the carriage, smoking under the moonlight beside Smirke—while Foker’s tandem raced past, bugle blaring, and the night’s magic lingered.

Two days later, fate intervened. As Dr. Portman strolled the Dean’s garden after dinner, young Master Ridley Roset tugged his mother’s skirt.

“Ma—*look up there!*”

And there, in full view of the Deanery, was Pen, pouring his soul at Miss Fotheringay’s feet, kissing her hand with theatrical ardor. The Doctor froze. Mrs. Dean’s face contorted. And Smirke’s rose-tinted illusions shattered in an instant.

Love, it seemed, was not just blind—it was *spectacularly* indiscreet.

Major Arthur Pendennis descended upon Fairoaks like a man who had just survived a particularly hostile encounter with a mail-coach—which, in fact, he had. The night had been an ordeal of snoring, Jamaica rum fumes, and a widow who seemed determined to suffocate him with both her feet and her refusal to open a window. By the time the coach rattled up to the lodge-gate, the Major was a vision of disheveled misery: yellow-faced, wig askew, and radiating the kind of rheumatism that makes a man question all his life choices.

His man, Morgan, materialized as if by dark arts, whisking the Major away to perform the sacred rituals of Restoration—curling wigs, inserting teeth, and applying enough cologne to mask the lingering scent of coach-borne suffering. Only then did the Major deem himself fit for human company.

Helen Pendennis, ever tactful, had the good sense not to ambush him. She knew the Major preferred a grand entrance, not unlike a stage actor who needed a moment in the wings before facing his audience. Meanwhile, young Laura—whom the Major tolerated with the enthusiasm one might reserve for a persistent moth—fled at the sight of him, presumably to warn Pen of the approaching storm.

Pen, as it happened, was deep in melodramatic consultation with his tutor, Mr. Smirke, in the orchard. The two had spent the last forty-eight hours in a fevered symposium on the subject of Miss Fotheringay—her virtues, her genius, her tragic beauty. Smirke, a man whose own heart had recently been ambushed by sentiment, nodded along with the fervor of a convert. By now, Pen had almost convinced himself (and nearly his mother) that marrying an actress twice his age was not only reasonable but *poetic*.

Helen, torn between maternal terror and the peculiar pleasure of self-sacrifice, had begun to waver. *If Pen wanted a Hottentot princess, would she not kneel and kiss the royal feet?* But an actress—a woman who *blushed on command*—was a harder pill to swallow. Still, the sight of her son, wild-eyed and composing bad poetry at midnight, had nearly broken her.

Enter the Major, freshly polished and ready for battle.

"**My dear sister,**" he began, with the air of a man about to dismantle a delusion, "**we are not, under any circumstances, marrying into a traveling theatrical troupe.**"

Helen fluttered. "**But Arthur is so unhappy—**"

"**Unhappy!**" The Major scoffed. "**My dear madam, unhappiness is the natural state of man. I myself was once crossed in love by the niece of an Earl. Fifteen thousand pounds! Gone! And yet here I stand, wig intact, dignity unmarred.**"

Helen, who had her own memories of youthful heartbreak, sighed. The Major pressed his advantage.

"**Imagine it—your drawing room overrun with her *relations*. A mother who drops her *h’s*, a father who reeks of greasepaint. Why, Lady Snapperton wouldn’t even *look* at them!**"

Helen paled. The Major leaned in. "**Let me handle this. I shall be gentle. But mark my words—this nonsense ends today.**"

And with that, the stage was set. Somewhere in the wings, Pen was rehearsing his grand declaration of love. The Major was loading his rhetorical pistols. And Helen? She was wondering, not for the first time, why raising a poet had to be so *exhausting*.

Once upon a time—as all good stories ought to begin—there was a certain young scholar of Cambridge, the Reverend Francis Bell, who found himself exiled to a sleepy village for the long vacation. His reason? A need to escape the hallowed halls of academia and, more pressingly, the clutches of a certain Miss Martha Coacher, to whom he had rashly pledged his undying affection at the tender age of seventeen while helping her pick peas. (A word of advice to young gentlemen: never propose matrimony in a vegetable patch. The symbolism is unfortunate.)

Miss Coacher, a lady of advanced years (by which we mean she had seen at least three seasons pass without securing a husband), had endured more romantic disappointments than a heroine in a three-volume novel. An apothecary had jilted her; a dashing dragoon had galloped away, never to return; and three of her father’s pupils had trifled with her heart before young Bell stumbled into her garden—and her designs. She accepted his boyish devotion with the serene patience of a woman who knows that a bird in the hand, even if it’s a scrawny Cambridge undergraduate, is worth two in the bush.

Alas, time is an unkind mirror. By the time Bell had wrangled his fellowship, won academic laurels, and grown into his limbs, Miss Coacher had not grown any younger, nor—more critically—any sweeter in temper. The woman who had once seemed a goddess of the pea vines now revealed herself to be a tartar of the first order. Bell, trapped by his own youthful fervor and a mountain of impassioned letters (which Miss Coacher threatened to publish in every court in the kingdom should he renege), found himself shackled to a shrew.

Enter Helen Thistlewood, Bell’s cousin—young, lovely, and tragically impressionable. Four months of moonlit walks and shared poetry later, and poor Bell was torn between honor and happiness. Honor won, as it so often does in these dreary tales, and Bell married Miss Coacher, who promptly expired—conveniently—of island fever after delivering a final, triumphant, "I told you so."

Bell, now a widower, married again (this time for love), only for fate to deal another blow. His second wife died in childbirth, leaving behind a motherless daughter, Laura, who was promptly shipped off to the care of Helen—now Mrs. Pendennis—with a locket of hair and a plea for kindness.

Thus, Laura Bell became Laura Pendennis, an orphan with two thousand pounds and no noble connections to speak of—a fact that Major Pendennis found particularly offensive.

---

Meanwhile, young Pen, our hot-headed hero, stood quivering outside the drawing-room door, gathering his courage like a knight preparing to face a dragon. The dragon in question? His uncle, the Major, who had—with infuriating ease—disarmed Pen’s dramatic entrance with a genial, *"Well, Pen, my boy, tell us all about it."*

Pen, who had braced himself for thunderous disapproval, found himself floundering like an actor who had rehearsed a soliloquy only to discover the audience wanted comedy. He stammered. He blushed. He very nearly cried.

The Major, seasoned diplomat that he was, refrained from laughter (barely) and instead employed the oldest trick in the book: flattery disguised as reason.

*"A man of honor keeps his word,"* he conceded, *"but must he rush headlong into ruin? Consider your mother. Consider your prospects. Consider, for heaven’s sake, the fact that you are proposing to marry a woman who has spent more time on stage than in a drawing room."*

Pen, torn between defiance and doubt, pledged not to elope—though his heart remained steadfastly in Miss Fotheringay’s hands (or at least in her greenroom).

The Major, victorious but wise enough not to gloat, sipped his claret and reflected that youth, while charming, was dreadfully inconvenient.

And so Pen was left to nurse his wounded pride, Laura to her obscurity, and the Major to his unshaken certainty that the world was a far better place when governed by the cool heads of gentlemen who knew better than to pick wives from provincial theaters.

(But then, when has love ever listened to reason?)

Let all true connoisseurs of London society—those who cherish the sacred privilege of gliding into its most exclusive circles—pause and admire the magnanimity of Major Pendennis. For here was a man who, in the prime of the season, deserted the metropolis: abandoned his morning papers, his club-hopping afternoons, his confidential teas with dowagers, his rides in Rotten Row, his opera stall, his lightning dashes to Fulham on weekends, his ducal nods at grand soirées, and—most tragic of all—his name in the *Morning Post* the next day. All this he sacrificed to imprison himself in a rustic backwater with a widow, her moonstruck son, a sentimental curate, and a ten-year-old girl. Few understood the depth of his martyrdom.

His letters arrived, splendidly franked, and he presented their glittering invitations to Helen with a sigh. It was a spectacle of noble melancholy—this great man declining party after party—though Helen, bless her, merely smiled at his mournful references to the *Court Guide*. Young Pen, however, drank in every word. The Major’s tales of dukes, beauties, and political intrigues were a golden education.

"Pen, my boy," the Major would intone gravely, "you must study Debrett as devoutly as Scripture. Not the fanciful pedigrees—ours excepted, of course—but the alliances. Why, just last month, young Suckling (some scribbler, I believe) made a fatal jest about Admiral Bowser at Lord Hobanob’s dinner. Little did he know Lady Grampound, Bowser’s daughter, sat beside him, and Lord Grampound, Bowser’s son-in-law, opposite! The fool prattled on, digging his social grave. He’ll never dine in South Street again, mark my words."

Thus the Major schooled Pen during their terrace promenades or over claret. He lamented Sir Francis Clavering’s absence from the estate ("You might’ve been MP, my boy!") and sighed over Lord Eyrie’s empty country seat ("His daughters, Pen! Lady Emily! Lady Barbara! But no—you’ve chosen your path").

Pen absorbed it all. The Major’s wisdom wasn’t lofty, but it was *practical*—a creed for climbing, not for saints. And the Major, being a man of honor (with his eyes *wide open*, as he put it), saw it as his duty to pry Pen’s own eyes open.

Take churchgoing. The Major was devout—in the country. "In town, the ladies uphold piety; here, *we* must set an example. The Duke of Saint David’s sings hymns from his family pew—splendid effect. You’re a figure here, Pen. The Claverings aside, you’re top of the parish. You could stand for Parliament—if you don’t throw yourself away on some unmeetable girl."

This refrain wore on Pen. Flattery works; soon, Pen *believed* he was too fine for Costigan’s daughter. The Captain’s whiskey-laden breath haunted him. How could he bring that unshaven sot to Fairoaks? Even Emily’s letters—three trivial notes—were scrutinized. "Cautious, isn’t she?" the Major mused. "Older, too." Then, with masterful remorse: "Forgive an old fool who only wants the best for the Pendennises!"

Pen, aflame, swore eternal fidelity—to Emily, to the Costigans, even to the frowning Captain, who clasped him with a damp hand and growled, "Betray my girl, and I’ll run ye through, me boy!" (The foils on the wall gleamed ominously.) Yet Pen *winced*. That grubby paternal grip, that reek of spirits—how reconcile it with Helen’s drawing room?

His visits to Chatteris grew fewer. The Major’s silent surveillance unnerved him. Once, en route to the *Competitor* coach, he met Morgan, the Major’s valet, "on an errand." Coincidence? Unlikely. Morgan had been busy: quizzing waiters at the George, probing barracks gossip (Costigan banned from the mess for drunkenness), even cozying up to minor actors. All confirmed the Captain’s debts and thirst—but not a whisper against Emily.

"Her reputation’s spotless," Doctor Portman admitted grudgingly after interrogating Mrs. Creed, the pew-opener. "She’s never alone with Pen—always a child or that old tutor, Bowser, about."

The Major groaned. "A passing flirtation, one could overlook. But *virtue*? That’s the devil. Comes of being coddled by women."

"Major!" the Doctor reproved. "A *suitable* attachment would be ideal. This infatuation, however—"

"—Forbid it, and he’ll elope. We must bide our time."

At last, the Major had an idea. "Why not see her ourselves?"

So one evening, while Pen dined dutifully at home, the two ancients dined at the George (where a dragoon ogled his chop with peculiar vigor) and proceeded to *Hamlet*.

Miss Fotheringay’s Ophelia was, as ever, stately and identically tragic. The Major’s opera-glass gleamed. "The boy’s not blind, I’ll grant that," he muttered.

"Remarkable presence," the Doctor conceded, clapping heartily.

Then the Major spotted the eager dragoon—the same from the George—leaning from his box, applauding wildly. Emily’s eyes flicked toward him, demurely.

"Ah," said the Major, snapping his glass shut. "Just as I thought."

They left before the farce. The Doctor retired to the Deanery; the Major stalked back to the George, plotting.

Major Pendennis strolled back to the George Inn with the leisurely grace of a man who knows the world is his oyster—only to find his pearl of a valet, Mr. Morgan, lurking by the door like a well-dressed spider.

“Sir,” murmured Morgan, oozing deference, “before you retire, there’s a young gentleman in the coffee-room who might amuse you. Not Mr. Arthur, no—but his bosom friend, Mr. Harry Foker. Lady Agnes Foker’s boy. He’s been snoring over his dessert since noon and has only just roused himself to demand coffee. Thought you might fancy a word.”

The Major, ever the opportunist, swept in. There sprawled young Foker, blinking like an owl in daylight, surrounded by the ruins of a feast—empty decanters, crumpled napkins, and the ghost of a plum pudding. He had meant to attend the theater, but a post-prandial nap had claimed him instead.

“Evening paper, sir?” Foker offered cheerfully, thrusting the *Globe* at the Major as if it were a peace treaty.

“You are too kind,” the Major purred, bowing. “Unless I’m mistaken, you bear the unmistakable stamp of the Fokers—Rosherville’s finest. I’ve had the pleasure of your mother’s acquaintance.”

Foker nearly choked on his own candor. “Beg pardon—took you for a commercial traveler. And you are…?”

“Major Pendennis, guardian and uncle to your friend Arthur, who speaks of you with *such* affection.” The Major slid into a chair. “Might I join you? I once dined with your grandfather—a man of impeccable taste.”

“Sir, you honor me,” Foker declared, rallying. “So you’re Pen’s uncle! Capital fellow, though—” he leaned in, wine-loosened lips twitching, “—between us, a bit of a *flat*.”

The Major’s eyebrows arched like drawn sabers. “A flat? My dear boy, elaborate.”

Foker grinned. “About women, specifically. Got himself smitten with an actress—Miss Fotheringay, real name Costigan. Fine girl, but her father? A whiskey-soaked old rogue. Pen’s gone and *proposed* to her.” He slapped the table, rattling the china.

The Major clutched his soda-water like a lifeline. “You don’t say!”

“Oh, it’s common knowledge. Derby Oaks—know him?—was dangling after her first. We call him Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays; Pen’s Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays. She’s playing them like a fiddle.”

“And you, Mr. Foker,” the Major said admiringly, “appear to be the rare man who *doesn’t* fiddle.”

Foker smirked. “Not clever, just observant. Miss Fotheringay’s got a confidante, Miss Rouncy, who writes her letters—spelling’s atrocious otherwise. Had a falling-out, though. No Rouncy, no love notes.”

The Major’s eyes gleamed. “A *specimen* of Miss Rouncy’s hand would be invaluable.”

Foker winked. “Now, now, Major. Mustn’t spill *all* the ink.”

The conversation spiraled into revelations: Pen’s supposed fortune (“Two thousand a year? His mother scrapes by on five hundred!”), the Costigans’ dubious arithmetic, and Foker’s gleeful role as accidental informant. By dawn, the Major had a pilfered letter in his pocket and a spring in his step.

At the Deanery, he regaled Dr. Portman with the night’s exploits. “Costigan’s next,” he declared, rolling up his sleeves metaphorically.

“Godspeed, dragon-slayer,” chuckled the Doctor.

And so, with the air of a man marching to a duel, the Major rapped on Costigan’s door—only to be greeted by the man himself, foil in hand, sweating from a bout with Sir Derby Oaks.

“Major Pendennis, I presume?” boomed the Captain.

“The honor is mine,” the Major lied smoothly.

The game was afoot.

The Major and Captain Costigan were both seasoned soldiers, well accustomed to keeping their cool under fire. The same could not be said for the rest of the party assembled in Cos’s sitting room, who appeared somewhat flustered by young Pendennis’s sudden arrival. Miss Fotheringay’s normally sluggish pulse quickened, her cheeks blooming with a robust blush as Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks glowered in her direction. Meanwhile, the hunched old man in the window seat—who had abandoned his attempt to copy theater music due to the vigorous fencing display between the two gentlemen—peered up with keen interest as Major Pendennis, impeccably booted and effortlessly polished, swept into the room with a bow for every occupant.

"Me daughter—me esteemed friend, Mr. Bows—and me gallant young pupil, Sir Derby Oaks," announced Costigan with a grand flourish, introducing each in turn to the Major. "One moment, Major, and I’m at your service!" With that, he dashed into his adjoining chamber, where he performed a rapid transformation: a hasty twist of his lank hair with an ancient brush, a swift exchange of his old stock for a fresh one (courtesy of Emily), and the donning of a pristine collar and his finest coat—reserved for special occasions, such as his daughter’s benefit night.

Sir Derby, not to be outdone, followed suit, emerging shortly in a snug shell-jacket that clung to his muscular frame—an ensemble he, Miss Fotheringay, and perhaps even poor Pen considered exceedingly dashing.

While the gentlemen prepared themselves, the Major engaged the actress in polite conversation, beginning, as all good English dialogues must, with remarks upon the weather.

Costigan soon reappeared, resplendent in his "shoot" (as he called it). "No need to apologize for receiving ye in me shirtsleeves earlier, Major," he declared with magnificent courtesy.

"An old soldier is never better employed than when teaching a young one swordplay," replied the Major gallantly. "I’ve heard you were quite skilled with a blade yourself in your day, Captain Costigan."

"Ye’ve heard of Jack Costigan, then?" the Captain exclaimed, visibly pleased.

Whether the Major truly recalled the man or had merely gleaned details from his nephew mattered little—Major Pendennis was a gentleman of unimpeachable honor, and he solemnly affirmed that he distinctly remembered Captain Costigan singing at Sir Richard Strachan’s table during the Walcheren campaign.

Bows, overhearing this, looked thoroughly bewildered.

"Ah, but we’ll reminisce another time," the Major smoothly continued, not wishing to commit too deeply. "Today, I come to pay my respects to Miss Fotheringay." He delivered another bow, so exquisitely polished that it would have flattered a duchess.

"My nephew raves about your performances, madam," the Major said. "Though Arthur is young and prone to wild enthusiasm, I confess I was eager to judge for myself. Having now seen you, I must say you surpass even our finest actresses. You are as majestic as Mrs. Siddons."

"Faith, I’ve always said so," Costigan interjected, winking at his daughter.

Milly rose, clearing a seat by removing an unfinished satin garment, and presented it to the Major with one of her most elegant curtseys.

"You are as poignant as Miss O’Neill," the Major continued, seating himself, "and your singing reminds me of Mrs. Jordan in her prime. Your bearing, too—have you ever seen the Mars, Miss Fotheringay?"

"There were two Mahers in Crow Street," Emily replied thoughtfully. "Fanny was tolerable, but Biddy was nothing special."

"The Major means the god of war, my dear," her father clarified.

"Not that Mars," the Major said with an amused glance toward Sir Derby, who had reentered in his snug jacket. "Though I suppose Venus might be forgiven for thinking of him." The jest sailed harmlessly over Miss Fotheringay’s head, and Sir Derby, scowling, seemed no more enlightened.

The Major was delighted. A rival! And a surly one at that. Perhaps this could work to his advantage.

"I fear I interrupted your fencing," he said, "but my time in Chatteris is short, and I wished to meet my old comrade and the lady who has so enchanted our stage. You were not alone in admiration last night—I saw a reverend gentleman quite transported by your Ophelia, and Sir Derby here flung a bouquet fit for a queen." He nodded toward the flowers on the mantelpiece.

"I’m very fond of flowers," Miss Fotheringay said, casting a languishing look at Sir Derby, who remained obstinately sour.

"'Sweets to the sweet,' as the play says?" the Major ventured.

"’Pon my life, I wouldn’t know," Sir Derby grunted. "Not much of a literary man."

"Indeed?" The Major feigned surprise. "Not like your father, then? A fine scholar—I knew him well."

"He was a decent doctor," Sir Derby muttered. "Now, if you’ll excuse me—Cos, farewell. Miss Fotheringay, good morning." And with that, he stomped out, his saber clattering, his temper evident as he kicked poor Tommy Creed’s peg-top into the street.

The Major suppressed a smile. "A fine-looking soldier," he remarked.

"A credit to the army," Costigan agreed. "A man of refined manners and princely fortune."

"A perfect champion," the Major said dryly.

"He’s best on horseback," Bows muttered.

Hospitality was next on the agenda. Costigan insisted on refreshments, and the Major, though not hungry, graciously accepted—knowing that small courtesies endeared one to hosts. Soon, Milly was dispatched for Madeira and biscuits, leaving the gentlemen to talk.

Once alone, the Major turned to business.

"Captain," he said, "let us speak plainly. My nephew is not the heir to vast estates. His father was an apothecary. He’ll have little more than five hundred a year."

Costigan’s face darkened. "Are ye joking with me, Major? The boy led me to believe—"

"Every shilling my brother left goes to his widow," the Major cut in. "Arthur may inherit someday, but his mother is young. She might remarry. She might outlive him. Do you truly wish your daughter tied to a boy with no prospects?"

Costigan’s rage flared. "Am I to understand," he thundered, "that your nephew is a liar who trifled with my child’s heart?"

"If he misled you, then yes," the Major said coolly. "But before you challenge me to pistols at dawn, consider this: I’m acting in your daughter’s interest as much as my nephew’s. A marriage without means is misery."

Costigan, though bristling, wavered. "I’ve letters—promises!"

"Publishing them would only shame your daughter," the Major pointed out. "And as for dueling—well, I’ve faced fiercer men than you."

Costigan drew himself up. "A friend shall call upon you at the George, Major."

"Let him make haste," the Major retorted, tipping his hat. "Good day, sir."

And with that, he departed, leaving Captain Costigan to his fury—and his dwindling Madeira.

The illustrious Mr. Garbetts, Principal Tragedian—a man whose legs were the envy of the theater and whose arm could snap a poker like a breadstick—was, by all accounts, Captain Costigan’s closest confidant. Their friendship, cemented over shared whisky and unpaid bills at the Magpie Hotel, was of the sort where signatures were borrowed more often than shillings. Mrs. Garbetts, however, viewed Miss Fotheringay with the same warmth one reserves for a tax collector, owing to her husband’s earlier, unsuccessful courtship of the fair Emily. But that’s another story, and this one has enough drama already.

Summoned posthaste by the Captain—who was in a state of agitation best described as "half-tragic, half-spirits"—Garbetts arrived to find Costigan brandishing dueling pistols older than Dublin itself.

“Run, Tommy,” the Captain had barked earlier, dispatching a small boy to fetch both Garbetts and two hot whiskies (priorities firmly in order). Now, with liquid courage bolstering his resolve, Costigan composed a letter to Major Pendennis. It was a masterpiece of threats and flourishes, demanding the Major cease all interference in young Arthur’s nuptial ambitions—or face consequences involving horsewhips and honor. The letter was sealed with the Costigan crest (the only surviving relic of family grandeur) and entrusted to Garbetts for delivery.

Alas, Garbetts returned looking as cheerful as a man who’d just spotted his own ghost.

“Ye saw ’im?” growled Costigan, leveling a pistol at an imaginary foe (and narrowly missing a vase).

“I saw *someone*,” Garbetts admitted. “But not the Major. Tatham was there.”

The name alone was enough to make Costigan sputter into his drink. Tatham! The very man who’d once sworn to have him arrested over a trifling matter of debt. Garbetts, a practical soul with a family to feed, refused to be entangled in duels, pistols, or any activity that might shorten his theatrical career. The meeting dissolved in mutual disgust, leaving Costigan to brood over his wrongs and his whisky.

Meanwhile, Miss Fotheringay returned from rehearsal, trailing the ever-faithful Mr. Bows. She found her father mid-tirade, the air thick with the scent of spirits and melodrama.

“Prepare yourself, me child!” Costigan wailed, flinging his arms wide.

“Ye’re drunk before dinner again,” Emily observed, sidestepping his embrace with the ease of a woman well-practiced in dodging paternal theatrics.

“It’s me sorrows, darlin’!” he protested. “That villainous Major has revealed Arthur’s a pauper! A pauper, I tell ye!”

Emily, unperturbed, set about polishing a pair of satin slippers with bread crumbs (a thrifty trick for Ophelia’s costume). “If he’s got no money, there’s no use marrying him,” she remarked, as though discussing the weather.

Costigan spluttered. Bows smirked. The Captain’s dreams of carriages and grandeur crumbled like stale cake.

“He’s a good lad,” Emily conceded, rubbing at a stubborn scuff. “But what’s the use of a husband who can’t keep ye in satin shoes?”

Bows, ever the cynic, suggested she marry him anyway—for love, if not luxury. Emily, practical to her core, merely laughed. “There’s others as likes me, Bows. Older. Poorer. Fools, the lot of ’em.”

And so, with the matter settled, she bundled up Pen’s love letters—pages of fevered verse and boyish devotion—into a neat parcel, tied with string, as casually as if they were a grocer’s order.

Somewhere, ten miles away, Pen’s heart was breaking.

Emily, untroubled, called for dinner. The beef-steak pudding, at least, was dependable.

Meanwhile, back at Fairoaks, the Pendennis household was in a state of mild bewilderment over the Major’s prolonged absence. Had he been swallowed by a particularly persuasive solicitor? Kidnapped by a rogue band of theater enthusiasts? The suspense was unbearable—or at least mildly inconvenient.

When Dr. Portman and his wife stopped by Helen’s lodge-gate, they delivered a note from the Major, which explained, in the driest possible terms, that he had been detained in Chatteris by *urgent legal matters* (a phrase which, as everyone knows, could mean anything from reviewing a will to losing a particularly vicious game of whist). Notably absent from this missive was any mention of his morning’s *entertainment*—a spirited verbal joust with one Captain Costigan, in which the Major had, by all accounts, been thoroughly outmaneuvered.

Young Pen, meanwhile, had developed a sudden aversion to Chatteris, now that his uncle was lurking about like a well-dressed specter. The thought of the Major perched on the Dean’s grass-plat, spyglass in hand, monitoring his nephew’s romantic endeavors, was enough to make any young man reconsider his life choices. And so, Pen avoided the town, preferring the solitude of a walk—though even this innocent pleasure was spoiled by the looming threat of encountering his uncle’s immaculately polished boots.

Ah, young love! That most ridiculous of human conditions. Pen’s affections were of the silent, pining variety—visible only in the violent ogling of his eyes, which, to the untrained observer, might have suggested a man in the throes of indigestion rather than passion. Let us spare the reader the agony of revisiting his love letters, those overwrought scribblings of devotion, which, in the cold light of day, would no doubt make even the most sentimental among us cringe. No, we shall not reproduce them here. Some things are best left tied up with Miss Fotheringay’s string and sealed away forever.

The Major, having retreated from his encounter with Captain Costigan in a state of concentrated fury, resembled nothing so much as a teakettle left too long on the boil. *“The impudent bog-trotting scamp!”* he fumed. *“Dare to threaten me! Dare to suggest his daughter could marry into the Pendennis family! And then have the audacity to send a challenge!”*

The idea of dueling with a tipsy tragedian was, of course, absurd. What would people say? *“Major Pendennis shot in a dispute over an actress! How very middle-class.”* Unthinkable.

When Dr. Portman inquired after the skirmish, the Major waved him off with vague mutterings about *“delicate matters”* and *“unfinished business.”* He then returned to his hotel, where he took out his frustrations on his long-suffering valet, Morgan, who later confided to a fellow servant that the Major had been *“dammin and cussin upstairs and downstairs like a admiral in a nor’wester.”*

Mr. Foker, ever the agent of chaos, chose this moment to make his entrance—resplendent in a brocade dressing-gown and an embroidered skull-cap that could have doubled as a theatrical prop.

*“O, you’re busy—call again another time,”* he announced cheerfully, already halfway out the door.

But the Major, despite his foul mood, found himself inexplicably cheered by the sight of Foker’s absurd attire. *“Come in, come in,”* he said, managing a strained smile.

Foker, delighted to be of service, proceeded to mediate the situation with all the tact of a bull in a china shop. He recounted the morning’s drama, including the unfortunate intervention of Mr. Garbetts (Costigan’s chosen second, who had promptly fled upon recognizing the Major’s lawyer). The Major, despite himself, burst out laughing.

*“I don’t think there’ll be any fight,”* Foker declared, mimicking the tragedian’s grand exit. *“But if there is, Mr. Tatham here can always send the bailiffs to separate you!”*

Never one to let social awkwardness stand in the way of a good meal, Foker extended an impromptu dinner invitation to both the Major *and* Miss Costigan—along with her ever-present shadow, Mr. Bows.

*“Lobster salad and champagne,”* Foker promised, knowing full well that no actress could resist such an offer.

And so, in a twist that would have made a farce writer proud, Major Pendennis found himself breaking bread with the very woman whose father had, mere hours earlier, threatened to shoot him. The General himself, retrieved from the Magpie Hotel (where he had been weeping into his ale over a sentimental ballad), arrived late to the feast, promptly embracing the Major as his *“dearest friend.”*

The next morning, Pen received a letter—one he had been dreading. It was from Costigan, delivering the fatal news: the engagement was off. Miss Fotheringay, it seemed, had been *made aware* of Pen’s financial shortcomings.

Pen’s reaction was, predictably, dramatic. *“It’s a lie! A trick!”* he roared, crumpling the letter.

But the Major, ever the pragmatist, merely sighed. *“She spells ‘affection’ with one ‘f,’ Arthur. Surely you can do better.”*

Helen, ever the doting mother, attempted to console him. *“My dear boy, think of your future—”*

*“I’ll see her myself!”* Pen declared, bolting for the door.

Alas, his efforts were in vain. Miss Fotheringay, now under strict paternal supervision, refused to see him. The General cut him in the street. Pen, in true romantic fashion, took to lurking outside the theater like a particularly lovesick ghost.

And so, the affair ended—not with a duel, not with a grand reconciliation, but with a whimper, a fever, and the solemn realization that young men have been surviving heartbreak for centuries.

Pen would live. But he would *not* be cheerful about it.

If you’ve ever had the misfortune of attending a play where the actors outnumber the audience, you’ll understand the dire state of the Chatteris Theatre the night Mr. Bingley, with all the enthusiasm of a man who has never once doubted his own genius, performed *Pizarro* to a crowd that could comfortably fit inside a hackney cab.

Our poor Pen, lovelorn and hollow-eyed, slumped in his box like a man who had just been informed his favorite pub had run out of ale—forever. He stared at the stage with the intensity of a starving man at a bakery window, but the object of his affections, the radiant Miss Fotheringay, had eyes only for two gentlemen in the neighboring box.

Now, these were no ordinary spectators. One was a small, shrewd-faced fellow in black, looking as if he’d just won a hand of cards he hadn’t even played. The other? A walking advertisement for the excesses of the aristocracy—tall, portly, dripping in velvet, braiding, and enough rings to stock a jeweler’s shop. When he flourished his handkerchief (white kid gloves, naturally), the entire theatre was treated to a wave of bergamot and musk so potent it could have revived a corpse.

This, ladies and gentlemen, was Mr. Dolphin, the legendary London impresario, accompanied by his ever-present shadow, Mr. Minns. The moment his presence was detected, the entire cast—previously performing with all the energy of a sedated sloth—snapped to attention. Mrs. Bingley, who had been muttering her lines like a woman afraid of waking a sleeping baby, suddenly bellowed like a sergeant major. Bingley himself flung himself about the stage with the vigor of a man who had just chugged three cups of strong coffee. Even Miss Fotheringay, whose acting repertoire consisted primarily of standing still and being beautiful, managed to inject a flicker of emotion into her performance—though whether it was ambition or indigestion was unclear.

Only when Rolla (Bingley, now visibly struggling under the weight of his own child, an unnaturally large infant who must have been fed a steady diet of raw meat) staggered onstage with the famous line—“*O God, there’s blood upon him!*”—did Mr. Dolphin stir. He clapped, he cheered, he turned to Minns and declared, “By Jove, Billy, she’ll do!”

“Who taught her that?” snorted Minns. “Last I saw her, she couldn’t act her way out of a paper bag.”

The answer, of course, was little Mr. Bows, lurking in the orchestra like a disgruntled puppet master. But no one was thanking *him*.

Now, you might wonder what brought a London titan like Dolphin to the theatrical wasteland of Chatteris. Simple: desperation. His grand Museum Theatre was hemorrhaging money like a nobleman at a gambling den. Hubbard’s *legitimate drama* had emptied pockets faster than it filled seats. The Cawdors had flopped. Even the lions and tigers—always a crowd-pleaser—had been shut down after one of them mistook Herr Garbage for a snack.

Enter Lord Steyne, patron of the arts and connoisseur of dancers (particularly the ones who could wink at his box while mid-air). A casual suggestion from his circle—*“Why not take a look at this Fotheringay woman?”*—sent Dolphin scurrying to Chatteris like a man chasing the last lifeboat off a sinking ship.

Meanwhile, Pen, oblivious to the machinations of the theatrical elite, moped in his box like a kicked puppy. Sir Derby Oaks, ever the rival, showered the stage with bouquets and basked in Miss Fotheringay’s fleeting glances. Pen? He got nothing. Not a smile, not a sigh—just the crushing realization that his heart had been trampled by a woman who probably couldn’t spell *sentiment* if you spotted her the *S*.

It was Bows, the sardonic little orchestra man, who finally took pity on him. “You’re not the first fool she’s ruined,” he said, lighting a cigar as they stood on Chatteris bridge. “She hasn’t got a heart. She’s like this cigar—once the fire’s gone, she’ll toss you aside without a second thought.”

And as the last ember of his cigar plunged into the dark water below, Pen realized, with something like horror, that for the first time in weeks, he was thinking about someone other than himself.

*(And thus concludes tonight’s performance of *Heartbreak in Three Acts*. Curtain falls. No encores.)*

Until the enemy—otherwise known as Miss Fotheringay—had retreated from the field of battle, Major Pendennis maintained a strict, if subtle, occupation of Fairoaks. He did not *officially* monitor Pen’s every move, but through a network of informants (gardeners, grooms, and the ever-gossipy Mrs. Pybus), the Major kept a ledger of the boy’s sighs, strolls, and ill-advised sonnets.

Ah, the exquisite agony of young love! If you, dear reader, have ever been jilted, deceived, or merely left standing at a dance with a wilted corsage, you may sympathize with Pen’s plight. Nights spent tossing like a ship in a storm! Days wasted composing verses so dreadful they’d make a gravestone weep! The boy was a walking tragedy—or at least, a moderately entertaining farce.

His mother, ever the devoted spectator of his melodrama, fretted silently. The Major, meanwhile, offered sage advice between sips of claret: *"My dear madam, the boy will recover. A woman’s rejection is merely Nature’s way of preparing him for the next one. Vanity, not heartbreak, is the true culprit."*

Mrs. Pendennis, whose own romantic history was as brief as a curtsy, did not argue. She simply knitted, sighed, and occasionally hid sharp objects—just in case.

Fairoaks, though picturesque, was a prison to a man of the Major’s metropolitan tastes. His days were a grim parade of backgammon with the widow (who cheated), dinners with Dr. Portman (who lectured), and the thrilling arrival of the evening post. To stave off boredom, he took up self-doctoring, studied the *Annual Register* for its gripping accounts of the American War, and practiced his cane-twirling in the terrace walk.

Pen, meanwhile, alternated between brooding like Byron and fishing like a man who hoped the carp might offer better conversation than the locals. His poetic output soared—unfortunately, quality did not match quantity. (Sample line: *"O cruel nymph! Why dost thou spurn my heart? / As spurned as eels in Major’s frying pan."*)

Enter Mr. Foker, stage left, in a tandem that scattered chickens and dignity alike. His visits brought levity—and fresh gossip. The village, already a simmering pot of intrigue, now boiled over with the news of Pen’s theatrical misadventures.

Clavering’s high society (if one could call it that) reacted as if Pen had proposed to a fire-breathing dragon rather than an actress. Mrs. Portman, that indefatigable herald of doom, spread the tale with evangelical zeal. Soon, even the butcher’s boy was quoting *Hamlet* at Pen, though with less eloquence and more cabbage-stinking breath.

The climax came when young Hobnell of Wapshot’s Academy, emboldened by cider and stupidity, staged an impromptu graveside parody of Pen’s woes. Pen responded as any self-respecting romantic hero would: with a right hook that sent Hobnell tumbling into an open grave.

The Major, ever the strategist, dispatched Foker to negotiate peace terms. A note was sent. Hobnell, after consulting his fellow scholars (and, presumably, a smuggled flask), agreed to a truce—or at least, to stop reenacting Shakespeare in Pen’s presence.

And so, the village returned to its usual pastimes: judging, whispering, and pretending not to judge. Pen, though bruised in pride and knuckles, lived to brood another day.

**Moral:** *A broken heart mends faster when you’ve a good horse, a bad poem, and at least one friend willing to punch your enemies.*

Pen’s latest scandal—a duel-that-wasn’t with young Hobnell over some theatrical slander—sent Chatteris into a frenzy of gossip. Dr. Portman huffed about unchristian behavior, Major Pendennis chuckled into his cravat, and poor Helen Pendennis nearly fainted at the thought of her son brawling like a common ruffian.

When confronted, Pen drew himself up with all the dignity of a tragedian delivering his final soliloquy. "Mother," he declared, "a gentleman *must* defend his honor. Would you have me slink away like a coward?" He then turned to his uncle, who was struggling not to laugh.

"Between schoolboys, my boy, this is absurd," the Major said, waving a hand. "Pistols at dawn? Next, you’ll be demanding satisfaction from the baker for undercooked rolls."

Helen wrung her hands. "It’s *wicked*, Arthur. And that Foker boy is a terrible influence."

"Foker," Pen pronounced gravely, "is a man of the world."

The Major coughed into his glove to disguise a snort.

Despite Helen’s misgivings, the wheels of academia turned inexorably. Pen was to depart for Oxbridge in October, and the household braced for impact. The Major waxed poetic about the illustrious connections Pen would forge—young lords! future statesmen!—while Helen envisioned tavern brawls and gambling debts.

Dr. Portman, ever the pragmatist, nodded approvingly. "The boy needs discipline. And Latin. Preferably far from here."

Pen, meanwhile, alternated between sighing over lost love and scribbling dreadful poetry. His tutor, Smirke—a man whose romantic aspirations rivaled Pen’s own—listened with tragic solemnity, all while stealing glances at Helen, who remained blissfully unaware of his pining.

Chatteris, deprived of fresh drama since the Fotheringay debacle, seized upon Pen’s exploits with gusto.

**Mrs. Pybus:** "He tried to *hang himself* in the orchard, I heard!"
**Captain Glanders (half-drunk):** "Nonsense! It was the actress who jilted *him*. Cost him five hundred pounds to hush it up."
**Mrs. Speers:** "I heard a *thousand*."

Pen, upon hearing these rumors, nearly challenged the entire town to a mass duel.

Smirke, meanwhile, was in agony. With Pen’s departure looming, his daily visits to Fairoaks—his only excuse to bask in Helen’s presence—were endangered. Madame Fribsby, the sentimental milliner, fed his delusions with sighs and meaningful glances.

"*Mon cœur* suffers!" she whispered dramatically. "When the son leaves, the mother will be *so lonely*..."

Smirke blushed. He composed sonnets. He considered throwing himself at Helen’s feet.

Alas, his dreams were dashed when the Major casually mentioned that Pen’s tuition would soon be unnecessary. Smirke’s face fell so dramatically that one might have thought he’d been told the church was switching to *optional* piety.

While Pen brooded, Smirke sighed, and Helen fretted, the rest of Clavering carried on. The Fotheringay continued her performances, utterly indifferent to the chaos she’d left in her wake. The Major daydreamed of London parties. And Madame Fribsby? She sighed over her romance novels and wondered why real life couldn’t be half as exciting.

Every soul is an island—some are just more prone to dramatic floods than others.

The Reverend Mr. Smirke had, as usual, made his dutiful pilgrimage to Fairoaks that afternoon, ostensibly to supervise young Pen’s studies—though, in truth, the studies were mostly of the poetic and melancholy variety these days. Meanwhile, Mrs. Portman, having concluded some weighty matters with Mrs. Pybus (likely involving the latest scandalous embroidery patterns), found the weather so irresistibly pleasant that she extended her constitutional all the way to Fairoaks. There, she settled in for a cozy chat with dear Helen Pendennis and the ever-majestic Major.

With the air of someone disclosing state secrets, Mrs. Portman leaned in. “My dear,” she murmured, “I have it on the *very best* authority that our quiet little curate, Mr. Smirke, has been harboring a *most* romantic attachment—for years! And kept it *entirely* to himself!”

Helen’s teacup paused mid-air. “An attachment?” she repeated, voice steady but eyes widening. “And who, pray tell, is the fortunate object of Mr. Smirke’s affections?”

“Well!” Mrs. Portman trilled, “when he first arrived, everyone assumed he was smitten with *Myra*—so much so that we had to stop inviting him to dinner to quash the rumors. Then people whispered it was *someone else*—but I *always* denied it, of course, especially when they suggested—”

“*Me?*” Helen gasped, as if the idea were as shocking as a sudden invasion of highwaymen. “The impertinence! Mr. Smirke was engaged as Arthur’s tutor—nothing more!”

“’Pon my honor, it *is* outrageous,” the Major grunted, lowering his newspaper just enough to glare over his spectacles.

“That Mrs. Pybus has a tongue longer than the road to London,” Helen sniffed.

“Oh, I *always* said it was nonsense,” Mrs. Portman assured her. “And now, at last, the truth emerges! Our demure curate has been secretly engaged to a Miss Thompson of Clapham Common for *ages*! Delightful, isn’t it? Such a relief for poor Myra—unmarried curates are *so* awkward to have about. Now, not a *word* of this, mind you—strictly confidential!”

Helen, with admirable sincerity, declared herself *thrilled* by the news. Once Mrs. Portman had departed, she and the Major exchanged glances of profound relief. Helen even reproached herself for her recent coldness toward Smirke, who had, after all, been endlessly attentive to Arthur.

“Gratitude is all very well,” the Major pontificated, “but familiarity is *not* to be encouraged. The man is a hired tutor, not a social equal. You’ve been *too* kind to him.”

Helen disagreed. Now that Arthur’s departure loomed, she recalled Smirke’s many kindnesses—his errands, his gifts to Laura, his patient tutoring. Guilt gnawed at her. So when Smirke lingered in the hall after lessons, she surprised him by stepping forward, cheeks pink, and inviting him to dinner.

That evening, Helen was *radiant* with hospitality, while the Major treated Smirke like a particularly dim footman. Pen, meanwhile, took full advantage of his newfound privilege as wine-pourer, and by the second bottle, both he and Smirke were waxing poetic—Pen about the cruelty of women, Smirke about their divine purity.

“To *her* health!” Pen roared, sloshing claret onto the table. “Your *mystery* lady, Smirke! Who is she? Confess!”

Smirke, flushed and trembling, gulped his wine. “Per-perfection itself,” he stammered. “A being of… of *unparalleled* virtue—”

Pen, now thoroughly soused, guessed wildly: “Madame Fribsby? Old Miss Pybus? *Not* my mother—ha!”

The silence that followed could have frozen the remaining wine.

“Arthur,” Smirke whispered, eyes damp, “for two years, my heart has known no other idol.”

Pen sobered instantly. “You’re *mad*,” he hissed, ringing for coffee like an affronted duke.

Smirke slunk away, ponyless dignity intact, while Pen brooded on the lawn. Helen, noticing his pensive mood, asked why Mr. Smirke had left so abruptly.

“Indigestion,” Pen lied. Then, bursting with the absurdity of it all, he confessed the whole scene to his mother later—who blushed, scolded, and finally laughed until tears came.

The Buccaneers of Chatteris—a society of gentlemen who believed fervently in good drink and bad decisions—were hosting a farewell dinner for Captain Costigan. Thanks to Emily’s newfound fortune, the Captain had paid off all his debts (including a *particularly* longstanding tab at the Grapes), prompting mine host Solly to declare him “a gentleman at bottom.”

Eighteen men toasted Costigan with more enthusiasm than accuracy. The Captain, overcome, delivered a slurred but moving tribute to Chatteris—its beauty, its hospitality, its *jovialitee*—before weeping openly at the thought of leaving.

“To Miss Fotheringay!” Hicks bellowed.

“To the *Drama!*” Jubber added.

Bingley tried to speak but was drowned out by Costigan, who launched into tales of Gibraltar, the Duke of York, and (for some reason) the Siege of Flushing.

By midnight, the Captain was carried home, his pockets lighter but his heart full. The Buccaneers, meanwhile, slept where they fell—dreaming of glory, claret, and the immortal *jovialitee* of Chatteris.

Our friend Pen was not sorry when his mentor, Major Pendennis, took leave of him after their arrival in Oxbridge. The Major, having dutifully discharged his obligation, was more than ready to resume his own pursuits—preferably those involving good company and fine shooting.

Pen, now left to his own devices, was introduced to university life by his lively companion, Mr. Foker, who ensured he mingled with the best set. The Major had done his part; now it was time for Pen to carve his own path—or, more accurately, to amble along it with all the grace of a young man who believed the world owed him admiration.

Pen began his academic career with a flourish, attending lectures with an air of diligence that would have impressed his mother. Mathematics, however, soon proved an insurmountable foe. He found himself outmatched by a few unrefined young men—the sort who wore thick shoes and cared not for fashion—and so he wisely abandoned the exact sciences in favor of more gentlemanly pursuits, namely Greek and Latin.

His mother, ever indulgent, was relieved. She had heard dreadful tales of students overworking themselves into early graves, and she much preferred her darling boy to preserve his delicate constitution. Pen, though unaware of any lurking mortal frailty, humored her by promising not to study *too* hard—a vow he kept with remarkable consistency.

Pen soon discovered that lectures were tedious, and private study far more suited to a man of his intellect. He announced to his mother that he would read independently—a decision she applauded, despite knowing nothing of Homer beyond his being "some old Greek fellow."

When he returned home for holidays, it was not without fanfare. His wardrobe had expanded considerably: morning shooting jackets with enviable buttons, evening waistcoats embroidered to excess, and cravats so elaborate they might have been architectural marvels. His dressing-case gleamed with silver, his fingers glittered with rings, and his new French watch made his father’s old chronometer look like a relic from the Dark Ages.

He had also developed a fondness for cigars—fine ones, imported—which he insisted were essential to literary composition. His mother, though skeptical, indulged him, even going so far as to light one for him while he lounged with a novel. "If I asked you to burn the house down, I believe you would," Pen laughed. She likely would have.

Pen’s education was not limited to the classics. He consumed French novels with the same enthusiasm as his cigars, though he assured his mother (and poor scandalized Laura) that this was purely for linguistic practice. Wine, too, became a scholarly necessity. Having exhausted his father’s cellar, he ordered fresh stock from London, casually adding it to the household accounts. Mrs. Pendennis, though alarmed by the sums, paid without protest. After all, shouldn’t her son enjoy himself while he was home?

His allowance was generous—too generous, perhaps. His father had left a fund specifically for his education, and Pen spent it with the cheerful disregard of a man who had never earned a shilling. Fine books, fine clothes, fine horses—he acquired them all, not with extravagance, but with the quiet assurance of one who believed such things his due.

By his second year, Pen had established himself as a figure of note. He dressed impeccably, quoted poetry with ease, and spoke of past heartbreaks (namely, the infamous Miss Fotheringay) with such tragic flair that younger students regarded him with awe. He had *suffered*, they whispered. He had *lived*.

His influence was such that when he adopted a crimson satin cravat, half the university followed suit. When he sighed over a sonnet, a dozen imitations sprang up. He was, in short, exactly the sort of man who thrives in a place where reputation outweighs achievement.

Yet for all his polish, Pen was not immune to the arithmetic of indulgence. His tailor’s bills mounted, his wine merchant grew prosperous, and his collection of rare books expanded at a rate that would have alarmed a man of thriftier inclinations. But Pen was no such man. He moved through life with the serene confidence of one who had never been forced to balance a ledger—and who had no intention of starting now.

And so, Arthur Pendennis of Boniface strolled through Oxbridge, admired by many, envied by more, and wholly convinced that the world was his to enjoy. Whether it would remain so indulgent was a question for another day.

Before Mr. Foker bid farewell to Oxbridge (with more fanfare than a retiring opera diva), a new figure slunk into Boniface College—one Mr. Horace Bloundell, late of Camford University, where he’d been *politely* asked to leave after a series of *misunderstandings* involving tutors, a missing ceremonial mace, and a donkey.

Bloundell came from the ancient Suffolk Bloundells—Bloundell-Bloundell Hall, Bloundellshire, as the wags called it—though his lineage was more *debatable* than his gambling debts. He’d briefly worn the crimson pantaloons of a dragoon before swapping them for the sober black of a clergyman (a profession chosen, one suspected, because the living came with a particularly fine wine cellar).

When Major Pendennis visited Pen, he was introduced to a gallery of Oxbridge’s finest: Lord Plinlimmon (so polite he’d apologize to a chair for sitting on it), Magnus Charters (who declared republicanism between sips of his grandfather’s claret), and Bloundell, who wore more jewelry than a pawnshop window and exuded the charm of a cat in a fishmonger’s.

The Major, ever astute, took one look at Bloundell and whispered to Pen, *“That man is a walking bad decision.”*

Pen, of course, was dazzled. Bloundell had *seen things*—gambling hells, dubious clubs, and (allegedly) the inside of a Parisian jail. He spoke of lost fortunes and midnight escapades with the casual flair of a man who’d read too many penny dreadfuls.

Soon, Pen was swept into the seductive whirl of dice games, where Bloundell’s green wineglass became a chalice of ruin. The stakes? Mostly Pen’s allowance, his dignity, and (on one memorable night) a pair of boots he’d sworn were lucky.

Then came the London trip. Under Bloundell’s tutelage, Pen discovered the *cultural delights* of Covent Garden—namely, how quickly one’s purse could vanish between the theatre and a certain military club where men with waxed mustaches bet on cockroach races.

It was here Pen saw Miss Fotheringay again. Time had not been kind—or perhaps Pen’s eyes had simply cleared. Her once-mesmeric voice now struck him as a touch too *shrill*, her tragic sighs more *rehearsed* than heartfelt. The realization hit him like a bad oyster: *I pined for this?*

Yet Bloundell, ever helpful, assured him she’d had *dozens* of admirers, each more generous (and gullible) than the last. Pen, ever credulous, swallowed every word—along with far too much Maraschino.

By term’s end, Pen’s finances were in tatters, his reputation *seasoned* with scandal, and his only consolation was that he’d at least *lost* his money with panache.

Youth is a splendid fire—bright, warm, and prone to burning down the house.

Let it be known to all students of heraldry and aristocratic lineage that the noble family from which Helen Pendennis hailed bore a rather peculiar crest: a mother pelican, generously bleeding into the beaks of her ravenous offspring. A touching allegory, no doubt, though one might question the life choices of a bird willing to be pecked to death by its own children. But such is the way of mothers—human ones, at least—who seem to derive some inexplicable joy from sacrificing themselves for their wayward brood. Whether it’s a reckless husband, a dissipated son, or a particularly troublesome brother, they’ll gladly bleed themselves dry if it means keeping their beloved reprobates afloat. And let’s be honest, gentlemen, how many of us have indulged them in this pastime? Who hasn’t, at some point, bestowed upon his female relations the exquisite pleasure of forgiving him?

Now, our young Arthur Pendennis had, until recently, been the pride of his uncle Major Pendennis—a man not ordinarily prone to pride unless it reflected well upon himself. The Major had watched with satisfaction as Arthur strolled through Oxbridge in the company of young lords, basking in borrowed nobility. He had even gone so far as to host modest dinners for these illustrious youths, serving up his finest claret and his most polished anecdotes (some unfit for repetition, others simply unfit). The Major, who had never been accused of excessive generosity, found himself playing the lavish host, all for the sake of Arthur’s social advancement.

But alas! The boy had squandered it all—his prospects, his mother’s money, his uncle’s pride—in a spectacular display of idleness and extravagance. And now, having failed his examinations with a resounding thud, Arthur found himself in the unenviable position of a man who has publicly tripped over his own feet. The shame! The humiliation! The sheer melodrama of it all!

Convinced that all of England would soon be whispering his disgrace, Pen fled the scene like a tragic hero—or, more accurately, like a man who had just realized he’d left the stove on. He wandered the countryside in a daze, his academic gown flapping pathetically in the January rain, until he was scooped up by an unlikely savior: his old acquaintance Spavin, who had, against all odds, managed to scrape through his own exams.

“Plucked, are you?” said Spavin cheerfully. “Happens to the best of us! I’ve been plucked three times myself. You’ll get used to it.”

Pen, who had not considered failure something one could simply *get used to*, stared at Spavin with the hollow-eyed gaze of a man who has just been told to cheer up after losing his hat, his wallet, and his dignity in quick succession. Nevertheless, he allowed himself to be bundled into Spavin’s garish postchaise, where he was deposited at a coaching inn to catch the mail to London.

Once in the city, he skulked about like a man expecting to be recognized at every turn—though in truth, London had far more pressing scandals to discuss than one plucked university student. He endured a painful interview with his uncle, who alternated between outrage at the debts and bewilderment at the concept of academic failure.

“*Plucked?*” the Major repeated, aghast. “Is this some barbaric university ritual? Were you publicly stripped? Flogged?”

Pen, momentarily distracted from his despair, had to explain that no, it simply meant he had failed his exams. The Major, somewhat relieved that his nephew hadn’t been subjected to medieval torture, swiftly moved on to the more pressing matter of the bills—seven hundred pounds’ worth of them.

“You’ve managed to spend a small fortune and have nothing to show for it but a collection of IOUs,” the Major observed dryly. “A talent, I suppose, though not one I’d recommend cultivating further.”

Pen, in a fit of remorse, declared he would confess everything to his mother but would ask her for no more money—a noble sentiment, albeit one that ignored the rather glaring issue of how he intended to live without funds.

After a few miserable days of pacing London’s streets, dodging acquaintances, and wallowing in self-pity, Pen received two letters: one from his tutor, urging him not to abandon his studies, and another from home—a letter that reduced him to his knees in penitent tears. And so, with a heart full of remorse and a stomach full of breakfast (for even the most dramatic crises must yield to hunger), Arthur Pendennis boarded the coach back to Chatteris, ready to face the music.

Or at least, ready to sit very quietly while others played it for him.

The Major’s letter, naturally, sent Doctor Portman galloping to Fairoaks with the grim enthusiasm of a man delivering bad news. There’s a peculiar energy in the step of a person bearing unpleasant tidings—as if the sooner the blow is struck, the sooner the healing can begin. And so the good doctor, after reading the missive aloud to his wife and daughter (accompanied by a symphony of condemnations about young Pen’s descent into ruin), marched off to break the widow’s heart—or so he assumed.

But Helen Pendennis, it turned out, had already read Pen’s letter. And instead of collapsing into despair, she felt an odd sense of relief. The storm she had long feared had finally broken, and her boy—her reckless, foolish, beloved boy—was coming home to her, repentant and tender. What more could she want?

The Rector’s lecture on duty, discipline, and the sacred importance of a university degree bounced off her like raindrops off a duck’s back. What good was a degree if it made Pen miserable? Why must he be thrust into a den of temptation, far from his mother’s care? As for his debts—well, of course they would be paid. Wasn’t his father’s money his own?

Meanwhile, a certain domestic awkwardness had arisen between Pen and his adoptive sister, Laura. Once upon a time, their affectionate greetings had included a fraternal kiss—a habit of childhood now abruptly abandoned. The girl he had left behind had transformed into a young lady of sixteen, tall and slender, who greeted him with a curtsey, a proffered hand, and a blush that spread precisely over the spot his lips had once claimed.

Now, describing female beauty is a hazardous business (and, frankly, not one I care for—virtue being infinitely more attractive than a pretty face), but Laura Bell at sixteen was undeniably striking. Pale, with a faint rosy tint that flared under emotion, she had eyes so large and luminous that critics accused her of deploying them like weapons. Her lashes, long and dark, provided a modest veil, though some insisted even this was calculated for effect. Her mouth might have been deemed too wide by the finicky, but her smile was irresistible, revealing perfect teeth, and her voice was so soft it seemed to hum like a melody.

Laura had not been blind to Pen’s recent failings. His letters home had grown rare and terse. His vacations were spent in grand houses, not with his mother. His talk had become cynical, his manners careless. He dismissed church, avoided walks, and lounged in dressing gowns while Helen fretted. And then there was the Fotheringay scandal—an actress! A drunken father! The very thought made Laura’s blood boil.

Yet the moment she heard Pen was in trouble, her anger evaporated. Debts? A trifle! A failed degree? The examiners must have been prejudiced. She rallied to his defense with a fervor that left Doctor Portman baffled and Helen tearfully grateful.

That night, as the two women lay awake, Laura hatched a plan. For years, Helen had supported her as if she were a charity case. Laura would now repay her—with interest. The next day, they would withdraw her modest savings (a sum Helen had prudently set aside) and send it to Pen. No more debts, no more suffering. Helen, overwhelmed, could only embrace her and weep.

When Pen finally arrived—traveling by coach to save money, shivering atop it like a penitent—he found Fairoaks waiting, warm and forgiving. No reproaches, no lectures. Just love.

But peace did not settle easily on Pen. He sold his horse, curtailed his indulgences, and moped about like a ghost. He avoided Dr. Portman, attended church dutifully, and scribbled gloomy verses. Laura, watching him, suggested—with some hesitation—that he return to Oxbridge. Not for glory, but for redemption.

And so, with a sigh, he went. A plucked man is a pitiable creature at university—a ghost among the living. Pen kept to himself, studied grimly, and emerged with his degree. But London, when he visited, offered no comfort. His uncle received him coldly.

Back at Fairoaks, the days dragged. Helen grew paler, Laura quieter. Pen, trapped between guilt and inertia, wrote tragedies and dreamed of escape. The house, once lively, now echoed with silence.

And so the prodigal returned—not to feasting, but to the quiet ache of home. The fatted calf had been killed, but the taste was bittersweet.

While the good folks of Fairoaks were dozing through their daily routines like a pair of well-worn slippers, the grand old manor across the River Brawl was shaking off decades of dust and cobwebs with all the subtlety of a bull in a china shop.

Yes, dear reader, Clavering Park was about to be inhabited again—an event so shocking it sent ripples through the county faster than a scandal at a vicar’s tea party. The news spread like wildfire: at Clavering Market, at Cackleby Fair (where the pigs paused mid-oink to gossip), and even at Tinkleton Church, where the congregation temporarily forgot about salvation in favor of speculation.

Now, let us introduce Sir Francis Clavering, a man whose life story could be titled *How to Squander a Fortune Without Really Trying*. After a brief and disastrous stint in the military (where his greatest battle was against his own gambling debts), Sir Francis had spent years drifting through European spas, casinos, and boarding houses, perfecting the art of being a charming nuisance. His luck turned—or so he thought—when he married the widowed Jemima Augusta Snell Amory, freshly returned from India with a fortune hefty enough to make even a miser weep with joy.

Lady Clavering, as she now was, had a reputation as mixed as a continental breakfast. Some whispered she was "new money" (the horror!), others that her origins were "regrettably murky" (even worse!). But none of that mattered once her bank balance entered the room. Suddenly, the haut monde of Paris couldn’t get enough of her. She funded convents, missionaries, and even a particularly ambitious scheme to convert the Pope—all while throwing parties so lavish they made Versailles look like a parish bake sale.

Meanwhile, back at Clavering Park, the long-abandoned mansion was receiving its first visitors in years. Sir Francis rolled up in a carriage like a returning conqueror, flanked by his ever-loyal (and ever-loud) friend, Captain Ned Strong—a man whose mustache alone deserved its own military rank.

The housekeeper, Mrs. Blenkinsop, nearly fainted when they arrived. She mistook the dashing Captain Strong for Sir Francis, prompting the real baronet to pipe up, "Wrong fellow, madam! *This* is your rightful lord!"—gesturing to himself with all the enthusiasm of a man announcing a tax audit.

Together, they toured the cavernous halls, Captain Strong waxing poetic about "capabilities" (by which he meant grand balls, a theater in the library, and possibly a jousting arena), while Sir Francis mostly wondered when lunch would be served.

The town of Clavering, naturally, lost its collective mind. The arrival of a baronet! A wealthy lady! A mysterious daughter! Captain Strong, with his bottomless charm and even deeper repertoire of war stories, became the local celebrity overnight. He knew every farmer, flirted with every market girl, and even got the sternest old dowagers tittering behind their fans.

And what of Miss Amory, the enigmatic daughter? Ah, dear reader, she was a puzzle wrapped in a sonnet, dipped in mystery—a young lady who painted, wrote poetry, rode like an Amazon, and, according to Captain Strong, was "a paragon, a muse, a *femme incomprise*."

Pen, ever the skeptic, declared he "hated clever women." Laura, sweet as sugar but sharp as a needle, replied, "Thank you," with a look that could curdle milk.

And so, with new faces, fresh gossip, and enough drama to fuel a dozen drawing-room farces, Clavering prepared itself for the most entertaining social upheaval since the last time someone served the wrong wine at dinner.

Let the games begin.

Every household has its hidden torment, its skeleton rattling in the closet—and if misery loves company, then take comfort, dear reader, in knowing that even the grandest estates and wealthiest families are not immune to domestic discord. Our charming little Blanche, with her dulcet voice and angelic smiles, might seem the very embodiment of sunshine, yet she was, in fact, the chief irritant of Clavering House, a tiny pebble in the shoe of its inhabitants, grinding away at their patience with relentless precision. Who could have guessed that such a delicate, poetry-spouting creature could be the source of such universal vexation?

One evening, as Sir Francis Clavering and his ever-loyal confidant, Captain Strong, lounged by the billiard table, wreathed in the contemplative smoke of their cigars, the Baronet unburdened himself with characteristic bluntness.

“Strong,” he said, squinting at a particularly tricky shot, “I wish to blazes your wife would kick the bucket.”

*Crack!* The billiard balls scattered. Strong chuckled. “So do I. But mark my words, she’ll outlive us all—stubborn as a cockroach. Why the sudden enthusiasm for her demise?”

“Because then you could marry Missy,” Sir Francis drawled. “She’s not entirely hideous. Ten thousand pounds, too—a tidy sum for a broke old soldier like you.”

Strong laughed outright. “Not for twice the money! The girl’s a little demon.”

“Gad, I can’t stand her,” the Baronet groaned. “Every day, she finds some new way to needle me. Last night, she had the governess in tears before the soup was cold. Then I caught her terrifying little Frank with ghost stories—poor lad was howling like a banshee. And today at lunch, she set my wife off blubbering again.”

“What did she do this time?” Strong inquired, amused.

“Started in about her ‘dear father,’” Sir Francis said with a grimace. “Dragged out some Keepsake engraving, swore it was his likeness, demanded to know where he was buried. Hang her father! Every time she brings him up, Lady Clavering turns on the waterworks. Today I lost my temper, told her *I* was her father now, and—well, you can guess how that went.”

“And what did she say to that?” Strong prodded, grinning.

“Said I wasn’t fit to lace his boots,” Sir Francis muttered. “Called him a ‘man of genius and fine feeling’—implying, of course, that I’m a dull-witted brute who married her mother for money.”

“Well, didn’t you?”

“Just because it’s true doesn’t make it pleasant to hear!” the Baronet retorted. “That girl has a knack for twisting the knife. I swear, Strong, if she were to fall down a well tomorrow, I’d throw a party.”

Thus, it became clear that Miss Blanche Amory, for all her poetic airs and literary pretensions, was not the household’s darling. She fancied herself a misunderstood genius, languishing among philistines—her mother, whose spelling was atrocious, and her stepfather, whose intellectual horizons began and ended with the racing forms. And so, like any proper tragic heroine, Blanche made sure everyone knew of her suffering.

If life denied her sympathy (and in her view, it did), then why should she feign cheerfulness? A poetess *must* lament! And so Blanche’s lyre played only dirges—elegies for her withered hopes, sonnets for her cruelly frostbitten affections. Her actual misfortunes were few, but her capacity for melancholy was boundless. She could summon tears at will, and indeed, she practiced often, for sentiment, like any habit, grows with indulgence. (Horace, that wise old Roman, compared it to dropsy—once you start, you can’t stop.)

By sixteen, Blanche had devoured every French romance Balzac and Sand ever penned. She had loved Prince Rodolph in the schoolroom, debated women’s rights with Indiana before she’d outgrown pinafores, and wept over Lelia’s tragic fate as other girls wept over misplaced hair ribbons. Her heart was a museum of past passions—each one intense, each one fleeting, like a child’s discarded dolls.

Blanche’s long-suffering maid, Pincott, knew the full weight of her mistress’s whims. The girl had been a Parisian seamstress before entering Blanche’s service, and now she toiled from dawn to midnight, altering gowns to suit the Muse’s ever-shifting fancies.

“I *ought* to dismiss you,” Blanche would muse, as Pincott trembled over a misplaced stitch. “You’re always sniveling, always begging for the doctor. But I endure you—for your poor parents’ sake, you understand.”

Or: “Pincott, your wretched face gives me a migraine. Perhaps you should wear rouge. You look like a ghost.”

Or, after an hour of hair-pulling while Blanche read novels: “You may kiss me. Good night. Have the pink dress ready by morning.”

And yet, Blanche considered herself a benevolent mistress. She even wrote verses about Pincott’s loneliness—tender little trifles, composed between demands for fresh tea and tighter lacing.

Meanwhile, in Clavering town, Madame Fribsby, the milliner, had insinuated herself into the household’s good graces. Her cards—printed in both French and English—declared her the purveyor of the latest Parisian fashions, and soon, Mrs. Bonner, Lady Clavering’s maid, was a regular at her soirées, sipping green tea and feasting on scandal.

But the most devoted of Madame’s new acquaintances was Monsieur Mirobolant, the French chef, whose flamboyant attire (crimson waistcoat, checkered trousers, gilded cane) had initially provoked hoots from the local urchins. Madame Fribsby, ever the gallant, rescued him from their mockery, and soon he was paying homage to her with lobster mayonnaises and preserved fruits, addressing her as *“la vertueuse Fribsbi”*—though his heart, alas, was pledged elsewhere.

Thus did life at Clavering Park roll on—Blanche sighing over her romantic woes, Sir Francis grumbling into his brandy, and the servants scrambling to keep up. And if the Muse’s tears flowed rather too freely, well—such is the price of genius.

Lady Clavering was, to put it kindly, not the sharpest quill in the inkpot. Sir Francis had once remarked—with the brutal honesty of a man who’d married for money—that his wife’s intellect was roughly on par with that of a particularly dim garden shrub. And while this was uncharitable, it wasn’t entirely *inaccurate*.

Her education had been a slapdash affair—two years in "Ackney" (a place she stubbornly refused to correctly pronounce) before being shipped off to Calcutta at fifteen. It was on this voyage, aboard the *Ramchunder East Indiaman*, that she met her first husband, Mr. Amory—third mate, professional scoundrel, and all-around disaster. The details of their courtship were best left unexplored, though it involved mutiny, irons, and Captain Bragg (a man whose personality matched his name).

Amory, ever the enterprising rogue, eventually weaseled his way into marrying the wealthy attorney’s daughter, despite her father’s understandable objections. What followed was a spectacular career of failure—indigo planter, newspaper editor, and finally, fugitive from Calcutta’s creditors. By the time he fled to New South Wales, even the local judges had tired of his antics.

Left in the wreckage, Lady Clavering (then Mrs. Amory) returned to Europe with her daughter Blanche, a long-suffering nurse named Bonner, and a deep-seated terror of Captain Bragg’s judgmental glares. She bounced between boarding houses across the continent, leaving behind a trail of half-finished curries, paid-up rent, and bewildered fellow guests who couldn’t decide if her mangled English was charming or alarming.

By the time she stumbled upon Sir Francis Clavering in Rome (himself dodging creditors), she was more than ready for a fresh start. Grief over Amory’s death was conspicuously absent.

Blanche had spent her formative years trailing after her mother like a melancholy shadow, absorbing all the melodrama of European boarding schools and the occasional French pension. She fancied herself a poetess, a misunderstood soul, and—most importantly—a *victim*.

Little Frank’s arrival had been a particular blow. Once the presumed heiress, Blanche now found herself eclipsed by a shrieking, sticky toddler who had the audacity to be male and, therefore, infinitely more valuable. Resentment festered. She wrote maudlin verses about her “angel brother” while simultaneously pinching him under the table.

Arthur Pendennis, ever the brooding philosopher, had developed a hopeless infatuation with Blanche. Unfortunately, his primary emotions were self-doubt and a crippling fear of rejection.

*"How could he, a penniless dreamer, aspire to a girl like Blanche? She lived in a manor! She had servants! Meanwhile, his mother counted coins at Fairoaks!"*

Helen Pendennis, ever perceptive, saw through Blanche’s act. The girl was all flutter and no substance—a selfish creature wrapped in pretty verses. Laura, initially enchanted, soon reached the same conclusion after witnessing one too many tantrums directed at governesses and hapless maids.

The final straw came when Blanche, in a fit of pique, reduced her maid Pincott to tears. Laura, usually the soul of patience, erupted.

*"You call yourself sensitive, yet you torment anyone beneath you!"* she snapped. *"You write poems about love and then bully your own brother!"*

Blanche, unaccustomed to defiance, gaped like a startled goldfish. Laura stormed out, leaving the Muse to sulk in her boudoir, no doubt composing a sonnet about betrayal.

Earlier that day, Blanche had played her trump card—love poems Pen had written her.

*"Oh, Laura, you mustn’t tell a soul!"* she’d whispered, fluttering the pages like a conspirator.

Laura read them, her stomach twisting. They were achingly earnest, full of nymphs and undying devotion. She handed them back without a word.

Blanche, mistaking silence for awe, trilled a Rossini aria, smug as a cat with cream.

But Laura was done. By the time they linked arms for luncheon, she had already decided: Blanche Amory was a fraud wrapped in lace and self-pity.

And as for Pen? Well. That was a problem for another chapter.

The rift between the girls was brief—Laura too quick to forgive, Blanche too capricious to hold a grudge. Hostilities, never lasting in Miss Amory’s nature, had scarcely been roused by their quarrel. For what does vanity care for accusations of wickedness? Blanche, far from affronted, seemed almost gratified by Laura’s indignation—a reaction only possible because both understood the unspoken cause.

Laura sighed, resigning herself to the truth: the romantic fervor of their friendship had cooled, leaving only the pale warmth of ordinary regard.

Blanche, ever the dramatist, composed verses to mourn her desertion—lines dripping with melancholy, lamenting love’s neglect. But grief proved fleeting. New neighbors arrived from London, their households brimming with daughters, and Miss Amory promptly selected a fresh confidante. The pony-carriage no longer clattered to Fairoaks; the footmen’s visits dwindled. When Laura called, Blanche wore an air of martyrdom, which her friend met with amused indifference.

Yet if Blanche consoled herself with new companions, the historian must note she did not lack for masculine solace. A garden stroll, a dance, a moment by the drawing-room window—each became a stage for her artful performance. A glance, a sigh, a tone of tender appeal, and another heart was ensnared before she moved on to the next.

When the Claverings first settled at the Park, audiences for Blanche’s theatrics were scarce. Pen enjoyed exclusive rights to her coquetry—until autumn brought the county’s elite back to their estates. The Earl of Trehawk and Dowager Countess of Rockminster led the procession of carriages rolling up the avenue, and soon Mirobolant’s culinary talents were in constant demand. The footmen, now overworked, abandoned their dalliances with Fairoaks’ maids. Blanche, too, abandoned the riverside walks where Pen once lingered, rod in hand, waiting in vain for his Undine.

Ah, that hollow tree—their makeshift post-office! Beneath moss and stone, Pen left verses and letters, only to find them untouched, the pink scented replies vanished. Had he spoken plainly, might she have answered? But neither was earnest; it was a game, and when Blanche tired of play, Pen’s pride curdled into resentment. He fancied himself tragically slighted, nursing wounds as if this were his first heartbreak.

At Clavering Park, he found her surrounded by visitors—once, she pleaded exhaustion before a ball; another time, she strolled with the Bishop of Chatteris and his family, who eyed Pen with frosty disdain. Lady Clavering, ever kind, whispered, *"The Bishop thinks you a sad young man! What have you done to vex your dear mother?"*

Dinner invitations followed, but Pen secured only a fleeting word with Blanche. *"You never come to the river now,"* he accused.

*"Undine has left the stream,"* he persisted, poetic even in pique.

*"She never should have gone,"* Blanche replied. *"It was foolish. Only play. Besides—"* She met his gaze, then dropped her eyes. *"You have consolations at home."*

Did she wait for him to speak? Perhaps. But thoughts of Laura—loyal, steadfast Laura—and his mother’s hopes silenced him. Blanche slipped away, leaving Pen to brood under the moonlight.

Meanwhile, Laura observed them. She conversed with Mr. Pynsent, heir to Lord Rockminster, whose straightforward humor charmed her. When Blanche summoned her to sing, Pynsent lingered by the piano, enthralled—until Miss Amory’s performance sent him retreating.

*"A fine girl,"* Pynsent remarked to his companion, Wagg. *"The tall one, with the ringlets."*

Wagg, ever the cynic, smirked. *"And the other? The Park’s darling?"*

*"A simpering puppet,"* Pynsent scoffed. *"All sighs and sidelong glances."*

The next day, the gentlemen called at Fairoaks. Wagg, sharp-eyed and mocking, cataloged the modest household—the gardener’s worn waistcoat, laundry drying on bushes, Pen’s discarded college attire. *"Snug little crib,"* Pynsent mused, unbothered by its simplicity.

Inside, Helen received them with quiet grace, while Laura parried Pynsent’s playful queries about Pen’s political ambitions. *"He ought to stand for the county,"* Pynsent said. *"A man of his talents."*

Pen arrived to find his home invaded. Though he masked his irritation, the arrival of tea—thick bread-and-butter borne in by Betsy—shattered his pretensions. Wagg’s smirk deepened; Pynsent, oblivious, drank his tea without comment.

Later, Wagg regaled the Clavering table with merciless mimicry of Fairoaks’ rustic charms. Blanche laughed, but Pynsent snapped, *"I’ve never met better-bred women. If you mock them, it says more of you than them."*

Lady Clavering beamed. *"Mr. Pynsent, you’re a good feller."*

At Fairoaks, Helen noticed Laura’s watchful gaze on Pen—the flicker of scorn as he lounged, listless, adrift. *"What has happened?"* Helen pressed.

*"Nothing,"* Laura said. Then, softly: *"He withers here. He needs purpose. Other men strive—Pynsent, even Captain Strong. Pen? He does nothing."*

Helen’s voice trembled. *"We have enough. He could be happy."*

*"No,"* Laura replied. *"Men like him must work, must make their mark. He laughs at Wagg’s fame, yet writes nothing himself. He was meant for more than this."*

The widow fell silent. In the garden, Pen stared at the horizon, as if willing the world beyond to claim him.

Arthur Pendennis had stationed himself in the shadow of some calico drapes like a brooding villain from a penny dreadful, glowering as Miss Laura Bell took to the floor for her first quadrille. Her partner, Mr. Pynsent, moved through the figures with the solemnity of a man marching to his own execution—back straight, face grave, as if the fate of the empire rested upon his correctly executed chassé. Laura, meanwhile, was all radiance—her brown curls bouncing, her cheeks pink, her smile so infectious that even Lady Fogey (who considered herself the final arbiter of London fashion) leaned over to Lady Rockminster and declared, “That girl will *do*.”

Lady Rockminster, who had never entertained a doubt that any protégée of hers would fail to “do,” peered through her lorgnette and pronounced Laura “pleasingly unspoiled.” (The same could not be said for Miss Blanche Amory, Laura’s vis-à-vis, whose shoulders were in constant motion, her eyes darting, her entire being screaming, *Look at me! Admire my Parisian grace!*—while poor Captain Broadfoot, her partner, lumbered about like a dragoon who had mistaken the ballroom for a battlefield.)

Pen, meanwhile, nursed his sulk like a fine vintage, refusing to dance, until Lady Clavering—ever the peacemaker—beckoned him over. He approached with the grace of a startled heron, bowing stiffly to Lady Rockminster (who ignored him) and then to the resplendent Mrs. Amory, who glittered like a jeweler’s display case.

Young Fogey of Eton, aflame with adolescent ardour, claimed Laura’s hand next. “I like you ever so much better than that French girl,” he blurted, earning a laugh that made Pen scowl harder. *Good*, he thought. *Let her see what she’s missing.*

The waltz arrived, and Pen, seizing his chance, swept Blanche onto the floor—only to collide catastrophically with Captain Broadfoot and Miss Roundle (a young lady whose strawberry-ice gown contained enough fabric to sail a small ship). Down they went in a heap: Pen sprawled, Blanche shrieked, and Miss Roundle sat dazed, her coiffure now resembling a startled pheasant’s nest. The room erupted in laughter—except for Pen, who turned the color of a boiled lobster, and Blanche, who promptly abandoned him for the first available rescuer: a dashing figure with a star on his breast.

“*Mon Dieu*,” murmured Pen. “It’s the cook.”

Alas, Mirobolant *was* the cook—but also a Gascon with a wounded heart and a keen sense of honor. Blanche, oblivious, clung to his arm until Strong intervened with a hissed warning in Spanish. The chef retreated, casting a glare at Pen that promised future retribution (likely involving a skewer).

Meanwhile, in the refreshment room, Colonel Altamont—resplendent in his Nizam of Lucknow regalia—was holding court, drinking champagne like water and eyeing every passerby with the intensity of a man who either wanted to duel them or sell them a horse. When Blanche entered, he fixed her with a stare.

“Betsy Amory, by Jove!” he declared.

Blanche blinked. “I beg your pardon?”

But before further revelations could unfold, a commotion erupted in the ballroom—because, of course, no country ball is complete without at least one scandal, one collision, and one misplaced chef.

Civil war had erupted—not on a battlefield, but in the corner of a ballroom, where gentlemen and ladies clustered like flustered hens around a window. Outside, the street crowd hurled commentary as if they were spectators at a boxing match: *"Give 'im what for!"* and *"Where's the bobbies when you need 'em?"*

At the center of the storm stood two men: Arthur Pendennis, his dignity freshly trampled by a disastrous waltz, and Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant, Sir Francis Clavering’s temperamental chef, whose ringlets trembled with outrage. The chef had approached Pen with all the solemnity of a diplomat delivering a declaration of war.

*"Vous me comprenez, Monsieur Pendennis?"* Mirobolant hissed, rolling his *r’s* like a Gascon artilleryman. *"Quand je vous dis que vous êtes un lâche?"*

Pen, who had been nursing his wounded pride by staring moodily at the sea, now found himself accused of cowardice by a man who spent his days wrestling soufflés.

*"A what?"* Pen sputtered.

*"A lâche,"* Mirobolant repeated, hand on hip, as if he were a musketeer awaiting a challenge.

Pen’s response was swift and British: *"The consequence is, I’ll fling you out the window!"*

Chaos ensued. Ladies shrieked. Fiddles screeched to a halt. Captain Broadfoot and another burly officer wedged themselves between the two like human barricades. Laura Pendennis appeared, her face a picture of alarm, while the ever-opportunistic Chevalier Strong materialized from the refreshment room, surveying the scene with amusement.

*"What’s happened?"* Strong inquired in Spanish, as one does when diffusing Franco-British hostilities.

*"I am the Chevalier de Juillet!"* Mirobolant proclaimed, thumping his chest. *"And he has called me—a cook!"*

*"Damned impudent frog-eater!"* muttered a sympathetic gentleman.

Laura, ever the peacemaker, intervened. *"Pen, won’t you apologize? He’s a foreigner—his feelings are delicate."*

Pen grumbled but relented, scribbling a conciliatory note later that night: *"Met Cooky downstairs. Apologized. Glad I did. P.S. Dance all you like with Pynsent—see if I care."*

Laura, clutching the note, sighed in relief. Her cousin was still capable of grace beneath the bluster.

Downstairs, Strong found Mirobolant pacing like a caged tiger. *"Pistols or swords?"* the chef demanded.

*"My dear Chevalier,"* Strong said, *"we mustn’t spill blood over a misunderstanding. Mr. Pendennis meant no offense—he merely forgot that in England, cooks don’t escort ladies to supper."*

When Pen finally faced Mirobolant, he delivered a halting French apology—*"sincèrement fâché"*—which Strong applauded like a theater critic. The chef, mollified, declared Pen a *"galant homme,"* and the two shook hands, their honor intact.

*"A natural gift,"* Pen replied airily—unaware Strong was laughing *at* him, not *with* him.

The next day, Helen Pendennis watched her son with barely concealed anticipation. *"Laura’s in the garden,"* she hinted, eyes twinkling.

Pen, however, felt less like a romantic hero and more like a man marching to his own execution. *"I suppose I must marry her,"* he thought gloomily. *"Two women have decided it—what choice have I?"*

He found Laura among the roses, gloved and grinning. *"Still cross about last night?"* she teased.

*"You laughed at me,"* Pen accused.

Pen scowled. *"It’s easy for you to mock. You’re not the one who took a tumble before all of society."*

Laura’s smile softened. *"Oh, Pen. Must you always take yourself so seriously?"*

He exhaled. *"Perhaps not. But must you always be so… *you*?"*

She laughed, and for a moment, the weight of obligation lifted. Whether Pen would propose—or flee to London to pursue fame and fortune—remained to be seen. But for now, the garden was peaceful, the roses were blooming, and Laura’s laughter was, as ever, impossible to resist.

Arthur Pendennis, Esq., late of Fairoaks and Oxbridge, now found himself rattling toward London atop the Alacrity coach, a vessel of destiny packed with hopeful youths, snoring merchants, and at least one gentleman who smelled profoundly of cheese. The wheels turned, the night deepened, and with it, Arthur’s resolutions.

*I shall be prudent*, he vowed to the stars. *I shall resist temptation. I shall make my mother proud—though, admittedly, the resisting temptation bit may prove tricky if temptation wears a particularly fetching bonnet.*

By dawn, the coach clattered into B——, where Pen had once devoured sausages as a schoolboy and now devoured sausages as a man. Progress, of a sort. The sun rose, the horn blared, and Pen—no longer a dawdling boy but a man of the world (or so he told himself)—smoked, joked, and pretended not to notice the alarming sway of the coach as it hurtled toward Babylon.

London! That great, grimy enchantress. As the city sprawled before him, Pen’s chest swelled. *Here is my battlefield*, he thought, *where I shall conquer or be trampled underfoot by creditors, critics, and cleverer men.*

His introduction to metropolitan life was swift. A shabby fellow passenger, one Mr. Doolan of the *Star* newspaper, claimed intimate acquaintance with every literary lion from Tom Hood to Sydney Smith. Pen, wide-eyed, absorbed every word—until Doolan casually mentioned that the renowned Mr. Hurtle, critic and terror of poets, was at that very moment strolling through Brompton with an umbrella.

*Hurtle!* Pen nearly toppled from the coach in his eagerness to glimpse the great man. "A Boniface man," he whispered reverently.

"Faith, he is," Doolan agreed, as if this settled the matter.

No sooner had the coach deposited him at the Gloster Coffee-house than Pen spotted Harry Foker, now adorned with a tuft of chin hair and a cab so grand it might have been stolen from a coronation.

"Pen, my boy!" Foker bellowed, waving a whip like a scepter.

Within hours, Pen found himself in a private theater box beside Miss Blenkinsop, an actress whose ringlets defied gravity and whose papa, the "veteran Blenkinsop," snored peacefully in the corner. The play was a farce—both onstage and off, for the box’s occupants critiqued the performers with the solemnity of statesmen dissecting a treaty.

"That’s Mirabel’s wife," someone remarked, pointing to the leading lady.

Pen’s heart lurched. *Mirabel’s wife?* His first love, the divine Miss Fotheringay, now wed to some gouty diplomat! He peered at her, half-expecting pangs of regret—but found only bewilderment. *Did I truly sigh over her?*

The evening spiraled into cigars, claret, and a blur of introductions. By morning, Pen was a fledgling member of the Megatherium Club, lighter by a third of his funds, and considerably heavier in head.

Lamb Court, Temple, became his new abode—a place so grim that even the rats wore expressions of existential despair. His roommate, George Warrington, a bearded, beer-swilling barrister with the manners of a bear and the pedigree of a gentleman, welcomed him with a clap on the back that nearly sent Pen into the fire.

"Your uncle’s here," Warrington announced days later, as Major Pendennis picked his way through the tobacco fog of their chambers.

The Major, surveying the chaos, sighed. "Boys will be boys," he muttered, though whether in resignation or approval, even he could not say.

Thus began Pen’s London career—a comedy of errors, ambitions, and the occasional noble intention, all set to the tune of clattering hooves and the eternal question: *What on earth have I gotten myself into?*

The legal sanctuaries of London—those ancient inns of court where barristers congregate like hermit crabs in venerable shells—cling stubbornly to the customs of bygone days, as if time itself had taken the bench and ruled against progress. While modern prisons boast ventilation and workhouses scrub their floors with zeal, the Temple remains a glorious relic of medieval discomfort. Here, men pay a small fortune to sleep in chambers barely fit for a scullery maid, where the only running water arrives via a bedmaker’s grudging jug.

Consider old Grump of the Norfolk Circuit, a man who had dwelled beneath Warrington and Pendennis for three decades, cursing the innovative shower-baths that dripped through his ceiling like an indoor weather system. Grump, who hadn’t voluntarily touched water since the reign of George III, declared the practice a dandy’s frivolity. And who could blame him? His forefathers had thrived in glorious filth—why break tradition now?

Yet for all its archaic inconveniences, the Temple holds a peculiar charm. Walk its halls, and you tread the same stones as legal titans of yore—Coke, Eldon, Chitty—ghosts who whisper precedent from the shadows. The place teems with life of every variety: the penniless barrister gnawing his nails in a garret one year, dining in Belgravia the next; the aged scholar hoarding books and solitude like a miser; the relentless workhorse Paley, who traded poetry for pleadings and never looked back.

And then there was Pen.

Young Arthur Pendennis had arrived with vague ambitions of legal grandeur but found himself far more enchanted by the Temple’s social symphony. His studies were punctuated by strolls in the park, rowing excursions, and evenings of modest revelry—always with a pipe in hand and Warrington, his newfound comrade, at his side. Warrington, a baronet’s son with a taste for shilling taverns and a disdain for pretension, was the perfect antidote to Pen’s occasional bouts of vanity.

Dining in Hall became a spectacle in itself. Pen, seated beside the opportunistic Lowton—a man who eyed the benchers’ leftovers with the intensity of a starving bloodhound—observed the ecosystem of aspiring lawyers: the Irish braggarts, the dandies who never actually ate, the political orators, and the bookish drones murmuring case law into their soup.

“That’s Boosey the bencher,” Lowton whispered, pointing at a bald man attacking a bowl of dubious liquid. “Probably turtle. They *love* turtle.”

“Fascinating,” Pen replied, suppressing a laugh.

But the true highlight was Warrington’s arrival—an unkempt figure in a frayed gown who slid onto the bench beside Pen with the ease of an old friend. Their bond was instant, forged over college tales and shared port (Pen, abandoning his earlier pretensions, drank heartily). By night’s end, Pen had resolved to abandon his lonely lodgings and move into Lamb Court, where tobacco and camaraderie awaited.

Thus, our hero settled into the rhythms of Temple life—a world where ambition and idleness waltzed in equal measure, and where the only certainty was that someone, somewhere, would grumble about the plumbing.

Arthur Pendennis, now a self-proclaimed connoisseur of London’s underbelly, flung himself into the city’s murkier corners with the enthusiasm of a man who’d just discovered that gin could, in fact, be consumed before noon. He fancied himself a scholar of humanity—studying coalheavers like rare beetles, observing boxers as if they were exotic fauna, and generally behaving like a prince slumming it among his future subjects.

Warrington, his guide in these escapades, was a man of simpler tastes. He preferred the company of men who could lift barrels or swallow swords over the dandies of Mayfair. “Society,” he’d declare between sips of gin, “is just the same fellow in different waistcoats. But here—*here* you find *character*.” Pen, meanwhile, strutted through taprooms with the air of a young duke inspecting a peasant revolt, nodding graciously at the rabble as if they ought to be flattered by his presence.

Their evenings followed a noble routine: mornings spent pretending to study law (or, more accurately, skimming newspapers for scandal), afternoons nursing headaches, and nights gallivanting through London’s less reputable establishments. Youth, after all, is the only time a man can mistake exhaustion for vitality.

One such haunt was the **Fielding’s Head**, a pub so ancient it might’ve served drinks to Henry Fielding himself—assuming he’d been the sort to enjoy a ballad between verdicts. The place was run by **Cutts**, a landlord whose voice could rouse a dead man—or at least make him request a last pint. His specialty was patriotic songs of the *“God Save the Queen and Pass the Whisky”* variety, delivered with enough fervor to make grown men weep into their ale.

It was here that Pen stumbled upon an old acquaintance—**Captain Costigan**, now a permanent fixture of the Back Kitchen, holding court like a deposed monarch. The Captain, perpetually halfway to oblivion, greeted Pen with the warmth of a man who’d just found a sovereign in his pocket—only to promptly forget his name.

“Me dear boy!” he bellowed, sloshing his drink. “Me dear… er… lad! Of course I remember ye! Pendennis! No—wait—*Pendragon*? No matter! Have a drink!”

Pen, torn between amusement and pity, endured the Captain’s ramblings—mostly about his daughter’s marriage to **Sir Charles Mirabel**, a tale that grew grander with each retelling (and each pint). The Captain’s finances, much like his memory, were fluid. One moment he was flush with phantom wealth, the next begging half-crowns with the dignity of a bankrupt king.

Nearby, hunched over a battered piano, was **Bows**, the theater accompanist from Pen’s youth. The old musician played on, ignored by the crowd, until Pen recognized a tune from *The Stranger*—a melody that once made his heart race for **Emily**, the actress who’d long since vanished into respectability.

“Still remember the old songs, eh?” Bows chuckled. “Shame the Captain doesn’t. Then again, he doesn’t remember his own name most nights.”

The Back Kitchen was a carnival of London life: medical students arguing over cadavers, clerks sneaking illicit pints, even the odd politician slumming it between votes. The night’s entertainment peaked with **Hodgen**, the bass singer, delivering *“The Body Snatcher”*—a grisly ballad so chilling it emptied the room faster than a shout of *“Round’s on the house!”* (which, incidentally, never happened).

As the crowd thinned, two newspapermen—**Hoolan** of the *Dawn* (Liberal) and **Doolan** of the *Day* (Tory)—held court, swapping insults about rival editors between bites of kidney pie.

“Our governor says the public don’t care for these press feuds,” sighed Doolan.

“Aye, and that’s why your rag’s dull as ditchwater,” Hoolan shot back.

Warrington leaned in. “Watch this—they’ll be sharing a cab home by dawn.”

Pen, now thoroughly schooled in London’s contradictions, sipped his drink. The city was a stage, and everyone—drunken captains, hack writers, even himself—was merely playing a part.

And oh, what a farce it was.

Pen, in the midst of his modest but lively bachelor indulgences—humble in cost, if not in spirit—found himself staring down the barrel of an uncomfortable truth: his funds were nearly exhausted. The last golden soldier in his pocket was preparing to desert, and no reinforcements were in sight. His club subscription had claimed a hefty portion of his reserves; his snug little chambers had swallowed another chunk for furnishings. Now, with a solitary five-pound note left, he faced the grim arithmetic of necessity.

Warrington, his companion in these frugal revels, remained blissfully unaware of Pen’s financial straits. To him, Pen was a young gentleman of means—an only son, with a mother in the country and an uncle who dined among the great. The gold chains, the lordly dressing-case, the aristocratic ease with which Pen tossed cabmen their fares (always too generously), all suggested a man untroubled by petty economies.

But the truth, when it emerged, came starkly—as Pen gloomily contemplated his last note over Warrington’s ale-pot.

*"The last rose of summer,"* Pen declared with mock grandeur. *"Its companions have long since withered—and now the final petal drops."* He laid bare the whole sorry tale: his mother’s slender income, his own collegiate extravagance, Laura’s quiet generosity. Warrington listened, pipe in mouth, eyes sharp with interest.

When the confession ended, Warrington rapped his pipe clean and delivered his verdict. *"Impecuniosity is the finest tonic for an honest man. It’s the only way to teach him to grip fortune by the throat. You’ve been coddled, Pen—spoiled by women. But a man with health and brains who can’t feed himself? He ought to fling his last coin into the Thames and follow it."*

*"Inspiring,"* Pen retorted. *"But how, precisely, does one live?"*

*"How do you think I live?"* Warrington shot back.

Warrington’s face darkened. *"That ran dry years ago. No, I write."* He admitted it almost grudgingly, as if confessing a vice. *"Reviews, articles—whatever pays. And you could do the same."*

*"I?"* Pen was incredulous.

*"You’ve got the knack for it,"* Warrington insisted. *"Those scraps of verse you’ve scribbled—there’s real wit in them. A spark of the true fire, not just lamp-oil."*

Pen flushed with pleasure. To be called a poet—even by Warrington—was intoxicating.

*"I think you’d better try,"* Warrington said dryly.

And so, opportunity knocked—in the form of *The Spring Annual*, a gilt-edged volume of fashionable verse, patronized by lords and literary lions alike. A plate awaited verses—a damsel at a church porch, a lurking admirer—and Percy Popjoy’s feeble lines had been deemed unfit for public consumption. Warrington, ever the opportunist, snatched the commission for Pen.

*"Now, earn your supper,"* he commanded, tossing the engraving at him.

Pen labored; Warrington dined. By nightfall, the verses were done—a neat, sentimental trifle, just right for the *Annual’s* audience. Warrington approved, and the next morning, he marched into Bacon’s publishing house to negotiate.

Pen, meanwhile, paced Paternoster Row like a man awaiting sentence. The grand edifices of literature loomed around him—Bacon’s austere shop, Bungay’s gaudy rival establishment, each a temple to the written word. He imagined his lines in print, his name among the fashionable contributors.

Warrington emerged triumphant, waving a check. *"Ten guineas. Not bad for a morning’s work."*

Pen was exultant. *"I’m not a beggar yet, by God! I can pay Laura back. I can stand on my own feet!"*

Warrington grinned. *"Don’t buy a castle in the air just yet. But yes—you’ve tasted earned bread. It’s bitter, but it’s yours."*

They celebrated with claret at the club—a luxury Pen hadn’t indulged in for months. The toast was drunk: *"To the Spring Annual!"*

And then, as if fortune had only been waiting for this proof of effort, another letter arrived—this time from Captain Shandon of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. A new opportunity. A fresh start.

Pen’s career as a man of letters had begun.

Captain Shandon, with all the flourish of a man announcing a revolution, unveiled his grand prospectus for *The Pall Mall Gazette*. It was a masterpiece of dramatic outrage—a call to arms for the gentlemen of England, who, according to the Captain, were under siege from every direction: radical upstarts, scheming industrialists, and the unwashed masses who had the audacity to question their betters.

*"The monarchy is sneered at!"* Shandon declared, slapping the paper for emphasis. *"The Church is undermined by dissenters and atheists! Our glorious institutions—those very pillars that made England the envy of the world—are left defenseless against the barbarians who respect neither history nor law!"*

Bungay, the publisher, nodded along like a man who had heard this tune before but was happy to tap his foot to it again. Warrington, meanwhile, burst into laughter.

"You see how stirred our friend Bungay is," Shandon remarked, grinning. *"That’s* the true test. I’ve trotted out the Duke of Wellington and Waterloo a hundred times, and they never fail to rouse the patriotic spirit."

The Captain then lamented how the aristocracy had been *too* secure, *too* indifferent, leaving their interests in the hands of hired pens and political lackeys. But no more! The time had come for gentlemen to take up their own defense—no more letting Grub Street scribblers dictate the narrative.

*"That’s a dig at Bacon’s crew,"* Shandon said, winking at Bungay, who thumped his stick on the floor in approval. *"Oh, give it to 'em, Captain! Nobody slashes like you!"*

The prospectus promised a journal of unimpeachable loyalty—to Crown, Church, and Country—written *by* gentlemen *for* gentlemen. No radical rags or dissenting drivel here! *The Pall Mall Gazette* would be the voice of the elite, the informed, the *right sort*.

Warrington, amused, ruffled the hair of Shandon’s young daughter, Mary, who promptly retreated to the safer company of Arthur Pendennis, playing idly with his watch chain. Pen, flattered by her preference, sat quietly while Shandon continued his grand oration.

*"We cannot, for obvious reasons, reveal our contributors,"* Shandon said, pausing dramatically. Warrington snorted. *"Ah, you laugh! But mark this—we have allies in Parliament, in every embassy in Europe! Our sources are unparalleled! While other papers grovel for scraps, we dine at the table of power!"*

Bungay, who had dozed off twice during this performance, woke long enough to declare it *"all right"* before shuffling out, tossing a penny to little Mary as he left.

Once the business was settled, Shandon launched into sparkling anecdotes about high society—stories of lords, ladies, and scandalous suppers, all delivered with the air of a man who moved in such circles daily. His wife listened, enraptured, believing every word. *Hadn’t he once been invited to dine with an earl?* (The invitation card was still tucked reverently in her sewing box.)

Pen, fascinated and faintly melancholy, watched the scene—the shabby prison room, the hopeful wife, the clever, broken man spinning tales of grandeur between sips of gin. When the party finally dispersed, Shandon bolted for the tavern, the five-pound note from Bungay already burning a hole in his pocket.

Left alone, Mrs. Shandon brewed a pot of tea—that universal solace of disappointed women. Little Mary nibbled a sweet from Jack Finucane, Shandon’s ever-loyal shadow, who dropped by with treats and blind admiration.

*"He won’t keep that five pounds long,"* Bungay predicted as they walked away. He was right. By nightfall, Shandon had settled old tavern debts, lost half-crowns at cards, and returned home as penniless as he had begun.

Later, over brandy, Pen mused on the tragedy of wasted genius.

*"It’s a shame,"* he said, *"a man of his talent, rotting in debtors' prison."*

Warrington scoffed. *"Talent doesn’t excuse vice. If a man drinks away his earnings and ignores his debts, why should he escape consequences? The world owes him nothing."*

Pen argued for compassion. Warrington retorted with brutal practicality. The debate ended as most did—with Warrington ordering another drink and Pen sighing over the romance of doomed brilliance.

That night, as Pen lay awake, thoughts of Shandon’s charm, his wasted potential, and his long-suffering wife swirled in his mind. Meanwhile, Warrington’s snores rumbled next door—a man untroubled by sentiment, or at least very good at pretending to be.

The indomitable Captain Shandon, ever the schemer, had managed to secure his loyal friend John Finucane—affectionately known as "Jack"—the position of sub-editor for the forthcoming *Pall Mall Gazette*. Finucane, a man whose devotion to Shandon bordered on the saintly, was more than willing to serve. After all, it was in Finucane’s cramped Upper Temple chambers that Shandon had often taken refuge from creditors, and where poor Mrs. Shandon had wept over her husband’s misfortunes. Finucane had fed them, comforted them, and even listened to little Mary’s bedtime prayers with solemn reverence.

Now, with a new venture afoot, Finucane found himself dining with the publisher Bungay—a man whose business acumen was only matched by his love of brandy. Over a meal that involved more arithmetic than appetite, Finucane laid out his grand vision for the *Gazette*: the typefaces, the market reports, the society gossip, all meticulously planned. Bungay, though slow on the uptake, was dazzled by Finucane’s rapid calculations and declared him "a clever fellow."

But Finucane had another mission—one closer to his heart. He knew the exact sum needed to spring Shandon from the Fleet Prison, and he was determined to see his friend free. With a mix of charm and pathos, he painted a picture of Mrs. Shandon’s suffering and little Mary’s fading health, until even the hard-nosed Bungay relented.

"Sixty-five pounds," Bungay grumbled. "Half now, half docked from his salary."

Finucane nearly knocked over the table in his joy.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Shandon, ever the picture of genteel endurance, paid a visit to Mrs. Bungay—a woman whose fashion sense was as bold as her personality. Dressed in a riot of shot silk and brandishing a blue parasol, Mrs. Bungay might have intimidated a lesser soul. But Mrs. Shandon, in her faded black silk, held her ground. Little Mary, clinging to her mother’s skirts, was lured away by the promise of sweet biscuits, and soon even Mrs. Bungay’s stern demeanor softened.

By the time Finucane returned, triumphant, the women had worked their own magic. Mrs. Bungay, moved by the plight of the child (who bore an uncanny resemblance to her own lost daughter), persuaded her husband to advance the funds.

And so, with Bungay’s grudging blessing and Finucane’s boundless enthusiasm, the wheels were set in motion. The creditors were appeased, the paperwork signed, and Captain Shandon’s prison stay came to an end—at least for the time being.

As Mrs. Shandon rode home in Mrs. Bungay’s carriage, little Mary between them and Finucane grinning like a madman in the back, one couldn’t help but think:

On the appointed evening, our two heroes presented themselves at Mr. Bungay’s residence in Paternoster Row—not the bustling trade entrance, where hopeful authors lurked like spectral petitioners, but the private door through which the resplendent Mrs. Bungay made her daily exits, sweeping into her carriage under the envious gaze of Mrs. Bacon across the street.

Mrs. Bacon, still tragically carriage-less, had perfected the art of passive aggression. Whenever Flora Bungay’s chaise rolled into view, she would fling open her drawing-room window and station her four cherubs on full display, as if to say, *"Behold the fruits of my virtue—you, madam, are but a gilded husk!"* And thus, armed with maternal artillery, Emma Bacon volleyed silent triumph at her childless rival.

As Pen and Warrington arrived, a cab and carriage pulled up to the Bacon residence. Out tumbled Dr. Slocum, his bulk as ponderous as his prose, followed by two waistcoats so blindingly white they could’ve been mistaken for beacons of literary pretension.

"Ah, Bacon’s assembled his own circus," Warrington muttered. "That’s Hoolan from Bungay’s stable—though you’d hardly recognize him without his habitual ink stains." Indeed, Messrs. Hoolan and Doolan emerged from a cab, bickering over the fare like schoolboys. Doolan, clad in funereal black, sported gloves so large they might’ve doubled as dinner napkins, which he admired with the pride of a man who’d just invented hands.

Inside, a squadron of stiff-backed footmen (gloved in Berlin’s finest) announced the guests with all the subtlety of town criers. Mrs. Bungay, resplendent in red satin and a turban that defied gravity, performed cursory welcomes before retreating to her window to tally the carriages arriving at Mrs. Bacon’s—each one a dagger to her social aspirations.

The guest list was a veritable menagerie of ink-stained oddities:

- **Mr. Bole**, the magazine’s true editor, lurking in the shadow of the nominal figurehead, Mr. Wagg.
- **Mr. Trotter**, a poet who’d traded sonnets of despair for the steady wage of a publisher’s reader.
- **Captain Sumph**, a professional name-dropper whose entire personality was a footnote to Lord Byron’s biography. ("*I once wrote Shelley’s verses for him at Eton—poor chap couldn’t rhyme to save his life!*")

Mrs. Bungay hung on Sumph’s every word, for nothing thrilled her more than secondhand aristocracy. Had he arrived in his own carriage, she’d have commissioned a trilogy on the spot.

Then—**the Honourable Percy Popjoy** arrived, lacquered boots first, his face a perfect study in cheerful vacuity. Nature, having gifted him a chin-tuft, abandoned further efforts, leaving the rest of his countenance as smooth as a billiard ball.

"*Quiz?* Me? Never made a joke in my—hullo! Pendennis! Warrington! What the devil are *you* doing here?" Percy exclaimed, promptly forgetting his hostess existed.

The grand finale was the entrance of **Mr. Wagg** (a walking pun factory) and **Mr. Wenham** (a man so unctuous he could’ve lubricated a steam engine). Wagg, ever the showman, regaled the room with tales of Baymouth and Clavering Park, while Wenham murmured flattery with the precision of a Swiss watch.

**Miss Bunion**, the evening’s poetical spectacle, was not the ethereal muse Pen had imagined. Instead, she was a formidable woman who creaked into the room like a ship under full sail, trailing straw from her omnibus ride. ("*Sixpence all the way from Brompton—what economy!*") She ate with gusto, ignored Pen entirely, and later confessed she’d mistaken him for "one of those Mayfair prigs."

Dinner was a parade of pretension:

- **Wagg** declared the sherry "filthy" (it was).
- **Popjoy** praised the *cordon bleu* chef (the dishes were from Griggs’ catering).
- **Shandon**, outshone and over-policed by Bungay’s footmen, drank in grim silence while his wife watched him with quiet despair.

Conversation veered from Popjoy’s "novel" (ghostwritten from an old magazine serial) to the launch of Bungay’s new paper, the *Pall Mall Gazette*—a title Wagg insisted should be called *The Small Beer Chronicle*.

"*Why Pall Mall?*" Wagg sneered.

"*Because the editor’s Irish, the publisher’s in Paternoster Row, and the office is in Catherine Street,*" Shandon snapped. "*Must everything make sense?*"

And so, amid clinking glasses and clashing egos, the feast unfolded—a masterclass in ambition, envy, and the eternal quest for a second helping of praise.

The *Pall Mall Gazette* burst onto the scene with all the subtlety of a fireworks display in a library. Immediate success! Whispers swirled that a powerful political faction lurked behind its pages, and illustrious names were tossed about like confetti at a society wedding. Were these rumors true? Well, discretion forbids us from confirming *or* denying—but let’s just say that a certain earth-shattering article on foreign policy, widely attributed to a certain noble lord (whose ties to the Foreign Office were as obvious as his tailor’s bills), was in fact penned by none other than Captain Shandon, hunched over a sticky table at the *Bear and Staff*, a printer’s boy panting at his elbow. Meanwhile, a series of dazzling financial essays, believed to be the work of a towering parliamentary statesman, flowed from the ink-stained fingers of Mr. George Warrington in the Upper Temple.

Coincidence? Perhaps. But then again, Percy Popjoy (son of Lord Falconet, a pillar of the aforementioned political party) was spotted more than once clomping up the stairs to Warrington’s chambers. And the *Gazette* did seem to possess an uncanny knack for insider information—the kind that didn’t just fall off the back of a hansom cab.

Then there were the poems. Ah, the poems! Signed boldly *P. P.*, they thundered across the page with all the delicacy of a drunkard reciting Homer. And lo! Percy’s dreadful novel received such rapturous praise in the *Gazette* that one might suspect either bribery or temporary insanity.

Young Arthur Pendennis, meanwhile, steered clear of politics but dove headfirst into literary criticism with the enthusiasm of a terrier in a rat pit. The *Gazette*’s offices in Catherine Street became his second home, manuscripts bulging from his pockets, his chest puffed with the intoxicating thrill of seeing his name—nay, his *genius*—in print.

Here, too, labored Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, a man who wielded scissors and paste like a surgeon performing a particularly violent amputation. With the diligence of a bloodhound, he scoured every newspaper in the kingdom for gossip about the aristocracy. Deaths, dinners, scandals—nothing escaped his keen eye. It was a sight to behold: Finucane, munching on a greasy chop from the cookshop, recounting the grandest balls of the season as if he’d waltzed through them himself—though his trousers were frayed and his shirt had seen better days.

Pen found Finucane’s contradictions endlessly amusing. Here was a man who wrote of dukes and duchesses with the familiarity of a butler, yet whose most frequent companions were tavern rats and ink-stained hacks. His prose occasionally stumbled—mistaking a viscount for a baron, perhaps—but the *Ballinafad Sentinel* suffered these blunders, not the lofty *Pall Mall Gazette*, where Finucane’s pen was kept on a tight leash.

As for Pen’s own contributions, he approached them with the solemnity of a scholar composing a dissertation. His reviews were sharp, witty, and—most dangerously—*honest*. This, alas, did not always sit well with Mr. Bungay, the publisher, who viewed journalism as a blunt instrument for crushing rivals, not a forum for fairness.

One day, Shandon confronted him. “Pendennis, what in blazes is this? You’ve gone and *praised* a Bacon publication!”

Pen blinked. “You mean to say I must declare good books bad simply because they’re printed by the enemy?”

Shandon sighed, the sigh of a man who has long since sold his scruples for a steady income. “My dear boy, do you think we run a critical journal to hand out bouquets to the competition?”

“I think we run it to tell the truth,” Pen retorted, chin high.

Shandon laughed. “Ah, the idealism of youth! Reminds me of my own naivety—before I realized that principles are a luxury, like champagne, and most of us drink porter.”

Pen left that day, more determined than ever to cling to his integrity, even as the cynical laughter of his elders rang in his ears.

But fate, ever mischievous, had a lesson in store. The very next issue of the *Gazette* featured a brutal evisceration of the *Spring Annual*—a publication which, unbeknownst to the world, contained Pen’s own verses (hidden under a pseudonym, naturally). Since Pen had refused to review it, Shandon had handed the task to the notorious Mr. Bludyer, a critic whose pen dripped more venom than a cobra’s fang. With savage glee, Bludyer tore the anthology to shreds, then promptly sold his review copy for brandy money.

And so Pen learned two invaluable truths: first, that the literary world is a battlefield where no good deed goes unpunished; and second, that one’s finest verses may one day buy a stranger’s bottle of gin.

Let us glide swiftly over a few uneventful months in the life of Mr. Arthur Pendennis, for while the daily grind of a literary man may be of thrilling interest to himself, it is seldom so to the reader. We last left our hero firmly entrenched in the trenches of Grub Street, a humble scribe for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, where he toiled with all the dignity of a man who has exchanged lofty dreams for hard cash.

A professional writer’s life, much like that of a clerk, a curate, or a subaltern, is a monotonous parade of deadlines, ink-stained fingers, and the occasional sting of criticism—both given and received. Pen, though no stranger to indolence, found himself laboring under the whip of necessity, churning out reviews with the brisk efficiency of a hackney cab horse. His critiques were flippant, occasionally unjust, but seldom malicious—unless, of course, the unfortunate subject was an actor, in which case no mercy was shown.

Yet let us not weep too profusely for poor Pegasus, condemned to trot in harness. The beast of poetry must earn his oats like any other, and Pen was not one to demand special exemption from the vulgar demands of rent and taxes. He wrote because he must, and if his reviews were sometimes dashed off with more haste than reverence, well, so be it. The literary world of his day was not so overstuffed with genius that his hasty judgments did much harm.

By diligent scribbling—reviews, magazine articles, even the occasional anonymous letter to the *Chatteris Champion*—Pen managed to scrape together a modest income. So modest, in fact, that when he proudly presented his mother with a hundred pounds toward his debt to Laura, the good widow nearly fainted with maternal pride. To her, this act was nothing short of saintly restitution, and she devoured every word he wrote with the fervent conviction that her son was the wittiest, wisest man in London.

Meanwhile, Pen was making his way in society, thanks to his uncle’s introductions and his own agreeable manners. He was that rare creature: a gentleman who wrote for amusement rather than bread, and thus was welcomed in circles where mere drudges of the pen would not dare to show their ink-stained cuffs. He dined with dukes, debated with critics, and lounged in club windows—yet still found time to haggle with publishers in the back rooms of newspapers, where ink and sherry flowed in equal measure.

His days were a whirl of contradictions: breakfasting with bishops, lunching with booksellers, evenings divided between the opera and the tavern. He was young enough to enjoy it all, and wise enough (thanks to Warrington’s sardonic eye) not to take himself too seriously.

But while Pen was thus engaged, London society marched on without him. The Season was in full bloom, and the *Morning Post* dutifully chronicled the triumphs of Lady Clavering and her daughter Blanche, newly arrived in town and setting tongues wagging with their lavish entertainments.

Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, that polished relic of a bygone era, held court in his dressing gown, sipping tea and absorbing the latest scandals from his valet, Morgan—who, like all great domestic spies, knew everything worth knowing about everybody who was anybody.

“The Claverings are making a splash,” Morgan observed, as he scraped the Major’s chin with a razor. “Three parties a week, dinners besides. Her ladyship’s fortune won’t hold out—mark my words.”

“She had a damned good cook at Fairoaks,” the Major mused, with more interest in the memory of sauces than the state of the Clavering finances.

“Gone now, sir. That Frenchman, Mirobolant—there was a row about him.”

“A row?”

“Tried to climb up to Miss Amory’s window, sir. Got the garden hose turned on him. Then there was talk of poison, and a duel, and all sorts of nonsense.”

The Major’s eyebrows shot up. “Surely Blanche didn’t encourage the fellow?”

Morgan’s face remained a perfect blank. “Servants never know, sir. But where there’s smoke…”

Later that day, as the Major lounged in the window of Bays’s Club—that venerable gathering place of aged rakes—he spotted his nephew strolling down St. James’s Street with young Popjoy, who was busy declaring all the old members “a chamber of horrors.”

Pen laughed, but the Major rapped the glass, beckoning him inside.

“Your uncle wants you,” Popjoy said. “Notorious old rogue, that one.”

Pen grinned. “Aren’t they all?”

And with that, he stepped into the club, leaving the younger reprobate to his own devices—while the Major prepared to impart some choice wisdom, no doubt tinged with a lifetime of cheerful cynicism.

Thus did Mr. Arthur Pendennis navigate the treacherous waters of London life—now a critic, now a clubman, now a son, but always, inevitably, himself.

Morgan the valet might have been the only soul in London privy to the true state of Lady Clavering’s finances, but the rest of society remained blissfully deluded by the shimmering mirage of her supposed fortune. The city buzzed with tales of indigo plantations, opium clippers, and sacks of diamonds wrested from the grasp of indebted Indian princes. Her bank balance, it was whispered, stretched into such astronomical figures that even mathematicians gasped at the zeros. The legend of the *Begum Clavering* had taken root long before her ladyship’s carriage ever rolled into Grosvenor Place.

Enter Colonel Altamont—a man whose exploits were as dubious as his conversion to Islam and whose current mission in England involved the attempted sale of the Nawaub of Lucknow’s fabled nose-ring diamond, *The Light of the Dewan*. A more unlikely envoy could scarcely be imagined, yet here he was, mingling with London’s elite, his presence only adding to the mystique of the Clavering name.

Such was the magic of money. The British aristocracy, for all its pretensions of exclusivity, had a charming habit of rolling out the red carpet the moment wealth came knocking. Titles might be hereditary, but a full purse was the quickest path to acceptance. Dukes dined at your table. Earls’ sons courted your daughters. And if your past was a tad checkered? Well, a thick stack of banknotes had a way of smoothing over even the most scandalous wrinkles.

Sir Francis Clavering, alas, was a man who had spent his life skating on the thin ice of financial ruin. His pockets were perpetually empty, his creditors eternally impatient, and his only true talent lay in signing promissory notes he had no intention of honoring. Enter the Chevalier Strong—a man whose loyalty to Sir Francis was matched only by his talent for turning disaster into a mildly profitable farce. Strong had overseen the furnishing of the Claverings’ London residence with the gusto of a man spending his own money (which, in a roundabout way, he was).

The house itself was a masterpiece of bewildering extravagance. The dining room was a temple to medieval excess, complete with high-backed chairs that looked as though they’d been looted from a Gothic abbey. The library boasted leather-bound books (all uniformly sized for maximum aesthetic harmony) and busts of philosophers who would have wept at the thought of their likenesses presiding over Sir Francis’s naps.

But the drawing rooms—ah, the drawing rooms! Here was a carnival of gilded excess. Carpets so plush they swallowed footsteps whole. Chairs so delicate they seemed designed for fairies rather than flesh-and-blood guests. Porcelain shepherdesses simpered from every corner, while a pair of shrieking cockatoos provided a soundtrack of perpetual chaos. It was, in short, a monument to the very best bad taste money could buy.

Lady Clavering surveyed it all with cheerful bewilderment. "I don’t know why they’ve gone and made it look like a museum," she remarked, "unless it’s because me and Clavering are middle-aged relics ourselves!"

Blanche, meanwhile, winced at every misstep her mother made—whether linguistic or social. When Lady Clavering extended a warm (if grammatically mangled) invitation to Pendennis and his uncle for a "snug little dinner," Blanche’s expression suggested she was contemplating matricide.

Pen, for his part, found the whole affair deliciously entertaining. The Clavering household was a theater of the absurd, and he was more than happy to play audience. Blanche’s barbs, Lady Clavering’s blunders, Sir Francis’s palpable terror whenever the name *Altamont* was mentioned—it was better than a comedy at Drury Lane.

Major Pendennis, ever the strategist, saw only opportunity. "Marry money, my boy," he counseled as they strolled away. "Love in a cottage is all very poetic, but a well-stocked larder is far more sustaining."

Pen laughed. The game was afoot, and he was more than ready to play.

The evening of Sir Francis Clavering’s dinner arrived, and with it, Major Pendennis—who had no better engagement—and his nephew Arthur, who desired none. Upon entering the drawing-room, they found their host and hostess in a state of subdued animation, accompanied by their ever-present factotum, Captain Strong. The Major, whose aristocratic sensibilities were perpetually on guard, regarded Strong with thinly veiled disdain, privately dismissing him as "Clavering’s damned house-steward." Fortunately, the arrival of Mr. Welbore Welbore, a dull but impeccably respectable country squire, soothed the Major’s ruffled dignity.

The party swelled with the addition of Lady Rockminster (who had her own reasons for cultivating the Claverings), Lady Agnes Foker, and her son Harry—our old acquaintance—whose presence promised at least a measure of youthful absurdity. Last to grace the gathering was Miss Blanche Amory, resplendent in a white silk gown that displayed her shoulders to devastating effect. Foker, awestruck, whispered to Pen that she was a "stunner," a sentiment Arthur echoed silently, though with considerably more irony.

Blanche, in a masterful display of selective charm, was exquisitely gracious to both young men—fluttering her lashes at Foker while bestowing nostalgic smiles upon Pen, reminiscing about Fairoaks and inquiring tenderly after Laura and Mrs. Pendennis. Harry, unaccustomed to feminine attention, found himself uncharacteristically loquacious, chattering about opera, souprames of volaille (and the mysterious fate of fowl legs), and the immortal Taglioni.

“Miss Amory is a sylph herself,” Pen remarked dryly.

“What a delightful tenor you have, Mr. Foker,” Blanche cooed, her voice dripping with honey. “I should love to sing with you.”

Pen, recalling identical overtures once directed at himself, suppressed a smirk.

Dinner progressed with the usual blend of forced conviviality and quiet desperation. The ladies eventually withdrew, leaving the gentlemen to their wine and the rapidly deteriorating company of one another. The conversation, already strained, was interrupted by an uninvited guest—Colonel Altamont, who, in a state of advanced refreshment, had stationed himself outside the dining-room window, peering in with the enthusiasm of a man who believes himself the life of every party.

A scuffle ensued when the ever-vigilant Policeman X attempted to dislodge him. Altamont, resplendent in brass buttons and a Tyrian-purple wig, declared his intimate acquaintance with the Clavering household, bellowing for Strong and Sir Francis by name. Strong, with the air of a man well practiced in damage control, ushered the Colonel inside before the altercation could escalate.

The intrusion sent Sir Francis into visible distress, while Major Pendennis studied the interloper with narrowed eyes. Altamont, meanwhile, made himself at home, demanding the finest wine and addressing his host as “old corkscrew” with alarming familiarity.

“Very singular man,” Strong murmured by way of explanation. “Resided long in India—native courts, you know—peculiar habits.”

“Evidently,” the Major replied, his tone drier than the claret.

Altamont, upon recognizing Pen, launched into slurred reminiscences of the Baymouth ball, then abruptly turned his bleary gaze upon the Major. A peculiar tension crackled between them before the Colonel, muttering something about “Captain Beak,” staggered out as suddenly as he had arrived.

The evening never quite recovered. Coffee was served; the gentlemen rejoined the ladies; Blanche performed at the piano with calculated brilliance, coaxing rapturous praise from the Major, who had, not long before, dismissed her as a frivolous minx.

As the party dispersed, Foker clung to Pen, extolling Blanche’s virtues in increasingly incoherent terms, while the Major—unusually pensive—whispered a stern directive: *Call on them tomorrow. They’ve been uncommonly civil.*

Pen, bemused, wondered what game his uncle was playing. But then, in the Clavering household, the game was always afoot—and the players, as ever, delightfully unreliable.

Since that fateful—yet thoroughly enchanting—evening in Grosvenor Place, Mr. Harry Foker’s heart had been in a state of upheaval that would astonish even the most seasoned philosopher. This was, after all, the same Harry Foker who had once dispensed such sage advice to young Pen, whose early cynicism and worldly wisdom had seemed unshakable. A gentleman of leisure, accustomed to indulging his every whim, ought by all rights to have grown even more jaded with each passing year, caring for little beyond his own comfort. And yet, here he was, hopelessly ensnared by the oldest trap in the world: love.

The Back Kitchen, usually a sanctuary of gin-twists and raucous laughter, held no joy for him that night. The jokes of his companions fell flat. Even the legendary Mr. Hodgen, warbling his newest horror-ballad *“The Cat in the Cupboard”* (destined, no doubt, for immortality), failed to rouse more than a mechanical *“Bravo, Hodgen”* from our distracted hero. When he finally stumbled home and collapsed into bed, sleep offered no refuge—only feverish dreams haunted by a pair of clear grey eyes and a melody that looped incessantly in his mind: *“Yet round about the spot, ofttimes I hover…”*

How dull and flavorless his former pleasures now seemed! His past flirtations with actresses, his lively debates over boxing matches, even his cherished collection of French prints (some of which made his dear mother blush)—all of it paled beside the radiant vision of Miss Blanche Amory. Even the prospect of a raucous dinner at the Elephant and Castle with the ever-charming (and ever-thirty-seven) Miss Calverley filled him with a profound sense of existential despair.

His mother, the ever-doting Lady Agnes, noted his pallor at breakfast.

*“It isn’t the billiards,”* Harry sighed.

*“No, no, it isn’t the Back Kitchen either,”* Harry groaned, sinking deeper into gloom.

Ever the diplomat, Harry steered the conversation toward safer waters—specifically, the previous night’s dinner at the Claverings’.

Lady Agnes, always eager to agree, nodded along.

*“The Claverings are very good people,”* Harry ventured.

*“Hmm,”* said Lady Agnes, in a tone that suggested she found Lady Clavering about as distinguished as a side of boiled mutton.

*“Oh, she’s not exactly *high ton*, but devilish good-natured. And Sir Francis—well, he’s a brick after dinner. We ought to have them over.”*

And so, with a little coaxing, the Claverings were duly invited—*including* Miss Amory, despite Lady Agnes’s reservations (*“That little creature!”*). Harry’s triumph was complete.

That afternoon, Harry embarked upon a toilette of unprecedented complexity. He rejected pantaloons of every conceivable stripe, cursed his excessively glossy boots, and finally, in a moment of desperation, turned to his valet, Anatole.

And so, his locks artfully arranged, Harry set forth—not to fulfill his prior engagement with Miss Calverley (though honor compelled him to send the drag for her later), but first to take a *small detour* past Grosvenor Place, where he gazed wistfully at a certain window, and then—oh, necessity!—to Lamb Court, Temple, to call upon his *dearest friend*, Arthur Pendennis.

Now, if any young ladies should read this, let it be known: love has a way of making the most indifferent objects suddenly fascinating. A man you scarcely noticed yesterday may today seem the very axis upon which the world turns. And so it was with Harry Foker. Pen, who had lived two years in London with scarcely a dozen visits from him, was now *absolutely essential* to Harry’s existence.

Why? Because Pen had been near *her*. Because Pen knew her habits, her haunts, her very thoughts! What would Lady Ann Milton (that unfortunate cousin to whom Harry was vaguely promised) have said, had she known the tempest in her betrothed’s heart?

But such is the way of love—capricious, cruel, and entirely indifferent to prior arrangements. And as for our poor Harry? Well, he was only suffering what Achilles, Ajax, and even Lord Nelson endured before him.

The game was afoot.

Poor Foker found the Richmond dinner to be the dullest investment of guineas he had ever made. "Good heavens," he mused, "was there ever a time I actually enjoyed this lot?" The cracks in Rougemont’s beauty were now painfully visible—her rouge applied with the subtlety of a circus clown. Calverley’s attempts at slang were as grating as a knife on porcelain. And Lord Colchicum, wedged between Mademoiselle Coralie and her mother like a stuffed pheasant in a game pie—what a spectacle! An English peer reduced to a Franconi’s afterthought!

"Penny for your thoughts, Foker dear?" Miss Rougemont purred, plucking the cigar from her alarmingly crimson lips. The table was a ruin—melting ices, crushed pineapples, cigar ash sprinkled like seasoning over fruit.

"Does Foker *have* thoughts?" drawled Mr. Poyntz. "The ladies are offering a reward for any sign of intellect, old boy."

"What *is* he on about?" hissed Miss Calverley to her neighbor. "That drawling, sneering toad."

Meanwhile, Mademoiselle Coralie appraised Foker with Gallic amusement. "Quel drôle de petit homme! He looks scarcely twenty."

"Would that I were," sighed Lord Colchicum, tilting his wine-dark face toward his claret.

"Youth? Pah! Give me a seasoned man," declared Madame Brack, pilfering another pinch from his lordship’s snuffbox.

The conversation spiraled into realms unfit for print—though it must be noted that not all dancers share Calverley’s charms, nor all peers Colchicum’s tastes. Major Pendennis, for instance, would have gladly joined such revels—provided they were discreet. "Let the young sow their wild oats," he often told Pen. "I’m no puritan, but a man of the world." (Though in his ledger, "young" extended comfortably into the seventies for certain fashionable gentlemen.)

Foker drove his guests home in grim silence, deaf to their shrieks of farewell. Dismissing the drag, he wandered moonward, casting lovelorn glances at a certain house in Grosvenor Place. Policeman X, observing this melodrama, remarked to Sir Francis’s coachman, "Another swell lurking about—small chap, looks lovesick."

Thus began Foker’s campaign of dogged pursuit. He materialized at every Amory engagement—French plays (despite his lamentable grasp of the language), soirées (to his mother’s delight, who believed he’d turned over a new leaf), even dancing academies (though he claimed Parisian instruction).

One Sunday, Major Pendennis steered Pen toward Saint Benedict’s—and, conveniently, the Clavering pew. "Church, then lunch with the Begum," he declared. "She adores impromptu guests."

Pen chuckled. "Miss Amory’s latest conquest is Foker. She near devoured him at Lady Agnes’s dinner."

"Pish! The boy’s engaged to his cousin. Money marries money, Arthur." The Major’s eyes gleamed. "Though between us, Blanche may be richer than supposed. Thirty, forty thousand—who knows?"

Pen scoffed. "Her father was a ship’s mate!"

"Tchah! Birth matters less than a full purse," retorted the Major, adjusting his impeccable neckcloth as they entered the church.

Inside, Pen spotted Foker among the servants, his gaze riveted to a yellow bonnet (Lady Clavering) and a pink one (Blanche). Post-sermon, the Begum swept all three into her barouche. "You *must* come to lunch!"

Blanche was effusive. "Oh, Mr. Pendennis, your novel! *Walter Lorraine*—I wept!"

"Every tear is my triumph," Pen replied, with a flourish.

Foker blinked. "Walter who? Died of measles at Grey Friars, didn’t he?"

Luncheon unveiled Blanche’s full arsenal—flattery for Foker ("*Adore* your cane, your *exquisite* waistcoat buttons!"), teasing for Pen ("You’ve grown *wickedly* clever!"), and a sigh that could melt lead.

Seizing the moment, the Major proposed a Greenwich excursion. "A bachelor’s frolic! My treat—or rather, Arthur and Foker’s."

The day was a triumph. Foker secured balcony tête-à-têtes; Pen endured his uncle’s relentless matchmaking ("She’ll be richer than you think, my boy!"); and Blanche, between mouthfuls of whitebait, declared Greenwich "heavenly."

As Sir Hugh’s brougham rolled home, the Major chuckled. "A fine day, begad! And mark me, Arthur—that girl’s a golden opportunity."

Pen gazed out at the darkening Thames. Opportunity, perhaps. But at what cost?

Somewhere in the dusty archives of Arthur Pendennis’s youth—tucked between an old Oxbridge waistcoat and a cravat that had seen better days—lay the manuscript of a novel. Not just any novel, mind you, but *the* novel. The kind of novel written by a young man who had just discovered three things:

1. German poetry.
2. That love hurts (especially when the object of affection prefers someone richer).
3. Ink is cheaper than therapy.

Titled *Leaves from the Life-book of Walter Lorraine*, it was, in Pen’s own later estimation, "a spectacular display of youthful despair, with bonus philosophical brooding." The hero, Walter, was a masterpiece of self-inflicted torment—a Byronic figure who scowled magnificently, sighed artistically, and suffered in iambic pentameter. Passages were underlined with the fervor of a man who believed his heartbreak was the first of its kind. Tears had once blotted the pages—though whether from genuine emotion or the strain of trying to rhyme "despair" with "fair" was now unclear.

Years later, when Pen rediscovered this relic of his literary growing pains, he did what any sensible man would: he laughed until his ribs ached. Then he read passages aloud to his long-suffering friend Warrington, who was busy trying to light his pipe with the fire-tongs.

"*False as thou art beautiful! Heartless as thou art fair!*" Warrington intoned, waving the manuscript like a bad actor in a melodrama. "*O Leonora! What evil spirit hath sent thee to torture me so?*"

"Good heavens," Pen groaned. "I wrote that after Lady Mirabel—then plain Miss Fotheringay—smiled at someone else at a ball. I believe I wore a black armband for a week in mourning for my own dignity."

Warrington, ever the critic, flicked ash onto the page. "It’s dreadful. Also, I suspect half of it’s plagiarized."

"*Inspired*," Pen corrected. "All artists borrow. The great ones steal outright."

"Then you, my friend, are a highwayman of the highest order."

Still, beneath the purple prose and adolescent angst, there were flickers of something worthwhile—a turn of phrase here, a genuine ache there. Warrington, despite himself, admitted it might be salvageable with ruthless editing and perhaps less use of the word "alas."

Thus began the great resurrection of *Walter Lorraine*. Pen trimmed, tweaked, and (reluctantly) deleted entire soliloquies about the cruelty of fate. Warrington contributed scathing commentary and the occasional helpful suggestion, like "burn it" and "start over."

News of Pen’s literary endeavors soon reached the ears of Bacon and Bungay, rival publishers whose enthusiasm for books was matched only by their enthusiasm for undercutting each other.

Bungay, a man who judged manuscripts by their weight, was initially skeptical until Warrington casually mentioned that Pen moved in *fashionable circles*. (This was technically true, if one counted being glared at by dowagers at parties as "circulating.")

Bacon, not to be outdone, arrived at their shabby chambers, tripped over a laundry bucket, and nearly impaled himself on a collapsing chair. "Charming," he muttered, eyeing the peeling wallpaper.

Warrington, ever the showman, gestured to a pile of society invitations on the table. "Pendennis dines with lords and ladies, yet deigns to share mutton with us. Such humility!"

Bacon, now convinced Pen was a literary genius in disguise, made increasingly extravagant offers. Bungay, hearing rumors of this, panicked and upped his bid. Pen, watching this dance of desperation, felt like a debutante at her first ball—flattered, bewildered, and mildly concerned he’d trip over his own feet.

The final sum Pen received for *Walter Lorraine* shall remain a mystery, lest every young scribbler in England abandon honest work for the fickle muse of fiction. Suffice it to say, it was enough to:

- Startle Major Pendennis, who muttered, "People *pay* for this?"
- Prompt Warrington to suggest a celebratory binge at the nearest tavern.
- Allow Pen to repay Laura—a gesture that moved her to tears and Dr. Portman to an impromptu kiss on Mrs. Pendennis’s hand (a scandalous moment everyone agreed never to mention again).

As for the novel itself? It sold. Not brilliantly, not disastrously, but enough to confirm two universal truths:

1. No one knows what the public will like.
2. If you must write a novel, try not to weep directly onto the manuscript. Blotting paper exists for a reason.

And so Pen, richer in coin and (marginally) humility, moved forward—still a fool, but now a *published* fool.

Which, in literary circles, is practically a virtue.

Shepherd’s Inn lurks like a disgraced relation behind the grand chambers of Lincoln’s Inn and the Temple. Tucked away in a labyrinth of soot-stained alleys where sunlight dares not linger, it is a place where ambition goes to die—or at least to get very drunk. The surrounding lanes are a carnival of grime: vendors hawk dubious wares, children splash through eternal puddles, and ballad-singers croak out political libels with all the charm of a dying crow.

At the heart of this cheerful squalor lies the Inn itself, a quadrangle frozen in time, where the statue of Shepherd, its founder, stands guard over a patch of yellowing grass. Once, perhaps, lawyers dignified these chambers. Now, they house a far more entertaining breed: failed speculators, theatrical has-beens, and gentlemen whose names appear in the *Law List* only under the "Also Ran" section.

Take, for instance, Mr. Campion of Campion & Co., whose West End office boasts a Mayfair address and a wife who serves tea to the aristocracy. Here in Shepherd’s Inn, however, he is simply *Campion*, and his mustache is the only thing about him that remains undeniably first-rate. His cab, emblazoned with heraldic flourishes, gleams like a misplaced jewel in this dingy setting—a fact not lost on Captain Costigan, who observes it daily from his perch in No. 4, third floor.

Costigan, our old friend, is a man of many talents—none of them profitable. He holds court in the porter’s lodge, regaling Mrs. Bolton (a former dancer, now reduced to nodding along to his tales) with stories of his daughter, *Lady Mirabel*, and the grand society she moves in. The truth, as ever, is less glamorous. Lady Mirabel does visit—once in a blue moon—and has even left her card, now yellowing in his mirror. But her patience wears thinner than the Captain’s excuses, especially when he writes pleading letters about bailiffs, broken windows, or phantom illnesses—all of which she dutifully pays for, only to later discover him frying mutton chops in his shirtsleeves.

Meanwhile, next door at No. 3, the Chevalier Strong presides over a lively establishment shared with the mysterious *Colonel Altamont*, agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow (or so he claims). Strong’s chambers are a haven of good food, bad decisions, and even worse company—a revolving door of adventurers, each with a scheme to make millions if only someone would lend them twenty pounds. There’s Jack Holt, the tobacco smuggler; Tom Diver, the sunken treasure enthusiast; and Filby, the ex-dragoon turned preacher turned actor turned whatever pays this week.

And then there’s Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., the group’s reluctant patron, who tolerates their society with the air of a man who knows too much. Altamont, in particular, vexes Strong—a boorish, volatile figure whose idea of refined entertainment is heckling actors and weeping at melodramas. *"I wish he was dead!"* Clavering once snarled when pressed about their connection. A curious sentiment from a baronet, but then, Shepherd’s Inn has never been a place for conventional gentlemen.

So here they all are—a collection of misfits, dreamers, and rogues, bound together by empty pockets and the faint, foolish hope that tomorrow might be different. And if not? Well, there’s always the Back Kitchen, where the whisky flows and the stories grow taller with every glass.

The morning after the disastrous dinner at Grosvenor Place, Colonel Altamont emerged from his quarters in Shepherd’s Inn and stumbled into Captain Strong’s sitting room, where the Chevalier lounged with a cigar and the morning paper. Strong, ever the picture of ease, had already demolished a hearty breakfast—fried eggs, rashers, and all—courtesy of the ever-useful Mr. Grady. Though he had little affection for his fellow lodger (and wouldn’t have shed a tear if Sir Francis Clavering’s murderous wishes had been fulfilled), Strong maintained a cordial front. After all, a man must have *someone* to talk to, even if that someone is a walking calamity.

The Colonel’s first act was to bellow for pale ale, which he downed in one heroic gulp. Wiping his mouth with the sleeve of his dressing gown (a garment that did little to hide the stark contrast between his dyed whiskers and suspiciously fair roots), he sighed with the relief of a man who had narrowly escaped the gallows.

"Nothing like a pint when the coppers are hot," he declared. "Drank a dozen Basses in Calcutta once—no, was it Lucknow?"

Strong smirked. "I had the beer ready. Knew you’d need it after last night’s performance."

Altamont groaned, clutching his head. "Liquor turns me into a madman, Strong. Can’t stop once I start. Last night, I meant to sip tea like a proper vicar, but then that cursed sample-bottle of Hollands winked at me from the cupboard. Next thing I knew, I was at some gambling den, losing every shilling you’d given me. Tried to squeeze more out of Clavering—ended up here, God knows how."

Strong exhaled a thoughtful plume of smoke. "How *do* you always pry money out of Clavering?"

The Colonel’s grin was wolfish. "He owes me."

"Debts don’t usually loosen Frank’s purse strings," Strong mused.

"Then perhaps he just adores me," Altamont sneered. "Or perhaps you’d best stop asking questions, eh?"

Strong let it drop. He’d find answers elsewhere—preferably from someone less likely to shoot the messenger.

Altamont, now in a confessional mood, launched into tales of his ruinous vices: brandy, dice, and women. "They’ve been my downfall since I was fifteen," he admitted. "Take Paris—met a Countess there, eyes like midnight, jewels like a queen. Swept through a thousand pounds thanks to her. Thought I was living like a king till some ‘Honourable Deuceace’ woke me with a bill for thirty louis I didn’t remember lending. Next came Bloundell-Bloundell and a ‘Marquis,’ waving more IOUs. Before I knew it, I’d coughed up 560 Napoleons—plus 300 more trying to win it back."

He sighed. "Was I swindled? Probably. But the Countess wept when she thought I’d be shot. Called me ‘Ferdinand,’ would you believe it?"

Strong raised an eyebrow. "I thought your name was Jack."

"A man can have many names," Altamont muttered, flushing behind his whiskers.

Their tête-à-tête was interrupted by the arrival of Sir Francis Clavering himself, red-faced and sputtering. "You drunken scoundrel! Using my name at the tables again?"

Altamont waved him off. "Only fifteen pounds, Governor. Hardly worth the fuss."

"Fuss? You crashed my dinner, reeking of gin, and now you’ve dragged my credit through the mud!" Clavering roared. "One more stunt like this, and I’ll cut you off entirely!"

Altamont’s grin turned dangerous. "Careful, Baronet. Push me too far, and I might just push back."

The air thickened with the promise of future explosions. Strong sighed. Another day in Shepherd’s Inn, another disaster in the making.

Major Pendennis, having indulged in the fine fare of Grosvenor Place and the maritime delights of Greenwich, found his affection for the Clavering family growing warmer by the day—or perhaps it was the generous portions of Lady Clavering’s dinners that stoked the flames. His visits became as frequent as a debtor at a bailiff’s door, and his attentions to the lady of the house so persistent that one might suspect him of either deep admiration or a calculated scheme. Being a seasoned navigator of London’s drawing rooms, he was well-equipped to guide her ladyship through the perilous waters of high society.

*"Would you care to attend the grand spectacle at Gaunt House?"* he inquired with the air of a man offering salvation. *"Or perhaps the breakfast ball at Viscount Marrowfat’s? Everyone who is anyone—and several who are decidedly not—will be there. Even royalty might deign to nod in your direction!"*

Sir Francis Clavering, meanwhile, remained as popular as a tax collector at a pauper’s banquet. The world, ever unkind, had taken to viewing him with the enthusiasm one reserves for spoiled fish. Blackballed at two clubs in swift succession, he consorted chiefly with the House of Commons’ most disreputable members—men who made even hardened politicians shudder. Captain Raff, the honourable member for Epsom (recently departed for the Levant under mysterious circumstances), Hustingson of Islington (now conveniently governing Coventry Island), and Bob Freeny, the dead shot of Booterstown, were among his chosen companions.

*"If you need Clavering for a vote,"* sneered Mr. Hotspur, the party whip, *"you’ll likely find him in a gambling den or a debtor’s prison. Mark my words, he’ll squander the Begum’s fortune on thimblerig and end his days on a prison hulk."*

Yet if Hotspur, despite his disdain, could endure Clavering for political expediency, why shouldn’t Major Pendennis cultivate the family for reasons of his own?

*"The man has an excellent cellar and a tolerable cook,"* the Major mused to his nephew Arthur. *"As long as he keeps his mouth shut, he’s harmless. And if he insists on losing his money to rogues, who am I to interfere? Every man has a skeleton in his cupboard—why rattle the bones when the rest of the house is so hospitable?"*

He then turned his avuncular charm upon Pen, nudging him toward the ever-charming Miss Amory. *"Come now, my boy, the girl is clearly fond of you. Or are you too grand now for anything less than a duke’s daughter?"*

Pen, inflated by recent successes and his uncle’s flattery, waved off the suggestion with the weariness of a man who has seen too much of the world at twenty-five. *"Marriage? Not for me. I’ve outgrown such fancies. My first love was a fool, and I’d have been a greater one to marry her."*

The Major, ever the tactician, shifted targets—next attempting to mold young Master Clavering into a suitable protégé. The boy, a terror in short trousers, had already mastered vice with the proficiency of a seasoned rake. By ten, he was filching his father’s cigars and drinking champagne with the gusto of a cavalry officer. The Major, undeterred, urged Pen to take the lad under his wing—or at least under his wallet, for young Clavering had a habit of departing with more sovereigns than he arrived with.

Meanwhile, the Major played confidant to Lady Clavering, extracting her sorrows over tea and sympathy. *"Ah, Major,"* she sighed, *"I may have diamonds and carriages, but I’m no happier than my scullery maid."*

Blanche, ever the dramatic, lamented her fate with the flair of a tragic heroine. *"The men of today are boobies, Major! Guardsmen without wit, clerks without prospects. I might as well enter a convent!"*

*"Nonsense,"* the Major soothed. *"The world is more democratic now. A clever woman with a clever husband can conquer society—provided she has the means and the nerve."*

And so the game continued, with the Major maneuvering his pieces with the precision of a chess master—though whether his endgame was fortune, influence, or mere amusement remained to be seen.

Ah, dear reader, let us now turn our attention to the noble Henry Foker—temporarily misplaced like a favorite sock—who has been, as one might expect, utterly consumed by the grand and tragicomic opera of love.

For those of you yet unacquainted with this all-consuming pastime, allow me to illuminate its absurdities. Love, my young friends, is the most extravagant hobby a person can adopt. It robs you of sleep (as you lie awake composing sonnets to your beloved’s eyebrows), ruins your punctuality (for why rise early when you can languish in dreams of *her*?), and renders you entirely useless in all practical matters. Your work? Neglected. Your family? Forgotten. Your dignity? Left in tatters at the doorstep of the adored one’s residence, where you lurk like a poorly dressed footpad, hoping for a glimpse of her shadow in the window.

Such was the pitiable state of Mr. H. Foker, a man who, having been indulged in every whim since infancy, now flung himself into love with the enthusiasm of a terrier chasing a particularly elusive rat. And let no one scoff at his folly! Have we not all, at some point, dispensed sage advice while merrily tripping over our own shoelaces? Hypocrisy is the spice of life, my friends—unless, of course, you are one of those impeccable beings who float through existence without ever desiring, stumbling, or blushing. If so, I salute you—from a distance, for your perfection is frankly exhausting.

Now, where were we? Ah, yes. Foker, pining like a melodramatic hero, trailed after Miss Blanche Amory with the persistence of a debt collector. When the Claverings attended Lord Steyne’s grand ball at Gaunt House (an affair so lavish it made the guests’ names bleed together in the next day’s papers like alphabet soup), Foker was there, quivering with devotion.

Blanche, ever the strategist, alternated between coaxing sighs from him (“*Alas, I have no one to ride with!*”) and reducing him to speechless adoration. Meanwhile, Arthur Pendennis, that self-satisfied scribbler, swanned about, offering unsolicited critiques of everyone’s fashion, French, and general existence.

Highlights of the evening included:
- **Lady Clavering** devouring the buffet with the focus of a siege engine, while Major Pendennis trailed after her like a despairing footman.
- **Sir Francis Clavering** slipping away to more familiar haunts—namely, a gambling den where the company was as refined as yesterday’s dishwater.
- **Blanche** expertly playing Foker and Pen against each other, because why settle for one admirer when you can juggle two?

As dawn crept over London, the revelers emerged, blinking like owls. The Major, who had sacrificed his health and sanity for politeness, resembled a man who had fought a losing battle with a feather duster. Lady Clavering, meanwhile, radiated contentment, having consumed enough delicacies to sustain a small village.

And so our heroes dispersed—Pen pontificating, Foker lovelorn, and Blanche calculating. Love, vanity, and ambition twirled on, much like the dancers at Gaunt House: some gracefully, some stumbling, and all convinced they were the star of the show.

*Fin.* (For now.)

The exertions of Major Pendennis’s final night at Gaunt House had left the old gentleman in such a state of exhaustion that he promptly retreated to Buxton, groaning all the way, to soak his aches in the healing waters. Meanwhile, Parliament had dissolved for the season, Sir Francis Clavering had fled town with his family, and the wheels of high society had ground to a halt. London, once teeming with life, now lay deserted—a ghost of its former self.

The fashionable world had scattered like leaves in the wind. The lawyers had ridden off on circuit, the dandies had decamped to Continental spas or Scottish moors, and even the bootmakers, those humble purveyors of polished elegance, had vanished to German gambling tables. The parks were silent, the sermons at St. James’s preached to half-empty pews devoid of notable sinners, and the brass bands of Kensington Gardens had packed away their instruments. Only Clarence Bulbul, chained to his Treasury desk, trotted mournfully through Rotten Row, comparing its emptiness to the vast solitude of the Arabian desert.

As for Pen? Left to his own devices in chambers, he found himself unexpectedly at leisure—his editor, Captain Shandon, having absconded to Boulogne-sur-Mer for the sake of his health, leaving Arthur in charge of the *Pall Mall Gazette*.

Now, for all his claims of being a man *blasé* and weary of life, Pen remained, in truth, a robust young fellow with an undiminished appetite—both for good food and good company. If he couldn’t dine at a club, he was perfectly content in a tavern; if he couldn’t rub shoulders with wits or beauties, he’d make do with whoever crossed his path. He frequented Greenwich steamboats, Hampstead jaunts with his colleague Finucane, and—most notably—the gaslit extravagance of Vauxhall Gardens, where he was on jovial terms with the performers.

It was on one such evening, after penning a particularly brilliant leader (one that even Shandon might have grudgingly approved), that Arthur decided to treat himself to Vauxhall’s fireworks. Ticket in pocket, he strolled across Waterloo Bridge, the stars overhead twinkling in mild competition with the yet-unlit spectacle ahead.

Before the enchanted gates, however, he encountered a scene: Captain Costigan, hat rakishly askew, embroiled in a heated negotiation with the ticket-taker. Two ladies stood beside him—one flustered, the other on the verge of tears.

"Bedad, sir, if ye doubt me honour—" the Captain was protesting, his brogue thickening with indignation.

"Lor, Capting!" the elder lady sighed.

The young one, meanwhile, had turned her face away, her voice trembling. "Never mind, Captain Costigan. I don’t want to go at all."

But her eyes, bright with unshed tears, told another story.

Pen, ever the gallant, stepped forward. "Costigan! Pendennis, remember me?"

The Captain seized his hand with theatrical gratitude. "Me dear boy! Me preserver!"

A quick explanation followed—a lost ticket, dashed hopes, and a girl who had never seen Vauxhall’s wonders. Arthur, incapable of resisting such an appeal, promptly offered his own pass and his purse.

Fanny Bolton—for that was the girl’s name—blushed as she took his arm. She was no society belle, merely the lodge-keeper’s daughter from Shepherd’s Inn, but her dark eyes sparkled with excitement. As they passed through the gates, the Gardens burst before them in a riot of light, and Fanny gasped, clinging to Pen’s sleeve.

"O—law, how beautiful!"

Pen, amused and touched, felt a flicker of his old enthusiasm. "What wouldn’t I give to see it all again for the first time?" he mused.

Costigan, meanwhile, had resumed his grand airs, strutting like a monarch among commoners. He bowed to acquaintances, bellowed *bravo* at singers, and generally comported himself as if the Gardens existed solely for his entertainment.

The evening unfolded in a whirl of music, fireworks, and chance encounters—some welcome (like Foker, glumly wandering as if the world had ended), others less so (such as Huxter, the irrepressible medical student, who invited Pen to a "stunning gin-punch" with all the grace of a man offering a tumbler of ditchwater).

But Pen’s attention remained chiefly on Fanny, whose wonder at every spectacle was as refreshing as it was charming. She was no polished beauty, but there was an artless grace in her delight—a reminder of simpler pleasures.

As the rockets burst overhead, casting showers of gold over the laughing crowd, Arthur Pendennis—cynic, man of the world, self-proclaimed weary soul—found himself, for a brief moment, genuinely happy.

And that, perhaps, was the greatest amusement of all.

Arthur Pendennis awoke with an uncharacteristic spring in his step—no midnight brawl with Costigan, no indignant summons from Huxter—just the rare luxury of a London man greeting the day without the usual dread of ledgers, lawsuits, or literary deadlines. (Though, to be fair, Pen’s "labors" at the *Pall Mall Gazette* were hardly the stuff of martyrdom. Even Pegasus, when hitched to a daily column, becomes a glorified cab horse, more concerned with his oats than his odes.)

Still, Pen’s mind buzzed with the memory of little Fanny Bolton—her artless chatter, her blushes, the way her voice fluttered like a moth around a candle. *What a relief*, he mused, *after the calculated simpers of ballroom Blanche or the yawn-inducing graces of society misses*. That a creature so fresh could sprout in the soot-choked flowerpot of Shepherd’s Inn was a marvel. And old Bows, of all people, teaching her to sing! The thought amused him. Bows—that threadbare philosopher with the heart of a gentleman—was a character begging to be immortalized in print.

Thus resolved, Pen (having no pressing need to feign industry on a Saturday) set off for Shepherd’s Inn, ostensibly to call on Bows, though his boots, his gloves, and the rakish tilt of his hat suggested other motivations.

Meanwhile, in the Bolton household, Fanny had scarcely slept. Visions of Pen—his gleaming shirt-studs, his laugh, the way he’d *almost* kissed her—danced behind her eyelids. Compared to Emma Baker’s cheesemonger or Betsy Rodgers’ puffed-up attorney’s clerk, Arthur might as well have been a prince from the pages of Minifer’s Circulating Library. (Not that Fanny would admit to devouring those three-volume romances. Certainly not.)

So when Pen materialized under the archway, white-hatted and absurdly handsome, Fanny’s heart performed a quadrille in her chest. She nearly bowled over her siblings in her haste to the wicket. Mrs. Bolton, initially wary, soon thawed under Pen’s charm offensive—his inquiries about the undertaker business (!), his theatrical gossip, his *generous* offer of play tickets. (Astley’s! Fanny could already smell the sawdust.) By the time he perched on a child’s chair—*oh, the sacrifice!*—both women were his devoted allies, trading giggles like conspirators.

Then Bows appeared.

The old musician’s face curdled at the sight: Pen, holding court like Macheath among the innkeeper’s daughters.

"Ah, Bows!" Pen boomed, blissfully oblivious. "Just the man I wanted to see!"

Bows’ grip was limp, his smile a grimace. *Plague on him*, Fanny thought—though whether she meant Bows or Pen, even she couldn’t say.

*In which Pen, like many a young fool before him, mistakes a housemaid’s crush for a pastoral idyll—and Bows, like many an old cynic, sees the train wreck coming.*

Arthur Pendennis strode into Shepherd’s Inn with all the swagger of a man who had not a care in the world—or at least, who wished very much to appear that way.

“How d’ye do, Mr. Bows?” he called out, his voice a little too loud, his cheerfulness a touch too forced.

Bows, that gaunt and melancholy figure, responded with a dry nod, while Fanny Bolton—poor Fanny—blushed so violently that one might have mistaken her for a hastily painted tavern sign. The air between them was thick with things unsaid, and the ensuing handshake between Bows and Pen was about as warm as two icicles colliding.

Pen, ever the optimist in his own mind, attempted to lighten the mood. He launched into a spirited retelling of the previous night’s escapades at Vauxhall, complete with an impression of Captain Costigan’s drunken negotiations with the check-taker. Alas, his performance fell flatter than a week-old glass of champagne. Fanny managed a nervous giggle, Mrs. Bolton stared in polite confusion, and Bows—well, Bows looked as though he were mentally composing a funeral dirge.

The silence that followed was so profound one could hear the boot-heels of the loitering ticket-porter clacking against the cobblestones outside.

“You were coming to see me, sir,” Bows remarked at last, his tone drier than a solicitor’s ledger. “Would you care to ascend to my chambers? They’re rather elevated, but—”

“Oh, I’m no stranger to garrets!” Pen interrupted with a breezy wave. “Shepherd’s Inn is positively bustling compared to Lamb Court.”

“Indeed,” Bows said, with the air of a man who had just spotted a particularly stubborn stain on his cravat. “Though I was going to observe—and pray don’t take this as discourteous—that the air three flights up is generally considered more salubrious than that of a porter’s lodge.”

Pen’s candle of indignation flared. “Sir! Am I to understand—”

“You were kind enough to mention an intention to visit my humble abode,” Bows interjected smoothly. “Shall we?” He gestured toward the door with the solemnity of a tragedian delivering his final line.

And so, with a stiff bow to Mrs. Bolton and a lingering glance at Fanny (who blushed anew, as if her cheeks were in some sort of crimson competition), Pen allowed himself to be led away.

---

Upstairs, the atmosphere was no less charged. Captain Costigan, freshly emerged from his quarters and radiating an aroma best described as *eau de whiskey*, greeted Pen with a handshake that threatened to dislocate something.

“Ah, me dear boy!” he boomed, blissfully unaware that he was perhaps the least welcome participant in the room. “Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel have been pressin’ me most urgently to join ’em at Baden-Baden—”

Pen nodded gravely, recalling with some amusement the days when he had regarded the Captain’s tales with something akin to awe. Now, they rang as hollow as a tin drum.

Meanwhile, Bows sat stiffly in his impeccably ordered room—a stark contrast to Costigan’s chaos—his gaze occasionally flickering toward the lithograph of Miss Fotheringay (now Lady Mirabel) above his bed. The irony was not lost on Pen: here was a man who had once pined for an actress, now playing guardian to a girl who had unwittingly caught *his* wandering eye.

The visit concluded with handshakes all around, though Bows—ever the watchful sentinel—followed Pen out, trailing him like a shadow. Pen, irritated, finally turned on him.

“Confound it, must you dog my steps?” he muttered, though he was laughing to himself by the time he reached the Strand—a laugh that tasted faintly bitter in his own mouth.

That evening, in a futile attempt to distract himself, he drifted from the Panorama (too dull) to his club (too quiet) to a comic theatre (too garish). Mrs. Leary’s ankles, which had once seemed the height of allure, now left him cold.

And so, with a sigh and a shrug, Arthur Pendennis returned to his lodgings, his thoughts a tangled mess of guilt, defiance, and the gnawing suspicion that he was making a spectacular fool of himself.

The Temple Garden, once the picturesque backdrop for Shakespeare’s warring roses, had long since been abandoned by fashion. The soot-choked air had even discouraged the hardiest of roses from blooming, not that the lawyers and clerks scurrying past its gates much noticed. The garden’s charm was now the domain of sentimental antiquarians and bookish dreamers—those who could still conjure the ghosts of Sir Roger de Coverley, Oliver Goldsmith hunched in a summer-house fretting over his tailor’s bill, or the great Dr. Johnson lumbering along in his snuff-stained wig, trailed by his wine-flushed Scottish shadow.

Arthur Pendennis, being of a literary and romantic bent, was just such a dreamer. And so, on a tranquil Sunday evening—when the Temple lay deserted, its barristers feasting in Belgravia or Tyburnia, its clerks nursing modest pints at the Club—he found himself wandering the garden’s gravel paths, lost in thought.

The evening was absurdly picturesque. The Thames sparkled, the warehouses glowed rosy in the sunset, and the distant church bells rang as sweetly as those of Clavering, stirring memories of his mother’s arm linked in his on the terrace at home. The whole scene was so painfully idyllic that Pen, overcome by some unnameable emotion, abruptly stood, muttered, *“No, by Jove, I’ll go home,”* and marched straight into two small children.

“*Missa Pendennis!*” squealed one—Betsy-Jane, the bolder of yesterday’s tiny admirers. Her sister, Amelia-Ann, burst into tears when Pen hoisted her aloft in a fit of spontaneous affection. This commotion summoned their attendants: Mrs. Bolton in her finest cashmere shawl and Miss Fanny Bolton, whose eyes lit up like Paper Buildings at midnight.

What followed was a masterpiece of coincidence—the sort that only happens in novels (or, occasionally, to young men who’ve recently mentioned their fondness for evening strolls). Fanny, it transpired, had spent the night voraciously reading *Walter Lorraine*, Pen’s own novel, after a heroic quest through every lending library in the neighborhood.

“*Oh, I do like it so!*” she breathed, cheeks aflame.

Pen, torn between gratification and alarm, stammered promises to send her a copy before fleeing the scene—only to collide with the glowering figure of Mr. Bows.

What ensued was less a conversation than a duel. Bows, the embittered old fiddler, accused Pen of toying with Fanny’s heart. Pen, indignantly aristocratic in his duck trousers and multicolored cravat, retorted that he was hardly a villain from a melodrama. Yet when Bows—his voice cracking—pleaded not as a moralist but as a lonely old man who loved the girl like a daughter, Pen’s defiance crumbled.

By the end, apologies were exchanged, hands were shaken, and Pen vowed solemnly to steer clear of temptation. As Bows trudged away, Arthur shut his door with a sigh, wondering if the bells ringing across the city were laughing at him.

Ah, Clavering—that quaint little hamlet where the grass grows green, the tea kettles whistle, and the tongues wag even faster than the church bells on a Sunday. Our dear friend Pen had the misfortune of hailing from this bucolic paradise, though its society was about as thrilling as a lukewarm cup of Mrs. Pybus’s elderflower cordial.

Among the local luminaries was Mr. Samuel Huxter, a medical student whose visits home were treated with the reverence of a papal audience. Armed with stale gossip from London and the wit of a man who had spent too much time dissecting cadavers, Sam was the life of every party—provided the party consisted of his mother, a few sycophantic neighbors, and a bottle of his father’s questionable sherry.

Then there was Mr. Hobnell, the erstwhile victim of Pen’s youthful pugilism, now a squire of some standing—or at least, standing *enough* to afford a new bonnet for his wife once a year when he ventured to London to "go a bit wild" (which, for Hobnell, meant staying out past eleven and eating an oyster). His union with Miss Sophy Huxter had elevated the Huxter clan to the dizzying heights of minor gentry, a fact Sam never tired of mentioning between rounds at the pub.

Now, it so happened that Pen, in a fit of self-imposed exile from the enchanting Fanny Bolton, had thrown himself into the noble pursuits of literary criticism and solitary brooding. His reviews for the *Pall Mall Gazette* had grown so savage that editors winced before opening his missives. Desperate for distraction, he wandered into the Back Kitchen, that hallowed den of wit and whiskey, where Bows presided over the piano like a melancholic king.

Alas! Who should darken the doorway but Sam Huxter himself, fresh from the theater and brimming with the social grace of a bull in a china shop. Spying Pen, he swaggered over, comrades in tow, and—with the subtlety of a fireworks display—proceeded to poke the bear.

"Jolly night at Vauxhall, eh?" Huxter winked, as if he and Pen had shared a delightful picnic rather than a public spat.

Pen, who had been nursing both his drink and his dignity, fixed him with a glare that could curdle milk. "Have I, at any point, invited your commentary on my affairs?"

Huxter blinked, unaccustomed to resistance. "No need to get shirty, old chap! Only making conversation."

"Then make it with someone else," Pen replied, rising with all the icy grandeur of a man who had just decided he would rather be anywhere else. He exited to the raucous laughter of Huxter’s friends, who found their companion’s social blundering even more entertaining than the evening’s ballad about body snatchers.

Meanwhile, in Clavering, the village rumor mill was churning at full capacity. Sam, never one to let a grudge go to waste, had penned a letter to Squire Hobnell, generously embellished with tales of Pen’s metropolitan debauchery. By the time the news reached Mrs. Pybus, it had mutated into a full-blown scandal involving secret orgies, stolen inheritances, and at least one imperiled milkmaid.

Madame Fribsby, ever the philosopher, sighed into her lace handkerchief. "Men are deceivers ever," she murmured, casting a wistful glance at the portrait of her own long-lost dragoon.

Mrs. Portman lamented the moral decay of youth. The Simcoes nodded gravely, though they had never read a word Pen had written (heaven forbid!). Even Captain Glanders, that boisterous brute, found the whole affair hilarious—until his wife banished him to the stables for his lack of proper outrage.

The climax arrived when poor Helen Pendennis, having received an anonymous letter (because nothing spices up small-town life like cowardly poison-pen missives), rushed to Dr. Portman in a state of near collapse. Laura, ever loyal, insisted it was all lies—but Helen, in her heartbreak, turned on her. "This is your doing! If only you had married him, he wouldn’t have fallen into the arms of some… some *actress*!"

(It should be noted that Helen’s definition of "actress" was roughly synonymous with "agent of Satan.")

Dr. Portman, torn between pastoral duty and the very human urge to indulge in gossip, attempted damage control. "Now, now, my dear Mrs. Pendennis—let us not leap to conclusions. The letter is anonymous! What sort of villain hides behind—"

But Helen was already planning her son’s redemption tour: immediate marriage to the temptress, a swift return to Fairoaks, and a lifetime of repentant hymn-singing. She fainted clean away before she could book their tickets.

And Laura? Ah, Laura stood firm, her heart aching but her spirit unbroken. For if there was one thing Clavering’s rumor mill could not grind down, it was the quiet certainty of a woman who knew the truth—even if no one else cared to hear it.

Thus ended another day in Clavering, where the only thing more relentless than the rain was the collective judgment of its inhabitants.

Doctor Portman’s sternly worded epistle was dispatched to London with all the urgency of a man who had both moral righteousness and a postage stamp to spare. The good clergyman then turned his attention to soothing Mrs. Pendennis, who was in a state of agitation best described as "frenzied maternal despair." He assured her—with the confidence of a man who had never actually met a London temptation—that Pen would surely repent upon reading his letter. Helen, meanwhile, was forbidden by the local apothecary from storming the capital in a fit of maternal fury, which was probably for the best, as London was hardly prepared for a distraught widow wielding a parasol like a cavalry saber.

Alas, Pen never received the Doctor’s sermon on morality, for it languished in his chambers, buried beneath a pile of unpaid bills and half-empty gin bottles. Days passed, and Helen’s anxiety festered like a bad oyster. Laura, ever the saint, bore the brunt of this domestic tempest with the patience of a martyr—though even saints have their limits, and one must wonder if she ever fantasized about locking Helen in a closet just for a moment’s peace.

Pen, meanwhile, had resolved to avoid Fanny Bolton with the determination of a man who knows he really ought to do the right thing—right up until he doesn’t. He threw himself into work, exercise, and general dissipation, which is to say, he smoked, drank, and overthought himself into a fever. There is nothing quite so tragic as a young man’s attempt at self-denial, especially when his idea of discipline is substituting brandy for gin.

Soon, Pen was bedridden, a victim of his own excesses and the general wretchedness of bachelor life. Let it be known that illness in chambers is a fate worse than debtors’ prison. There is no kindly hand to fluff your pillow, no sympathetic ear to hear your groans—just the grim specter of Mrs. Flanagan, your laundress, who alternates between weeping over your condition and drinking your liquor.

Enter Captain Costigan, that gallant old rogue, who arrived like a faded cavalry charge to check on the invalid. He found Pen in a state best described as "romantically disheveled"—unshaven, feverish, and muttering about Vauxhall and Fanny. The Captain, ever the optimist, diagnosed him with "a touch of the passions" and prescribed fresh air, good company, and possibly a duel if his honor required it.

Meanwhile, Fanny—poor, deluded Fanny—was nursing her own heartbreak. Bows, the melancholic musician, had attempted to reason with her, painting Pen as a man bound by society’s chains, unable to marry beneath his station. Fanny, of course, interpreted this as proof of Pen’s tragic nobility rather than his practical cowardice. She wept, she vowed eternal devotion, and she likely drafted several unsent letters declaring her willingness to be his humble servant, his scullery maid, his *anything*, if only he would glance her way once more.

Her mother, ever the pragmatist, assured her that Pen would return. "Men always do," she said, with the confidence of a woman who had seen more than one young fool come slinking back after a fit of conscience.

But Pen did not return. Instead, he lay sweating and miserable, while the world moved on without him. His uncle, Major Pendennis, arrived in town, heard of his nephew’s condition, and—after a brief internal debate about whether fevers were contagious—decided that a shooting party with Lord Steyne took precedence over familial duty.

And so our hero languished, caught between fever and folly, while the women in his life either wept over him or scolded him from afar. Such is the way of young men—forever making grand gestures and then collapsing under the weight of their own dramatics.

Will Pen recover? Will Fanny pine forever? Will the Major ever prioritize something over a partridge hunt?

Stay tuned.

Fanny’s heart lodged itself squarely in her throat as she took in the two women before her. The elder—Arthur’s mother, no doubt—had the same fever-wild eyes as her son, though hers burned with alarm rather than delirium. The younger, a statue in muslin, might as well have been carved from marble for all the warmth in her expression. Mercy? Sympathy? Not a drop. Fanny’s gaze darted to the Major lurking behind them. The old fox had the decency to glance away, though she caught the flicker of his eyelids lifting just enough to inspect her.

“I—I wrote to you yesterday, ma’am,” Fanny stammered, her limbs conducting a silent symphony of tremors.

“Did you?” Mrs. Pendennis’s voice could have frosted a punchbowl. “How fortunate. I’ll take over the nursing of my son now. You may stand down.”

Fanny’s teeth clacked like castanets. “But—you mustn’t go in yet! He’s not—”

A shriek of laughter erupted from Pen’s room, followed by a spirited rendition of what sounded like a tavern ditty, complete with fist-thumping percussion against the wall.

“He’s been like this since yesterday,” Fanny whispered.

Mrs. Pendennis paled but rallied magnificently. “How reassuring. Let me pass.” She swept by, Laura gliding in her wake like an ice floe. The Major brought up the rear, tossing Fanny a look that might have been pity—or perhaps just regret that the show was over.

Left in the passage, Fanny collapsed onto a bench. She’d have laid down her life for Pen, and *this* was her thanks? Not a word, not a glance. Just the crisp rustle of skirts brushing her aside.

When Dr. Goodenough arrived, he found her hunched like a discarded shawl at the door.

“Nurse! How’s the patient?”

Fanny jerked her chin toward the room. “Ask *them*.”

“Ah. The mother, I take it?”

A nod.

“You ought to be in bed yourself, child.”

“Please—just let me stay here. I’ll be quiet as a church mouse.” Her voice broke. “I only want to know if he—”

The doctor sighed, patted her onto the bench previously occupied by Pen’s printer’s devil, and vanished inside.

Mrs. Pendennis had installed herself like a sovereign claiming a throne. Pen’s medicines were lined up with military precision. Her Bible lay open—less for comfort, one suspected, than as a moral barricade. Fanny’s shawl and bonnet had already been exiled to the study table like contraband.

Pen, meanwhile, beamed at his mother as if she’d brought him ices from Gunther’s. “Mother! Capital of you to come!” His chatter was a merry, nonsensical stream—Fairoaks, college, something about a donkey—while Laura stood rigid, each peal of his laughter striking her like a blow.

The Major hummed occasional consolation. It went ignored.

Enter Dr. Goodenough, stage left, to a chorus of relieved sighs. The man might as well have had wings.

“Ah, Doctor!” Mrs. Pendennis clasped his hand. “Tell me there’s hope.”

The doctor delivered the usual sop—youth, strong constitution—then whisked the Major aside.

“Critical,” he murmured. “We must bleed him. And—ah. Who’s the girl outside?”

The Major coughed. “The, er, *little person* who let us in. Uncommonly pretty, if I may say.”

Goodenough’s lips twitched. Even in life-or-death moments, the world insists on its absurdities.

Fanny, now deputized as courier, trudged off with two notes: one for the apothecary (lancet at the ready), the other for the doctor’s servant (elixirs to compound). She returned to find herself gently but firmly scrubbed from the narrative. No more nursing. No more lingering.

Yet for two days, a small, persistent shadow haunted the staircase, intercepting apothecary boys and laundresses for news. On the third, the doctor’s carriage rolled up to Shepherd’s Inn.

“Crisis passed,” he told Fanny. “He’ll live.”

She wept into her hands.

Mrs. Pendennis, in a burst of gratitude, resolved to bestow upon Goodenough the family’s treasure: a silver-gilt vase commemorating some long-ago recovery from scarlet fever. Hippocrates and serpents adorned it. The inscription was florid.

The doctor demurred. “A brother practitioner’s widow? Unthinkable.”

(He did, however, attempt to plead Fanny’s case. The widow’s face at the mention was such that he promptly dropped the subject. Some rivalries, he reflected, were beyond the reach of mandragora—or even Dr. Locock.)

Thus ended the crisis. Pen lived. Fanny faded. And life, as it tends to do, rolled on.

Let us not mince words, dear reader—our hero, Arthur Pendennis, had fallen victim to that most humiliating of human conditions: a full recovery. Having endured the melodramatic throes of fever, the indignity of leeches, and the horror of a shaved head (a fate worse than death for any self-respecting dandy), he awoke to find his grand passion for Miss Fanny Bolton had vanished as thoroughly as his feverish delusions.

Yes, the great love affair was over. The mighty flame that had once scorched his heart now barely warranted a shrug. He lay in bed, marveling at his own absurdity. What had he seen in her? She was neither the wittiest, nor the most elegant, nor even the prettiest girl in London. The truth was, the passion had never truly resided in *her*—it had been a figment of his own overheated imagination, now as defunct as yesterday’s gruel.

Ah, but there is nothing quite so mortifying as realizing you’ve made a fool of yourself for no lasting reason. Pen, ever the philosopher, dissected his expired infatuation with clinical detachment. He felt no guilt, only a mild amusement at his own expense. After all, he *had* resisted temptation at its peak—a fact he now congratulated himself upon with the smugness of a man who has narrowly avoided stepping into a puddle.

Meanwhile, Helen Pendennis hovered over him with the relentless devotion of a mother who believes her son has narrowly escaped perdition. Every sigh, every spoonful of broth, was administered with the solemnity of a priestess performing sacred rites. And Pen, ever the dutiful (if occasionally ungrateful) son, basked in it. Who wouldn’t? There is no comfort quite so luxurious as being fussed over by a woman who has convinced herself you’re a miracle of survival.

Of course, there remained the delicate matter of Fanny. The poor girl had nursed him through the worst of his illness, only to be relegated to the status of a mildly embarrassing anecdote. Helen, ever vigilant, had already tried and convicted Fanny in the court of maternal suspicion. Why bother with evidence when a mother’s intuition is so much more efficient? The mere fact that the girl had been near Pen was proof enough of her wicked designs.

Across the Channel, Captain Shandon—ever the astute literary critic—remarked to his long-suffering wife that Warrington’s hand had reclaimed the *Pall Mall Gazette*. "The man writes like a butcher who’s studied anatomy," Shandon declared, waving his glass for a refill. "Precise, brutal, and always drawing blood." Mrs. Shandon, who understood none of this, nodded agreeably and poured another round.

Back in London, Warrington had thrown himself into Pen’s work with the zeal of a man who enjoys a good fight. His reviews were savage, his essays incisive, and his admiration for Pen’s own style embarrassingly public. "The boy has a finer touch than any of them!" he boomed to Helen, who swelled with maternal pride despite her lingering suspicions about her son’s moral character.

And then there was Laura. Sweet, steadfast Laura, who had arrived in London armed with enough tearful anecdotes about Pen’s childhood virtues to fill a three-volume novel. She and Helen had reached an understanding—one of those delicate feminine treaties in which everything is said without actually being spoken aloud. Laura, having confessed her love for Pen (a revelation that surprised precisely no one), now moved through the world with the quiet resignation of a saint who has already chosen her martyrdom.

Enter George Warrington—rough, brilliant, and entirely unaccustomed to the company of women who didn’t view him as either a boor or a bank account. Laura, with her gentle sincerity and accidental wit, was a revelation. They walked in the Temple Gardens. They talked. They even—heaven forbid—laughed. The Major, ever the matchmaker (provided the match didn’t interfere with his own convenience), encouraged these outings with the enthusiasm of a man who has just discovered a new use for his spare time.

As for Pen? He lounged, he convalesced, he accepted the offerings of sherry and jellies sent by the Marquis of Steyne (via the Major’s embellishments) with the air of a prince receiving tribute. Warrington made speeches. Laura played the piano. The Major lost at cards. And in the lamplit streets below, a pale girl in a black bonnet sometimes paused to listen to the music drifting from the windows before being led away by a shabby old gentleman.

Life, as it always does, moved on. But for now, in this quiet interlude, there was warmth, and music, and the unspoken understanding that some convalescences are of the heart as much as the body.

(And if you dare suggest that any of this implies rebellion against the natural order of master and servant, I shall deny it vehemently. I am, as ever, a firm believer in the sacred tradition of domestic tyranny. Let there be no confusion on that point.)

Good Helen, ever since her son’s illness, had assumed the role of his personal secretary, caretaker, and—let’s be honest—unofficial censor. Buttons were sewn, stockings darned, and letters intercepted with the efficiency of a seasoned spy. If Arthur’s drawers contained any secrets, they were now at the mercy of maternal scrutiny. And if among his laundry lurked a certain Fanny Bolton’s correspondence? Well, the postman’s knock had been silenced (the brass knocker removed for the sake of the patient’s nerves), and all missives, whether from creditors or admirers, passed through Helen’s trembling hands.

Pen, ever the free-spending gentleman, had his share of unpaid bills—tailors, hatters, and bootmakers all clamoring for settlement. Helen, whose frugality bordered on parsimony, dipped into her modest savings to settle these trifling debts. Many a young man would gladly surrender his correspondence at such a price. A clear conscience, after all, is the only thing that makes the postman’s knock a welcome sound.

Yet one letter weighed heavier than the rest. Helen, with all the subtlety of a detective, had plucked it from the pile—a timid, scrawling thing addressed to *Arthur Pendennis, Esq.* She hadn’t opened it. Not technically. But it burned a hole in her pocket all the same.

At Richmond, while Arthur and Warrington idled on the terrace, Helen cornered Major Pendennis. "Brother," she whispered urgently, "what do you know of this—this *Fanny Bolton* affair?"

The Major’s face puckered into an expression that could have been a smile or a grimace. "My dear Helen," he said smoothly, "I know nothing, and I wish to know nothing. Young men will be young men. If you think our boy is a—"

"*Do not say it!*" Helen cut in, her voice quivering with indignation.

The Major, ever the diplomat, bowed. "I did not begin this conversation, my dear. But if you insist on agitation, allow me to advise you: if you’ve suppressed any letters, you may provoke a quarrel with Arthur that will outlast this little… indiscretion."

Helen’s resolve crumbled. "There was only one letter," she confessed, producing the offending article. "Only a few words—poorly spelled—begging to see him."

The Major, suppressing a laugh (for the letter was indeed a masterpiece of bad grammar and melodrama), took it gingerly. "Ah. A heartbroken plea from a lovelorn girl. Hardly evidence of villainy."

"But if he has wronged her—"

"*Wronged her?* My dear, he took her to Vauxhall Gardens, not the altar. The girl’s father is a brute, her mother insists on her innocence, and Arthur was delirious when she visited his chambers. There’s no scandal here—only a tempest in a teapot."

Helen wavered. The Major pressed his advantage. "If you confront him, he may, in a fit of chivalry, offer marriage. Is *that* what you want?"

The widow paled. The Major had struck home.

"Then let us *distract* him," he proposed. "A tour abroad—the Rhine, Switzerland, anywhere. Change of scene, new faces. Time, my dear, is the great healer of all youthful follies."

Helen, desperate for any remedy, seized upon the idea. "Yes! We must go—immediately!"

Meanwhile, on the terrace, Pen was outlining his grand literary plans to Warrington. A novel! A tragedy! Warrington, ever the skeptic, laughed—until Pen began declaiming lines with such fervor that passersby turned to stare.

Laura Bell arrived, fresh from a visit to Lady Rockminster, bearing flowers and game. The Major, ever gallant, roused himself from a doze to compliment her roses. Laura, unimpressed by his flattery, turned the conversation to Helen’s failing health.

"She is worn out," Laura said gravely. "Dr. Goodenough fears for her."

The Major nodded sagely. "Arthur’s illness, and certain *other anxieties*, have shaken her." He glanced meaningfully at Laura, who flushed.

(*Ah, the old fox knows how to twist the knife,* she thought.)

But the plan was set. They would travel. Arthur, informed of the scheme, embraced it with the enthusiasm of a schoolboy released from lessons. His mustaches, grown in anticipation of Continental sophistication, were, alas, an unfortunate shade of red—but no matter.

Warrington alone declined. "I’ve no money for touring," he said bluntly.

Pen protested, but Warrington was firm. "Someone must mind the shop."

And so the stage was set. Helen, clutching her savings (and, perhaps, still that unopened letter), prepared to whisk her son away from temptation.

As for Fanny Bolton? Her occupation, it seemed, was well and truly gone.

Helen Pendennis had developed a new, entirely scientific theory: the moment her son Arthur regained his strength, his unfortunate infatuation with Fanny Bolton would flare up like a poorly managed grease fire. Though she hadn’t uttered the girl’s name since her conversation with the Major, Helen’s surveillance of Arthur had reached levels that would make a private investigator blush. Under the flimsy pretext of his "delicate health," she scarcely let him out of her sight, intercepting his mail with the efficiency of a seasoned spy.

Arthur, meanwhile, eyed each letter at the breakfast table with the nervous energy of a man expecting a bomb. His mother’s gaze never wavered from her teacup, but he could *feel* it—like a hawk tracking a particularly dim-witted rabbit. Would today be the day Fanny’s handwriting appeared? His heart performed acrobatics at the mere thought. And yet, day after day, no letter came. Was he relieved? Annoyed? It was hard to say. Breaking things off cleanly might have been the sensible thing, but since when has love (or whatever this was) ever been sensible? No, the human heart prefers a messy, drawn-out collapse—like a slow-motion carriage wreck.

Pen, still too weak to walk half a mile without resembling a wilted lettuce, was nonetheless deemed unfit for unsupervised carriage rides. Helen, however, had no authority over George Warrington, who one morning announced he had business in London. She *knew*. Of course she knew. Warrington was going to sniff out news of Fanny like a bloodhound with a vendetta.

And so he was. Arthur had confessed the whole sordid tale—his near downfall, his miraculous escape, and his lingering sense of obligation. Warrington, who had seen enough of life’s disasters to last three lifetimes, nodded gravely. "It’s hard to struggle, Arthur, and easy to fall," he said. "The best courage is to run away."

"Spoken like a man who’s run from something himself," Pen observed.

Warrington’s face darkened. "Let’s just say I know what wreckage looks like."

Meanwhile, in the labyrinthine underbelly of London, another drama unfolded. Mr. Huxter, aspiring surgeon and amateur mimic, had become a regular at the Back Kitchen, where he entertained his cronies with spot-on impressions of one Captain Costigan—a man whose dignity was inversely proportional to his blood-alcohol level. The Captain, blissfully unaware of his role as comic relief, held court with tales of his daughter’s triumphs and his own military (and alcoholic) exploits. Huxter, ever the opportunist, milked the act for all it was worth.

One morning, Huxter—fresh from a night of revelry at Vauxhall—spotted the Captain swaying down Henrietta Street like a ship in a storm, pursued by a pack of jeering street urchins. Seizing the chance for both charity and entertainment, Huxter swooped in, offering his arm. The Captain, after some slurred protests, finally accepted, and Huxter guided him through the maze of alleys to Shepherd’s Inn, where Costigan promptly tripped on an orange peel and went down like a felled oak.

Huxter, now in full medical mode, diagnosed a compound fracture (with possible whisky complications) and installed himself as the Captain’s physician. This gave him a splendid excuse to loiter around the Inn, where he soon ingratiated himself with the Boltons—particularly Fanny, who looked as though she’d been through a wringer. Huxter, oblivious to the finer points of her distress, showered her with jests, toys for her siblings, and an alarming amount of unsolicited cheer.

Mrs. Bolton, never one to keep a scandal to herself, soon filled Huxter in on *the incident*—painting Pen as a villain of Shakespearean proportions. Huxter, already nursing a grudge against Arthur, was all too eager to believe it. That is, until Bows set him straight with the *actual* story, at which point Huxter sheepishly retracted his threats to punch Pen’s head.

Not that it mattered. Fanny, lost in her own private misery, barely noticed Huxter’s antics. Bows, ever the tragic spectator, watched with a bitterness only years of unrequited affection can cultivate. "Of course," he muttered to himself. "The moment I care for someone, someone younger and handsomer comes along."

When Warrington arrived as Pen’s emissary, Bows greeted him with the warmth of a man who’d long ago given up on pleasant surprises. "Ah," he said dryly. "Let me guess—you’re here about Fanny. Tell me, does the great man wish to toss his handkerchief her way again? Or has his family decided she’s suffered enough?"

Warrington, to his credit, didn’t flinch. He delivered his message—thanks for the aid during Pen’s illness—and endured Bows’ sardonic retorts with the patience of a saint. Or at least a man who’d dealt with worse.

And so the wheels of fate turned, grinding hopes and hearts alike. Somewhere in the mess, Fanny stirred her gruel, Bows nursed his grievances, and Pen—blissfully unaware of the chaos in his wake—pondered the mysteries of life, love, and why his mother still wouldn’t let him check his own mail.

Major Pendennis, ever the man of his word (provided it cost him nothing), had dutifully assured Warrington that young Arthur’s unfortunate entanglement with the gatekeeper’s daughter was well and truly severed. Pen himself, after a brief sulk over the blow to his vanity, consoled himself with the knowledge that Miss Fanny was unlikely to perish of a broken heart, sparing him any messy consequences.

Thus liberated, the entire party embarked on their grand Continental tour—Arthur Pendennis, gentleman of leisure, traveling with Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell, while George Warrington, Esq. (aged 32, height 6 feet, hair black, beard equally so, etc.), secured the necessary passports and shepherded the group from Dover to Ostend. From there, they meandered through Bruges and Ghent before descending upon Brussels and eventually the Rhine.

Now, we shall not bore the reader with the usual travelogue—Laura’s wide-eyed rapture at the Gothic splendor of cathedrals, Helen’s horrified fascination with Catholic excess (barefoot friars! jeweled saints! people *dancing* on Sundays!), or Pen’s general air of detachment, as if the whole affair were a mildly tedious dream. Suffice it to say, the widow clutched her prayer book like a shield against popery, while Laura, ever observant, noted the growing shadows in Helen’s eyes. The poor woman was tormented—had she lost her son’s heart? Was there some secret chamber within him now locked against her?

Meanwhile, Warrington—ever the self-sacrificing bachelor—played courier, translator, and general factotum with surprising enthusiasm. He fetched *Galignani’s*, arranged donkey rides, and even endured the Kurhaus band’s relentless waltzes, all while stealing glances at Laura, who remained oblivious to his silent pining. (A tragic figure, our George, doomed to admire from afar while Pen, the oblivious oaf, lounged about recovering his appetite and his waistline.)

Enter Major Pendennis, rejuvenated by a stay at Lord Steyne’s estate (where he had regaled the company with scandalous anecdotes and made himself indispensable). Having exhausted the pheasant population, he now descended upon Rosenbad, impeccably dressed and trailing his long-suffering valet, Morgan. The Major, ever the social strategist, lost no time in introducing Pen to a Russian princess (whose past was as colorful as her rouge) and other assorted nobility, while Helen shuddered at the gambling tables and extracted a promise from her son never to play. (Pen, to his credit, kept his word—though the Major sighed at such provincial morality.)

Then came the bombshell—a letter from Fanny Bolton, discovered among Pen’s forwarded mail. The poor girl, heartbroken and bewildered, pleaded for some token of remembrance, some shred of kindness. Pen, incensed, confronted his uncle, who denied all involvement with the air of a man who very much *had* been involved. Realizing the culprit could only be his mother, Pen stormed out, leaving the Major to mutter about "fat in the fire" and Helen’s impending meltdown.

Back at their lodgings, Pen raged to Warrington, vowing vengeance on whoever had driven Fanny away. Warrington, ever the voice of reason (or at least restraint), pointed out that attacking one’s own mother was poor form—even if she *had* behaved abominably. But Pen, in high dudgeon, was beyond listening. The stage was set for a spectacular family row, and the curtain was about to rise.

Our poor widow—with the tireless assistance of Martha, who clucked over the German oddities like a perplexed hen—had arranged a modest feast in honor of Major Pendennis’s arrival. Yet only the Major and his two younger companions partook, for Helen pleaded illness, and Laura, ever loyal, kept her company. The Major, blissfully oblivious (or perhaps strategically so) to the storm brewing in the silence of his dining companions, chattered away like a man determined to outtalk discomfort.

By evening, Helen emerged, leaning on Laura, her face veiled by the fading light so Arthur couldn’t see the pallor of grief etched upon it. She moved to Pen, whom she had avoided all day, and clasped him in a trembling embrace. Her fingers were clammy, her voice a whisper. Pen, already a cauldron of resentment, stiffened.

*She plays the martyr while she persecutes me,* he thought bitterly.

"You look ill, my boy," Helen murmured.

"I’ve had letters from London," Pen snapped, his chest tight. "One, in particular, has wounded me more than any before. It seems my correspondence has been *intercepted*—and a woman who has shown me nothing but devotion has been cruelly cast out. By *you*, Mother."

Warrington lunged forward. "For God’s sake, stop! Can’t you see she’s—"

"Let him speak," Helen gasped.

Laura whirled. "Oh, by all means, Arthur—finish her!"

Pen’s temper flared. "You call *me* cruel? It’s *you* who’ve hounded me with suspicions, who’ve scorned anyone beneath your precious station! Well, I’ll tell you what I’ll do—I’ll return to that girl you drove away and bring her back as my wife. Let your pride choke on *that*."

Helen swayed, her breath shallow. "Arthur, do you mean—?"

Laura clamped a hand over her mother’s wrist. "Hush. Let him dig his grave."

The Major, puce with outrage, sputtered, "By Gad, sir, if you mean to disgrace the Pendennis name by shackling yourself to a scullery maid, then *wash my hands of you*! My brother Jack would sooner have died than—" Here his voice cracked, and to everyone’s shock, the old man wept.

Pen’s fury faltered. Warrington, seizing the lull, stepped into the dim light. "Perhaps it’s time I shared my own tale," he said quietly. "A *cautionary* one."

And so he did—of a boyhood infatuation, a disastrous marriage to a woman who’d never loved him, and the ruin of his name. "A *gentleman* can’t marry beneath his station without paying for it," he finished grimly. "Pen, your mother acted harshly, but not without reason."

Helen, trembling, suddenly surged forward. "Bless you, Mr. Warrington!" she cried, seizing his hands. Then she turned to Pen, her eyes alight. "Arthur—*you never meant it*! You’d never truly go back to her!"

Pen, bewildered by her joy, found himself folded into her arms. "*Forgive me*," she sobbed. "*I was wrong—thank God, I was wrong!*"

Later, in the quiet of her room, Helen clung to him. Moonlight silvered her face as she whispered, "You’ll *never* see her again?"

Pen swore it—though Helen, in a twist of guilt, insisted, "*You must, to make amends.*"

Then, as the clock struck nine—the hour she’d once knelt with him in childhood prayer—they murmured the Lord’s Prayer together. Helen’s voice faded first. "*Amen*," she breathed.

And then, softly, she was gone.

Fairoaks stood silent, its heart extinguished. The cottage gossips clucked—*such a pity, but the widow’s money’ll keep young Arthur awhile*. Laura alone wandered the empty rooms, tracing the ghost of Helen’s touch.

Pen, hollow-eyed, fulfilled his mother’s last wishes: a pension for Fanny Bolton (delivered with Warrington’s dry remark that *banknotes make poor balm*), a diamond ring for Laura, and a silver pot for Dr. Portman.

And as for Helen? The world scarcely noted her passing—save for those few who’d loved her. But in the hush of Fairoaks, her absence was a roar.

**"Nothing shall change in your room,"** Pen told Laura. **"It’s yours. Always."**

Laura, clutching letters from her long-dead father—found among Helen’s things—whispered, **"Yes."**

For Helen had loved *both* their ghosts. And now, they were hers to tend.

The great English festival—where all of London abandons decorum for the thrill of Epsom Downs—had gathered a curious assortment of our story’s characters. Among them, in a carriage that strained under the weight of both its occupants and Mrs. Bungay’s finery (she was dressed, as one might say, in the entire contents of a haberdasher’s shop), sat the modest Mrs. Shandon, whose friendship the publisher’s wife guarded with the tenacity of a bulldog with a bone. Nearby, Mr. Bungay, having fortified himself with a lunch of heroic proportions, was hurling sticks with the reckless abandon of a man who had forgotten he possessed a bald head. Shandon, meanwhile, lurked among the gipsies and ale-sellers like a disgraced prophet, while the ever-faithful Finucane attended the ladies like a guardian who had misplaced his charge.

Enter Mr. Archer, a man whose chief talent was knowing everyone worth knowing—or at least claiming he did. With a bow that suggested he had practiced in front of a mirror, he regaled Mrs. Bungay with a stream of aristocratic gossip.

"Yonder sits the Prime Minister," he announced, as if he had personally arranged the seating. "His lordship advised me to back Borax, but I fancy Munmeer—superior breeding, you see." He then proceeded to point out dukes, ambassadors, and other lofty personages with the confidence of a man who had dined with them all—or at least stood near them at a public function.

"The Chinese Ambassador," he continued, lowering his voice as if sharing state secrets, "once dined at my table. Brought his own cook, the savage. One day, while I was out, the brute butchered my wife’s poor Blenheim spaniel—stuffed it with forced meat and served it as a second course!"

Mrs. Bungay gasped. "Law!"

"My wife was inconsolable. The Ambassador hasn’t dined with us since—though the Lord Mayor rather enjoyed the dish. Tastes like duck with green peas, if you can believe it."

"I can’t!" cried Mrs. Bungay, who was now thoroughly scandalized and delighted in equal measure.

Archer, warming to his theme, gestured toward a lady in blue. "That’s Lady Flamingo—rumored to be marrying the Ambassador and moving to Pekin. She’s binding her feet for the occasion, though I doubt she’ll manage it. My wife has the smallest foot in England—child’s size—but it’s nothing next to a Chinese lady’s."

Mrs. Bungay, ever eager for more gossip, then inquired about the carriage where Mr. Pendennis was seated. "He’s grown quite grand, hasn’t he? Still in mourning, I see."

"Eighteen hundred a year in land, twenty-two thousand in the funds," Archer declared, as if reading from Pendennis’s bank ledger. "His uncle, the Major, had me tie it up—can’t have him squandering it all on ducks and drakes."

Finucane, unimpressed, snorted. "That’s not Lord Rochester—it’s Tom Staples from the *Morning Advertiser*."

Archer blinked. "Is it? Well, I’m terribly short-sighted. That other fellow with the opera-glass? Lord John, of course. And the tall one? Sir James."

"You only know ’em from seeing ’em in the papers," Finucane muttered.

Archer ignored him. "The Duke of Hampshire never misses a Derby. Told me just yesterday he’s been to sixty-five of them—first attended on a piebald pony with the Prince of Wales. Missed only two: once for measles at Eton, once for Waterloo."

Mrs. Bungay, now thoroughly dazzled, could only gape.

Meanwhile, atop a battered stagecoach, a rowdy assembly of "gentlemen" (loosely defined) were bellowing their support for the race. When Podasokus triumphed, one particularly exuberant soul—known only as "the Colonel"—leapt up, nearly collapsing the roof in his ecstasy.

"Supper for ten, lads!" he roared. "Damn the expense!"

His companions, a collection of dubious characters who looked as if they’d been assembled from the backrooms of disreputable taverns, cheered—though several were already calculating how best to relieve the Colonel of his winnings before the night was out.

Nearby, in a humble cab, sat a far more wholesome party: a fresh-faced girl, her lobster-devouring mother, and their jovial escort, Mr. Sam Huxter. Sam, having spent the journey firing off jokes at every passerby, was now the picture of gallantry—until Arthur Pendennis happened upon them.

Fanny Bolton’s face drained of color. Mrs. Bolton babbled nervously. Sam’s fists clenched inside his pockets.

Arthur, ever the gentleman, affected not to notice the tension. "Thirsty work, the races," he remarked, accepting a glass of lukewarm beer with the grace of a man used to far finer vintages.

As he drank, a vision in dove-colored silk passed by—Blanche Amory, arm-in-arm with Captain Strong. She shot Pen a mischievous glance, while Fanny’s gaze followed her with wistful admiration.

"Who’s that?" Fanny breathed.

"A neighbor of mine," Arthur said airily. "Lady Clavering’s daughter."

Fanny’s imagination immediately wove a tragic romance—one in which she was *not* the heroine, and Sam Huxter most certainly was not the hero.

When Arthur departed, the sun seemed to dim for poor Sam. His once-jovial mood soured. Even the pea-shooter he’d brought for amusement lay forgotten.

But Fanny, ever the diplomat, coaxed him back to good humor with smiles and flattery. By the time they returned home, Sam was once more her devoted swain—though not before a passionate reconciliation involving tears, declarations, and a hasty embrace that left Mrs. Bolton flustered.

As for Arthur? He rejoined Blanche’s circle, where she teased him mercilessly about his "little country friends."

"*Je l’ai vue*," she said with a smirk. "*Elle a de bien beaux yeux. Vous êtes un monstre!*"

Pen laughed. "Monster? Hardly. She’s well protected—by her mother *and* her sweetheart."

Blanche’s eyes glittered. "One never knows what might happen when a girl has a mind of her own—and a monster like you pursuing her."

Pen’s smile faltered. There was something unsettling in her tone—something that made him think of Laura, and how differently *she* would have spoken.

But the moment passed. The races went on. And life, as it always does, carried its players forward—whether they were ready or not.

Nearly a year has slipped by since we last checked in on our dear Arthur Pendennis—a year in which he has undergone transformations more remarkable than a caterpillar’s. The black coat of mourning has been cheerfully discarded for the dashing blue of a Cornet in the Clavering Yeomanry Cavalry, and his wig, that melancholy concession to premature baldness, has been pensioned off entirely. His hair, though somewhat sparse, now braves the public gaze once more, and he has even enjoyed the singular honor of being presented at Court by none other than the Marquis of Steyne—an event orchestrated by his ever-ambitious uncle, the Major, who insisted that no gentleman should delay such an essential ceremony.

The Major, it must be said, has been rather vocal about his nephew’s financial disappointments. Pendennis’s inheritance, once optimistically rumored to be substantial, has settled at a modest fifteen hundred a year—enough to command a modicum of respect from publishers (who now bow slightly lower) and mothers of plain daughters (who calculate eligibility with ruthless precision). After all, if the dazzling Myra is destined for an earl, why shouldn’t poor, lopsided Beatrice make do with a literary gentleman of tolerable means?

One such mother, Mrs. Hawxby, made a valiant effort to ensnare Arthur during a country-house visit, deploying Beatrice as bait—first with billiards lessons, then with exclusive pony-carriage privileges. Alas, the Major, ever the vigilant guardian, swooped in to rescue his nephew before vows could be extracted, though not before Mrs. Hawxby declared Arthur a heartless cad.

Meanwhile, the Clavering household has been a spectacle of genteel misery. Lady Clavering, whose grammar is as unreliable as her husband’s morals, sighs over her lavish dinners, lamenting, “I’d rather eat a turnip in peace than a pineapple with this racket!” Sir Francis, that perennial disappointment, has managed to lose seven thousand pounds on the Derby—an achievement that even his most forgiving creditors find impressive.

Enter Major Pendennis, the self-appointed savior of the family’s affairs. Having pried into every closet (literal and metaphorical) of the Clavering estate, he now dispenses advice with the confidence of a man who knows where the skeletons are buried. He gently reminds Lady Clavering that her daughter Blanche has been shortchanged in favor of her wastrel son and suggests—oh so casually—that a union between Blanche and Arthur might be mutually beneficial.

“Think of it,” he urges. “Arthur has ambition, wit, and a tolerable seat on a horse. With your influence, he could rise in Parliament. Why let Sir Francis waste a seat he never uses?”

Blanche, for her part, views Pen with the mild approval of a shopper considering a satisfactory but unexciting purchase. The Major, ever practical, assures her that convenience is the surest foundation for matrimony. “Love-matches,” he sniffs, “usually end with the wife running off to Switzerland with someone else’s husband.”

And so, our hero drifts into a sort of romantic limbo—neither engaged nor entirely free, fond of Blanche but not feverishly so. It’s a far cry from the grand passions of his youth, but then again, time has a way of sanding down the sharp edges of desire.

Are we not all, dear reader, just the same souls who once dreamed grandly beneath nursery ceilings? Whether we now stand triumphant on the deck of success or cling to some wreckage of our hopes, the child within remains—sometimes laughing, sometimes weary, but always, unmistakably, ourselves.

Our long-suffering Begum, initially furious at her husband’s latest bout of deception and recklessness, refused to bail Sir Francis Clavering out of his gambling debts and declared she would separate from him, leaving him to face the consequences of his own folly. After the disastrous Derby day, the disgraced baronet was in such a state that he avoided everyone—his turf companions, whom he owed money, and his wife, who had long been his reluctant banker.

When Lady Clavering inquired the next morning if Sir Francis had returned home, she was informed he had not, though a messenger had been sent for his clothes and letters. Strong, anticipating a summons from his desperate friend, soon received a note begging him to visit "Mr. Francis" at Short’s Hotel in Blackfriars. The Baronet, ever the gentleman of convenient falsehoods, had fled rather than face his troubles.

The Boots of Short’s establishment, who delivered Clavering’s message to Grosvenor Place, quickly deduced the identity of the mysterious guest and relayed the news to the household. Soon, every servant knew Sir Francis was hiding under an alias at a Blackfriars inn. His coachman spread the gossip to other gentlemen’s servants, and before long, the rumor reached Tattersall’s, where bets were already being placed on whether Clavering would flee to the Levant.

By that afternoon, the Baronet’s study was littered with unpaid bills—from the French cook, the tradesmen supplying her ladyship’s table, and even Miss Amory’s extravagant millinery accounts. Strong, sifting through the pile with a grimace, was intercepted by Mrs. Bonner, Lady Clavering’s fiercely loyal housekeeper.

"She won’t pay another penny," Mrs. Bonner snapped. "Tell Sir Francis he can rot in that tavern for all we care. We’ve made him, bailed him out, even put him in Parliament—and this is his gratitude? No more. Our money stays ours."

Yet when Strong met with Lady Clavering, he found her wavering. He painted a pitiable picture of the Baronet—weeping, remorseful, terrified of disgrace. "Bind him to promises if you must," Strong urged, "but don’t abandon him. Think of your son’s name."

Mrs. Bonner scoffed. "Promises? He’ll break ’em by week’s end."

Meanwhile, at Short’s Hotel, Clavering alternated between self-pity and desperation. "I’m ruined, Strong," he moaned, clutching a glass of curaçoa. "If Pendennis knows—if anyone knows—I’ll hang myself!"

Strong, unmoved, retorted, "Then stop whining and behave like a man for once."

The Baronet’s debts were settled quietly, but his reputation sank further. Lady Clavering retreated to Tunbridge Wells, leaving him to skulk around London, dodging creditors and drowning his shame in drink. His only refuge was Strong’s chambers, where he played at being a jovial host, though his laughter rang hollow.

Altamont, flush with Derby winnings, played the generous rogue—buying trinkets for servants, treating old Costigan to dinners, and even sending Miss Amory a hideously ornate writing desk. Yet beneath the bravado, tensions simmered.

"You’re a fool to trust Clavering," Strong warned Altamont. "One day, he’ll turn on you."

Altamont merely grinned. "Let him try."

When Clavering, penniless and whining, begged Altamont for a loan, the latter toyed with him mercilessly. "Twenty pounds? Only if you sign a promissory note for fifty."

"Anything!" Clavering cried. "I’ll pay it—I swear!"

Altamont laughed. "Relax. Here’s the twenty. But remember—I own you now."

And so, amid debts, deceit, and desperation, the players in this farce carried on—each clinging to their illusions, each one step closer to ruin.

A short time after Colonel Altamont’s miraculous windfall at Epsom—which, let it be noted, was won not by skill but by sheer, dumb luck—the gallant gentleman embarked on his grand Continental tour. The society pages, ever eager to document the movements of the *ton*, reported his departure with typical understatement:

*"Among the distinguished company aboard the Soho to Antwerp last Saturday were Sir Robert, Lady, and the Misses Hodge; Mr. Serjeant Kewsy (who, we are assured, is every bit as dull as his title suggests); Mrs. and Miss Kewsy (who, alas, are no livelier); and Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, & Co. (who, we suspect, drank the ship dry)."*

The Colonel traveled in the style befitting a gentleman—or at least, a gentleman who had recently come into money. His wardrobe was resplendent, his stomach impervious to the sea’s treachery (unlike poor Serjeant Kewsy, who reportedly turned "a shade of green not found in nature"), and his faithful Irish valet trotted behind him, looking as though he still couldn’t believe his master had two pennies to rub together.

Before setting sail, Altamont treated his old chum, Captain Strong, to a farewell feast at Blackwall—a feast paid for, naturally, by Altamont himself, now that fortune had smiled upon him.

"Strong, my boy," the Colonel bellowed between gulps of brandy, "if you’re strapped for cash, now’s your moment! I’m flush, I tell you—flush as a tick on a fat dog! Name your sum!"

Strong, with the dignity of a man who has borrowed too often to borrow again, declined. "No, no, my dear fellow—I’m perfectly solvent. Well, not *solvent* enough to repay you just yet, but solvent enough to scrape by without further humiliations."

They parted as friends—or as near to friendship as two men of their respective characters could manage. Later, Strong mused to himself:

*"Here’s a man who’s been a convict, a scoundrel, and a general liability—yet he’s ten times the gentleman Clavering is. Clavering wouldn’t know honesty if it bit him on the ankle. And what’s Altamont’s great crime? Signing his father-in-law’s name? Pah! Half the House of Lords has done worse before breakfast!"*

Still, Strong refused to dip into Altamont’s newfound generosity again. Some lines, once crossed, cannot be uncrossed—especially when one’s pride is the only thing left intact.

---

Meanwhile, back in Shepherd’s Inn, Strong found that life without his disreputable companion was… *quieter*. Too quiet. Gone were the days of shared brandy, shared schemes, and shared disgust at Clavering’s latest folly. Now, the Chevalier was left to his own devices—which, tragically, included *painting his own boots* and *broiling his own mutton chops*. A grim fate indeed for a man of his refined tastes.

He briefly considered reuniting with his estranged wife, the fiery Spanish Juanita (who had once brained him with a dish in a fit of conjugal enthusiasm), but thought better of it. Some wounds, even those of the heart, are best left un-reopened.

---

As for Sir Francis Clavering, his fortunes remained as dismal as ever. The "pony" he’d extracted from Altamont had vanished faster than a politician’s principles—lost on a dubious boxing match in Essex, where his chosen pugilist had the poor taste to *kill* his opponent in the thirteenth round.

"Damme, Strong!" Clavering wailed. "Three-to-one odds, and I was *certain* of thirty pounds! Now Lightfoot’s dunning me for fourteen, and my lady won’t part with a farthing. Can’t you find *somebody* to discount a bill for me?"

Strong, with the stern resolve of a man who has finally hit rock bottom (and brought a shovel), refused. "Not a penny, Sir Francis. I’ve sworn off your schemes—and if you try to swindle Lady Clavering again, I’ll be the first to warn her."

This noble declaration was met with a torrent of curses, a vow of eternal vengeance, and a slammed door. (Clavering, it must be said, had a flair for the dramatic when thwarted.)

---

Elsewhere in Shepherd’s Inn, young Sam Huxter—erstwhile walnut thief of Clavering, now aspiring medical man—was making a name for himself as Sir Francis’s latest admirer. He gushed about the Baronet’s "old English gentility," much to the amusement of Fanny Bolton, who knew better.

"*Borrowed* half a sovereign off you, did he?" Fanny laughed. "Mar’s been owed seven shillings since Whitsuntide, and he looks the other way when he sees her!"

Sam, ever the stickler for grammar, corrected her speech while Fanny corrected his judgment—an equitable arrangement, if ever there was one.

Reader, you may wonder: *Why all this philosophizing in a tale of rogues and rascals?*

But consider—when a man inherits wealth, does he not also inherit the sycophants? The tenants who once doffed caps to his father now doff them to him. The wine in his cellar improves with age, and so, presumably, does his self-importance.

And yet, let us not judge young Pendennis too harshly for enjoying his good fortune. Grief fades, hats turn from black to white, and life, inexorable life, marches on.

(Though if *you* ever find yourself broiling mutton chops in a garret, take heart—even the great Napoleon had his off days.)

Major Pendennis had recently acquired enough scandalous intelligence about the Clavering family to make even his scheming old heart hesitate. The idea of saddling his nephew Arthur with a wife whose fathers-in-law were a rogue’s gallery of disreputable characters was hardly an advantageous match. One of these paternal specimens, if spotted in public, would likely be sprinting toward the nearest border before the law could lay hands on him—assuming he didn’t trip over his own disgrace first. The other? Well, let’s just say his presence was about as welcome as a skunk at a garden party.

Lady Clavering, bless her naive heart, had already poured a fortune into the bottomless pit of her husband’s debts, and if she kept it up, her heirs would inherit little more than a title and a mountain of IOUs. Miss Amory, for all her charms, came with a family tree that had more than one suspiciously gnarled branch.

Yet the ever-resourceful Major Pendennis saw another angle. And so, after much maneuvering—more elaborate than a quadrille at a royal ball—he finally cornered Sir Francis Clavering for a little chat.

Tracking down the Baronet was no easy feat. A man drowning in debt has a remarkable talent for vanishing, reappearing only in the most obscure corners of London—cheap taverns, dingy gambling dens, anywhere his creditors wouldn’t think to look. The Major’s first attempt to lure him out involved an invitation to a "quiet dinner among friends" at Greenwich. Sir Francis, smelling a trap, declined at the last minute, leaving Pendennis to dine alone with his dignity and a plate of cold whitebait.

Undeterred, the Major tried again, requesting a brief meeting. Sir Francis agreed with suspicious promptness—*"Four o’clock tomorrow, Bays’s, PRECISELY."* (The underlining was aggressive, as if to suggest punctuality was his greatest virtue.) Of course, four o’clock came and went, and no Clavering. The man had all the reliability of a weathervane in a hurricane.

Meanwhile, Strong—poor, loyal Strong—had discovered that Clavering had already broken his latest vow of financial sobriety. Another bill had been signed, another debt incurred, with the ever-helpful Mr. Abrams facilitating the transaction. The Baronet’s signature was becoming as common—and as worthless—as a tavern’s promise of "fresh" oysters.

When Strong confronted Pendennis about it, the Major feigned ignorance with the skill of a seasoned diplomat. "Altamont? Oh, that fellow! I’ve heard the name. Can’t say I know him. But if you suspect he’s blackmailing Clavering, well, that’s between them. Discretion, my dear Strong, discretion!"

Strong, unimpressed by this performance, left in a huff. Pendennis, now intrigued, turned to his valet Morgan—who, it turned out, had been quietly amassing a small fortune by investing in Clavering’s debts.

"Morgan, my good man," the Major began, "I hear you’ve been dabbling in finance. Buying up Sir Francis’s bills, eh?"

Morgan, ever the picture of humble servitude, admitted to it. "A modest venture, sir. One must make the most of one’s opportunities."

The Major nearly choked on his own astonishment. His *valet* was a financier? And apparently a shrewd one at that. The world truly was upside down.

After some prodding, Morgan revealed that he had insider knowledge—thanks to his master’s own loose lips—about Lady Clavering’s willingness to settle her husband’s debts. He had been buying up Clavering’s IOUs at a discount, waiting for the next payout.

Armed with this information, Pendennis finally tracked down Sir Francis in the back room of a disreputable tavern, where the Baronet was nursing a drink and his wounded pride.

"Ah, Clavering," the Major said cheerfully. "Fancy meeting you here. I’ve just been hearing about your latest financial masterpiece."

Sir Francis scowled. "If you’re here to lecture me, save your breath. My affairs are none of your business."

"Oh, but they are," Pendennis replied smoothly. "You see, when you drag your family’s name through the mud, it becomes everyone’s business. And I’ve taken a particular interest in ensuring your charming stepdaughter doesn’t suffer for your sins."

Clavering groaned. "What do you want?"

"A little honesty would be refreshing. But since that’s in short supply, let’s settle for a promise: no more bills, no more schemes. Or next time, I might just let the wolves have you."

With that, the Major tipped his hat and strolled out, leaving Sir Francis to his drink and his regrets.

Meanwhile, Morgan—ever the opportunist—was already calculating his next investment. After all, in London’s high-stakes game of debt and deception, there was always money to be made. Especially when fools like Clavering kept playing.

On a charming common near Tunbridge Wells, Lady Clavering had installed herself in a delightful villa—a retreat necessitated by certain *disagreements* with her husband at the close of an unfortunate London season. Naturally, Miss Amory accompanied her mother, and Master Clavering, home for the holidays, provided Blanche with her chief occupation: quarreling. Not that the boy minded much—home amusements were dull compared to cricket, horses, and the society of other thirteen-year-old gentlemen, who descended upon the Begum’s villa in alarming numbers. They devoured tarts, overindulged in champagne, raced imaginary steeds across the lawn (to the horror of their hostess), and occasionally smoked themselves into queasy disgrace, rendering the dining-room uninhabitable for Blanche.

Now, Blanche, being a creature of refined sensibilities, found the company of young gentlemen who still considered belching an art form rather tiresome. Change, however, was her delight—poverty, a cottage, even a night in a dungeon with only bread and water would have charmed her for a week, provided it was *novel*. Tunbridge, therefore, suited her well enough. She wandered the woods, sketching trees with more enthusiasm than accuracy. She devoured French novels. She attended every play, ball, or traveling conjurer Tunbridge Wells offered. She slept excessively. She quarreled with her mother and Frank each morning before seeking fresh diversion—sometimes the village school, where she alternated between smothering the girls with affection and undermining the poor schoolmistress, then scolding both for their deficiencies.

And, of course, she went to church—a darling little Anglo-Norman *bijou*, built approximately yesterday and lavishly adorned with painted windows, gilded scripture, and saints’ heads that looked suspiciously like the local squire’s relatives. Here, Blanche set to work on an exquisitely high-church altar cover, securing for herself a fleeting reputation as a saint. The clergyman, poor man, was thoroughly taken in—at first.

This clergyman was none other than our old friend Smirke, once tutor to Pen and hopeless admirer of Helen. Since those days, he had consoled himself with a bride from Clapham (selected by his late mother) and developed ecclesiastical tendencies that would have horrified her. Collars were shorn, hair grew apostolically long, neckties abandoned. He fasted on Fridays. He read Roman Hours. He *hinted* at hearing confessions. In short, he was the most harmless of men, yet Muffin of the Dissenting chapel declared him a Jesuit in disguise, while Mr. Simeon Knight at the old church denounced him as a Papist infiltrator.

Smirke had built his chapel-of-ease with his mother’s legacy. One shudders to imagine her ghostly outrage at the candlesticks now gracing the *altar* (formerly a table), or the letters he signed on the Feast of Saint Whoever. Yet none of this was done with malice—merely earnest enthusiasm.

Then came Blanche.

For two blissful weeks, Smirke was convinced she was an angel. Her singing! Her piety! Her *soul*! His wife, Belinda, paced the churchyard, eyeing the two vacant plots beside their children’s graves and contemplating a third—or perhaps a nunnery. The parishioners whispered of Romish plots. Blanche, meanwhile, poured on the charm like syrup—then, abruptly, lost interest. She rapped a child’s knuckles. She yawned during sermons. Smirke, bewildered, concluded she was perhaps *not* a celestial being after all. Belinda rejoiced. Blanche forgot them both entirely.

Enter Arthur Pendennis.

Smirke, still nursing bruised ideals, gushed about Blanche’s *precious soul* and *heavenly music*. Arthur, amused, observed his old tutor’s transformation—the long hair, the ecclesial airs, the genuine kindness beneath the absurdity. And Blanche? Blanche was delighted to have a visitor who brought London gossip and didn’t reek of tarts. She laughed at his jokes. She sang his verses. She sighed over moonlit gardens.

They walked. They reminisced. They discussed Laura (*dearest Laura!*). Blanche was all sentiment and sisterly affection—until, of course, she wasn’t.

One evening, under a particularly poetic moon, Arthur smoked at his window. From a nearby casement, Blanche’s voice sighed, *“Oh, heavenly!”*

Pen laughed. “Don’t report me for smoking.”

“I adore it!” she declared, then—remembering *les moeurs*—retreated with a flutter.

The next day, they rambled through Penshurst’s park, Blanche’s laughter ringing like chapel bells. Smirke, watching her instruct village children, marveled at her patience. Arthur marveled too.

“Do you truly love the country?” he asked.

“I never wish to see London again,” she sighed. “Here, one’s thoughts bloom like wildflowers—you know, the kind that *die* in London.”

“Then why sigh?”

“Because now that you’re here, I shall miss you when you leave.”

Cue *Mes Larmes*.

Ah, the rural idyll! Two jaded London souls, playing at Phyllis and Corydon among the roses, each convinced—for a moment—that the other was the romance they’d been missing. How many marriages, one wonders, begin in such fleeting folly?

Arthur Pendennis, though ordinarily frank with his friend Warrington, found himself curiously evasive when it came to the subject of Blanche Amory. He spoke freely enough of his old tutor Smirke, his wife, and their abrupt departure from Clapham to Rome—yet when questioned about Blanche, his replies grew vague. She was, he admitted, clever and good-natured, perhaps even wife material under the right guidance—but as for marriage? No, his romantic days were behind him. He was content as he was.

Meanwhile, dainty letters arrived at Lamb Court, sealed with elegant ciphers. Had Warrington been nosier—or had the cipher been crackable—he might have noticed Arthur’s correspondence bore the initials *B.A.* Pen replied with gallant wit, town gossip, and likely verses, for Blanche’s muse rhymed obligingly with *branch*, *stanch*, and *launch*. Indeed, some of Pen’s love poems—later published in *Lady Violet Lebas’s Roseleaves Annual*—had first been sent to Blanche, adorned with Pinkney’s illustrations of society ladies.

"Verses are harmless enough," Major Pendennis remarked, catching Pen scribbling one such effusion at the Club. "Correspondence between old friends is natural—so long as you don’t *commit* yourself, my boy. Never write a letter you can’t disown. I’ve dodged more traps than you’ve had hot dinners." The Major, growing garrulous, regaled him with cautionary tales of men ruined by ill-considered letters—young Spoony, entrapped into marrying a widow twice his age; Louisa Salter, whose love notes cost her Sir John Bird.

Pen laughed, though he reddened—perhaps recalling his own past folly with the Fotheringay. "Don’t worry, sir. I learned my lesson." The Major beamed, mistaking evasion for prudence.

Had Warrington been consulted, he’d have warned that half-hearted gallantry was worse than boyish sincerity. But Pen kept silent, knowing his friend would scorn his maneuvering.

Meanwhile, misfortune struck Strong. After refusing Altamont’s loan—a noble but costly gesture—creditors descended like vultures. Tradesmen, once trusting, now brandished unpaid bills. Sheriff’s officers lurked at Shepherd’s Inn, forcing Strong into siege tactics: barricading his door, peering through curtained windows at furious duns. Allies like Huxter and Fanny Bolton smuggled supplies, signaling with coded whistles. His boldest escape? A leap across the water-pipe to Costigan’s window—a route he’d shown Altamont but wisely concealed from the perpetually indebted Captain.

Just as despair set in, salvation arrived: Altamont, fresh from Baden-Baden luck, paid off Strong’s debts. Over claret at Richmond, the trio celebrated. "To good fellows!" Altamont toasted, though his gaze lingered oddly on Pen. "May every man find a friend like Strong."

Later, Pen recounted the tale to Blanche, emphasizing Fanny’s devotion and Altamont’s intervention. Blanche, piqued, teased: "Jealous, Mr. Pendennis? Did Fanny wound your pride by choosing Huxter?"

Pen shrugged. "Perhaps. Or perhaps I dislike stories where love ends in compromise—where Psyche’s quest concludes with a tobacco-stained apothecary."

"Life *is* compromise," Blanche retorted. "Or do you still believe in fairy-tale endings?"

"Believe? No. But I’ve stopped pretending." He took her hand. "You’re not the tallest woman in the world, nor I a hero. But you’re clever, you sing beautifully—and I hope you’ll be good company."

Blanche arched a brow. "*Merci*. What a thrilling proposal."

"Honest, at least," said Pen. "I’ve had enough of illusions."

The great house at Clavering Park had never been a cheerful place, even in its better days, but now, under the reign of Sir Francis Clavering, it had sunk into a state of gloom worthy of a penny dreadful. The bankrupt baronet, having exhausted London’s patience (and credit), had retreated to his ancestral halls, where he wandered through a handful of rooms like a disgraced specter, shooting game he couldn’t afford to eat and counting the hours until he could escape again. His sole attendants were the housekeeper and her assistant—Lightfoot, his former valet, having defected to Lady Clavering’s service and, in a twist of romantic economy, married her maid, Mrs. Bonner, securing a tidy nest egg and a snug future as landlord of the Clavering Arms.

Pen, ever the opportunist, arrived in September under the pretense of a friendly visit, though his true purpose was to secure the borough seat his uncle had all but purchased for him. Sir Francis, predictably, was more interested in Pen’s purse than his company, and within days of his arrival, Arthur found himself subsidizing the baronet’s “urgent business” in Chatteris—which, judging by the racetracks and billiard halls he frequented, was of a most pressing nature.

Left to his own devices, Pen embarked on a charm offensive that would have made a seasoned politician blush. Gone was the haughty young dandy Clavering remembered; in his place stood a man who shook hands like a pump handle, laughed at farmers’ jokes, and feigned interest in dissenting preachers with the dedication of a zealot. He dined with factory partners, flattered the wives of Liberal clergymen, and even endured a house call from the local apothecary, Mr. Huxter, after a conveniently timed “indisposition.” Old Pendennis would have swelled with pride at such hypocrisy.

Yet beneath the performance, Pen was not entirely at ease. Walking home one evening, he paused on the bridge over the Brawl, struck by the sight of Fairoaks in the distance—its windows alight, the old piano within still faintly audible in his memory. The past tugged at him, whispering of simpler days when his ambitions were pure and his heart unjaded. But sentiment was a luxury he couldn’t afford, not with an election to win.

Meanwhile, in London, Blanche Amory—ever the restless spirit—had dragged poor Mrs. Bonner on a shopping expedition, ostensibly to buy farewell gifts before the maid’s nuptials. Blanche, with her flair for dramatic generosity, selected a Bible for Bonner (who could hardly read) and a dictionary for her mother (who never would). But her true errand was a whim: a visit to Shepherd’s Inn to bestow charity (and a few cast-off dresses) upon Fanny Bolton, the humble girl who had once nursed Arthur.

Blanche’s arrival at the dingy lodgings was a spectacle in itself—her silk skirts brushing against scrubbed floors, her parasol a beacon of frivolity in the grim hallways. But her charitable mission was derailed by an encounter far more shocking: the sudden reappearance of her long-lost (and very much alive) father, the rogue Amory, who burst from his rooms like a specter from a bad novel, embracing his shrieking daughter and sending Mrs. Bonner into hysterics.

Captain Strong, ever the diplomat, swiftly barred the door against curious neighbors (including the indignant Costigan), while Amory, utterly unfazed, doused the women with eau-de-Cologne as if reviving them from a melodrama. The afternoon’s planned river excursion was forgotten, Blanche’s benevolence toward Fanny abandoned, and the secret of Amory’s resurrection left hanging—like one of Blanche’s unfinished verses—in the air.

Thus ended a day that had begun with electioneering and ended in familial chaos, proving once more that in the world of Pendennis, even the best-laid plans could unravel at the tug of a scandal.

While Arthur Pendennis busied himself with schemes of political ambition in his home county, word reached him that Lady Rockminster had descended upon Baymouth like a well-dressed hurricane, bringing with her none other than Laura Bell—his dearest, most inconveniently virtuous friend. At the news of her arrival, Pen felt a pang of guilt sharper than a tailor’s needle. Laura, the living legacy of his saintly mother, was the one person whose good opinion he truly coveted. How, then, would she react to his latest masterpiece of self-interest—his calculated engagement to Blanche Amory and his eager scramble for a parliamentary seat?

The thought of facing Laura’s clear, judgment-free gaze (somehow more terrifying than any tribunal) left him sweating like a schoolboy caught fibbing to his tutor. He dispatched a letter to Baymouth, stuffed with witty asides, exaggerated affection, and the sort of playful sarcasm that clever men use to disguise their own discomfort. Yet for all his polished prose, Pen couldn’t shake the feeling that he was a fraud—a man who had traded his principles for a title and a rich wife.

Laura, meanwhile, had flourished under Lady Rockminster’s eccentric patronage. The dowager, a woman who treated politeness as a personal insult, had dragged Laura through the finest salons of Europe, where she acquired all the necessary accomplishments—singing, dancing, and the subtle art of not rolling her eyes when addressed by a pompous marquis.

What made Laura remarkable, however, was not her skill at the pianoforte but her baffling, unshakable cheerfulness. While others might crumble under the whims of a capricious patroness, Laura met every slight with a smile, every snub with gratitude. Even Lady Rockminster’s long-suffering maid—a woman who had endured four decades of verbal abuse—eventually surrendered to Laura’s charm, nursing her through a fever in Paris while muttering, *“If that Amory girl were half as kind, the world would be a better place.”*

By the time Pen arrived in Baymouth, Laura had conquered the household. The chef wept when she praised his soufflé. The footmen, normally as expressive as marble statues, got drunk in celebration of her recovery from illness. Even Lady Diana Pynsent—who had once declared Laura “too good to be interesting”—admitted grudgingly that the girl was, in fact, *“rather agreeable.”*

Pen, expecting to find Laura cowed by Lady Rockminster’s tyranny, was instead met with a radiant young woman who bounded down the stairs and embraced him with alarming enthusiasm. (The footmen, trained in the art of aristocratic indifference, did not so much as blink.)

“Arthur!” she exclaimed, holding him at arm’s length. “You look terribly guilty. Have you been gambling? Or is it just the usual political scheming?”

“I—well—that is—” Pen stammered, uncharacteristically flustered.

Laura laughed. “Oh, don’t worry. I already know about Blanche. And Parliament. And the fact that all of Clavering assumes you’ll be married before the year’s end.”

Pen gaped. “You *know*?”

“Of course! Blanche wrote me the sweetest letter. She’s clearly *madly* in love with you.” (The skeptical arch of Laura’s eyebrow suggested otherwise, but Pen chose to ignore it.) “I’ve already planned everything—I shall be the devoted aunt, spoiling your children while Blanche dazzles London. It’s all perfectly settled.”

Something about her easy acceptance nettled him. Was his grand romantic pursuit so transparently transactional that even Laura saw through it?

No sooner had Pen begun to relax than Lady Rockminster swept in, fixing him with a glare that could curdle milk.

“So,” she announced, “you’re marrying that Amory girl.”

“It’s not yet—”

“Nonsense. Everyone knows. And while we’re at it, why on earth would you choose *her* over Laura?”

Pen choked. “I—that is—Laura is like a sister—”

“Bah! Men are all the same. Cowards, the lot of you. In my day, we eloped with whomever we pleased. Now you all marry for money and then complain about it.”

Laura, ever the peacemaker, intervened. “Arthur *must* marry Blanche. Who else would tolerate his dreadful speeches in Parliament?”

This, at least, coaxed a grudging smirk from Pen.

Later, as they rode across the downs, Pen fell into a brooding silence. The sight of Fairoaks—now leased to strangers—struck him with melancholy. “We’re all selfish,” he muttered. “Even Mother, in her way, wanted glory—if only through *me*. And now the world forgets her as quickly as it will forget us.”

Laura, plucking absently at the honeysuckle he’d given her, sighed. “If you despise the world so much, Arthur, why chase its prizes? Why not stay here, write books, raise children, and be happy?”

Pen scoffed. “You make it sound simple.”

“Isn’t it?”

For once, the silver-tongued orator had no reply.

**Final Thought:** *If only life’s choices were as clear as Laura’s conscience—but then, where would the fun be in that?*

Any regular patron of the Wheel of Fortune public-house—where, as you may recall, the illustrious "Confidentials" Club convened, and where Sir Francis Clavering once engaged in a rather tense discussion with Major Pendennis—would know that the establishment boasts three ground-floor chambers for patrons, in addition to the bar where the proprietress holds court. One is a common parlor, another is reserved for liveried servants, and the third, boldly labeled *Private*, is the sanctum of the aforementioned Confidentials, of which Messrs. Morgan and Lightfoot were esteemed members.

Now, Morgan—a man whose silence was as profound as his cunning was deep—had recently been privy to a most illuminating exchange between Major Pendennis and Captain Strong. This conversation had set his mind whirring like a well-oiled pocket watch, and so, when the Major subsequently visited the Wheel of Fortune, Morgan saw fit to station himself in the Confidentials’ room. By a happy accident of architecture, a certain corner of this chamber allowed one to overhear nearly every word uttered in the neighboring parlor. And as the discussion between Pendennis and Clavering was conducted at a volume most ungentlemanly, Morgan found himself in possession of the entire scandalous affair.

*"He recognized Altamont instantly in Sydney! Clavering’s no more married to her ladyship than I am! Altamont’s the man—a convict, no less! Young Arthur slithers into Parliament, and the old Major promises not to expose the farce. By Jove, what a serpentine old rogue! No wonder he’s so keen to shackle Blanche to Arthur—why, she’s worth a hundred thousand if she’s worth a penny, not to mention the parliamentary seat she’ll bring as a dowry!"*

Had anyone been observing Mr. Morgan at this moment, they would have witnessed a most extraordinary transformation of countenance—eyes alight with avarice, lips curled in a smile of pure, predatory delight.

*"If not for my age and society’s confounded prejudices,"* he mused, inspecting himself in the mirror, *"damme, James Morgan, you might’ve married the girl yourself!"*

But since matrimony was out of the question, Morgan resolved to monetize this intelligence instead. The secret, after all, was a veritable goldmine. Sir Francis Clavering, whose fortune hung in the balance, would pay handsomely for silence. Colonel Altamont, whose neck might well depend on discretion, would surely contribute. And as for that insufferable young upstart Arthur, preening as if he were a duke—well, he’d empty his coffers to keep the world from learning he’d wed a convict’s daughter and bought his seat in Parliament with blackmail.

Lady Clavering? If she wished to be rid of her husband, she’d pay. If she feared for her son, she’d pay just the same. And Blanche—ah, Blanche would surely reward the man who restored her birthright.

*"Damme,"* Morgan concluded, surveying the hand fate had dealt him, *"with cards like these, James Morgan is a made man. Every one of ’em will cough up. And with what I’ve already salted away, I can retire, bid the Major farewell, and live as a gentleman—with a servant of my own, by God!"*

Yet, despite these intoxicating prospects, Morgan exhibited remarkable self-restraint. He continued to perform his duties with robotic precision—polishing boots, curling wigs, enduring the Major’s tirades—all while his mind teemed with schemes.

Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, that fading ornament of society, had spent the autumn migrating between country houses like a disgruntled ghost. The Duke was abroad, the Marquis in Scotland, and so the Major was reduced to imposing upon minor baronets and plain squires. The world, he noted bitterly, was no longer what it had been. The men of his era were dead or doddering, and the new generation scarcely knew his name.

*"The breed is gone,"* he lamented. *"Replaced by cotton-spinners and sanctimonious curates. I’m an antique, a relic—and they laugh at me."*

His return to London in late October was marked by a series of petty misfortunes. The chimneys in his lodgings smoked abominably. His gout flared. His soup at Swindon had been an atrocity. He’d left his umbrella in a railway carriage. At Bays’s Club, he endured the company of the three most insufferable men in London: old Hawkshaw, who coughed like a consumptive seal; Colonel Gripley, who monopolized the newspapers; and Jawkins, who insisted on recounting every inn bill from his Continental tour.

And then, of course, there was Morgan.

That evening, as the Major returned to his lodgings in a state of profound irritation, Morgan—who had been fortifying himself with Mrs. Brixham’s brandy—was in no mood for subservience.

When Pendennis cursed him for smelling of liquor, Morgan’s restraint snapped.

*"Drunk, am I? A beast, am I? I’ve served you for twelve years, you pompous old relic, and I’ll not take another word of your abuse!"*

The Major, stunned by this revolt, seized a knife from the table. *"Get out,"* he hissed.

But Morgan merely laughed. *"Oh, I’ll go—but not before you settle accounts. This house? Mine. The furniture? Mine. Every stick but your pitiful traps. I’ve bought it all, Major, with money you’ll never see. You think me a dog? Then know this—your dog has teeth."*

And with that, Morgan stalked out, leaving the Major in a state of bewildered fury.

Alone by the fire, Pendennis simmered. He would need a new valet—a dreadful prospect at his age. Morgan had been *perfect*: his boot-varnish unrivaled, his mutton-broth sublime.

*"Confound the man,"* he muttered. *"The best servant in England—and now I must replace him."*

Just then, a timid knock interrupted his brooding. Mrs. Brixham, the landlady, stood trembling in the doorway.

*"Oh, sir,"* she wept, *"I am ruined! Morgan owns everything—even the portraits of my children. He has destroyed me."*

The Major sighed. *"My dear Mrs. Brixham, I fear I must leave your lodgings. There’s no help for it."*

And so, as the fire crackled and the hour grew late, Major Pendennis found himself adrift—outmaneuvered by his own servant, and painfully aware that the world had moved on without him.

The next morning, Major Pendennis’s shutters were thrown open by none other than Morgan—his soon-to-be-former valet—who entered with the same grave, unreadable expression as ever, bearing the Major’s freshly pressed clothes, shaving water, and the gleaming silver implements of his daily toilette.

“Ah, it’s you,” the Major observed from his bed. “Just so we’re clear—you’re not being taken back.”

Morgan, with the dignity of a deposed monarch, replied, “I ’ave no intention of returning to your service, Major Pendennis, nor to any man’s. But as you are still under my roof, I shall see to your comfort as befits a gentleman.”

And with that, he laid out the Major’s dressing-case with ceremonial precision, stropped the razor to a murderous edge, and performed his duties with the air of a man attending a funeral—his own, perhaps.

Once finished, he cleared his throat with ominous gravity. “Considerin’ you may require a respectable replacement, I took the liberty of engaging a young man last evening. ’E is ’ere.”

The Major, still horizontal, arched an eyebrow. “How very thoughtful.”

“He ’as moved in the first circles,” Morgan assured him. “Valeted for young Lord Cubley abroad. Delicate chest—can’t abide Scotland. Name’s Frosch.”

“Charmed, I’m sure,” the Major drawled.

Morgan stiffened. The Major’s politeness was somehow more infuriating than outright abuse. “Shall I send ’im in?”

“By all means. And Morgan—be a good fellow and *leave*, would you?”

Morgan withdrew, bristling. The Major’s excessive civility was worse than a slap.

Breakfast was a silent affair. The Major, now armed with a cane, oversaw the packing of his belongings—few enough, for a man who lived as lightly as a dandelion seed. His library consisted of:

- One Bible (his mother’s).
- A road book.
- A calf-bound copy of *Walter Lorraine* (his nephew’s novel, which he had never read).
- The Duke of Wellington’s *Despatches*.
- Various portraits of generals, sovereigns, and consorts (mostly gifts he had forgotten to return).

The Slavey—so called by Morgan, though known to Mrs. Brixham as “Betty”—dusted and packed under Morgan’s baleful eye. The man was a silent storm cloud, withholding his thunder but promising rain.

When the moment came, Morgan presented his accounts with the solemnity of a tax collector. The Major paid without comment.

Then, leaning in, Morgan lowered his voice. “Now, between men—I ’ave information.”

The Major feigned indifference. “Have you indeed?”

“Valuable information,” Morgan pressed. “About a certain *baronet* who ain’t as married as ’e lets on. And ’is lady? Married to another.”

The Major’s pulse ticked up, but his face remained impassive. “Ah. Eavesdropping, were we?”

Morgan smirked. “A servant ’as ’is ways, same as a gentleman. And this information—well, it could be worth somethin’.”

The Major’s jaw tightened. “Blackmail, then.”

“Call it *insurance*,” Morgan corrected. “Five hundred a year, and I stay quiet. Otherwise—”

“Otherwise?”

“Otherwise, I smash Clavering. Smash ’is marriage. Smash your nephew’s prospects. And you along with ’em.”

The Major’s hand drifted toward his desk drawer. “You forget yourself, Morgan.”

Morgan’s eyes flickered to the drawer. “Try it. I’ve a policeman below.”

The Major laughed—a dry, rattling sound. “This old thing?” He drew out a petite double-barreled pistol. “Hasn’t been loaded since the Siege of Seringapatam.”

Morgan shrieked like a scalded cat and bolted for the window. “MURDER! POLICE!”

The Major sighed and beckoned the constable upstairs. “This drunken fool’s been stealing from me. We’ll search his trunks.”

Morgan paled. Among his possessions were:

- Several fine shirts (borrowed indefinitely).
- A gold-headed cane (misplaced by the Major in ‘42).
- An opera-glass (conveniently forgotten).

The Major let him squirm, then relented. “Sign this confession, and we’ll call it even.”

The document was a masterpiece:

> *I, James Morgan, having enriched myself under Major Pendennis’s employ, confess to theft, lies, and general villainy. I return Mrs. Brixham’s extorted funds and declare myself a scoundrel.*

Morgan balked. “I’d sooner die.”

“Then die,” the Major said pleasantly. “Or sign.”

Morgan signed.

With his trunks loaded, the Major tipped his hat to Mrs. Brixham, who blessed him through tears. Morgan, glowering from the window, received a mocking salute in return.

And so the Major departed—not with a whimper, but with the quiet satisfaction of a man who had just checkmated a blackmailer without spending a penny.

That evening, Arthur Pendennis arrived at the house to find Morgan lounging like a landlord—which, in fact, he now was.

“Uncle out?” Arthur asked.

“Major Pendennis ’as left,” Morgan intoned. “Permanently.”

Arthur blinked. “Where’s he gone?”

“Cox’s Hotel. Jermyn Street.” A pause. Then, with eerie calm: “Might I ’ave a word with you, sir? On a matter of… mutual interest?”

Arthur, amused, agreed.

And thus did Morgan, outmaneuvered by the Major, turn his gaze upon the nephew—with schemes yet untold.

Our dear friend Arthur Pendennis had only just arrived in London—a fleeting visit, you understand—having deposited some fellow travelers at their West End hotel before scurrying back to the sun-deprived (but not entirely cheerless) confines of Lamb Court. Freedom, after all, is a passable substitute for sunlight in the Temple, and though Templars may grumble, they do relish their liberty.

Pen’s manservant informed him that Warrington was also in residence, so Arthur promptly bounded up the stairs to his old friend’s chambers, where the familiar scent of pipe smoke and the rustle of newspapers greeted him like a well-worn glove.

Their reunion was conducted in the traditional British manner—brusque, unsentimental, and carrying beneath its gruff exterior enough warmth to melt a lesser nation’s heart.

“Well, young one!” said Warrington, glancing up from his papers.

“How are you, old boy?” replied Pen, extending a hand.

And thus, with all the poetic flourish of two men discussing the weather, did these two souls acknowledge a friendship that had weathered months of separation.

(Contrast this, if you will, with the effusive embraces of the Continent—*Ce bon coeur! Ce cher Alphonse!*—or the mustachioed kisses of German comrades. But no, the Englishman saves his raptures for his diary.)

They exchanged the usual pleasantries—Pen inquired after Warrington’s Swiss travels, to which the latter merely grunted about the inferiority of continental tobacco until Strasbourg. (Never mind the Alps; the man’s soul had been stirred, but he’d sooner discuss a decent smoke than admit it.)

Then it was Pen’s turn to recount his own recent misadventures—boredom in the country, the interminable slog of canvassing, and now a brief London respite before subjecting himself to the mortal tedium of Tunbridge Wells.

“And the seat in Parliament?” Warrington prodded.

“Oh, that’s settled,” said Pen airily. “Clavering retires when Parliament reconvenes, and I shall slip into his shoes like a well-oiled footman.”

“Under which banner? Liberal? Tory? Or are you striking out as an independent nuisance?”

Pen waved a hand. “Politics are all the same these days. I haven’t enough acres to be a Protectionist, nor enough hypocrisy to pretend I’d be one if I did. I’ll support Government—mostly—and perhaps dabble in a bit of reform. Sanitary measures, colonial policy, that sort of thing.”

Warrington smirked. “Ah, so we’re to be the enlightened aristocrat? Lecturing the working man by day, dining with Ministers by night?”

“I make no grand claims,” said Pen. “I shan’t pose as a Moses descending with divine wisdom. If I see a good cause, I’ll back it. If a decent opportunity arises, I’ll take it. No raptures, no revolutions—just a practical man making his way.”

“And after ambition,” Warrington mused, “comes love. How fares *that* campaign?”

Pen sighed dramatically. “I suppose I must marry sooner or later. Blanche is amiable enough—sings nicely, tolerates my smoking, and comes with a respectable fortune. My uncle assures me the Begum will be generous.”

“So we are to follow the money?”

“Life *is* a transaction, George,” said Pen, with a philosopher’s resignation.

A pause. Then Warrington, ever the tactician, ventured: “And Laura?”

Pen’s face darkened. “Laura,” he said, voice dropping, “is the finest creature God ever placed on this earth.”

Warrington studied him. “Took you long enough to realize that.”

“Doesn’t everyone recognize their blessings only once they’re lost?” Pen retorted bitterly.

The two lapsed into silence, each lost in thought—Pen in melancholy reflection, Warrington in quiet sympathy.

At length, duty called. “I must report to my uncle,” Pen declared. “The Major’s getting impatient—wants to see me settled with a wife, a seat, and an heir before he departs this world.”

“How touching,” Warrington deadpanned. “The old rogue dreams of dynasty even as he clings to his Pall Mall armchair.”

Pen grinned. “Well, the ‘new blood’ I’m introducing isn’t exactly aristocratic. Blanche’s lineage features ship captains, adventurers, and a baronet of dubious repute.”

“Ah, but every family needs its black sheep,” said Warrington. “Adds spice to the pedigree.”

Refreshed, scented, and resplendent in a freshly tied cravat, Pen prepared to depart.

“Off to dazzle some unfortunate ladies?” Warrington inquired, eyeing his friend’s meticulous grooming.

“Dining with Laura and Lady Rockminster, actually. They’re in town—brought them up from the country.”

Warrington’s eyebrows lifted. “Indeed?”

“Come along,” Pen urged. “The old dragon claims she’s tired of me already—says she’d rather hear *your* stories.”

After minimal persuasion (and a brief internal struggle with his better judgment), Warrington agreed.

And so they set off, two unlikely companions—one polished to a shine, the other comfortably rumpled—bound for Jermyn Street, where further complications (and perhaps redemption) awaited.

Dinner was in full swing when Arthur arrived, earning himself a scolding from Lady Rockminster for his tardiness. But Laura, ever observant, caught the pallor of his face and the tremor in his hands.

"You look dreadful," she whispered. "What’s happened?"

Arthur downed a glass of sherry in one gulp. "Extraordinary news," he muttered, glancing at the servants. "Later."

Throughout the meal, he fidgeted like a man awaiting execution.

"For heaven’s sake, stop drumming your feet!" Lady Rockminster snapped. "You’ve nearly crushed Fido and upended his cream. Observe how Mr. Warrington sits—like a civilized person."

By dessert—which felt interminable—their hostess had reached her limit. "This has been the dullest dinner in history. Clearly, you’ve business with Laura. I shall retire. No tea, thank you. Goodnight, Mr. Warrington—do call again when less preoccupied." With a regal toss of her head, she departed.

As Warrington made his farewells, Arthur gripped his arm. "Stay, George. You should hear this too. I don’t know what to do."

Laura’s pulse raced. "It’s about Blanche, isn’t it?"

Arthur nodded grimly. "When I went to my uncle’s lodgings, his servant Morgan—now mysteriously wealthy—informed me that my uncle had moved to this hotel. But that wasn’t the bombshell. Brace yourselves—Blanche’s father isn’t dead. The man’s an escaped convict. Clavering’s marriage to the Begum is void, and my uncle’s been blackmailing him with the secret to secure my parliamentary seat."

Warrington whistled. "So Blanche inherits nothing, and her father is—"

"—Altamont," Arthur finished. "Precisely. And Morgan, the cockroach, tried to sell me his silence for an annuity."

Laura’s hands clenched. "Blanche doesn’t know?"

"No. She thinks her father drowned honorably. Lady Clavering hinted at past misery but never this."

Warrington exhaled sharply. "You must break it off, Pen. No explanations—just cite ‘family reasons.’ Better they think you a cad than know the truth."

Laura whirled on him. "Would you abandon Blanche for her father’s crimes? If you’d married her, would you cast her off?" Her voice wavered. "Our mother wouldn’t have."

Arthur shuddered. "Our mother was a saint."

"And you’ll be one too," Laura said fiercely, embracing him. "Do what’s right."

Warrington groaned into his hands. *She’s right. That girl could steer a ship through hell.*

Before tea could salvage the evening, a servant announced Major Pendennis’s return. Laura shot Arthur a look—*Be firm, but kind*—and vanished.

The Major, hunched by the fire, grumbled about Morgan’s betrayal. "Damned ingrate! Made a fortune off me, then challenged me to a duel. I nearly knifed him." He rambled about tea, the decline of gentlemen, and—with sudden pathos—bequeathing Arthur his mother’s Bible. "You’ll find it when I’m gone," he sniffed.

Arthur steeled himself. "Uncle, Morgan told me everything."

The Major flinched. "Ah. The Amory business."

"You knew. You let me become engaged to a convict’s daughter."

"Nonsense! She’s Clavering’s heiress. Amory’s dead—legally, at least. Why spoil things?"

Arthur recoiled. "You’d have me profit from this lie?"

The Major wheedled, then ranted, then—horribly—dropped to his knees. "Don’t throw it all away! The title, the income—I beg you!"

Arthur hauled him up, sickened. "I’ll marry her. But no blood money. No seat bought with blackmail."

The Major collapsed into his chair. "Ring the bell," he croaked. "I’m done. Wolsey was right—*had I but served my God...*" He waved a limp hand. "Goodnight, nephew. Don’t bother visiting."

By morning, the old man had taken to his bed, refusing to see Arthur. The battle was over—and the victor felt no triumph.

When Arthur Pendennis, swathed in his finest dressing-gown (a relic of better financial days), strolled into Warrington’s chambers the next morning, he found only Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, holding court over a kingdom of dust and abandoned inkwells. George himself had vanished—slipped away like a phantom, leaving behind nothing but a carpet-bag’s worth of absence and a note that read more like a telegram from a man fleeing creditors than a farewell from a best friend.

*"Dear Pen—Halfway home by breakfast. Christmas in Norfolk. My presence clearly unnecessary. Vale. G.W. P.S. Give my regards to your cousin."*

Pen stared at the note. Such brevity! Such efficiency! Such a *complete* avoidance of emotional labor! George might as well have signed it *"Yours in abruptness."*

Mrs. Flanagan, ever the dramatist, supplied the missing details. *"Found him at his desk, the poor dear, candle burned to a nub, bed untouched! Writing like a man possessed!"* Pen pictured it: George, hunched over his papers, the ink-stained martyr of Grub Street, while the dawn crept in like an uninvited guest. No doubt his latest article was a masterpiece—scathing, brilliant, and possibly dipped in just enough personal bitterness to make it *art*.

Pen sighed. *Why must the best of us always flee?* He glanced around the room—the open drawers, the chewed sealing wax (a nervous habit George would never admit to), the gap on the shelf where Helen’s Bible had been. *Ah. Of course.* The man had taken his heart with him.

Meanwhile, downstairs, Pen discovered another note in his letter-box—this one from Foker, of all people. The new Lord of Logwood and Brewery Fortune had been spotted emerging from a jeweler’s, clad in the kind of mourning attire that suggested grief was best endured with expensive accessories.

*"Foker!"* Pen hailed him.

*"Ah! Yes! Thank you! Very busy! Goodbye!"* Foker blurted, leaping into his black brougham like a man avoiding a subpoena.

Pen watched him go, bemused. *Ah, the mantle of wealth sits uneasily on young shoulders.* One day, you’re a carefree youth; the next, you’re a solemn heir, buying diamond trinkets (for yourself? For a chorus girl? For the ghost of your father’s disapproval?).

At Lady Rockminster’s, the conversation took a turn for the scandalous.

*"That foolish Ann Rosherville!"* the dowager huffed. *"Thrown away fifteen thousand a year to marry her father’s chaplain—a red-haired widower with two children!"*

Pen blinked. *"That’s… impressively self-destructive."*

Laura, ever the romantic, interjected with a sigh. *"Love conquers all."*

*"Love conquers common sense, you mean,"* Lady Rockminster sniffed.

Then came the letters—oh, the letters! Blanche’s effusive invitations, Laura’s determined replies, the *"dearests"* piling up like unpaid bills. And then—the bombshell.

*"What is this letter from Arthur?!"* Blanche’s latest demanded. *"What mystery is this?!"*

Ah. *That* letter.

But that, dear reader, is a revelation for another time. For now, let us leave Pen in suspense—and perhaps a little smug that he’s managed to stir the pot without even trying.

*To be continued…* (Because of course it is.)

Arthur Pendennis sat in his chambers, turning over a perfumed letter in his hand—one of those delicate, gilt-edged missives that seem to carry the scent of a woman’s vanity along with them. The words were elegantly penned, the sentiments artfully arranged, but the meaning? Ah, that was another matter entirely.

*"Dear Blanche,"* the letter began, *"you are always dreaming of pretty dramas and romantic heroines. But tell me, are you prepared to play the part fate has assigned you—not the triumphant lady who inherits a palace, but the forsaken one who discovers she has married a beggar?"*

He read it again, chuckling darkly to himself. Blanche’s reply had been equally theatrical—full of wounded pride, poetic sighs, and a refusal (or was it an acceptance?) wrapped in so many layers of ambiguity that even Pen, no stranger to artifice, found himself baffled.

*"I will write more when I am more collected,"* Blanche had concluded dramatically. *"My racking brain renders thought almost impossible. I long to see Laura!"*

Pen tossed the letter aside with a grim smile. How neatly life had contrived to mock him! His grand ambitions—Parliament, wealth, social triumph—had crumbled like Alnaschar’s porcelain, and now even love seemed to be slipping through his fingers in the most absurd fashion.

A knock at the door interrupted his musings. His servant announced a visitor—*Mr. Samuel Huxter.*

Huxter entered, twirling his walking stick with nervous energy, his face a picture of mingled defiance and sheepishness.

*"Mr. Huxter on particular business!"* Pen greeted him with exaggerated politeness. *"Pray, what can I do for you?"*

Huxter shifted uncomfortably. *"I’d rather not speak before the servant."*

Once alone, the young surgeon blurted out his confession. *"I’ve gone and done it, sir. I’ve married Fanny."*

Pen’s eyebrows shot up. *"Married her? Without a word to anyone?"*

*"It was a quiet affair,"* Huxter admitted. *"St. Clement’s, no fuss. But now the governor’s coming to town, and there’ll be the devil to pay."*

Pen couldn’t help but laugh. *"So you’ve eloped with the girl, and now you want me to smooth things over with your father?"*

*"If you wouldn’t mind, sir,"* Huxter said, with a hopeful grimace. *"He respects you. And Fanny—well, she’s a good girl, sir. Just a bit lively."*

*"She’s got half the medical students from Bartholomew’s dangling after her,"* Huxter admitted glumly. *"But she’s my wife now, and I mean to keep her."*

Before Pen could respond, another knock sounded—this time, it was Bows, the old musician, his face weary, his voice gruff.

*"Mrs. Huxter sent me,"* he announced. *"She thought you might need reminding of your promise."*

Huxter flushed. *"I was just going—"*

*"Yes, yes, off you go,"* Bows said dryly. *"Your young friends from the hospital are waiting. No hurry, of course."*

With a hasty bow, Huxter fled.

Bows sighed, sinking into a chair. *"She’s a clever one, that Fanny. Played us all, she did. Even me."*

Pen studied the old man’s face. *"You cared for her."*

*"More fool I,"* Bows muttered. *"But she’s made her choice. Let’s see how long it lasts."*

Left alone once more, Pen turned Blanche’s letter over in his hands again. Was it rejection? Or merely a test? And did it matter, when his own heart seemed so uncertain?

With a shake of his head, he reached for his hat. There was only one person whose counsel he truly valued now.

The train to Tunbridge Wells was, in Arthur Pendennis’s opinion, moving entirely too fast for a man nursing a spectacularly bruised ego. As the countryside blurred past, he treated himself to a thorough self-examination—a pastime at which he excelled, though it rarely improved his mood.

*Ah, the grand tragedy of Arthur Pendennis,* he mused. *Defiant when I should yield, yielding when I should stand firm. Refuse an angel like Laura? Naturally. Chase after Blanche and her fortune? Naturally. And now here I am, hurtling toward what promises to be the most awkward conversation of my life, having successfully alienated everyone who ever cared for me. Bravo, Arthur. A masterclass in self-sabotage.*

He glanced around the carriage for distraction. A priest sat across from him, eyes glued to his breviary, likely pondering salvation—or possibly the dismal state of railway food. Next to him, a red-faced gentleman scowled at the holy man as if he personally blamed him for the Reformation.

*There’s conviction for you,* Arthur thought. *No doubts, no second-guessing. Just pure, uncomplicated certainty. Meanwhile, I can’t even decide whether I’m a villain or a victim.*

By the time the train hissed to a stop, Arthur had mentally drafted a noble speech for Blanche—a magnanimous blend of apology and proposal, sprinkled with just enough self-deprecation to be charming.

*Blanche, my dear, I come to you not with wealth or grandeur, but with a heart that has, through its own foolishness, learned humility. Let us marry at once, if you’ll have me, and I swear to be the most devoted of husbands—barring, of course, the occasional lapse into brooding introspection.*

Alas, fate (and Blanche) had other plans.

Upon arrival, Arthur was met not by the welcoming embrace of his beloved but by a baffled footman who seemed deeply suspicious of any man arriving with a carpetbag.

"Lady Clavering is not at home," the man intoned, as if this were a profound metaphysical truth rather than a simple evasion.

Undeterred, Arthur pressed on—only to stumble upon a scene straight from a particularly melodramatic farce.

There, in the drawing room, sat Blanche at the piano, trilling a tragic ballad with all the fervor of a doomed opera heroine. Beside her, gazing moon-eyed, was none other than Harry Foker, looking as though he’d been struck by lightning and was rather enjoying it.

And on the piano? A gleaming ruby-and-diamond bracelet, coiled like a serpent waiting to strike.

Arthur burst out laughing.

"Ah! So *this* is the secret!" he exclaimed. "Foker, my dear fellow, don’t look so guilty. If constancy like yours can’t win a lady’s heart, then the world is truly lost!"

Blanche, ever the performer, clasped her hands to her breast. "It is not wealth, nor rank, nor jewels that move me—"

"—Only the sheer, overwhelming *volume* of them?" Arthur suggested.

Blanche ignored him, turning soulful eyes to Foker. "It is the purity of your devotion, dearest Henry!"

Foker, overcome, looked ready to expire on the spot. "I’m not worthy!" he gasped. "Tell me to kiss the ground—I’ll do it! Tell me!"

Blanche graciously declined to order any ground-kissing (for now) but did permit him to weep over her hand. Arthur, torn between amusement and pity, decided it was time for a strategic retreat.

"Clearly, my presence is superfluous," he said. "Blanche, I wish you every happiness—though I suspect you’ll have to manufacture most of it yourself. Foker, old friend, you’re a braver man than I."

As he turned to leave, Blanche detained him for a final, hissed aside.

"You never loved me," she accused.

"Did you ever love anyone who wasn’t a character in your own private drama?" Arthur countered.

She smiled—a sharp, knowing thing. "I require *emotions*, Arthur. You? You only ever provided *thoughts*. And what use are those?"

With that, Arthur made his exit, stepping over the prostrate form of Foker (still murmuring adorations) and into the waiting fly.

*Well,* he reflected as the carriage rolled away, *that could have gone worse. At least no one fainted.*

Then again, the day wasn’t over yet.

On the Tunbridge platform, Pen fidgeted like a man who had swallowed a live eel. The evening train to London crawled along at a pace that would make a snail look like a racehorse. At last—finally!—the lights of London twinkled into view. Pen vaulted from the carriage, flung a guinea at a cabman (who, being a hansom driver, nearly fainted from the shock of generosity), and sprinted up the stairs of Lady Rockminster’s hotel like a man pursued by creditors.

Laura sat alone in the drawing-room, pale as a ghost who had just read a bad review of its haunting technique. She looked up as Pen burst in—then came the moment. The great, life-altering moments are always absurdly brief. A glance, a handclasp, a murmured syllable—and destiny is sealed.

Lady Rockminster, emerging from her post-prandial nap, took one look at the scene and declared, "Upon my word, young people!" Her maid, Beck, gawped over her shoulder like an extra in a melodrama.

Pen, still on his knees (having apparently mistaken the drawing-room for a chapel), announced, "She is consoling me for my misfortune, ma’am."

"By letting you kiss her hand? Next, you’ll be proposing!" snapped her ladyship.

Pen switched targets and planted a gallant kiss on Lady Rockminster’s knuckles instead. "I’ve been to Tunbridge. Miss Amory has discarded me like last season’s hat. The villain who supplanted me is young, rich, and—worst of all—*handsome*."

"Rubbish. You’re weeping because a chit of a girl preferred someone else?"

"Not just *someone*—Henry Foker, Esq.! He’s been serenading her while lounging at her feet like a besotted spaniel. Presents exchanged, vows whispered—all while I was conveniently kept away by *dear* Laura’s imaginary ailments."

"So, having lost Blanche, you’ve settled for Laura?" Lady Rockminster arched a brow.

"He acted nobly," Laura interjected.

"I acted as she told me," Pen said, with the air of a man who has just discovered that love makes everyone slightly ridiculous.

The old lady harrumphed. "I’d have preferred Bluebeard."

To divert attention from his romantic pratfalls, Pen recounted Huxter’s matrimonial misadventures—how the young surgeon had impulsively wed Fanny Bolton and was now quaking at the prospect of facing his father.

Laura, ever the saint, declared they must assist the young couple at once. "Let’s visit them this instant!"

"At midnight?" Lady Rockminster scoffed. "They’re likely in their nightcaps, and so should you be."

Undeterred, they plotted for the morrow. Pen, ever the schemer, proposed a ruse: "You must pretend to be ill, Lady Rockminster. When old Huxter arrives, dazzled by your rank, we’ll spring the news of his son’s marriage."

"Poppycock," said the countess—but her eyes twinkled.

The next morning, Laura and Pen descended upon the newlyweds’ lodgings like benevolent fairy godparents. Fanny, trembling at the thought of her father-in-law’s wrath, gazed at Pen with the reverence of a disciple.

"*She* was the siren who tempted *him*?" Laura thought, eyeing Fanny’s unremarkable charms. "Men really *do* have peculiar taste."

When Huxter senior arrived, Pen and Laura ambushed him with flattery and aristocratic allure. The old surgeon, dazzled by Lady Rockminster’s coronet, was putty in their hands.

"Your son is a credit to the profession," Pen declared.

"A fine lad—though he ought to marry," Huxter mused.

"Ah! About that..." Pen exchanged a glance with Laura.

A door creaked open. In shuffled Sam and Fanny, looking like two children caught stealing jam.

"Papa—" Sam began.

"Married?! To *her*?" Huxter roared.

Fanny burst into tears—then, with the tactical brilliance of a seasoned actress, flung herself at the old man’s feet. Huxter, bewildered, found himself patting her head like a confused Saint Bernard.

"What’s your name, my dear?"

"Fanny, Papa," she sniffed.

And just like that, the battle was won.

That evening, as Laura mused on Fanny’s unremarkable looks and Pen’s past follies, she sighed. "Men are *so* odd."

Pen grinned. "And yet, you’ve agreed to marry one."

Laura smiled. "Against my better judgment."

Lady Rockminster snorted. "I *still* would’ve preferred Bluebeard."

And thus, with schemes, tears, and a well-timed coronet, another match was made—proving once again that love, like bad theater, thrives on improvisation.

A month has galloped by since our last encounter with this merry band of misfits, and—lo and behold!—fate (or perhaps lazy plotting) has seen fit to reunite them in the charming hamlet of Clavering. The place is buzzing like a beehive that’s just discovered sugar.

Leading the charge is one Frederic Lightfoot, formerly the beleaguered majordomo of Sir Francis Clavering, now the proud proprietor of the *Clavering Arms*. His advertisement in the *Chatteris Champion* (a newspaper of such unshakable integrity that it would sell its own grandmother for a decent headline) boasts of the inn’s refurbishments:

> *"Gentlemen of discerning taste will find our stables ideal for hiding from wives, our billiard room perfect for losing money, and our cellars stocked with spirits strong enough to make a teetotaler reconsider his life choices."*

Indeed, the old inn is livelier than a cat in a room full of rocking chairs. The Clavering crest has been repainted with such enthusiasm that the lion now appears to be winking. The local magistrates hold court in the card room, presumably to adjudicate disputes over who cheated at whist. Even Major Stokes, a man whose idea of excitement is watching grass grow, has been spotted indulging in Mrs. Lightfoot’s *Mulligatawny soup*—twice! A culinary miracle.

Meanwhile, the *Champion* breathlessly reports that Arthur Pendennis, our once-ambitious hero, has withdrawn from the parliamentary race, citing "personal reasons" (i.e., discovering that politics is even more tedious than he remembered). Rumor has it—and by "rumor," we mean the *Champion*’s editor making wild guesses—that Sir Francis Clavering may soon vacate his seat for a wealthier, better-connected fellow.

Lady Rockminster, having grudgingly accepted that Laura Bell will marry Pen instead of a duke (or at least a man with a better hat), has taken to the role of meddling fairy godmother with gusto. She informs her maid, Mrs. Beck—who already knows everything because servants *always* do—that the engagement is to proceed, finances permitting.

> *"Mr. Pendennis’s income is adequate, if one squints at it optimistically,"* declares her ladyship. *"The railroad might bring in something, assuming it doesn’t derail into a ditch. And I shall speak to my nephew Pynsent—not that he ever had a chance, mind you—to secure Arthur a government sinecure. Something undemanding, like Official Nap-Taker or Inspector of Hats."*

She then descends upon Major Pendennis, who is so startled by her visit that he nearly spills his sherry. The Major, ever the diplomat, pretends he’s *delighted* Pen’s engagement to Blanche Amory collapsed, though privately he’s calculating how much Laura’s hypothetical children might inherit.

Meanwhile, lurking in the shadows like a poorly paid villain in a pantomime, is Mr. James Morgan—former valet, current blackmailer, and general nuisance. He’s slithered his way back into the Clavering household, serving drinks with all the warmth of a glacier.

Pen, ever the busybody, corners Blanche and hisses: *"Morgan knows everything! Tell Harry before he finds out the hard way!"*

Blanche, who has the survival instincts of a fox in hunting season, waves him off. *"After the wedding, darling. Always after the wedding."*

Chevalier Strong, ever the voice of reason, sums it up: *"Morgan’s a snake, but he’s a snake who knows which boots to lick. Best leave him to it."*

And what of our dear Harry Foker, the human embodiment of a golden retriever? Blissfully unaware of the gathering storm, he’s too busy planning his wedding festivities, which promise to be the most extravagant spectacle Clavering has seen since the Great Pig Incident of ’23.

> *"There shall be feasting! Music! Speeches so long the guests will beg for death!"* he declares to anyone within earshot. *"Lightfoot! More wine for my friends! And for myself! Actually, mostly for myself!"*

Alas, not all is well in paradise. Young Lightfoot, now in charge of the inn’s liquor supply, is rarely sober enough to stand upright, while his wife weeps into her apron. Even Morgan’s presence casts a pall—though Foker, ever cheerful, can’t quite put his finger on why.

> *"I don’t like that fellow,"* he confides to Mrs. Lightfoot. *"He looks at me like he’s measuring me for a coffin. Then again, he looks at everyone like that."*

And so, the stage is set:
- **Pen and Laura** are betrothed, pending further interference.
- **Blanche and Foker** are days from wedlock, pending disaster.
- **Morgan** lurks, **Strong** schemes, and **Lightfoot** drinks.

Will Blanche confess? Will Morgan explode the whole affair? Will Foker notice *anything*?
