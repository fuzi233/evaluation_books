Time went on as it always did in that part of the world—slowly, as if each day must be kneaded and proved before it could be baked into the next. Spring deepened; the hedges thickened; the fields took their green again, and gardens broke into an honest, domestic riot of leaf and bloom. In the mornings you could smell damp earth and crushed young grass, and in the evenings the air had that cool, rinsed clearness which makes a hearth-fire seem not a luxury but a right.

Yet beneath the pleasant surface England was sore. War still drew its long, rasping breath across the land; it was heard in the thud of distant taxes, in the mutter of anxious men, in the clink of coin counted too often and too late. The poor were no less poor for the blossoming of lilac; the masters were no less harassed for the singing of larks. Trade—at least certain kinds of it—felt as if a hand lay on its throat. News from the Peninsula came in broken measures: a far-off report of success, then a long, dead stretch of silence, and between, Bonaparte’s loud proclamations, like brass instruments blaring over a village where people wanted, above all things, quiet.

Those who suffered most from the war’s grind found the struggle intolerable and, to their minds, hopeless. They clamoured for peace at any price. Men like Yorke and Moore—men pushed by circumstance to the lip of bankruptcy and looking down—insisted upon peace with the ferocity of frightened self-preservation. Meetings were held; speeches made; petitions prepared and carried about like baskets meant to collect a harvest. Terms did not matter; relief did.

A man alone may be capable of generosity; men in a mass are commonly capable chiefly of appetite. The merchant was not exempt. The mercantile classes—so clever at measuring out cloth or calculating freight—were apt to measure national honour by the yard and reckon dignity by the pound. They professed to loathe war because it was bloody, because it was barbarous, because they were, as they would have it, peculiarly humane. But the same lips that spoke of gentleness could be remarkably cold when they spoke of any class not their own. Many of them grudged the poor even their bread, as if hunger were an impertinence; they questioned the very right of those who did not “trade” to live at all. Whoever did not make money was accused of eating idleness like daily food. Long might it be, one hoped, before England became only a nation of shopkeepers.

Moore, as has been seen, was no self-sacrificing patriot; the position into which he had been forced made him doubly eager to save himself. When he felt ruin creeping near for the second time, none fought more fiercely to keep his feet. He did what he could to stir northern agitation against the war, and prodded men richer than he, men with more names and more influence, to do more.

Now and then a flash of clearer sense crossed him. When he heard of Europe threatened and arming; when he saw Russia rising in her stern, frozen strength to defend even her dark despotism from foreign heel and yoke, he knew England—free England—could not then go cap in hand with concessions to an unjust conqueror. And when dispatches came—Lord Wellington’s measured, modest words, truth spoken without trumpet—Moore’s heart confessed, if only for a moment, that a steady power was with Britain’s troops: slow, vigilant, enduring, the kind of power that wins in the end.

In the end. But that end looked distant, and between Moore and it lay the immediate crush of his own affairs. He could not live on eventual victories. He had his own fate to follow; he followed it with an almost savage vigour.

He followed it so far that he came to a decisive rupture with his old Tory friend, the rector. They quarrelled at a public meeting; later, their anger hardened into pungent letters printed for all to read. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, ceased to see him, and would not speak to him when they met. He made it plain, too, and without softening, that Caroline’s intercourse with Hollow’s Cottage must cease for the present. She was to give up her French lessons.

French, he observed, was a bad and frivolous tongue at the best; the literature of it was bad and frivolous likewise, and injurious to weak female minds. He wondered—parenthetically, but with pointed scorn—what noodle had first made it fashion to teach women French at all. Nothing could be more improper; it was, he said, like feeding a rickety child on chalk and water gruel. Caroline must give up the language, and give up her cousins. They were dangerous people.

He expected opposition—at least tears. He seldom troubled himself about Caroline’s movements; still, a vague certainty possessed him that she liked going to Hollow’s Cottage, and that she liked, too, Robert Moore’s occasional presence at the rectory. The “Cossack” had noticed something else as well: when Malone stepped in of an evening to be sociable—pinching the ears of an aged black cat that shared with Miss Helstone’s feet the footstool, borrowing a fowling-piece and cracking off shots at a tool-shed door while daylight lasted, carrying in, with boisterous brusquerie, the smell of gunpowder and the draft of open passage-doors—Caroline had a trick of vanishing. She would slip upstairs and remain invisible until she was called down to supper.

But when Robert Moore was the guest, there were no fireworks of noise. He did not torment the cat; he sometimes coaxed it from the stool to his knee and let it purr and climb, rubbing its head against his cheek with the complacence of a creature that knows gentleness by touch. There was no sulphurous perfume, no bang of firearms, no boasting. Yet Caroline stayed in the room and seemed to find deep content in stitching Jew-basket pin-cushions and knitting socks meant for missionary baskets.

She was very quiet; Robert scarcely addressed her. Nevertheless, Mr. Helstone was not one of those elderly gentlemen easily blinded. He watched them when they bade each other good-night. He had seen their eyes meet once—only once.

Some natures would have taken pleasure in that glance, because it held no harm and a little innocent delight. It was not a glance of mutual intelligence; there were no secrets between them. There was no craft, no concealment—only Moore’s eyes, looking into Caroline’s, recognising clearness and gentleness; and Caroline’s eyes, meeting his, confessing manliness and search. Each acknowledged the other’s charm in his or her own way. Moore smiled slightly; Caroline coloured slightly.

Mr. Helstone could have rated them both on the spot. They annoyed him. Why? He could not have explained. Asked what Moore deserved, he would have answered “a horsewhip.” Asked what Caroline deserved, he would have adjudged her a box on the ear. Pressed for a reason, he would have stormed against flirtation and love-making and vowed there should be no such folly under his roof.

Political reasons, plus these private irritations, fixed his resolution to separate the cousins. He announced his will one evening while Caroline sat at work near the drawing-room window. The light fell full upon her face, and, noticing it, he was struck by something he had not till then admitted to himself: she looked paler—quieter—than she used to look. He had observed, too, that Robert Moore’s name had not, for some three weeks, dropped from her lips, and that during the same space of time Moore had not come to the rectory. A suspicion of clandestine meetings haunted Mr. Helstone’s mind—because he had an indifferent opinion of women, and, where he did not understand them, he suspected. He believed they required constant watching.

In a tone dryly significant, he told her she was to cease her daily visits to the Hollow. He expected a start and a look of depreciation. The start came, but slight. No look at all was directed to him.

“Do you hear me?” he asked.

“Yes, uncle.”

“Of course you mean to attend to what I say?”

“Yes, certainly.”

“And there must be no letter-scribbling to your cousin Hortense—no intercourse whatever. I do not approve of the principles of the family. They are Jacobinical.”

“Very well,” said Caroline quietly.

She acquiesced. There was no vexed flush, no rising tears. The thoughtful shadow that had lain on her features before he spoke remained undisturbed; she was obedient.

Perfectly—because his mandate chimed with her own judgement already formed; because it had become pain to her to go to Hollow’s Cottage. Nothing met her there now but disappointment. Hope and love had quitted that small tenement, for Robert seemed to have deserted it.

Whenever she asked after him—which she did very seldom, since the utterance of his name made heat rush to her face—the answer was always the same: he was from home, or he was absorbed in business. Hortense feared he was killing himself by application. He scarcely ever took a meal in the house; he lived in the counting-house.

At church only did Caroline have the chance of seeing him; and there she rarely looked. It was too much pain and too much pleasure to look; it roused too much feeling, and she had learned, sharply, that her feeling was wasted.

Once, on a dark, wet Sunday—when the congregation was thin, and when especially certain ladies were absent whose observing eyes and tomahawk tongues Caroline feared—she allowed her gaze to seek Robert’s pew and rest awhile on its occupant. He sat alone. Hortense, guided by prudent considerations touching the rain and a new spring chapeau, had stayed at home.

During the sermon, he sat with folded arms, his eyes cast down, looking sad and abstracted. When depressed, the very colour of his face seemed darker; to-day cheek and forehead wore their most sober olive, as if all warmth had been drawn inward and locked away.

By instinct, as she studied that clouded countenance, Caroline knew his thoughts were running in no familiar channel; they were far from her and far from anything she could comprehend or share. Nothing they had ever talked of together was in his mind now. He was wrapped from her by interests and responsibilities in which she could have no part—not because she was unwilling, but because the world would not admit it.

She pondered on the subject with earnestness that tried to be practical. She speculated on his life, his fears, his fate; she mused over the mystery called “business,” and made herself attempt to understand it—not as a romantic riddle, but as a daily discipline: its liabilities, duties, exactions; the state of mind of a man of business, the sleeplessness it brewed, the sharpness it demanded. She wished, sincerely, to see things as they were. Here and there she caught a thin ray of truth and hoped it might be enough to guide her.

“Different, indeed,” she concluded, “is Robert’s condition to mine. I think only of him; he has no room, no leisure, to think of me. Love has been for two years the predominant feeling of my heart—always there, always awake. Quite other feelings absorb his reflections. He is rising now: service is over. Will he turn his head toward this pew? No—not once. He has not one look for me. That is hard. A kind glance would have made me happy till to-morrow. I have not got it; he would not give it; he is gone. Strange that grief should almost choke me because another human being’s eye has failed to greet mine.”

That Sunday evening, as Mr. Malone came, as usual, to spend it with the rector, Caroline withdrew after tea to her chamber. Fanny, knowing her habits, had lit a cheerful little fire, for the weather was gusty and chill. The flame caught on the coals with a faint crackle—one of those small, living sounds that make loneliness bearable; outside, the east wind sobbed at the casement, and the rain tapped with a steady, thin insistence, like fingers too tired to knock properly.

Closeted there, silent and solitary, what could she do but think? She paced noiselessly on the carpet, head drooped, hands folded. It was irksome to sit; reflection ran through her mind like water through a narrow channel—rapid, continuous, mutely excited.

Mute was the room, mute the house. The double door of the study muffled the gentlemen’s voices to a low rumour, as if male talk were a far-off sea. The servants were quiet in the kitchen, engaged with books their young mistress had lent them—books she had said were fit for Sunday reading. On Caroline’s own table lay another of the same sort, open; but she could not read it. Its theology was incomprehensible; besides, her mind was already teeming with another language—one made of remembered look and tone, of footsteps on gravel, of the hush in Hollow’s copse when the birds paused, and then began again.

Her imagination filled with pictures: Moore’s face and form; scenes where he and she had been together. She saw winter firelight in the rectory parlour, when the kettle sang its thin, comforting song and the cat purred like a small engine of content; she saw a hot summer afternoon in the bosom of Nunnely Wood, where the air smelled of resin and leaf-mould and bruised fern; she saw mild spring and mellow autumn moments in Hollow’s Copse, when the May cuckoo called and called as if naming something that could not be answered in human words.

And she saw, with a tenderness that was half hunger, those simple woodland feasts she had made without calling them such: September’s treasure of nuts and ripe blackberries gathered into a little basket, shaded with green leaves and fresh blossoms. She had taken pleasure in arranging it—choosing the darkest berries, wiping them with her handkerchief, cracking the nuts, testing their sweetness; and then, later, giving them to Moore berry by berry, nut by nut, like a bird feeding its fledgling—an intimate rite of sustenance that asked no declaration and yet meant more than declarations.

Robert’s voice seemed close in her ear; his few caresses appeared renewed. Then, because these joys were but air, they collapsed. The pictures faded; the voice failed; the visionary clasp melted cold from her hand. Where lips had once left a warm seal on her forehead, she felt now as if a sleety raindrop had struck and slid away.

She returned from an enchanted region to the real. For Nunnely Wood in June, she saw her narrow chamber; for bird-song, she heard rain on the pane; for the sigh of a south wind, the sob of the east; and for Moore’s manly presence, she had only the thin illusion of her own dim shadow on the wall.

Turning from that pale phantom—her outline, her drooped attitude, her colourless tresses—she sat down, because inaction suited the decline of her spirits. She said to herself, with a clearness that frightened her, “I have to live, perhaps, till seventy years. As far as I know, I have good health; half a century of existence may lie before me. How am I to occupy it? What am I to do to fill the interval of time which spreads between me and the grave?”

She reflected.

“I shall not be married, it appears,” she went on. “If Robert does not care for me, I shall never have a husband to love, nor little children to take care of. Till lately I reckoned securely on the duties and affections of wife and mother to occupy my life. I considered—somehow—as a matter of course, that I was growing up to the ordinary destiny, and never troubled myself to seek any other. Now I perceive plainly I may have been mistaken. Probably I shall be an old maid. I shall live to see Robert married to some one else—some rich lady. I shall never marry. What was I created for? Where is my place in the world?”

Again she mused, and the rain kept its fine, ceaseless patter, as if the sky were sifting a handful of grains over the roof.

“Ah! I see,” she pursued presently; “that is the question which most old maids are puzzled to solve. Other people solve it for them by saying, ‘Your place is to do good to others, to be helpful whenever help is wanted.’ That is right in some measure—and very convenient for the people who hold it. Certain sets of human beings are very apt to maintain that other sets should give up their lives to them and their service, and then they requite them by praise; they call them devoted and virtuous. Is this enough? Is it to live? Is there not a terrible hollowness—mockery—want—in that existence which is given away to others for want of something of your own to bestow it on? I suspect there is. Does virtue lie in the abnegation of self? I do not believe it. Undue humility makes tyranny; weak concession creates selfishness.”

Her thoughts—always sensitive to the way words were used as a bridle—moved on with a troubled precision.

“The Romish religion especially teaches renunciation of self and submission to others, and nowhere are found so many grasping tyrants as in the ranks of the Romish priesthood. Each human being has his share of rights. I suspect it would conduce to the happiness and welfare of all if each knew his allotment, and held to it as tenaciously as the martyr to his creed. Queer thoughts these that surge in my mind. Are they right thoughts? I am not certain.”

The fire sank; its small red heart beat lower and lower. The room, with its muffled male voices far away and the steady rain near at hand, felt like a world reduced to two kinds of sound: authority at a distance, weather at the window. She listened, without meaning to, and the very listening sharpened her sense of how much a life could be governed by what it habitually heard—by peace or by din, by gentle domestic noises or by harsh, public clamours.

“Well,” she continued, “life is short at the best. Seventy years, they say, pass like vapour, like a dream when one awaketh; and every path trod by human feet terminates in one bourne—the grave, the little chink in the surface of this great globe, the furrow where the mighty husbandman with the scythe deposits the seed shaken from the ripe stem; and there it falls, decays, and thence springs again when the world has rolled round a few times more.”

So much for the body. The soul meantime—so she had been taught—wings its long flight upward, folds its wings on the brink of the sea of fire and glass, and, gazing down through burning clearness, finds mirrored there the vision of the Christian’s triple Godhead—the sovereign Father, the mediating Son, the Creator Spirit. Such words, at least, had been chosen to express what is inexpressible, to describe what baffles description. The soul’s real hereafter—who should guess?

Her fire was down to its last cinder. Malone had departed. The study bell rang for prayers.

The next day Caroline spent altogether alone, her uncle being gone to dine with his friend Dr. Boultby, vicar of Whinbury. The hours moved, and still she talked inwardly in the same strain—looking forward, asking what she was to do with life. Fanny, passing in and out on housemaid errands, perceived that her young mistress sat very still, always in the same place, bent industriously over some work. She did not lift her head to speak, as was her custom; and when Fanny remarked that the day was fine and she ought to take a walk, she only answered, “It is cold.”

“You are very diligent at that sewing, Miss Caroline,” continued the girl, coming nearer to the little table.

“I am tired of it, Fanny.”

“Then why do you go on with it? Put it down. Read—or do something to amuse you.”

“It is solitary in this house, Fanny. Don’t you think so?”

“I don’t find it so, miss. Me and Eliza are company for one another; but you are quite too still. You should visit more. Now be persuaded: go upstairs and dress yourself smart, and go and take tea, in a friendly way, with Miss Mann or Miss Ainley. I am certain either of those ladies would be delighted to see you.”

“But their houses are dismal: they are both old maids. I am certain old maids are a very unhappy race.”

“Not they, miss. They can’t be unhappy; they take such care of themselves. They are all selfish.”

“Miss Ainley is not selfish, Fanny. She is always doing good. How devotedly kind she was to her step-mother as long as the old lady lived; and now, when she is quite alone in the world, without brother or sister or any one to care for her, how charitable she is to the poor—as far as her means permit! Still nobody thinks much of her, or has pleasure in going to see her; and how gentlemen always sneer at her!”

“They shouldn’t, miss. I believe she is a good woman. But gentlemen think only of ladies’ looks.”

“I’ll go and see her,” cried Caroline, starting up as if the very act of moving might shake off the heaviness. “And if she asks me to stay to tea, I’ll stay. How wrong it is to neglect people because they are not pretty and young and merry! And I will certainly call to see Miss Mann too. She may not be amiable, but what has made her unamiable? What has life been to her?”

Fanny helped her put away her work, and then assisted her to dress. She smoothed the soft, full curls until they shone, and tied the sash of Caroline’s brown silk frock with a practised neatness.

“You’ll not be an old maid, Miss Caroline,” she said, with a confidence that had more kindness than reason in it. “There are no signs of an old maid about you.”

Caroline looked into the little mirror and thought she saw some signs. In the last month she had altered: her complexion was paler; a wan shade circled her eyes; her face held a dejected quietness. She was not, in short, so fresh as she used to be. She hinted as much to Fanny, who would not answer directly—only said people’s looks did vary, and that at Caroline’s age a little falling away signified nothing; she would soon come round again, plumper and rosier than ever.

Having delivered this reassurance, Fanny wrapped her with singular zeal in warm shawls and handkerchiefs, piling them on as if to keep out not merely the chill of the air but the chill of thought. Caroline, nearly smothered with the weight, was fain at last to resist further additions; and thus prepared, she went out, carrying with her, into the spring day’s thin sunshine and soft wind, a heart full of unanswered questions and a resolve—small, perhaps, but honest—to seek companionship where the world least praised it.
