Ghysbrecht Van Swieten lay awake all night, consumed by unease. The storm raged outside, and though he feared thunder and lightning more than any man in Sevenbergen, he would have joined the search party had it not been for his dread of the sky’s fury. But when the tempest finally quieted, he rose without delay, slipped down the stairs, saddled his mule, and rode swiftly to the “Three Kings” inn.

There, he found his men sprawled in exhaustion—some slumped over chairs, others curled on tables, a few lying flat on the floor. He stormed into their midst, shaking them awake with sharp commands. They muttered apologies and recounted their fruitless efforts, praising themselves loudly for their diligence.

“Fools!” Ghysbrecht bellowed. “To let you go without me! I swear my life that he was there all along. Did you look under the girl’s bed?”

“No,” one replied. “There wasn’t room for a man.”

“How do you know?” Ghysbrecht snapped. “If you didn’t look, how could you be certain? You should have searched every inch—under the bed, inside it, tested each panel with your knives. Come now—up with you! I’ll show you how to hunt properly.”

Dierich Brower stirred slowly, rubbing his eyes. “If you find him,” he grumbled, “then call me a horse and not a man.”

Within moments, Peter’s house was encircled once more. Ghysbrecht left his mule with Jorian Ketel and, flanked by Dierich and the others, entered the dwelling. It was silent, empty—no sign of life, not even Peter himself. They climbed the stairs, scanning each corner, until suddenly one of the men gasped and pointed through Peter’s open window.

The others turned—and there, just beyond the fields, walking calmly side by side with Margaret and Martin, stood the fugitive they had sought.

Ghysbrecht roared with triumph, darting down the stairs and leaping onto his mule. With a cry, he spurred forward, and the entire party gave chase across the open land.
