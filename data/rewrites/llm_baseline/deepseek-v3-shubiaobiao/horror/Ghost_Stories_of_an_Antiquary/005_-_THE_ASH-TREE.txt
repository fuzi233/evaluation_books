Eastern England is dotted with those peculiar country houses—damp, faintly Italianate, crouched within their modest parks. Castringham Hall was such a place: white stucco over old brick, a pillared portico, and beyond the fringe of its woods, a mere choked with reeds. But what set it apart was the great ash-tree, its gnarled roots gripping the earth scarcely six yards from the western wall, its branches clawing at the upper windows as if eager to pry inside.

In 1690, when Suffolk still trembled under the shadow of the gallows, Castringham saw its own witch dragged to trial. Mrs. Mothersole was no pauper crone, but a woman of means—until Sir Matthew Fell, the hall’s master, swore he had watched her from his window at midnight. Clad only in her shift, she had climbed the ash-tree, hacking at its twigs with a sickle-shaped knife, muttering as she worked. Three times he pursued her; three times she vanished, leaving only the flash of a hare’s tail darting toward the village.

The jury heard worse tales—of curses, of milk turned sour—but it was Sir Matthew’s testimony that condemned her. At her hanging, she did not weep. Her final words, whispered like a promise: *"There will be guests at the Hall."*

###

A month later, beneath a full May moon, Sir Matthew and the vicar, Mr. Crome, supped together. The talk was dull—estates, tithes—but as they took their parting stroll, Sir Matthew froze.

"Look there," he hissed, pointing to the ash. "Something moves on the trunk—not a squirrel. Too many legs."

The vicar glimpsed it too—a dark shape, skittering upward. Then it was gone.

By morning, Sir Matthew lay dead in his bed, his body blackened, his face twisted in agony. The physician found no poison, only two pinpricks on his breast. The women who washed the corpse wailed when their hands touched his skin; their arms swelled monstrously, throbbing with pain for weeks.

Generations passed. The ash-tree thrived. Sir Matthew’s son barred the cursed chamber, yet cattle died mysteriously in the fields. His grandson, Sir Richard, scoffed at the tales—until the night the wind brought no storm, yet something rattled his window.

Exhausted, he moved into the sealed West Room. The air was thick with decay. When he flung open the shutters, the ash’s branches loomed, though no wind stirred them.

The Bishop of Kilmore, guesting the next day, eyed the ash with unease. "No Irishman would sleep near such a tree," he murmured. "They say it draws ill luck… and worse things."

Sir Richard shrugged. "It comes down tomorrow."

But the bishop frowned. "Last night—you heard its branches scrape your window?" He stepped back, measuring the distance. "Sir Richard, no branch touches that glass. Not unless something climbed it."

###

That night, the rattling returned. Not wind, not twigs—a *tapping*, deliberate, patient. Then a sound like something dropping softly onto the floorboards.

By dawn, the servants found the door locked from within. When they broke it down, Sir Richard’s body lay contorted, his face black, his mouth stretched in a scream.

And on the windowsill, pressed into the dust, was a mark like a hand—but the fingers were too long, and the palm was split with seams, as though something with claws had crouched there, waiting.

The ash-tree was felled that evening. When the axemen split its trunk, they found the heartwood riddled with holes—a labyrinth of tunnels, hollowed as if by gnawing.

And curled in the deepest chamber, dry and brittle, was the skeleton of a hare. Its skull had been cracked open, and within the cavity, nestled like a pearl in an oyster, lay a small, curved knife.
