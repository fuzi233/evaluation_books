It is to be hoped that the well-dressed lady of indeterminate age, spotted late that afternoon in a secluded corner of the Belleville hotel piazza, had not selected a narrative demanding intense mental focus. Her eyes—distinctively sharp yet kindly—seemed far more captivated by the spectacle unfolding before her than by the pages she diligently flipped through. The view was undeniably designed to entertain an idle mind. First lay the sea, a vast expanse of azure speckled with countless sails; next, the beach, animated by clusters of youths dressed in garish, multi-colored finery; followed by the village street; and finally, a lawn occasionally traversed by young couples clutching tennis rackets or golf clubs. Even children failed to draw her gaze twice, though there could be no mistaking her status as a spinster. Conversely, a young married pair or a tall, commanding elder gentleman invariably caused her eyes to drift from her book—a volume she held perilously close to her face, a habit a shrewd observer might critique. Such criticism, however, would not apply to the sprightly, albeit frail, octogenarian seated at the opposite end of the piazza. He appeared utterly absorbed in the novel displayed prominently before him, evidenced by the occasional smiles that broke the serene placidity of his benevolent countenance, suggesting the story was thoroughly amusing.

Yet, midway through his reading, and certainly before concluding his chapter, he abruptly closed the book, unfolded a newspaper to its full width, and began scanning the columns. Simultaneously, the lady at the far end of the piazza lifted her spectacles to peer at two gentlemen emerging from the grand doorway situated between her and the elderly reader. Did she recognize them, or was mere curiosity piqued? The manner in which she slammed her book shut and rose suggested she had identified old acquaintances among the distinguished pair now approaching slowly. If so, she offered no joy in their presence; after a brief indication of their arrival, she turned with visible embarrassment and retreated onto the lawn. There, standing with her back to them, she petted a small dog in a way that revealed a complete lack of genuine affection for the animal—an instinctual terror overriding her natural impulses. The two gentlemen, displaying total indifference to her proximity, walked past her to the end of the piazza before mechanically retracing their steps.

As their faces came within the line of sight of the octogenarian engrossed in his paper, he shifted the sheet ever so slightly—perhaps due to the sun glaring into his eyes, or perhaps to catch a glimpse of two remarkably striking men. If the latter, the timing was fortuitous, for they halted merely a few feet from his chair. One was elderly, matching or surpassing the age of the man observing him, yet belonging to that renowned Scottish lineage known for remaining robust and vigorous into their eighties. His toughness manifested not only in his upright, graceful posture but also in the calm, cold gaze of his eyes, which surveyed everyone and everything with unflappable composure. In stark contrast, the glance of his younger, equally stalwart companion recoiled with acute sensitivity from every individual encountered, except for the mild-tempered old newsreader near whom they paused for a brief exchange.

"I don't believe it serves me any purpose," the young man remarked in a gloomy tone. "I am wretched in her company, and doubly wretched when I attempt to forget myself in her absence. We should probably return. I would prefer sitting where she can see me rather than having her wonder... Oh, I shall be careful. But you must understand how unnerving the absolute silence I am forced to maintain regarding what is destroying us all. I am nearly as ill as she."

They withdrew, and the seemingly indifferent listener turned the page of his newspaper. Five minutes elapsed before they returned. This time, it was the elder gentleman who spoke, his voice lower and less distinct, either due to greater discretion or a lesser grip on his emotions. "Escape? To the south coast—she will forget to watch you for a clinging nature—impulsive, yet foolishly affectionate—you know that. No danger. Found out. Time. A cheerful home. Courage. Happiness. All forgotten." A gesture from the young man as he departed indicated he shared none of these optimistic sentiments.

Meanwhile, Miss Butterworth—who, surely, you have now recognized—also had her moments. Still stooping over the dog, which wriggled beneath her hand without attempting to flee, likely mesmerized by that hesitant touch unknown to dogs prior, her ears and attention were fixed on two girls chatting freely on a nearby bench as if they were alone. They were gossiping about a fellow guest at the hotel, and Miss Butterworth listened intently after catching the name "Adams." Among the snippets she gathered were: "But she is! I tell you she is sick enough to require a nurse and a doctor. I glimpsed her passing her room yesterday, and I've never seen eyes so large or cheeks so pale. Then look at him! He must adore her; he refuses to speak to another woman and spends the entire day pacing that small, stifling room. I wonder if they are newlyweds? They are young enough, and considering her attire..."

"Oh, spare me the discussion of clothes. I saw her on her first day and felt like a dowdy compared to her until she suddenly fell ill and could no longer wear those magnificent waists and jackets. I felt inadequate seeing that pale blue..."

"Oh, well, blue suits blondes. You'd look dreadful in it. I wasn't concerned with her clothing, but I feared our prospects ended if she chose to converse or even smile at the few men brave enough to stay a week here. Yet, she isn't a beauty; she lacks a good nose, a handsome eye, or even flawless skin. It must be her mouth, which is lovely, or her walk—did you notice her walk? It was as if she were floating, until she collapsed in that faint. I wonder why she fainted. Nothing happened, not even from her husband. Perhaps that was the trigger. I noticed for some reason he looked extremely serious—and when she tried to attract his attention several times without success, she simply dropped from her chair to the floor. That startled him. He hasn't left her side since."

"Do they appear very happy, do you, given how rich and handsome the couple is?"

"Perhaps he is dissipated. I've noted that the old gentleman rarely leaves them."

"Well, well, he may be dissipated; handsome men often are. But I wouldn't mind if—"

At that moment, the dog yelped and bolted. Miss Butterworth had unconsciously pinched it, likely in indignation at the direction the chit-chat was taking. This created a distraction, causing the young girls to depart and leave Miss Butterworth without occupation. However, a young man crossing her path soon provided ample food for thought.

"You recognize them? Is there no mistake?" he whispered.

"None; the one heading this way is the young man I observed leaving Mr. Adams's residence, and the other is the elderly gentleman who arrived shortly thereafter."

"Mr. Gryce advises you to return home. He intends to arrest the young man."

Sweetwater passed on. Miss Butterworth wandered to a seat and sat down. She felt a profound weakness, envisioning that young wife—sick, overwhelmed, battling her greatest fears—succumbing to this crushing blow with no woman nearby capable of offering even a shred of sympathy. The father did not strike her as the man to steady her faltering resolve or soothe her bruised heart. Beneath his polished exterior lay an icy demeanor that repelled this keen-eyed spinster. Recalling the coldness of his mannerisms, she felt seized by an irresistible urge to be near this young creature when the blow landed, solely to alleviate the tension in her own heartstrings, which currently ached sharply over the role she felt compelled to play in the matter.

When she rose to seek Mr. Gryce, she found him gone. Searching the piazza for the other two gentlemen, she saw them vanish around the corner toward a small smoking room. Unable to follow, she ascended the stairs. Meeting a maid in the upper hallway, she requested Mrs. Adams. Told that Mrs. Adams was ill, she was directed to her room at the end of a long corridor. Since all hallways terminated in windows beneath which sofas were placed, Miss Butterworth felt circumstances favored her. Taking a seat on the sofa before hers, she settled into a state of great complacency.

Instantly, a sweet voice drifted through the open transom of the door behind which her thoughts were already concentrated.

"Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I'm terrified of what he might be tempted to do or say down on those great piazzas alone."

"Mr. Poindexter is with him," replied a voice, measured yet kind. "Mr. Adams grew very weary, and your father persuaded him to go downstairs for a smoke."

"I must rise; indeed, I must rise. Oh! the camphor—the—"

A commotion ensued; the poor young wife had clearly fainted again. Miss Butterworth cast miserable glances toward the door.

Meanwhile, in that secluded smoking room, a harrowing scene unfolded. The two gentlemen had lit their cigars, sitting in forced postures indicating their discomfort with the tobacco, consumed only by courtesy. Suddenly, an old man entered—strangely serious, strangely at home, yet as strangely a guest as they were—and shut the door behind him.
