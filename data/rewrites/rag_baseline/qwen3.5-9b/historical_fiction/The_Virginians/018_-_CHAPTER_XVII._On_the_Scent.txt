The silence in the room was not empty; it was heavy with the residue of a sudden explosion. Young Harry Warrington's rebellion had struck Madame de Bernstein with such velocity that her only defense was the immediate, violent eruption of anger we witnessed moments prior. As she retreated, her exit was marked by two sharp, piercing glances directed at Lady Fanny and her mother—a silent accusation that hung in the air longer than her footsteps. Lady Maria, however, remained a statue of indifference behind her tambour-frame. Her hands moved mechanically over the fabric, her head barely lifting from the embroidery to register the aunt's departure or the electric exchange of looks between the matriarch and her daughter.

"So," the maternal gaze seemed to articulate, "despite everything, you have secured him?"

"Secure what?" Lady Fanny's eyes queried, wide and feignedly perplexed. Yet innocence was a currency she could not afford to spend. She possessed none of the calculated purity of Maria. Had guilt truly weighed upon her, she would have mastered the art of appearing blameless, composing a face tailored to deflect scrutiny. Instead, whatever turmoil swirled behind her irises, her mother-in-law stared with a ferocity that suggested a desire to excise them entirely.

Lady Castlewood, however, lacked the theatrical cruelty of the monsters in *King Lear*. She did not attempt to pluck her daughter's eyes in the drawing room. Such acts required privacy; she would retreat, wrap her arm possessively around her child's waist, and administer the punishment in solitude.

"So you do not intend to accompany the old lady to Tunbridge Wells?" she asked, masking her intent with a smile of impeccable breeding.

"And small blame to our cousin!" My Lord Castlewood interjected, his voice cutting through the tension. The figure behind the tambour-frame paused, her needle hovering. "A young man cannot dedicate his entire existence to idleness and holidays. He must blend utility with pleasure. Let him attend the concerts and pump-rooms of Tunbridge or Bath later. Mr. Warrington must learn to manage a great estate in America; let him observe how ours are conducted here. Will has already shown him the kennels and stables, and the fashionable pastimes. In autumn, we shall introduce him to English falconry and shooting; in winter, the hunt. Although relations with our aunt-kinswoman in Virginia have cooled, our blood remains the same. Before sending our cousin back to his mother, let us demonstrate the essence of an English gentleman's domestic life. I wish to read with him as much as to sport alongside him, which is precisely why I have pressed him to remain."

His lordship spoke with such transparent sincerity that his mother-in-law, half-brother, and sister were forced to pause in their conspiracy. These three often plotted in the shadows, grumbling against the head of the house. Yet, when he adopted this tone of open benevolence, his meaning became opaque. He did not assert facts; he constructed realities. It was a convention among them that Lord Castlewood harbored a noble desire to cultivate affection and educate his young relative, that he had developed a genuine fondness for the lad, and that Harry should linger near him.

"What is Castlewood's game now?" William whispered to his mother and sister as they slipped away down the corridor. "Wait! By George, I have it!"

"What, William?"

"He intends to manipulate him into gambling, to win the Virginia estate back from him. That is the plot."

"But the lad holds no claim to the Virginia estate if he loses," his mother noted.

"If my brother lacks a scheme, may I—"

"Hush! Of course he has a scheme. What is it?"

"He cannot mean Maria—she is as old as Harry's mother," Mr. William mused.

"Nonsense! With her aged visage, sandy hair, and freckled complexion! Impossible!" Lady Fanny exclaimed, letting out a sigh that betrayed her own suppressed thoughts.

"Of course, your ladyship fancied the Iroquois, too!" his mother retorted sharply.

"I trust I know my station and duty better, madam! Even if I had desired him, that is no justification for marriage. Your ladyship has taught me that lesson well."

"My Lady Fanny!"

"I am certain you married our father without loving him. You have confessed this a thousand times!"

"And if you did not love our father before the vows, you certainly did not fall in love with him afterward," Mr. William interrupted with a laugh. "Fan and I recall how our honored parents fought. Do we not, Fan? And our brother Esmond maintained the peace."

"Do not summon those dreadful, low scenes, William!" his mother cried. "When your father drank excessively, he became a madman; his behavior serves as a warning to you, sir, who share this abhorrent habit."

"I am sure, madam, you were no happier marrying the man you despised, and your ladyship's title brought scant comfort," Lady Fanny whimpered. "What utility is a coronet with the jointure of a tradesman's wife? How many are richer than we? Recently, a grocer's widow from London Bridge moved into our square at Kensington. Her daughters wear three gowns where I possess one. Though served by only a man and two maids, I know they consume and drink a thousand times better than we do, subsisting on scraps of cold meat while our great, flaunting, impudent, lazy lackeys loiter!"

"He! He! Glad I dine at the palace rather than at home!" Mr. Will declared. Through his aunt's influence with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal Powder-Closet, he held a minor post as Deputy Powder.

"Why should I not be content without any title save my own?" Lady Frances continued. "Many are. I dare say they find happiness even in America."

"Yes—with a mother-in-law who is a perfect Turk and Tartar, judging by reports, with Indian war-whoops echoing around you, and with the constant peril of losing your scalp or being devoured by a wild beast whenever you attend church."

"I would not go to church," Lady Fanny stated flatly.

"You would go with anyone who asked, Fan!" Mr. Will roared. "And so would old Maria, and so would any woman, that is the fact." He laughed at his own wit.

"Pray, good folks, what is this merriment?" Madame Bernstein peered in from the tapestried door leading to the gallery where they conversed.

Will explained that his mother and sister were quarreling—a rarity for Madame Bernstein, who knew them well—because Fanny desired to marry their cousin, the wild Indian, and my lady Countess forbade it. Fanny vehemently denied this. Since her mother first commanded her to avoid the young gentleman, she had exchanged fewer than two words with him. She understood her station. She feared neither scalping by wild Indians nor consumption by bears.

Madame de Bernstein looked bewildered. "If he is not staying for you, for whom is he staying?" she asked. "At the houses to which he has been escorted, you have ensured no woman appears before him unless she is either a fright or confined to a nursery. I believe the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid, Will."

"Hmph! That is a matter of taste, ma'am," Mr. William shrugged.

"The taste of Mr. William Esmond, as you say; but not of that boy. The Esmonds of his grandfather's upbringing would not seek courtship in the kitchen."

"Well, ma'am, every man to his taste. A fellow might fare worse than my brother's servants' hall. Besides Fan, there is only the maids or old Maria to choose from."

"Maria! Impossible!" Yet, as the word left her lips, a sudden possibility seized Madame Bernstein. This elderly Calypso might have captivated her young Telemachus. She recalled half a dozen instances in her own experience of youths infatuated with older women. She remembered the frequency of Harry Warrington's recent absences, which she had attributed to field sports. She also recalled how often, during those absences, Maria Esmond was likewise missing. Walks in cool avenues, whispers in garden temples, concealment behind clipped hedges, casual hand-squeezes in twilight corridors, sweet glances and lingering stares on the stairs—all these possibilities, suggesting a lively fancy and intimate worldly knowledge, surged through her mind just as she dismissed them as impossible.

"Impossible, ma'am! I don't know," Will persisted. "My mother warned Fan off him."

"Oh, your mother did warn Fanny?"

"Certainly, my dear Baroness!"

"Did she not? Did she not pinch Fanny's arm black and blue? Did they not fight over it?"

"Nonsense, William! For shame, William!" both implicated ladies cried in unison.

"Now, since we have heard how rich he is, perhaps it is sour grapes. And now, since he is barred from the young bird, perhaps he is hunting the old one. That is all. Why impossible? You know old Lady Suffolk, ma'am?"

"William, how can you speak of Lady Suffolk to your aunt?"

A grin spread across the young gentleman's face. "Because Lady Suffolk was a special favorite at Court? Well, others have succeeded her."

"Sir!" Madame de Bernstein cried, sensing offense.
