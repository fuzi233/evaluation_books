Twelve years had passed since I laid the body of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, to rest in the strange mausoleum he had designed—its heavy door bolted only from within, his casket left open beneath the dim light of Richmond’s old cemetery.

For over a decade, I had turned his final instructions over in my mind, just as I had revisited the pages of his impossible manuscript—the tale of a man who claimed no childhood, whose years stretched beyond memory. A man who swore he had lived ten years on Mars, fighting beside and against its savage green warriors, winning the heart of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and standing as a prince of Helium’s royal house.

And then, twelve years ago, his body had been found on the bluffs above the Hudson. Dead—or so the world believed. But I had always wondered. Had he somehow returned to that dying red planet? Had he reached Barsoom in time to save its people from suffocation, as his story foretold? Had he found Dejah Thoris waiting, or had he arrived too late, condemned to wander a corpse-strewn wasteland?

Or was he truly gone—lost to both Earth and Mars forever?

I was adrift in these thoughts one sweltering August evening when my servant, Ben, handed me a telegram. It bore only six words:

—*John Carter*

By dawn, I was on a train. Within hours, I stood at the threshold of his room.

The door opened.

There he was—unchanged. Not a line deepened on his face, not a shade dimmed in his steel-gray eyes. He might have been thirty, as he had been when I first knew him, a lifetime ago.

“Well, nephew,” he said, grinning. “Do I look like a ghost, or have you been at Ben’s mint juleps?”

“Juleps,” I admitted, “but seeing you helps.” I stepped forward, gripping his arm. “You’ve been back. To Mars. Dejah Thoris—did you find her?”

His smile softened. “I have walked Barsoom’s sands again. But the tale is long, and my time here is short.” He gestured to a thick portfolio on the table. “I’ve learned the secret, you see. The void between worlds is no longer a barrier. I may cross it at will—though my heart remains on Mars, with her.”

He looked at me then, something solemn in his gaze. “I came because I wanted to see you once more. To say goodbye. You’ll pass into that final mystery someday—one I’ve brushed against, yet never understood. Not even the therns, with all their ancient wisdom, hold the truth of it. I nearly died learning that.”

He tapped the portfolio. “This is for you. Share what you will, but don’t grieve if the world laughs. They aren’t ready to believe—not yet.”

That night, we walked together to the cemetery. At the vault’s entrance, he clasped my hand.

“Goodbye,” he said. “I may not return. My wife and son live on Barsoom, where lifetimes stretch for centuries. I won’t leave them again.”

Then the door sealed behind him. The bolts slid home.

I never saw him after that.

But in his notes, I found the story—his second journey to Mars, more incredible than the first. A tale of battles, of loss and triumph, of his relentless search for Dejah Thoris.

Some of it, I’ve set down here.

Much, I dare not tell.

The night was cold and clear as I stood on the bluff overlooking the Hudson, its waters flowing like a silent ghost beneath me. Ten years had passed since I last felt the pull of Mars—ten years of waiting, hoping, pleading with outstretched arms toward the red eye of that distant world. And now, as I raised my hands once more beneath the stars, the old longing surged through me with unbearable force.

A wave of nausea struck without warning. My vision blurred; my knees buckled. I collapsed at the edge of the bluff, my mind reeling—until suddenly, clarity returned. Memories of that Arizona cave, of my lifeless body lying within, flooded back. The same paralyzing dread gripped me, as if the unseen horrors of that place had followed me across the void. With a desperate effort, I wrenched free from the invisible bonds.

Then came the familiar snap—the severing of an unseen tether—and I stood beside my own empty shell, freed once more. Without hesitation, I turned my gaze to Mars, arms raised, and waited.

The transition was instant. The same biting cold, the same abyssal darkness—and then light. Blinding, searing light. I found myself sprawled on a carpet of crimson grass beneath a towering forest canopy, the air thick with the perfume of alien blooms. Strange trees, their trunks smooth as polished metal, stretched skyward, their leaves shimmering in hues no earthly palette could replicate.

But it was the sea that seized my attention—an endless expanse of blue stretching beyond the trees, its waves glittering beneath a bronze sun. Mars? It had to be. Yet nothing here resembled the barren wastes I knew. This was a world of cultivated beauty, of deliberate design.

Then I saw them.

Creeping across the meadow were creatures out of nightmare. Ten feet tall, their ghastly blue skin stretched over grotesquely humanoid frames. A single, lidless eye stared from their foreheads, white as death. Where a mouth should have been gaped only a ragged hole, raw and inflamed. Their hands writhed like serpents, tipped with razor talons—and from their armpits dangled smaller versions of themselves, twitching like malignant growths.

They moved with eerie purpose, their tendril-like fingers combing the grass. I tensed, torn between flight and fascination—until a shriek split the air.

The creatures froze. Their hair—if it could be called hair—rose like a nest of worms, quivering. Then, as one, they bounded toward the cliffs, their kangaroo-like leaps covering yards in a single stride. Against all caution, I followed.

What I found was a slaughter.

A handful of green warriors—two males, four females—stood back-to-back, their swords flashing as the plant men descended. The beasts fought like nothing I’d ever seen. One leaped high, its whip-like tail crushing a warrior’s skull midair. Another’s strike felled two females in a single blow. The last green warrior fought like a demon, carving a path toward the trees—but the horde closed in.

I didn’t think. I moved.

Snatching a fallen blade, I charged. The plant men hissed as my sword cleaved through them, their viscous green blood spraying like sap. The warrior turned, eyes widening—then grinned, his own blade joining mine in a lethal dance.

We fought shoulder-to-shoulder, the tide turning—until another shriek echoed above us.

On a ledge high on the cliffs stood a figure, screaming, pointing—not at us, but at the horizon. My stomach dropped. Across the meadow, the forest, the river, more were coming. Hundreds. And with them, new horrors loped on all fours, their speed terrifying.

“A fine death,” I muttered.

The warrior laughed, teeth gleaming. “Then let it be worthy of *John Carter*.”

I stared.

Before me stood Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark—my oldest friend, and the greatest warrior of Barsoom.

Tars Tarkas and I had no time to speak of what had brought us to this grim valley. Around us lay the twisted corpses of our attackers, but the eerie cry from the cliffs above had summoned reinforcements—a tide of nightmarish creatures surging toward us from every direction.

“To the cliffs!” Tars Tarkas roared. “Our only chance is to find shelter—a ledge, a crevice, anything!”

We sprinted across the crimson meadow, my strides shortened to match his. Behind us, the horde closed in, their shrieks and snarls filling the air. Three hundred yards of open ground lay between us and the sheer rock face, and as we ran, I scanned the cliffs for any sign of refuge.

The barrier of stone rose like a fortress, unbroken save for the lone figure still wailing his unnatural call. No cracks, no handholds—just smooth, impassable rock. To the right, the cliffs vanished into a dense forest, its emerald canopy brushing against the stone. To the left, the wall stretched unyielding into the horizon, merging with distant peaks. The river burst from the cliff’s base, but its path offered no escape.

Then the sun crested the cliffs.

Light struck the stone, and in an instant, the dull surface blazed to life—veins of gold, clusters of rubies, pockets of diamond glittering like a scattered trove. But what seized my attention were the dark gaps high above, barely visible beneath the forest’s edge. Caves.

“There!” I pointed.

Tars Tarkas grimaced. The trees were our only path upward, but Martians were no climbers. Still, we had no choice.

We veered toward the nearest trunk, the creatures at our heels. The fastest among them—a monstrous plant-man—lunged, his barbed limbs stretching for my throat. I spun, blade flashing, but the creature twisted, its whip-like tail slamming me to the ground. Before I could rise, it was on me, its many mouths seeking my flesh. I seized two writhing tendrils, muscles straining as I fought to keep its fangs from my neck.

Then I saw them.

Behind the plant-man, a wave of white apes surged forward—fifteen-foot-tall horrors, their too-human faces twisted in fury. I knew them too well. They were death incarnate.

The horde crashed over me. Fangs pierced my skin; cold, sucking lips fastened on my veins. I writhed free, my sword carving a bloody path, but for every beast I felled, three more took its place.

Tars Tarkas dropped from the branches, his great frame landing beside me. Back to back, we fought—two warriors against an army. Step by step, we retreated until our backs pressed against the tree’s massive trunk.

Then Tars Tarkas spotted it—a narrow opening at the base.

“Go!” he commanded.

I refused. “Not without you.”

He snarled, but there was no time to argue. As the creatures swarmed, I covered his escape, my blade a whirlwind of steel. When he was through, I leapt after him, just as the plant-men dragged me down. Tars Tarkas’ hands locked around my ankles, hauling me inside as I drove my sword through the last foe’s heart.

Breathless and bleeding, we listened as the beasts howled outside. The tree’s hollow interior offered scant refuge, but as my eyes adjusted, I saw it—a shaft leading upward, rungs carved into the wood.

We climbed.

Five hundred feet later, we emerged onto a trembling branch, its end dipping toward a narrow ledge and a waiting cave. One by one, we crossed, Tars Tarkas anchoring me as I scrambled onto the stone. Then, with a leather strap, I pulled him to safety.

Below, the valley sprawled—a sea of scarlet and emerald, watched over by the golden cliffs. For a fleeting moment, I thought I saw the glint of a spire amidst the trees, but it was gone before I could be sure.

The cave beckoned.

Inside, the tunnel stretched into darkness, its walls smooth, its path straight. We advanced, hands clasped to avoid separation, until a door barred our way. A press of my palm against its surface found the hidden mechanism, and it swung open.

Light spilled from within.

We stepped inside—and the door slammed shut behind us, locking with a click.

From the shadows, laughter rang out, cold and mocking.

The echoes of that terrible laughter still clung to the chamber’s walls like a curse as Tars Tarkas and I stood motionless, blades at the ready. Silence stretched between us, thick and suffocating, broken only by the distant drip of unseen water.

The Thark’s laughter was low, a sound that would chill any Earthman’s blood—not from fear, but from the grim amusement his kind took in the face of horror. I had seen it before: green Martians roaring with mirth as women and children writhed in torment during their barbaric games.

I forced a grin, though my grip on my sword tightened. "What in the name of all Barsoom is this place?"

His eyes gleamed in the dim light. "You mean you do not know?"

"I know only that I stand on Barsoom—though it feels nothing like the world I left behind."

His voice turned bitter. "After you vanished from the atmosphere plant, all believed you had taken the pilgrimage down the River Iss. To the Valley Dor, where the dead find peace. Or so the legends claim."

A cold weight settled in my chest. "Dejah Thoris—she lives?"

"She lives," he confirmed.

Relief warred with dread. "Then where are we?"

His answer was a blade of ice. "Where I hoped to find you—and another. The Valley Dor, John Carter. The promised Heaven of Barsoom."

The irony in his voice was sharper than any sword. This was no paradise. It was a slaughterhouse.

I thought of the countless souls who had journeyed here, lured by the lie of eternal rest, only to be torn apart by the very creatures we had fought. A legend spoke of one who returned, warning of monsters in the valley—but he had been slain for blasphemy.

Now we knew the truth.

A hollow voice slithered from the shadows: *"There is no hope. The dead return not."*

Then another, shrieking like a damned soul: *"Fools! Would you cheat Issus of her due?"*

Tars Tarkas growled. "Do we fight phantoms?"

"No," I said. "Only cowards who hide in the dark."

The chamber answered with a snarl. A banth, ten-legged and ravenous, lunged from nowhere. My blade found its heart—just as another beast sprang for the Thark.

They came like ghosts, materializing from the walls. But I had seen the trick.

"Watch the far wall," I hissed.

A mirror on Tars Tarkas’ harness showed the truth: a hidden door, rotating silently. Behind it, a white-skinned man with yellow hair pulled levers, unleashing horrors upon us.

Red Martians stood chained beside him, prisoners—or accomplices.

I gave the signal. We struck as the door turned, bursting into the adjoining chamber. The white man drew his pistol, but my sword sent it clattering away.

Steel rang as we dueled, his face twisting in fury. "Who are you?" he spat.

I gambled. "What if I were from the Temple of Issus?"

Fear flashed in his eyes. "Fate forfend!"

His hesitation cost him his life.

But his comrade struck from behind. Exhausted, I barely parried in time. The fight was a blur—until my heel caught on the first man’s corpse, sending me crashing to the stone.

Pain cleared my mind. My hand closed on cold metal—a discarded pistol.

The next move was mine.

The clash of battle dragged me back to consciousness. Disoriented, I blinked at the dim chamber around me—the chains, the prisoners, the savage beasts frozen in wary silence. Then the sounds came again: the scrape of claws, the snarls of banths, the ragged breathing of a warrior. Beyond the wall, Tars Tarkas fought for his life.

I staggered to my feet. The prisoners watched me—some with hatred, others with desperate hope. Among them, a young red Martian woman stood out, her copper skin gleaming in the faint light. Her eyes held intelligence, defiance. She had warned me earlier, saved me from an ambush.

"The door won’t yield to force," she called as I raised my sword against the golden panel. "The mechanism obeys a touch, not steel."

I turned. "You know how to open it?"

"Free me, and I will."

The keys were on the corpse of the fallen guard. She selected one with practiced ease, and the shackles at her waist fell away. Without hesitation, she pressed a needle-like key into a nearly invisible seam. The wall pivoted, and the floor beneath me lurched forward, carrying me into chaos.

Tars Tarkas stood battered but unbroken, his back to the corner, facing six snarling banths. His green hide was ribboned with wounds, his strength fading—yet he grinned as I entered.

Before I could strike, the woman stepped past me, unarmed, and uttered a single word.

The banths froze.

Another command, and they slunk to her heels like chastened hounds. One by one, she led them through the panel and sealed it behind her, leaving us in stunned silence.

Tars Tarkas exhaled. "I heard your fight," he rasped. "But the gunshot… I thought you dead."

I told him of the therns I’d slain. Together, we searched for an exit, but the walls defied us—until the panel slid open once more. The woman returned, her gaze sharp.

"Who are you," she demanded, "to defy the Valley Dor?"

"John Carter of Helium," I said. "And this is Tars Tarkas, a jeddak. We seek no death here—only escape."

Her lips curled. "Escape? The therns rule this hell. They eat the flesh of those who die in their traps. I was to be their next meal."

I recoiled. "Then help us. Expose their lies."

For a heartbeat, she hesitated. Then, with a nod, she freed the other prisoners—ten red Martians, weary but resolute. Armed with stolen blades and revolvers, we followed her through twisting passages, climbing toward a storeroom of weapons.

But fate intervened.

In a vast chamber, we faced a golden-circled thern, his scalp crowned with a wig of yellow locks. Thuvia shot him dead without hesitation.

"Look," she breathed, stripping the wig from his bald skull. "You wear his face now."

The disguise was flawless. Clad in the thern’s robes, I became Sator Throg, a Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle. The prisoners carried the corpse as we pressed on, arming ourselves in the storeroom before exhaustion claimed me.

As I collapsed onto the cold floor, Thuvia’s voice echoed in my fading thoughts:

"The therns are everywhere. Even if we leave this mountain, their reach is endless."

Sleep took me. The battle was far from over.

I awoke abruptly, disoriented, the echoes of gunfire still ringing in my ears. The cries of alarm had jerked me from exhausted slumber, and for a moment, I couldn’t recall where I was—until the scene before me snapped into focus.

A dozen therns stood at the far end of the storeroom, their rifles lowered, their faces twisted in shock and fear. Around me lay the bodies of my companions, cut down in their sleep. Only Thuvia and Tars Tarkas had survived, as I had, by sheer luck—we had been sprawled on the floor, out of the initial line of fire.

I seized the moment.

**“What is the meaning of this?”** I roared, my voice laced with fury. **“Do you dare raise weapons against Sator Throg?”**

One of the therns fell to his knees. **“Mercy, Master of the Tenth Cycle!”** he begged. The others edged toward the door, desperate to flee.

Thuvia’s whisper was urgent. **“Make them explain.”**

**“Why are you here?”** I demanded.

**“We hunt intruders,”** one stammered. **“A white man with black hair, and a green warrior.”** His gaze flicked suspiciously to Tars Tarkas.

Thuvia stepped forward, cool and poised. **“Then your hunt is over. The green one stands before you. And as for the other…”** She gestured to the corpse by the door—the real Sator Throg. **“We have done your work for you. In reward, Sator Throg granted us our freedom. And now you have slaughtered his slaves.”**

The therns shifted uneasily. Thuvia pressed her advantage.

I nodded. **“Do as she says.”**

As they gathered the dead, one man hesitated over Sator Throg’s body. His eyes darted to me, then back to the bald, lifeless face in his arms. I saw suspicion flicker—and then, as he turned away, the faintest smirk of triumph.

He knew.

The moment the last thern vanished, Thuvia turned to us. **“We must move. Now.”**

**“He’ll report us,”** I said.

**“Not directly. But he will go to Matai Shang. And once the Father of Therns hears…”** She didn’t need to finish.

I assessed our options. My wounds ached, my strength was depleted. On Barsoom, I’d have had access to the green Martians’ healing salves. Here, I had nothing but pain and fatigue.

Then the long, golden hair of the Holy Thern brushed my face.

**“This disguise might still serve us,”** I muttered. **“How long before the alarm is raised?”**

Thuvia’s expression was grim. **“There is no escape. The therns have fortified this place for millennia. The outer slopes are a labyrinth of death. The temples swarm with warriors. The gardens are patrolled. The ramparts are watched.”**

Tars Tarkas growled. **“Then we go by night.”**

Thuvia shook her head. **“The guards are fewer, but the banths prowl after dark. And the sentries never sleep.”**

**“What choice do we have?”** I said.

She led us through winding passages until we reached an opening overlooking the Valley Dor. The sun was setting, a bloody orb sinking behind the mountains. Below, the Holy Thern on his balcony pulled his scarlet robes tighter against the coming cold.

As the light faded, the valley transformed. The plant men emerged, grotesque silhouettes pruning the trees with their talons. Then the river Iss disgorged a canoe of doomed souls—red Martians, cultured and proud, stepping onto the crimson sward.

The herald’s wail pierced the air.

The plant men attacked.

I turned away, sickened.

**“The white apes will finish what they start,”** Thuvia said softly.

Darkness fell like a blade. No twilight, only sudden, suffocating blackness—until the moons rose, casting eerie light across the cliffs.

Thuvia guided us upward through the labyrinthine corridors. Twice, banths blocked our path, but a single word from her sent them slinking back into the shadows.

**“You have power over them,”** I remarked.

She shuddered. **“When Sator Throg threw me to the banths, they did not kill me. They obeyed. So he made me their keeper.”**

An idea struck me. **“Could we use them? Release them above to sow chaos?”**

She smiled faintly and began a low, purring call.

From the darkness, eyes gleamed. One by one, the great beasts joined us—fifty or more, padding silently beside and behind us, their hunger palpable. It was a surreal procession: Thuvia at the lead, the banths like an honor guard, Tars Tarkas towering above, and me, crowned with the stolen diadem of a Holy Thern.

We passed through chambers of horrors—hybrid creatures, malformed and blind, sprawled like damned souls in some hellish tableau. The banths sniffed, but Thuvia’s command held them back.

**“Why no therns?”** I asked.

At last, we reached the upper galleries. Thuvia paused before a steep incline.

**“Beyond this door lie the inner gardens. From here to the outer ramparts—four miles—every step is watched. Guards patrol the courts, the temples, the walls.”**

**“Who do they fear so much?”** I asked.

She opened the door. The scent of growing things washed over us. The banths bolted past, vanishing into the night.

Then—a cry. A shout of alarm from the temple roofs, spreading like wildfire.

Tars Tarkas drew his sword. Thuvia pressed close to me.

The hunt had begun.

Thuvia’s hand tightened on my arm as she pointed upward. My gaze followed her gesture, and there, against the twin moons of Mars, dark shapes flitted like carrion birds—silent, swift, and deadly.

“The Shadow Horde,” she whispered.

Before I could speak, the night erupted. Blasts of energy split the air as the black pirates opened fire from their hovering craft. The therns answered in kind, their rifles flashing from temple ramparts. The battle had begun.

The pirates descended in tightening spirals, their ships—small, agile things built for speed—darting like knife-wings through the chaos. Larger vessels circled high above, raining explosives upon the marble spires below. Then, as if on some unseen signal, the raiders plunged recklessly into the gardens, their ships skimming the ground as their crews leapt into the fray.

What followed was slaughter, pure and primal.

The black warriors moved with a grace that belied their brutality. Their skin gleamed like polished onyx under the moonlight, their features sharp and regal—yet their eyes burned with a hunger for carnage. They fought not as soldiers, but as demons unleashed, their curved blades drinking deep of thern blood.

I watched, transfixed, as the battle unfolded. Golden-haired therns fell beneath ebony swords. A pirate dragged a screaming girl toward his ship, only to be cut down by a dozen defenders. Another seized a statue of ruby, shattering it against a foe’s skull. The gardens, once serene, ran red.

Thuvia’s voice pulled me back. “This is why the therns keep a million warriors at their borders,” she said. “The Horde comes like this, always—never to conquer, only to reap.”

“Why not destroy their ships?” I demanded, eyeing the unguarded fliers.

She shook her head. “They tried, once. The Horde returned with a fleet that blotted out the moons. The therns hid in the depths for a full cycle before the shadow passed.”

A new terror erupted as the banths, our liberated beasts, joined the chaos. The great predators tore through friend and foe alike, their roars drowning even the clash of steel. The battle lines shattered into panicked flight.

*Now.* If ever there was a moment to escape, it was this.

I seized Thuvia’s wrist and signaled Tars Tarkas. We sprinted for the nearest flier—a small two-man craft. But as we scrambled aboard, I realized our fatal error. The Thark’s massive weight anchored us. The repulsion tanks couldn’t lift us all.

The pirates spotted us. With howls of triumph, they charged.

No time. I jammed the controls to full thrust, then leapt clear as the flier lurched upward. Thuvia’s cry—“My Prince!”—was cut short as the ship soared away.

Now, alone, I faced the horde.

My blade became a whirlwind. I fought as only a son of Earth could—with strength these Martians had never seen. They fell back, wary. One snarled, “You fight like a Dator. Pity your blood is pale.”

I grinned, bloody and reckless. “I’m no thern.”

Then—impact. Something heavy struck my back. I staggered, turned, and saw the answer to my prayers: an anchor line, dangling from a cruiser above. Without hesitation, I vaulted over the pirates, caught the chain, and swung into the void.

The ship banked hard, carrying me over the cliffs, out above the Lost Sea of Korus. I climbed, hand over hand, until I grasped the rail—

And looked straight into the eyes of a black pirate, his smile sharp as a dagger.

“Welcome,” he hissed.

The deck swallowed me in darkness.

The black pirate and I locked eyes in a frozen instant, two predators sizing each other up. Then his lips twisted into a savage grin as his ebony hand emerged from the deck’s edge, the muzzle of his revolver pressing cold against my forehead.

I moved faster than thought. My free hand shot out, clamping around his throat like a vice. His hiss—*"Die, cursed thern"*—turned to a choked gurgle as my fingers crushed his windpipe. The hammer fell on an empty chamber.

Before he could react, I wrenched him forward, forcing him to release the rail or be dragged over. His face purpled, eyes bulging. Desperate, he thrashed—then made his fatal mistake. He threw himself backward, clawing at my grip.

That was all the opening I needed.

With a heave, I sent him spinning into the void. His weight nearly tore me after him, but I clung to the anchor chain, watching his silhouette vanish into the abyss below. No scream escaped him. Good.

---

I hauled myself onto the deck, scanning the scene under the glow of the farther moon. Six or eight black warriors sprawled in sleep, their forms slack. Near a rapid-fire gun, bound and wide-eyed, a young woman stared at me—not with fear, but with sudden, blazing hope.

Her gaze flicked to the jewel in my stolen headpiece. She didn’t speak. A slight tilt of her chin warned me: *Beware the sleepers.*

I crept to her side. As I bent close, her whisper was a blade in the silence: *"Free me. I’ll fight with you."*

*"Some won’t wake at all,"* I murmured, slicing her bonds.

Her answering smile was a razor’s edge. Beautiful, yes—but beauty honed to cruelty. She held out a hand. *"A revolver. I’ll cover what your sword misses."*

I armed her and turned to my grim work.

---

The first two never woke. The third’s dying shriek tore through the night.

Then chaos erupted.

Five pirates surged to their feet. Phaidor’s revolver barked—one dropped. The rest charged me, blades flashing. Steel rang against steel, sparks flying in the dark. I fought like a cornered wolf, parrying, striking, driving my sword through a chest until the hilt lodged in bone.

Disarmed, I faced the last—a grinning brute who thought fists would finish me.

He learned otherwise.

My right hook snapped his head back. He crumpled.

Behind me, Phaidor laughed—a sound like silver dipped in venom. *"You’re no thern,"* she said. *"No Barsoomian fights like that. Who are you?"*

*"John Carter, Prince of Helium."* I eyed her. *"And you?"*

She paused, weighing me. *"Phaidor. Daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the therns."*

A groan interrupted us. The black pirate I’d knocked out stirred. I bound him swiftly.

*"Why not kill him?"* Phaidor tilted her head, as if asking why I’d spare a scrap of food.

*"I’m no executioner,"* I said.

She frowned, uncomprehending.

---

The bound man—Xodar, as he named himself—watched us with a smirk. When Phaidor demanded his origin, his pride flared. *"Xodar, Dator of the First Born. We are the oldest race, the true immortals."*

He spun a tale of Barsoom’s dawn: of the Tree of Life, of plant men and ancient wars. But his eyes betrayed him—darting past me, just once.

I whirled.

A battleship loomed in our wake, silent and deadly, closing fast.

The game had changed again.

I understood then why the black pirate had spun his strange tale—to distract me. For miles, he had sensed the approach of reinforcements. Had I not caught that fleeting glance toward the sky, the battleship would have been upon us, its boarding party descending like a swarm of deadly insects, extinguishing my fragile hope of escape.

But I was no novice in the dance of aerial combat. In an instant, I reversed the engines and sent our vessel plummeting a hundred feet. Above, the boarding party dangled from the battleship’s hull as it roared past. Then I angled sharply upward, thrusting the speed lever to its limit. My ship became a projectile, its steel prow aimed at the whirring propellers of the giant above.

If I could cripple them, we might yet slip free.

Dawn broke, revealing a hundred black faces peering over the battleship’s stern. A chorus of rage erupted. Orders were barked—too late. We struck the propellers with a rending crash. I reversed engines again, but our prow was lodged in the torn metal. For a heartbeat, we hung there—long enough for the enemy to swarm our deck.

There was no battle. We were simply overwhelmed.

Blades flashed toward me, but a command from Xodar stayed them. "Bind them," he ordered, "but do no harm."

His men obeyed, though the bonds they wrapped around my wrists might as well have been thread. Had I been Martian, they would have held. But Earth-born muscles could snap them when the time came.

Phaidor was bound beside me, our fates lashed together.

Soon, we were hauled aboard the battleship—a monstrous engine of war, crewed by a thousand black warriors. Their eyes gleamed with cruel fascination as they studied us, their jeers thick with vulgarity. Phaidor’s beauty drew particularly crude remarks, a stark contrast to the chivalry of Barsoom’s red men.

Xodar’s officers crowded close, intrigued by my origin. My close-cropped hair and thern-like complexion puzzled them, but the harness of a slain thern on my back suggested I was no friend of their ancient enemies. That earned me a sliver of respect.

The ship itself was a spectacle—gleaming decks of polished skeel wood, cabins inlaid with jewels, and officers adorned in harnesses crusted with gems. The commander’s trappings blazed with diamonds, a dazzling display against his ebony skin.

Zat Arras. The name alone was a curse. The Jed of Zodanga had long coveted Dejah Thoris, and now, with her father and grandfather absent, his ambition had turned to predation.

Kantos Kan’s voice dropped lower. **"He commands this fleet. And he has ordered your arrest."**

---

The flagship loomed like a steel claw against the sky. Zat Arras awaited us, his face a mask of cold triumph. **"Why are these men armed?"** he snapped.

**"They are not prisoners,"** Kantos Kan countered. **"Two are of Helium’s royal blood. The others are allies."**

Zat Arras’ lips curled. **"Then let them answer. John Carter—where have you been?"**

**"From the Valley Dor,"** I said. **"From the lies of Iss. I returned to save Barsoom from the slaughter hidden beneath its myths."**

**"Blasphemer!"** he roared. **"You think to escape death with lies?"**

The word *liar* was a spark to tinder. Before anyone could react, I had him by the throat, bending him backward over my knee. **"Call me that again,"** I growled.

His guards surged forward, but Kantos Kan’s whisper stayed my hand. **"A mutiny now will fracture Helium. For Dejah Thoris’ sake—stop."**

I released Zat Arras, leaving him gasping. **"We return to the *Xavarian*,"** I said, turning my back on him.

---

The flight to Helium was silent. Below, the capital’s towers blazed scarlet in the sun, but the streets seethed with unrest. Crowds packed the avenues, their faces torn between adoration and dread.

We were taken not through the cheering throngs, but to the Temple of Reward—where justice was both given and taken.

Zat Arras dared not let the people near us. He knew their love might outweigh their fear.

As night fell, the message came: **"You will stand trial at dawn."**

Through the temple window, I watched the silent masses below. Some wept. Others prayed.

And I? I saw only Dejah Thoris—lost, alone, prey to the horrors I had barely escaped.

Dawn would bring judgment.

But first, it would bring war.

The morning sun cast long shadows through the arched corridors of Helium as a detachment of Zodangan guards arrived at our quarters. Their armor gleamed coldly in the pale light, their faces set in grim resolve. They were not escorts—they were jailers.

We marched in pairs down the Aisle of Hope, flanked by rows of Zodangan soldiers, their spears a bristling wall of hostility. The great hall of the Temple of Reward loomed before us, its vaulted ceiling echoing with the murmurs of thousands. The air was thick with tension, with the unspoken understanding that justice here would be anything but blind.

The judges awaited us—thirty-one nobles, their expressions unreadable. Not a single friendly face among them. Zodangans, all. Men whose city had fallen beneath the swords of Tharks, whose pride had been shattered by the very man now standing trial. John Carter, Prince of Helium, was not walking into a court. He was walking into vengeance.

The crowd fell silent as we took our places upon the Throne of Righteousness. Zat Arras, his golden circlet gleaming, rose with the smug assurance of a man who already knew the verdict.

"John Carter," he intoned, voice dripping with false solemnity, "stand upon the Pedestal of Truth and hear the crimes for which you must answer."

He turned to the assembly, weaving a tapestry of heresy. "He has returned from the Valley Dor—from the very Temple of Issus! He has spat upon our sacred traditions, mocked the Holy Therns, and defied the Goddess of Death herself. By the ancient law, he who dies must remain dead. To defy this is to invite eternal silence."

A judge’s voice cut through the stillness like a blade. *"Death."*

Then—chaos.

Kantos Kan surged forward, his voice a thunderclap. *"Justice!"* He vaulted past the guards, defiance in every line of his body. "Will you condemn a man without defense? Without witnesses? Is this the law of Helium—or the whim of Zodanga?"

The crowd erupted. *"Justice! Justice!"* The cry became a roar, shaking the very pillars of the temple.

Zat Arras, his face a mask of fury, had no choice. He gestured sharply. "Speak, then. But remember—the sacred things of Barsoom are not to be blasphemed."

I turned not to the judges, but to the people.

"Men of Helium," I called, "do not ask me to seek fairness from those who crave my blood. I speak not for myself, but for *you*. For your wives, your daughters—for all who might one day be dragged into the horrors of Issus’ domain. I have walked the Valley Dor. I have seen the lies behind its divinity. The Therns are not holy—they are butchers. The Temple is not salvation—it is slaughter."

The silence was absolute. Even the judges leaned forward, caught despite themselves.

"You know me," I continued. "You know I do not lie. Would John Carter, who bled for Helium, who fought for her freedom, now spin tales to deceive her people? I ask only this—let me save you, as I once saved you from the sword. Let me tear the veil from your eyes before more are lost to the darkness of Dor."

A noble stood, his voice ringing. *"Speak, Prince of Helium!"* The crowd took up the cry, their will a force even Zat Arras could not deny.

For two hours, I laid bare the truth. The Therns’ cruelty. The false paradise. The fate that awaited all who journeyed south. And when I finished—

Zat Arras rose, his face twisted. "You have heard his blasphemy. The verdict is clear."

*"Death!"* The judges’ swords flashed upward in unison.

But the people of Helium had other ideas.

Kantos Kan tore his scabbard free and flung his blade at my feet. "There is *my* judgment."

Like a tide, they came—soldiers, nobles, citizens—pushing past Zodangan spears, their own swords clattering before me. A hundred. Then more. The hall trembled with the weight of rebellion.

Zat Arras screamed orders, but his voice was lost. The crowd’s fury was a living thing, hungry and barely leashed.

I raised a hand. *"Enough."* The word carried, and against all odds, they listened. "One drop of blood spilled here will drown Helium in war. I will not be its cause." I turned to Zat Arras, my voice low. "Take your victory. But mark this—if you value your life, declare a stay. A year. No more."

His jaw worked, but he was no fool. He knew the mob would tear him apart if he refused.

"By my mercy," he spat, "the sentence is delayed! One year—or until Tardos Mors returns. Now *go*."

No one moved.

It was *my* nod that finally parted the crowd. They left, but their anger lingered like smoke in the air.

Kantos Kan gripped my shoulder. "To your palace, my Prince. Let Zodanga choke on its triumph."

We marched through the streets, a procession of defiance. Blades rained at my feet, a silent oath from the people of Helium.

And then—a cry from the gates. A messenger. A thoat charging through the crowd.

Sola.

Her face was streaked with dust and desperation. She stumbled from her mount, her voice breaking.

"Dejah Thoris—they took her! The black pirates of Barsoom have stolen your princess!"

The world went silent.

Gone.

Not dead.

Once inside the palace, I guided Sola to the dining hall. After she exchanged the traditional greetings of the green Martians with her father, she began the tale of Dejah Thoris’ doomed pilgrimage.

"Seven nights past," Sola began, her voice low, "after her audience with Zat Arras, the Princess sought to flee the palace under cover of darkness. Though I did not know what had transpired between them, the anguish in her eyes spoke plainly enough. When I found her slipping through the shadows, I needed no words to understand her intent."

"Swiftly, I gathered twelve of her most loyal guards. They did not hesitate—each vowed to follow her, even to the cursed Valley Dor. We overtook her beyond the palace walls. Woola was at her side, but no others. She feigned fury, commanding us to return, but for the first time, we defied her. When she saw our resolve, her pride crumbled. She wept, embracing us, and together we turned southward into the unknown."

"For four days, we rode hard upon a herd of wild thoats, eating little, resting less. Then, at dawn on the fifth day, we saw them—a black swarm of warships blotting the horizon. Before we could hide, they descended like carrion birds. The guards fought to the last, but they were butchered without mercy. Only Dejah Thoris and I were spared."

"The Princess tried to fall upon her dagger, but one of the blacks wrenched it from her grasp. They bound us, rendering our hands useless. The fleet pressed northward—twenty battleships, flanked by swift scouts. That evening, one of their cruisers returned with another captive: a red-skinned woman snatched from the hills under the noses of three Heliumite warships."

"From their talk, we gathered they hunted fugitives who had escaped them days before. Their commander questioned her at length, then cast her into our cell. She was Thuvia of Ptarth, a princess who had taken the voluntary pilgrimage years ago. When she learned Dejah Thoris’ identity, she fell to her knees, kissing her fettered hands."

"Thuvia spoke of you, John Carter—how she had stood beside you and Carthoris that very morning. She told of your battles in the First Born’s domain, of your loyalty to Dejah Thoris. And when she confessed her love for you, the Princess wept—not in jealousy, but in grief for the cruel fate that had torn her from Helium so near your return."

"‘I do not blame you,’ Dejah Thoris told her. ‘Your heart is true.’"

"The fleet veered south when they realized you had eluded them. That night, a guard dragged me to the deck. ‘The Land of the First Born has no place for a green one,’ he sneered, then hurled me into the void."

Sola’s voice grew taut. "Yet fate spared me. The ship skimmed so low that I struck a thicket barely twenty feet below. By dawn, I saw the truth—I had been cast onto the peaks of a mountain range. The fleet had grazed the crest in the dark, never knowing their throw failed. I made my way to a Heliumite waterway, secured a thoat, and rode until I found you."

Silence gripped the room. Dejah Thoris—in the claws of Issus. My hands clenched. Then, like a surge of fire, certainty flooded me. I stood, blade raised.

"By my steel, I swear this: I will find her. I will free her. Or I will die."

A hundred swords flashed in answer, a hundred voices roared their allegiance. Carthoris stayed to rally them while I withdrew with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and Hor Vastus. We plotted late into the night.

Xodar assured us Issus would choose both women as handmaidens, buying us time. Kantos Kan and Xodar would refit our fleet with the secret propellers to navigate Omean’s waters. Tars Tarkas would sound the Tharks, rallying their hordes. Hor Vastus would gather an army sworn to my banner.

As we dispersed, a shadow moved on the balcony. We gave chase, but the spy—likely Zat Arras’ creature—vanished like smoke.

"Let him report whispers of a new Jeddak," I said grimly. "But if he heard our plans for Dejah Thoris, then let war come. I will not be stopped."

One by one, they swore upon their blades to stand with me, even against the throne itself.

Exhausted, I retired—only to wake in the grip of masked men. A gag stifled my curses as they hauled me through the palace’s hidden veins, down to the pits beneath. A coded knock, a door swung wide—and there, smirking amid torchlight, sat Zat Arras.

The chamber was cold, the air thick with the weight of impending malice. Zat Arras regarded me with a serpent’s smile, his voice slick with mock courtesy.

“Ah, what brings the Prince of Helium so graciously into my presence?”

The gag was torn from my mouth, but I held my tongue, my gaze unwavering. Contempt curled in my chest, tightening like a fist. The silence stretched, thick as the tension between us, until Zat Arras’ face darkened with fury.

With a flick of his wrist, he dismissed the guards, leaving only his two sycophants and me in the dim-lit chamber. When the door sealed shut, his voice dripped like venom.

“John Carter,” he began, slow and deliberate, savoring each word, “by the law of our faith and the decree of the court, you are condemned. The people cannot save you—*I* alone hold that power. Your life is mine to spare or sever as I choose. None would question it.”

He stepped closer, his breath hot against my face. “Walk free in Helium for a year, and the people will forget your sentence. But know this: Tardos Mors will never return. Nor will Mors Kajak. Nor Dejah Thoris. The throne must be filled.” His eyes gleamed. “Zat Arras *will* be Jeddak. Swear your loyalty, and your chains vanish. Refuse…” He let the threat hang, sharp as a blade.

The choice was a knife in my ribs. Refuse, and Dejah Thoris’ fate would be sealed—her life torn apart in the arena of Issus. But to bow to this traitor? Never.

I met his gaze. “No alliance exists between a prince of Helium and a usurper. I do not believe Tardos Mors is dead.”

Zat Arras laughed, a sound like cracking ice. “Soon, your beliefs will matter as little as your life.” He clapped his hands. “To the pits with him.”

The guards dragged me through winding tunnels, deeper into the bowels of Helium, until they shackled me in a cavernous cell. The walls bore the grim remains of past prisoners—skeletons chained in eternal silence. The last guard took the light, leaving me in darkness so complete it pressed against my skin.

Time blurred. Sleep came fitfully.

Then—a flicker of light. A young man stood before me, bearing a bowl of gruel.

“Zat Arras sends greetings,” he said, his voice flat. “His offer stands. Accept, and walk free.”

I refused.

Days bled into months. The youth returned twice daily, silent as the grave. I scraped at my chains, desperate for escape, for word of Carthoris, of Dejah Thoris—was she still alive?

Then, a gambit. The boy—Parthak—had noble bearing beneath his shabby garb. I tempted him with promises of fine harness and a jeweled blade from my palace, if only he would carry a note to Carthoris.

He hesitated. Then, days later, he brought writing tools.

My message was simple: *Deliver to Parthak what I promised.* If Carthoris obeyed, I would know he lived. If Parthak wore the gifts, Carthoris would know *I* lived.

But Parthak never returned.

Despair gnawed at me. Thirty days remained before Dejah Thoris would face the arena. The image of her torn apart by beasts clawed at my mind—

Then, footsteps. Not Parthak. A new jailer, mute as stone.

Until—

A shadow moved in the dark. I lunged, chain raised—

And froze.

The chain struck, but not fatally. He lived.

“Father,” he gasped, clutching his head.

Relief and guilt crashed over me. He had come through Parthak’s betrayal, through Xodar’s cunning—Hor Vastus had played the Zodangan’s cellmate, extracting my location.

Now, freed, we ascended to my palace, where Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, and the others waited.

The fleet was ready. A thousand battleships at Hastor. A quarter-million warriors from Thark. A million Heliumites sworn to secrecy.

“We sail tonight,” I said.

But gunfire erupted in the gardens—a spy, cut down too late.

Zat Arras knew.

The clock was ticking.

Two hours past dusk, our cruiser sliced through the thin air of Barsoom, leaving the spires of Helium behind. Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I reached Hastor as the twin moons cast their pallid glow over the waiting fleet. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already departed for Thark—our forces divided, yet united in purpose.

The transports stirred first, creeping southward like shadows. The battleships would follow, overtaking them by dawn on the second day.

At Hastor, Kantos Kan’s precision was evident. Within minutes, the first vessel lifted from its dock, and soon the night was alive with the silent ascent of warships, their lines stretching for miles—a serpent of steel ready to strike.

Only in the quiet of Kantos Kan’s cabin did I think to ask the date. His answer struck like a dagger. Three hundred sixty-five days had passed.

I had miscalculated.

Dejah Thoris was beyond rescue.

The weight of it threatened to crush me, but I buried the truth beneath cold resolve. This was no longer a mission of salvation—it was vengeance. Let the therns and the First Born learn the cost of their deception. Let Barsoom see the lies that had damned generations to the horrors of Issus.

If we could tear open the veil of superstition shrouding the Valley Dor, if we could reclaim its fertile lands from the jaws of fanatics and beasts, then her sacrifice would not be in vain.

---

Dawn of the second day revealed the transports, their hulls dark against the ice cap. Signals flickered between ships—coded, cautious. Radio silence was a necessity in war; the therns had ears everywhere.

Our scouts lanced ahead, flanking our formation, a web of watchers. Then—a warning.

Kantos Kan’s jaw tightened. “Zat Arras’ spies did their work well.”

I gave my orders swiftly: ten ships to seal the entrance to Omean. The rest formed a spearhead, a V aimed at the heart of the thern armada. We would carve a path for the transports, unleash the green warriors upon the temples, and let the therns drown in their own blood.

The fleet surged forward.

The enemy’s ragged lines filled the horizon, unprepared for the fury of our charge. Our battleships tore through them, the V widening like jaws. Transports shot through the gap, disgorging warriors into the gilded gardens of the therns.

Above, the sky became a maelstrom.

Kantos Kan’s ships wheeled in a deadly dance, circling the thern fleet, broadsides roaring. The enemy ships fell like stricken birds, their hulls splitting in midair. Yet even as victory seemed certain, a new shadow darkened the north.

Zat Arras.

His message crackled across the void: *“Surrender.”*

The therns, sensing our impending doom, redoubled their assault. Now we fought on two fronts—Zat Arras’ treachery at our backs, the therns before us.

I ordered the transports back to the gardens. “Make them remember this day,” I told the green horde. Then I turned to face Zat Arras’ flagship.

Our vessels clashed, grapplers biting into hulls. I led the boarding party, cutting through Zodangan soldiers until I stood before Zat Arras himself.

“Yield,” I demanded.

For a heartbeat, he hesitated. Then, with a snarl, he flung himself over the rail—into the abyss.

The battle raged on, a chaos of fire and steel. But the tides shifted. The therns and the First Born turned on each other, their ancient hatred boiling over. Seizing the discord, I regrouped our fleet, withdrawing southwest as the two factions tore themselves apart.

Night fell.

We had one chance left: a strike at Issus herself. Xodar would lead the transports to the temple grounds. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I would take the remaining ships through Omean’s depths, emerging beneath her sanctum.

But as we approached the shaft, a new horror awaited.

The First Born’s fleet had returned.

Our ten sentinel ships fled before them, harried and outnumbered. There was no time for caution. We plunged into the black maw of Omean, praying the enemy would mistake us for their own.

Four hundred ships slipped inside before the alarm was raised. The battle was swift—their harbor defenses were lax, their ships outdated. We captured their vessels, jammed them into the shaft to bar the way, and pressed onward.

On the island of the submarine, I found Yersted among the prisoners. His smirk faltered when I asked of Dejah Thoris.

“She lives,” he admitted. “Issus keeps her close—in the Temple of the Sun.”

A spark of hope. The Martian year was longer than Earth’s. I had not failed her yet.

But Yersted’s warning lingered: *“Issus will not give her up. If you set foot in that temple, Dejah Thoris will vanish into its heart—forever.”*

The Temple of the Sun. A revolving tomb. One chance each year to reach its chambers.

And I would tear it apart to find her.

Yersted’s warning left no room for hesitation. Time was slipping through my fingers like sand, and if I were to reach the Temple of Issus before Tars Tarkas’s assault at dawn, I had to move swiftly. The plan was clear: infiltrate its cursed walls, overpower the guards, and free my Princess. With Carthoris and our warriors at my back, victory was within grasp.

We began the arduous task of transporting our forces through the submerged passage leading from the submarine pool to the temple’s hidden gangways. Each trip was a gamble, but discipline held firm, and soon five thousand hardened fighters of Barsoom stood ready—red warriors, bred for war.

Carthoris alone knew the labyrinthine tunnels, forcing us to advance as one. Speed was our ally, but fate had other designs. As we prepared to leave the chamber, an officer pointed to the churning waters. At first, I thought another submarine pursued us—until I realized the truth. The water was rising.

Carthoris’s voice cut through the tension. *“The pumps of Omean have stopped. They mean to drown us. Move!”*

I gave the order, and the column surged forward, but the flood chased us like a living thing. The corridor sloped upward, yet the waters climbed faster, swirling at our ankles, then our knees. Panic flickered in the ranks—Martians feared drowning above all. But discipline held.

Then, disaster.

A branching corridor offered hope. I diverted the rear guard, ordering them into the new passage. The water reached our waists, then our chests. Men stumbled, gasped, vanished beneath the tide. Gur Tus, a stalwart dwar, stood with me as we dragged survivors forward. Djor Kantos joined the effort, and together we held the line until the last warrior passed.

But our respite was short-lived.

A cry echoed ahead—*“Fire!”*—followed by chaos. Flames blocked our path, smoke choking the tunnel. Retreat was impossible; the waters now lapped at our chins. Desperate, I led the survivors into another gallery, praying it led to safety. Two thousand made it through before the fire’s heat forced us back.

Then—betrayal.

A steel grating slammed down behind me, sealing my retreat. The First Born had anticipated every move. Trapped between fire and flood, I faced annihilation. The water rose, the smoke thickened. Only one path remained: dive into the blackness and swim.

The icy current swallowed me. Stroke after stroke, I fought toward what I hoped was salvation. My lungs burned. My vision dimmed. Just as despair took hold, my head broke the surface—air! Gasping, I staggered forward, driven by fury and purpose.

The Temple of Issus loomed ahead. A doorway beckoned. Without hesitation, I burst through—

And froze.

Before me stood not the horror of Issus, but the face I had crossed worlds to find.

Her eyes widened—terror, disbelief, then overwhelming joy. Words failed me. I could only step forward, arms outstretched, as she rushed into my embrace. The galaxy could have crumbled around us, and I would not have noticed.

She was alive.

And now, nothing would tear her from me again.

Dejah Thoris trembled in my arms, her voice a whisper against my shoulder. “John Carter,” she murmured, “even now, I can scarcely believe my eyes. When Thuvia told me you had returned to Barsoom, I dared not hope—not after all these years of silence. And when I learned where I was imprisoned, I feared even you could not reach me.”

Her grip tightened. “Moons passed without word. I had resigned myself. But then—the sounds of battle in the temple. I prayed it was you, that Helium had come for me.”

“Our son was with me,” I told her. “Carthoris leads those men you hear.”

Her eyes darkened. “Where is Issus?”

Dejah Thoris shook her head. “She sent me here before the fighting began. She was afraid—angry. Now I understand why.”

The clash of steel and shouts of combat echoed through the temple halls. I needed to join the fray, but I would not leave her unprotected. Then I remembered the pits beneath us—a temporary refuge.

When I explained my plan, she resisted. “I cannot bear to be parted from you again,” she whispered. “Not here, where that monster might find me.”

But duty prevailed. With a final kiss, I guided her through the hidden door, sealing her inside. The act tore at me, but there was no choice.

---

The battle raged like a storm. Black warriors of the First Born clashed with the red men of Helium in a brutal dance of survival. This was more than a skirmish—it was the turning point of an era. The old ways against the new.

Carthoris fought at my side, his blade as sure as mine. The floor grew slick with blood, the dead piling beneath our feet. Then, through the great windows, salvation appeared.

A tide of green warriors surged across the gardens, their thoats thundering. At their head rode Tars Tarkas, his lance raised high. With a roar, his legion charged. The black line shattered.

The tide had turned.

---

But my heart was elsewhere. I sprinted back to the chamber where I had left Dejah Thoris, Carthoris close behind.

The room was empty.

A silk lay trampled on the floor. A dagger. The door to the pits stood ajar.

I called her name—no answer. Rage and terror warred within me.

“Issus,” I snarled. “This ends now.”

---

The throne room was chaos. Issus cowered upon her dais, her slaves shrinking from her. Before her guards could react, I was upon her, my dagger at her throat.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I demanded.

Her madness answered. Shrieking laughter, spittle flying. “The Temple of the Sun!” she howled. “Her and Thuvia and Phaidor—all together! A year they’ll wait. And when the food runs out—” Her grin was a nightmare.

I nearly killed her then. But a fouler fate awaited.

“See your goddess!” I shouted to the gathered First Born. “A withered crone, not a deity. Take her. Let her people judge her.”

I cast her down, and the vengeful swarm closed in.

---

Xodar led us through smoke-choked halls to the Temple of the Sun—a marvel of gold and gemstones, now a prison. Bars separated me from Dejah Thoris. The chamber was turning, the gap narrowing.

“Stop it!” I demanded.

Xodar vanished to seek the keeper of the mechanism.

Through the bars, Dejah Thoris pressed her hand to mine. “Go,” she urged. “Save yourself.”

Phaidor lurked nearby, her eyes venomous. Then, in a flash—

A dagger. Thuvia lunged. Smoke billowed. A single scream.

The wall sealed shut.

---

They dragged me away, though I fought like a madman. The temple burned. The pits flooded.

But none of it mattered.

Somewhere beyond that cursed wall, Dejah Thoris waited—alive or dead, I did not know.

And Phaidor’s final words haunted me.

Only time would tell.
