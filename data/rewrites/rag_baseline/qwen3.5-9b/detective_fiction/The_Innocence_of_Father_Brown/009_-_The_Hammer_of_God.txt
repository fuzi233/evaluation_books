The village of Bohun Beacon sat atop a precipice so sheer that the church spire rising from its center appeared less a tower and more the jagged summit of a miniature mountain range. At the base of this ecclesiastical peak stood a smithy, perpetually glowing with the heat of furnaces and cluttered with the chaotic debris of forged iron. Directly opposite, separated only by a crude stone cross marking the intersection of cobblestone paths, lay "The Blue Boar," the sole tavern in the settlement. It was at this nexus, bathed in the grey, metallic light of dawn, that two brothers converged—one initiating the day, the other concluding it.

Reverend and Honorable Wilfred Bohun was a man of profound devotion, ascending the path toward austere rites of prayer and contemplation. Beside him, seated on the bench outside the inn in full evening dress, sat Colonel the Honorable Norman Bohun, his elder brother. The Colonel was far from pious; he sat nursing a drink that a philosophical observer might classify as either his final measure of the week or his inaugural sip of the next, entirely indifferent to the distinction.

The Bohuns represented one of the rare aristocratic lineages tracing directly to the Middle Ages, their heraldry having once witnessed the fields of Palestine. Yet, it is a grave error to assume such antiquity guarantees adherence to chivalric ideals. Traditions are the currency of the poor; the wealthy trade exclusively in fashion. The Bohuns had been Mohawks under Queen Anne and dandies under Victoria, but over the last two centuries, like many ancient houses, they had decayed into mere alcoholics and degenerate aesthetes, bordering on madness. The Colonel's predatory pursuit of pleasure possessed a bestial quality, and his habitual refusal to depart until morning suggested a pathological state akin to severe insomnia. He was a tall, formidable specimen, aged yet retaining startlingly golden hair. While his features were naturally leonine, his blue eyes were sunken so deeply they appeared black, set unnervingly close together. Long yellow moustaches framed a face etched with deep furrows running from nostril to jawline, giving his permanent sneer the appearance of a carved groove. Draped over his formal attire was a bizarre, pale yellow coat resembling a dressing gown more than an overcoat, topped with an ostentatious, broad-brimmed hat of vivid green—an Oriental oddity acquired at random. He took immense pride in these incongruities, convinced they somehow harmonized perfectly.

His younger brother, the curate, shared the golden hair and an inherent elegance, though he was clad tightly in black, his face clean-shaven, cultivated, and perpetually anxious. He seemed devoted solely to his faith, though whispers—particularly from the Presbyterian blacksmith—suggested his obsession was actually with Gothic architecture. Critics claimed his ghostly haunting of the church stemmed from a morbid thirst for aesthetic perfection, mirroring his brother's insatiable hunger for women and wine. Whether this charge held truth was debatable, yet the curate's practical piety was undeniable. He frequently knelt not before the altar, but in secluded corners: crypts, galleries, even the belfry.

At that moment, Wilfred paused at the churchyard gate, frowning slightly as he spotted his brother's hollow gaze fixed in the same direction. Assuming the Colonel was drawn to the sacred edifice, Wilfred dismissed the thought. His attention shifted instead to the smithy. Though the blacksmith was a Puritan and thus unlikely to harbor romantic interests, rumors persisted regarding a beautiful, celebrated wife. Wilfred cast a suspicious glance across the shed, and the Colonel rose with a laugh to address him.

"Good morning, Wilfred," the Colonel declared. "Acting as a conscientious landlord, I have watched sleeplessly over my flock. I intend to pay a visit to the blacksmith."

Wilfred looked downward. "The blacksmith is absent. He is currently in Greenford."

"I am aware," the Colonel replied, his laughter silent and knowing. "Precisely why I am seeking him out."

"Norman," Wilfred said, his eyes fixed on a pebble in the road, "have you ever feared thunderbolts?"

"What do you mean?" the Colonel asked. "Has your hobby shifted to meteorology?"

"I mean," Wilfred stated, refusing to raise his eyes, "do you ever consider the possibility that God might strike you down in the street?"

"Pardon me," the Colonel responded. "I perceive your hobby has devolved into folklore."

"I know your hobby is blasphemy," Wilfred retorted, struck in his most vital nerve. "But if you hold no fear of God, you possess ample cause to fear man."

The elder brother arched an eyebrow politely. "Fear man?"

"Barnes the blacksmith is the largest and strongest man within forty miles," Wilfred warned sternly. "I acknowledge you are neither a coward nor a weakling, yet he could easily toss you over the wall."

The words landed with impact, true as they were. The deep lines around the Colonel's mouth and nose darkened, the expression shifting into a heavy sneer. For a fleeting second, he stood frozen. Then, instantly, Colonel Bohun reclaimed his cruel, jovial demeanor, laughing to reveal two canine front teeth beneath his yellow whiskers. "In that event, my dear Wilfred," he remarked casually, "it appears prudent for the last of the Bohuns to emerge partially armored."

He removed the strange, round green hat, revealing it was lined internally with steel. Wilfred recognized it immediately: a lightweight Japanese or Chinese helmet, stripped from a trophy displayed in the ancestral hall.

"It was simply the first hat available," his brother explained breezily. "Always the closest hat—and invariably the closest woman."

"The blacksmith remains in Greenford," Wilfred said quietly. "His return schedule is uncertain."

With that, he turned and entered the church, bowing his head, crossing himself as if attempting to exorcise an impurity. He sought refuge in the cool twilight of the tall Gothic cloisters to purge the grossness of the scene, but fate ensured his morning of spiritual exercise would be interrupted by a series of minor shocks. Upon entering, the church—usually empty at this hour—witnessed a kneeling figure rise hastily to greet the daylight streaming through the doors. The curate halted, surprised. The early worshipper was none other than the village idiot, the blacksmith's nephew, a youth incapable of caring for the church or anything else. Known universally as "Mad Joe," he was a dark, powerful, slouching lad with a pallid face, straight black hair, and a mouth perpetually agape. As he passed the priest, his vacant countenance offered no clue to his thoughts or actions. Never known to pray, what supplications was he uttering now? Extraordinary ones, certainly.

Wilfred stood transfixed long enough to watch the idiot exit into the sunlight, seeing his dissolute brother hail him with feigned affectionate jocularity. The final image registered was the Colonel tossing coins into Joe's open mouth, adopting a solemn air as if attempting to feed the void.

This grim tableau of earthly stupidity and cruelty drove the ascetic to seek purification in prayer. He ascended to a gallery pew situated beneath a stained-glass window he cherished—a blue pane depicting an angel bearing lilies. Here, amidst the cold, sweet hues of silver blossoms and sapphire sky, he attempted to distance himself from the half-wit's fish-like visage and his evil brother, who paced like a starving lion.

Half an hour later, he was discovered by Gibbs, the village cobbler, summoned with urgency. Wilfred rose promptly; he understood that no trivial matter would draw Gibbs to the sanctuary. The cobbler, typically an atheist whose presence in church was already anomalous, moved with a startling respectfulness, his voice husky with sympathy.

"You must forgive me, sir," Gibbs whispered. "We deemed it improper not to inform you immediately. A dreadful occurrence has transpired, sir. I fear your brother..."

Wilfred clenched his frail hands. "What devilry has he committed now?" he cried, consumed by involuntary rage.

"Why, sir," Gibbs coughed, "I fear he has accomplished nothing, and will accomplish nothing further. I fear he is finished. You ought to descend, sir."

The curate followed the cobbler down a short spiral staircase emerging at a level slightly above the street. In one glance, Wilfred perceived the tragedy laid out beneath him like a topographical map. In the smithy yard stood five or six men, mostly dressed in black, including one in an inspector's uniform. Among them were the doctor, the Presbyterian minister, and a priest from the Roman Catholic chapel—the blacksmith's wife's congregation. The Catholic priest spoke urgently to the woman, a magnificent figure with red-gold hair, who wept blindly on a bench. Between these groups, just beyond the main pile of hammers, lay a man in evening dress, sprawled flat on his face. From his vantage point, Wilfred could identify every detail of the victim's costume, down to the Bohun rings on his fingers, yet the skull was reduced to a horrific smear, a star of blackness and blood.

Wilfred cast a single glance and rushed down the steps. The doctor, the family physician, saluted him, receiving barely a nod. He could only stammer, "My brother is dead. What does this signify? What is this terrible mystery?"

An uneasy silence descended. Then Gibbs, the most vocal of the assembly, spoke. "Plenty of horror, sir," he admitted
