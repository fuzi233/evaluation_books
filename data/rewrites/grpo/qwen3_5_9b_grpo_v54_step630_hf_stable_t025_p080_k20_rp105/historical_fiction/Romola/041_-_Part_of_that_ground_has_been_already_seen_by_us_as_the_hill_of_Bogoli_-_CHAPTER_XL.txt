The stone beneath Romola's back offered a cool anchor against the heat of her flight. Here, beneath the swaying cypress, the world felt vast and unclaimed. The familiar grid of Florence's walls receded behind her, replaced by the widening silence of the Tuscan road. Tito rode toward the city, his path diverging sharply from hers; with every hour, the distance between them grew, a physical manifestation of the separation she had chosen. The morning air was still, heavy with the scent of dry earth, and the great dip of the roadside created a natural gulf, isolating her from the sombre, brooding mountains beyond. For the first time, Romola felt truly alone—not abandoned, but solitary in the presence of the earth and sky, unmediated by human law or expectation.

Then, a voice cut through the stillness, close and immediate.

"You are Romola de' Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema."

She knew that voice instantly. It had vibrated through her life more than once, carrying a weight she could not ignore. Because she recognized it, she did not turn. She remained seated, though her heart hammered against her ribs, a mixture of awe and fierce rebellion warring within her chest. It was one of the black-robed monks, a figure daring to intrude upon her sanctuary. Yet, as he spoke, she felt as though destiny itself—a sceptred deity made flesh—had reached out and seized her.

"You are fleeing from Florence in disguise," the voice continued, imperious and clear. "I have a command from God to stop you. You are not permitted to flee."

Anger, hot and sharp, rose in Romola's throat. She refused to turn, resenting the scrutiny of a gaze she deemed presumptuous. Sitting motionless, she challenged him.

"What right have you to speak to me, or to hinder me?"

"The right of a messenger," the monk replied. "You have donned religious garb, yet you wear it as a disguise. You sought to pass unseen, but you were discerned. It is declared to me that you seek to escape the lot God has laid upon you. You wish to hide your true name and your true place, to craft a new identity ruled only by your own will. I have a command to call you back. My daughter, you must return to your place."

With every sentence, Romola's resistance hardened. She feared that if she showed even a flicker of submission, she would lose her resolve entirely. Her voice sharpened with irritation.

"I will not return. I acknowledge no right of priests and monks to interfere with my actions. You have no power over me."

"I know," the monk said, his tone softening yet remaining firm. "I know you have been raised in scorn of obedience. But it is not the poor monk who claims authority over you; it is the Truth that commands you. You cannot escape it. Either you obey, and it shall lead you, or you disobey, and it shall hang upon you like a chain you will drag forever. But you will obey, my daughter. Your old servant returns with the mules; my companion goes to fetch him. You will go back to Florence."

Romola sprang to her feet, her eyes blazing with defiance. She faced the speaker directly. It was Fra Girolamo. She knew him well; their heights were nearly equal, their faces level. She had prepared words of rejection, but they died in her throat. Meeting Fra Girolamo's calm gaze, she felt a strange, unfamiliar impression wash over her. Her anger sank, ashamed and irrelevant.

There was no transcendent beauty in Savonarola's face, nor was it conventionally handsome. It was strong-featured, refined only by the rigors of mind and body. Yet, the impact of his glance was profound. It conveyed a deep, abiding interest in her, a care that existed apart from any personal desire. It was the first time Romola had encountered a look where simple human fellowship manifested as a binding force. Such a gaze was the essence of a spiritual guide, and suddenly, Romola found it impossible to question his right to address her. She stood silent, staring at him.

"You assert your freedom proudly, my daughter," Savonarola said. "But who is so base as the debtor who thinks himself free?"

A subtle pallor flashed across Romola's face. "And you are flying from your debts: the debt of a Florentine woman; the debt of a wife. You turn your back on the appointed lot to choose another. But can man or woman choose their duties? No more than they can choose their birthplace or their parents. My daughter, you are fleeing from the presence of God into the wilderness."

As the anger melted, it was replaced by a new presentiment. Perhaps this man, whom she began to view with a vague reverence, held a valid law she had missed. Yet, it was impossible; he could not know the depths of her turmoil. Still, she could not simply refuse guidance. Driven by a desperate need to be reverent even as she resisted, the title she had never used before slipped from her lips.

"My father, you cannot know the reasons which compel me to go. None can know them but myself. None can judge for me. I have been driven by great sorrow. I am resolved to go."

"I know enough, my daughter," Savonarola replied, his voice illuminating her situation. "My mind has been so far enlightened concerning you that I know enough. You are not happy in your married life; but I am not a confessor, and I seek nothing reserved for the seal of confession. I have a divine warrant to stop you, which does not depend on such knowledge. You were warned by a message from heaven, delivered in my presence—warned before marriage, when you might still have lawfully chosen freedom. But you chose the bond. In wilfully breaking it, I speak to you as a pagan if the holy mystery of matrimony is not sacred to you, you are breaking a pledge. Of what wrongs will you complain when you yourself commit one of the greatest wrongs a woman and citizen can commit—withdrawal in secrecy and disguise from a pledge given before God and your fellow-men? Of what wrongs will you complain when you break the simplest law at the foundation of trust between man and man: faithfulness to the spoken word? Is this the wisdom you gained by scorning the Church's mysteries? Not to see the bare duty of integrity, where the Church would teach you to see not only integrity, but religion?"

Blood rushed to Romola's face, and she shrank as if struck. "I would not have put on a disguise," she began, but her voice faltered. She could not continue; the suggestion in the friar's words—that her conduct mirrored Tito's—shook her to her core.

"And to break that pledge, you fly from Florence," Savonarola pressed. "Florence, where there are the only men and women in the world to whom you owe the debt of a fellow-citizen."

"I should never have quitted Florence," Romola whispered, trembling, "as long as there was any hope of fulfilling a duty to my father there."

"Do you own no tie but that of a child to her father in the flesh?" Savonarola asked. "Your life has been spent in blindness, my daughter. You have lived with those who sit on a hill aloof, looking down on the life of their fellow-men. I know their vain discourse, filled with fancied wisdom about the past while scorning God's work in the present. Doubtless you were taught that pagan women felt what it was to live for the Republic, yet you never felt that you, a Florentine woman, should live for Florence. If your own people wear a yoke, will you slip from under it instead of struggling to lighten it? There is hunger and misery in our streets, yet you say, 'I care not; I have my own sorrows; I will go away.' The servants of God struggle for a law of justice, peace, and charity so that the hundred thousand citizens among whom you were born may be governed righteously. But you think no more of this than a bird spreading its wings to fly wherever it finds food. You have scorned the teaching of the Church, my daughter. As if you, a wilful wanderer following your blind choice, were not below the humblest Florentine woman who stretches her hands with her own people, craves a blessing for them, and feels a close sisterhood with the neighbor who kneels beside her, not of her own blood. She thinks of the mighty purpose God has for Florence and waits and endures because the promised work is great, and she feels herself little."

"I was not going away to ease and self-indulgence," Romola insisted, raising her head with a desperate need to vindicate herself. "I was going away to hardship. I expect no joy: it is gone from my life."

"You are seeking your own will, my daughter. You seek some good other than the law you are bound to obey. But how will you find good? It is not a thing of choice; it is a river flowing from the foot of the Invisible Throne, flowing by the path of obedience. I say again, man cannot choose his duties. You may choose to forsake your duties and avoid the sorrow they bring, but you will go forth anyway. What will you find, my daughter? Sorrow without duty—bitter herbs, and no bread with them."

"But if you knew," Romola pleaded, clasping her hands tightly, "if you knew what it was to me—how impossible it seemed to me to bear it."

"My daughter," Savonarola said, pointing to the cord around her neck, "you carry something within your mantle. Draw it forth and look at it."

Romola gave a slight start, but her impulse was to obey. A stronger will than her own grappled with her self-doubt. She drew forth the crucifix. Still pointing toward it, he spoke.

"There, my daughter, is the image of a Supreme Offering, made by Supreme Love because the need of man was great."

He paused. Romola held the crucifix, trembling. A sudden realization struck her of the vast distance between her present self and the girl who had first taken this object from the friar's hands. How many secrets lay ahead, just as there had been then in her young blindness? This thought helped subdue her fears. At the sound of Fra Girolamo's voice again, Romola made a quick, involuntary movement, pressing the crucifix against her mantle and looking at him with more submission than before.

"Conform your life to that image, my daughter. Make your sorrow an offering. And when the fire of Divine charity burns within you, and you behold the need of your fellow-men by the light of that flame, you will not call your offering great. You have carried yourself proudly, as one who held herself not of common blood or common thoughts; but you have been as one unborn to the true life of man. What! You say your love for your father no longer tells you to stay in Florence? Then, since that tie is snapped, you are without a law, without religion: you are no better than a beast of the field when robbed of her young. If the yearning of fleshly love is gone, you are without love, without obligation. See, then, my daughter, how you are below the life of the believer who worships that image of the Supreme Offering, who feels the glow of a common life with the lost multitude for whom that offering was made, and beholds the history of the world as the history of a great redemption in which he is a fellow-worker in his own place and among his own people! If you held that faith, my beloved daughter, you would not be a wanderer flying from suffering, blindly seeking the good of a freedom which is lawlessness. You would feel that Florence was the home of your soul as well as your birthplace, because you would see the work given you to do there. If you forsake your place, who will fill it? You ought to be in your place now, helping in the great work by which God will purify Florence and raise it to be the guide of the nations. What! The earth is full of iniquity—full of groans—the light still struggles with mighty darkness—and you say, 'I cannot bear my bonds; I will burst them asunder; I will go where no man claims me'? My daughter, every bond of your life is a debt: the right lies in the payment of that debt; it can lie nowhere else. In vain will you wander over the earth; you will be wandering forever away from the right."

Romola struggled internally against immense forces: the overwhelming personal influence of Savonarola, born of the energy of his emotions and beliefs, and her own consciousness that his words implied a higher law than any she had yet obeyed. Yet, the resisting thoughts were not yet overborne.

"How, then, could Dino be right? He broke ties. He forsook his place."

"That was a special vocation," Savonarola answered. "He was constrained to depart, else he could not have attained the higher life. It would have been stifled within him."

"And I too," Romola said, raising her hands to her brow, her voice anguished as if dragged to torture. "Father, you may be wrong."

"Ask your conscience, my daughter. You have no vocation such as your brother had. You are a wife. You seek to break your ties in self-will and anger, not because the higher life calls upon you to renounce them. The higher life begins for us, my daughter, when we renounce our own will to bow before a Divine law. That seems hard to you. It is the portal of wisdom, freedom, and blessedness. And the symbol of it hangs before you. That wisdom is the religion of the Cross. And you stand aloof from it; you are a pagan. You have been taught to say, 'I am as the wise men who lived before the time when the Jew of Nazareth was crucified.' And that is your wisdom! To be as the dead whose eyes are closed and whose ears are deaf to the work of God done since their time. What has your dead wisdom done for you, my daughter?
