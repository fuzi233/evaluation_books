It was nearly four in the afternoon. Eli was busy within the shop, while his eldest and youngest sons were away. Catherine and her little crippled daughter had grown increasingly anxious regarding Gerard; having waited in vain, they had ventured a short distance down the road, hoping against hope to catch a glimpse of him in the distance. Thus, Giles remained alone in the sitting-room—a space worthy of description, complete with its eccentric occupant.

The Hollanders have always been a peculiar and pioneering folk. They boast of inventing printing (with wooden type), oil-painting, liberty, banking, and even gardening. Most notably, centuries before the events of this tale, they invented cleanliness. While the English gentry of the time, clad in velvet jerkins and chicken-toed shoes, trampled upon floors covered in stale rushes—filthy receptacles teeming with bones, rotting food scraps, spittle, stray dogs, eggs, and every conceivable abomination—the hosier's sitting-room at Tergou presented a stark contrast. Its floor was paved with Dutch tiles, so brilliantly glazed and meticulously scrubbed that one might safely dine directly upon them.

A single large window dominated the wall. In its center stood a massive cross of stonework, raised in relief so distinctively that it appeared to the inhabitants as a literal cross, becoming a focal point of their daily prayers. The window panes themselves were tiny and diamond-shaped, fused together by strips of lead—a style still familiar today in our humble rural cottages. The room's seating consisted of rough, primitive chairs, save for one armchair. Its backrest rose perpendicularly from the seat, towering so high that a seated person's head would stop two feet below the apex. Crafted of oak and carved at the summit, this chair served as the throne for the household's unique resident.

Beside it stood a copper pail, designed to fit over the waist, filled with holy water, accompanied by a small besom for sprinkling blessings across the room. Dominating the scene, however, was a long, narrow, yet massive oak table. Clinging to its rim was a dwarf, his teeth locked onto the wood, his eyes blazing with intensity, his claws raised in the air like those of a pouncing vampire.

Nature, it seemed, had not created Giles out of malice. She had fashioned his head and torso with her usual diligence, but apparently, her attention wandered elsewhere, leaving the remainder to chance. The result was a human wedge, an inverted cone. One might justly quote Horace here: *"An amphora began to be shaped; why does the jar roll?"* His center of mass was entirely misplaced. If bisected, the upper half of Giles would outweigh the lower three times over. Yet, this very disproportion granted him abilities that would have baffled the ancient athlete Milo. His powerful arms carried no excess weight, allowing him to scale vertical poles with the agility of a squirrel or hang suspended from a tree branch by a single hand, dangling like a cherry on its stem. Had he possessed the ability to create a vacuum with his palms, as lizards do with their feet, he could have traversed ceilings with ease.

This pocket-sized acrobat was utterly obsessed with gripping the dining table with both hands to swing, and then—reaching the climax of his delight—he would seize the edge with his teeth, releasing his hands to cling to his massive ivory fangs like grim death itself. But even our highest joys are subject to interruption. Little Kate spotted Sampsonet in this precarious posture and froze in horror. True to her mother's nature, her heart belonged to the furniture, not to the twelve-mo gymnast.

"Oh, Giles! How can you?" she exclaimed. "Mother is coming. You're denting the table!"

"Go and tell her, little tale-bearer," snarled Giles. "You are the one destined for mischief."

"Am I?" Kate asked calmly. "That is quite new information."

"The biggest troublemaker in all of Tergou," growled Giles, tightening his grip.

"Oh, indeed!" replied Kate dryly.

Having launched this unexpected barb without suffering any visible backlash, she sat down quietly and began to cry. Just then, her mother entered the room. Giles immediately hurled himself beneath the table, peering out with a glare.

"What is happening now?" the woman demanded sharply. Turning her experienced gaze from Kate to Giles, noting his contorted position and sheepish expression, she hinted ominously at the prospect of ear-cuffing.

"Nay, mother," the girl said softly. "It was merely a foolish word Giles spoke. I wouldn't have noticed it at another time, but I am weary and worried about Gerard, you know."

"Let no one worry about me," a faint voice called from the doorway.

Gerard tottered in, pale, dusty, and utterly exhausted. Amidst a chorus of uplifted hands, cries of delight, and a mixture of curiosity and anxiety, he collapsed into the nearest chair. The long journey through Rotterdam, searching like a shadow for Margaret, and the arduous trek thereafter, had completely drained him. However, the elasticity of youth quickly restored him, and suddenly he was the center of an eager circle.

First, they demanded to hear about the prizes. Gerard recounted that he had been permitted to view the competitors' works, displayed in an immense hall awaiting the judges' verdict.

"Oh, mother! Oh, Kate!" he cried. "When I saw the goldsmiths' creations, I felt as though I ought to have fallen to the floor. I scarcely believed that all the goldsmiths on earth possessed so much gold, silver, jewels, and such exquisite craftsmanship. Truly, all arts are divine."

To satisfy the women, he proceeded to describe the reliquaries, feretories, chalices, crosiers, crosses, pyxes, monstrances, and other ecclesiastical marvels, alongside goblets, cups, watches, clocks, chains, and brooches, causing their mouths to water with desire.

"But, Kate," he continued, "when I turned to the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges, my heart sank. My own work seemed like dirt by comparison. For the first minute, I nearly wept; but I prayed for a purer spirit, and soon I was able to admire them, thanking God for those lovely works and for the skilled, patient craftsmen whom I acknowledge as my masters. Indeed, the colored pages were so beautiful I forgot everything else. Yet, the following day, when the other prizes were distributed, they announced the winner for writing. Do you guess whose name was called first?"

"Yours," Kate declared confidently.

The others scoffed at her.

"You may well laugh," Gerard said gently, "but the herald shouted 'Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou.' I stood frozen; they pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes. I found myself kneeling on a cushion at the feet of the Duke. He spoke to me, but I was so flustered I could not reply. Then, he reached to his side—not to draw his sword and sever my dull head—but instead bestowed upon me a gold medal, and here it is."

A roar erupted, followed by a frantic scramble.

"And then he gave me fifteen magnificent golden angels!" Gerard exclaimed. "I had seen one before, but never handled one. Behold!"

"Oh, Gerard! Oh, Gerard!"

"There is one for you, our eldest; one for you, Sybrandt; one for you, Little Mischief; and two for thee, Little Lily, because God has afflicted thee; one for myself, to purchase colors and vellum; and nine for her who nursed us all and risked the two crowns upon poor Gerard's hand."

The gold revealed their true natures. Cornelis and Sybrandt clutched their coins with simultaneous glares of greed and envy directed at Kate, who had received two pieces. Giles grabbed his share, rolled it across the floor, and gamboled after it. Kate set aside her crutches, sat down, and extended her small arms toward Gerard in a gesture of heavenly love and tenderness. The mother, initially stunned by the sudden shower of gold falling upon her apron, finally cried out, "Stop kissing him, Kate; he is my son, not yours. Ah, Gerard! My boy! I have not loved you as you deserve."

Then Gerard threw himself on his knees beside her, and she flung her arms around him, weeping with joy and pride against his neck.

"Good lad! Good lad!" cried the hosier, moved deeply. "I must go and tell the neighbors. Lend me the medal, Gerard; I shall show it to my good friend Peter Buyskens. He is always boasting about how his son Jorian won a tin mug for shooting at the butts."

"Ay, do, my man," Eli encouraged. "Show Peter Buyskens one of the angels. Tell him there are fourteen more where that came from. Mind you bring it back to me!"

"Wait a moment, father; there is better news to come," Gerard said, flushing with joy at the happiness he had inspired.

"Better than this?" Eli asked eagerly.

Gerard then recounted his audience with the Countess, and the house rang with jubilation.

"Now, God bless the good lady, and bless Dame Van Eyck! A benefice? Our son! My cares are at an end. Eli, my good friend and master, now we two can die happy whenever our time comes. This dear boy will take our place, and none of these beloved ones will lack a home or a friend."
