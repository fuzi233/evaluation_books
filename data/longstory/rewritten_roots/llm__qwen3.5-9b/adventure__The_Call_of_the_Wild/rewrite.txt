Buck did not read the newspapers, yet he sensed the storm gathering. Trouble was brewing—not just for him, but for every tide-water dog of strong muscle and thick, warm coat stretching from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, groping blindly in the Arctic dark, had stumbled upon gold. Steamships and transport lines were screaming about the discovery, drawing thousands northward. These men needed dogs: heavy beasts with powerful limbs to haul loads and thick fur to withstand the freezing bite of the wind.

Buck lived in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley at Judge Miller's estate. The house sat deep within the woods, half-hidden, with a wide, cool veranda wrapping around its four sides. Gravel drives wound through sprawling lawns beneath towering poplars to reach the rear, where the property expanded even further. Great stables housed a dozen grooms and boys; rows of vine-covered servant cottages lined the grounds alongside endless outhouses, grape arbors, lush pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was even a pumping plant for an artesian well and a massive cement tank where the Judge's boys cooled off in the heat. Over this entire domain, Buck reigned supreme.

Born here and raised here for four years, Buck was no ordinary pet. While other dogs existed on the vast estate, they counted for nothing. There were the kennel dogs who came and went, and the obscure residents like Toots, a Japanese pug, or Ysabel, a Mexican hairless breed—strange creatures who barely ventured outdoors. Then there were the fox terriers, a pack of at least twenty, who yelped threats at Toots and Ysabel whenever they spotted them through the windows, protected by a legion of maidens wielding brooms and mops.

But Buck was neither a house dog nor a kennel dog. He was the sovereign of the realm. He dove into the swimming tank, hunted with the Judge's sons, and escorted the Judge's daughters, Mollie and Alice, on twilight rambles. On winter nights, he lay at the Judge's feet before the roaring library fire. He carried the grandsons on his broad back, rolled them in the grass, and guarded their adventurous footsteps all the way to the stable yard and beyond, into the paddocks and berry fields. He stalked the terriers with imperial disdain and utterly ignored Toots and Ysabel. He was King—ruler over every creeping, crawling, and flying creature on Judge Miller's land, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a massive St. Bernard, had been the Judge's constant shadow, and Buck seemed destined to follow in his paw prints. Though smaller than his sire, weighing only one hundred and forty pounds due to his mother, Shep, a Scottish Shepherd, Buck compensated with the dignity bred from good living and universal respect. He carried himself royally. For four years, he had lived as a sated aristocrat, proud and slightly egotistical, insulated by his privileged position. Yet, he avoided the fate of a mere pampered pet. Hunting and outdoor pursuits kept his muscles hard and his frame lean. Like the cold-tubbing races, he loved the water, finding it a tonic that preserved his health.

Thus was Buck in the autumn of 1897, just as the Klondike strike began dragging men from across the globe into the frozen North.

Buck did not read papers, nor did he know that Manuel, one of the gardener's helpers, was a dangerous acquaintance. Manuel suffered from a fatal flaw: a love for the Chinese lottery. Worse still, he believed in "systems"—rigid betting strategies that required capital far beyond the wages of a gardener's helper, leaving him unable to support his wife and numerous children.

On a memorable night, while the Judge attended a Raisin Growers' Association meeting and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel betrayed Buck. No one witnessed them slipping away through the orchard, save for a solitary figure near College Park station. There, a stranger spoke gruffly to Manuel, and coins clinked between them.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver 'em," the stranger warned.

Manuel looped a sturdy rope around Buck's neck, sliding it under his collar. "Twist it, and you'll choke 'm plentee," he instructed. The stranger nodded.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He trusted the men he knew and assumed they acted with a wisdom surpassing his own. But when the stranger took hold of the rope's ends, Buck growled menacingly—a gesture of displeasure intended as a command. Instead, the rope tightened instantly, cutting off his breath. Rage surged through him. He lunged at the man, who met him halfway, grabbed him by the throat, and with a deft throw sent him crashing onto his back. The rope constricted mercilessly. Buck struggled in a frenzy, tongue lolling, chest heaving uselessly.

Never in his life had he been so vilely treated, and never so enraged. But his strength failed. His vision blurred, and consciousness fled as the train arrived and the men tossed him into the baggage car.

When he woke, his tongue hurt, and the violent jolting of the carriage told him where he was. The shrill whistle of a locomotive confirmed it. Having ridden in baggage cars often with the Judge, he knew the sensation. Opening his eyes, he saw only unbridled anger—the rage of a kidnapped king. A man reached for his throat, but Buck moved faster. His jaws snapped shut on the man's hand, refusing to release until the attacker's senses were choked out.

"Yes, he has fits," the man muttered, hiding his mangled hand from the baggageman drawn by the commotion. "I'm taking him up to Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks he can cure 'im."

Later, in a shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco waterfront, the kidnapper recounted the job. "All I get is fifty for it," he grumbled, wiping blood from his hand. "I wouldn't do it over for a thousand, cold cash."

"How much did the other guy get?" the saloon-keeper asked.

"A hundred," the kidnapper replied. "Won't take a sou less."

"That makes one hundred and fifty," the keeper calculated. "And he's worth it, or I'm a squarehead."

The kidnapper peeled back his bandage, revealing a lacerated hand. "If I don't get hydrophobia—"

"It'll be because you were born to hang," the saloon-keeper laughed. "Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight."

Dazed, suffering excruciating pain in his throat and tongue, with half his life throttled out, Buck tried to confront his tormentors. But they threw him down and choked him repeatedly until they managed to file off the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope was removed, and he was shoved into a cage-like crate.

There he lay through the weary night, nursing his wrath and wounded pride. Confusion clouded his mind. Who were these strange men? Why keep him penned in this narrow box? An oppressive sense of impending doom settled over him. Several times, when the shed door rattled, he sprang to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Each time, however, it was the bulging face of the saloon-keeper peering in by the sickly glow of a tallow candle. Every joyful bark that trembled in Buck's throat twisted into a savage growl. The saloon-keeper left him alone, and at dawn, four rough-looking men entered to lift the crate.

More enemies, Buck decided. They were ragged, unkempt, and evil. He stormed and raged at them through the bars. They merely laughed and prodded him with sticks, which he attacked with his teeth until he realized that was exactly what they wanted. Sullenly, he lay down and allowed them to load the crate onto a wagon.

From there, Buck began a journey through many hands. Express clerks took charge; he was carted in another wagon; a truck hauled him, mixed with boxes and parcels, onto a ferry steamer; he was trucked off the steamer into a great railway depot, and finally deposited in an express car. For two days and nights, this car was dragged at the tail of shrieking locomotives. Buck neither ate nor drank.

In his fury, he had met the first advances of the express messengers with growls, prompting them to tease him. When he flung himself against the bars, quivering and frothing, they laughed and taunted him, mimicking dogs, mewling, flapping their arms, and crowing. It was all absurd, he knew, making the insult to his dignity worse. His anger burned hotter. Hunger mattered less than the thirst, which drove him to a fever pitch. High-strung and sensitive, the mistreatment had pushed him into a fever, exacerbated by the inflammation of his parched, swollen throat and tongue.

He was grateful for one thing: the rope was gone. That had given them an unfair advantage. Now that it was off, he vowed, he would make them pay.

Buck's first day on the Dyea beach felt like a nightmare. He had never witnessed dogs fight like these wolf-like creatures until now, and his first encounter taught him a lesson he would never forget—a lesson learned vicariously, for if he hadn't survived to witness it, he wouldn't have profited from it. The victim was Curly.

They were camped near the log store, where Curly, in her usual friendly manner, had approached a massive husky nearly the size of a full-grown wolf. There was no warning. Buck didn't understand their silence or the greedy licks of their tongues.

Curly charged her opponent again. He met her rush squarely with his chest, tumbling her off her feet. She never rose. This was exactly what the surrounding pack had waited for. They closed in, snarling and yelping, burying her screaming form beneath a bristling mountain of bodies.

His dignity was crushed by this reduction to a draft animal, yet he was too wise to rebel. He buckled down with determination, doing his best despite the strangeness of it all.

The tent, lit by a candle, glowed warmly in the white expanse. When he entered it naturally, Perrault and François bombarded him with curses and cooking pots until he fled in humiliation into the freezing outdoors. Miserable, he wandered among the tents, finding none warmer. Savage dogs rushed at him, but he bristled and snarled, having learned quickly, and they let him pass.

Then, an idea struck him. He would check on his teammates. To his astonishment, they were gone. He searched the camp again, returned, and searched once more. Were they in the tent? Impossible; he had been thrown out. Where could they be? Drooping-tailed and shivering, he circled the tent aimlessly. Suddenly, the snow gave way beneath his paws, and he sank. Something wriggled under his feet. He recoiled, bristling in fear of the unknown. But a friendly little yelp reassured him. He investigated. A waft of warm air reached his nose. There, curled into a tight ball under the snow, was Billee.

Billee whined placatingly, squirming to show goodwill, and even licked Buck's face with his warm, wet tongue as a peace offering. Another lesson. *So that was the way,* Buck realized. Confidently, he selected a spot and began digging a hole. In moments, his body heat filled the confined space, and he drifted into a sound, comfortable sleep, though plagued by nightmares.

Morning came with the noise of the waking camp. Buck opened his eyes, confused. Snow had fallen overnight, burying him completely. The walls of snow pressed in on every side, triggering a primal surge of fear—the fear of the wild beast trapped in a cage. It was a regression to the instincts of his ancestors; as a civilized dog, he knew no traps and shouldn't fear them. His muscles contracted spasmodically, his hackles rose, and with a ferocious snarl, he bounded straight up into the blinding daylight, sending snow flying.

Within Buck, the ancient, primal beast was awakening and swelling with power. Fueled by the brutal demands of trail life, this inner force grew stronger every day, yet it remained a secret. His newly forged cunning granted him a steady poise and absolute control. He was consumed by the effort of adapting to this harsh new existence, leaving no room for carelessness. Consequently, he avoided conflict rather than seeking it, moving with a deliberate caution. He possessed no inclination for rashness, and despite the burning hatred simmering between him and Spitz, he showed no impatience and refrained entirely from aggression.

Conversely, sensing a dangerous rival in Buck, Spitz seized every opportunity to display his teeth. He actively bullied Buck, constantly provoking a fight that could only end in the death of one or the other. Had circumstances allowed earlier in the journey, such a confrontation surely would have occurred, but fate intervened through an unusual accident.

That evening, the team established a bleak and miserable camp on the shore of Lake Le Barge. Blinding driving snow, a wind sharp enough to cut like a white-hot knife, and encroaching darkness had forced them to stumble blindly for a spot to rest. Their situation could scarcely have been worse. Behind them rose a sheer wall of rock, compelling Perrault and François to build their fire and spread their sleeping robes directly on the frozen surface of the lake. Having discarded their tent at Dyea to travel lighter, they relied on a few pieces of driftwood to kindle a fire that slowly melted a hole through the ice, allowing them to eat their supper in the dark.

Tucked close beneath the sheltering rock, Buck constructed his nest. It was so snug and warm that he hesitated to leave it when François began distributing the thawed fish. However, upon returning for his share, he found his sanctuary occupied. A warning snarl signaled that the intruder was Spitz. The Thirty Mile River was wide open. Its wild waters defied the frost, holding ice only in the eddies and quiet spots. Six days of exhausting labor were required to traverse those thirty terrible miles. Terrible they were, for every step was taken at the risk of life for both dog and man. A dozen times, Perrault, probing the way, broke through ice bridges, saved only by the long pole he carried, which he positioned so it fell across the hole created by his own body. But a severe cold snap had arrived, the thermometer reading fifty degrees below zero. Each time he broke through, he was compelled, for the sake of his life, to build a fire and dry his clothes.

Nothing daunted him. It was precisely because nothing daunted him that he had been chosen as a government courier. He took all manner of risks, resolutely thrusting his weather-beaten face into the frost and pushing on from dim dawn to dark. By the time they were dragged out, they were half-frozen and nearly drowned. A fire was essential to save them. Coated solidly in ice, the two men kept them running around the flames, sweating and thawing, so close they were singed by the heat.

The trail was in perfect condition—hard-packed and clear of new snow. Temperatures hovered at fifty degrees below zero throughout the journey. Men rode and ran in turns, keeping the dogs on the jump with minimal stops. Over the Thirty Mile River, partially frozen, they covered in one day what usually took ten. In a single run, they raced sixty miles from the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Flying across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett (seventy miles of lakes), they moved so fast that the runner had to tow behind the sled at the end of a rope. By the last night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended the sea slope, the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet visible beneath them. It was a record-breaking run.

For fourteen consecutive days, they averaged forty miles per day. Upon reaching Skaguay, Perrault and François paraded through the main streets, throwing chests up and down, deluged with invitations to drink while their team became the center of worshipful admiration from fellow mushers. Soon, three or four notorious Western desperados attempted to rob the town, only to be riddled like pepper boxes. Public attention shifted elsewhere, followed shortly by official dispatches.

François summoned Buck, threw his arms around him, and wept. That marked the end of François and Perrault's tenure with the team. Like other men, they faded from Buck's life forever. A Scotch half-breed took command, leading a dozen teams back over the weary trail to Dawson.

The pace changed. This was no longer a race; it was heavy toil with a heavy load, serving as the mail train carrying news from the civilized world to those seeking gold under the Arctic pole. Buck disliked the monotony but bore it well, taking pride in the work like Dave and Sol-leks, ensuring his mates contributed their fair share. Days blurred into identical routines: cooking fires lit, breakfast eaten, camps broken, dogs harnessed, and the team moving out before dawn. Nights were spent pitching tents, gathering wood, fetching water, and feeding the dogs. To them, the meal was the highlight of the day, followed by an hour of loafing among the fifty-seven other dogs. Among them were fierce fighters, but three battles with the strongest brought Buck to absolute mastery; when he bristled and bared his teeth, they wisely stayed out of his way.

Best of all, Buck loved lying near the fire, hind legs tucked, forelegs stretched, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller's sun-drenched home in Santa Clara Valley, the cement swimming pool, Ysabel the Mexican hairless, and Toots the Japanese pug. More often, he recalled the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the epic battle with Spitz, and the delicious food he craved. He was not homesick. The memory of the Sunland was dim and distant, powerless to stir him. Instead, potent memories of his heritage surfaced—the instincts of his ancestors, dormant for years, now waking anew.

As he crouched by the fire, watching the flames, it seemed he was sitting by a different fire entirely. Before him sat another man: shorter-legged, longer-armed, with stringy, knotted muscles instead of rounded ones. His hair was long and matted, his head slanting back under it. He made strange sounds, terrified of the encroaching darkness, clutching a stick with a heavy stone tied to the end. He was nearly naked, wearing only a ragged, fire-scalded hide, with thick fur matting his chest, shoulders, and limbs. He did not stand upright but leaned forward from the hips, moving with a peculiar, catlike springiness and an alertness born of perpetual fear.

At other times, this hairy man squatted by the fire, head between his knees, elbows resting on his knees, hands clasped above his head as if shedding rain. Beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck saw glowing coals appearing two by two, always paired, which he recognized as the eyes of great beasts of prey. He heard the crash of their bodies through the undergrowth and their nocturnal cries. Dreaming there by the Yukon bank, these visions of another world made the hair rise along his back and stand on end across his shoulders and neck until he whimpered low and suppressedly, or growled softly. Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail train, with Buck and his companions leading the pack, stumbled into Skaguay. They were creatures of ruin, utterly exhausted and broken down. Buck's massive one hundred and forty pounds had shriveled to one hundred and fifteen. While his fellow dogs were naturally lighter, they had shed proportionately more weight than he. Pike, the habitual liar who had spent his life feigning injuries, was now limping with genuine agony. Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub suffered from a badly wrenched shoulder blade.

Every footstep was a tragedy. Their feet, raw and sore, struck the trail with a heavy thud that jarred their entire bodies, doubling the fatigue of the day's journey. Nothing was physically wrong with them save for a profound, soul-crushing exhaustion. This was not the weariness born of sudden, intense exertion, from which one might recover within hours. It was the deep, draining fatigue of months of relentless labor. There was no reserve energy left, no capacity for recuperation. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was depleted. The cause was clear: in less than five months, they had traversed two thousand five hundred miles, enduring only five days of rest during the final eighteen hundred. By the time they reached Skaguay, they were on their last legs, barely able to keep the traces taut, and on downhill slopes, they struggled merely to avoid being run over by the sled.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers assumed a lengthy stop was imminent. Having covered twelve hundred miles with only two days of rest, they felt entitled to a period of leisure. However, the mail service was clogged with Alpine congestion due to the flood of prospectors rushing to the Klondike, leaving families and loved ones behind. Furthermore, official orders mandated that fresh batches of Hudson Bay dogs replace the current team, deemed worthless for the trail. Since dogs counted for little compared to dollars, the decision was made to sell them.

Three days passed, confirming how truly weak and drained Buck and his mates had become. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, two men from the United States approached and purchased the entire team, harness included, for a mere song. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man with pale features, watery eyes, and a mustache that curled aggressively upward, mocking the drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a boy of nineteen or twenty, armed with a Colt revolver and a hunting knife, his belt bristling with cartridges. This belt was his defining feature, advertising a callowness that was sheer and terrifying. Both men were completely out of place in the North, an anomaly that defied explanation.

Buck observed the transaction, hearing the money change hands between the buyer and the government agent. He sensed that the Scotch half-breed and the mail drivers were departing his life forever. When driven to the new owners' camp, Buck found a scene of utter sloppiness: a tent half-stretched, dirty dishes piled up, and chaos everywhere. Most disturbingly, he saw a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

Buck watched them anxiously as they began dismantling the camp. Their movements lacked efficiency, filled instead with frantic effort. John Thornton's recovery from his frostbitten feet the previous winter had been thorough. Though he still limped slightly when he first rescued Buck, the warming spring weather soon erased the trace of his injury. Lying by the riverbank during those long, languid days, watching the water flow and listening to the birdsong, Buck slowly regained his strength alongside his master.

Rest is a profound healer after traveling three thousand miles. Buck became lazy as his wounds knit together, his muscles swelling and flesh returning to cover his bones. Yet, they were all loafing—Buck, John, and the two dogs, Skeet and Nig—waiting for the raft that would carry them downriver to Dawson. Skeet, a small Irish Setter, had befriended Buck immediately upon his rescue, unable to resent his weak state. Possessing a natural "doctor" trait common in some dogs, she diligently washed and cleansed his wounds each morning after breakfast until Buck looked forward to her care as much as to John's. Nig, a massive black hound mix with laughing eyes and boundless kindness, shared this affection, though less demonstratively.

To Buck's astonishment, these dogs showed no jealousy. They seemed to absorb the generosity of John Thornton. As Buck strengthened, they dragged him into ridiculous games that even John couldn't resist joining. Thus, Buck romped through his convalescence into a new existence. For the first time, he knew genuine, passionate love. This was unlike anything he had known at Judge Miller's estate in the sunny Santa Clara Valley: not merely a working partnership with the sons, a pompous guardianship with the grandsons, or a stately friendship with the Judge himself. John Thornton ignited a feverish, burning adoration within him.

John was the ideal master. While others tended their dogs out of duty or business necessity, John cared for them as if they were his own children, compelled by an innate inability to withhold love. He remembered every kind word and cherished long conversations ("gas") with them. He had a unique habit of gripping Buck's head roughly between his hands, resting his own forehead against Buck's, rocking him back and forth while hurling insults that meant only love to Buck. To Buck, this rough embrace and the sound of murmured oaths were supreme joys; at each jerk, his heart seemed ready to burst from his chest with ecstasy. When released, Buck would spring to his feet, mouth grinning, eyes eloquent, throat vibrating with unsounded song, remaining motionless until John would reverently cry, "God! You can all but speak!"

Buck also expressed love through a trick akin to aggression: seizing John's hand in his mouth and biting down so fiercely that the flesh bore the imprint of his teeth for hours. Just as Buck understood John's oaths as words of love, John understood this feigned bite as a caress.

Most often, however, Buck's love was pure adoration. While he thrilled at John's touch, he rarely sought it. Unlike Skeet, who nudged constantly for pets, or Nig, who rested his great head on John's knee, Buck preferred to adore from a distance. He would lie for hours at John's feet, alert and eager, gazing upward to study every fleeting expression and movement of his master's face. Sometimes he watched from afar, tracing the outline of John's body. Often, the intensity of Buck's gaze would pull John's head around, and they would lock eyes in silent communion, their hearts shining outward from their gazes.

Initially, Buck feared losing John. Having been owned by transient masters since arriving in the North, he harbored a terror that John might vanish as Perrault, François, and the Scotch half-breed had vanished. Even in his dreams, he would wake in the chill to creep to the tent flap, listening to John's breathing. Despite this fierce loyalty, the primal wildness awakened in him by the North remained potent. He possessed the faithfulness born of fire and shelter, yet he retained his cunning and ferocity. He could not steal from John, but from anyone else, he hesitated not. His thefts were clever, allowing him to evade detection.

His body was scarred by countless fights. He fought with the ferocity of his ancestors, learning from Spitz and the police dogs that there was no middle ground: master or be mastered, kill or be killed. Mercy was a weakness that led to death. Behind him sat the shadows of all the dogs he had known—half-wolves and wild wolves urging him on, tasting the meat he ate, drinking the water he drank, and dictating his moods. These ancient spirits beckoned him daily, pulling him away from the fire and the beaten earth toward the deep forest. There, a mysterious call thrummed, compelling him to plunge into the woods, knowing not why, yet obeying the imperative summons. Yet, whenever he returned to the soft earth and green shade, the love for John drew him back to the fire.

John alone held him. Others might praise or pet him, but he remained cold beneath their touch, walking away from overly demonstrative strangers. When John's partners, Hans and Pete, finally arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until they approached John, tolerating them only passively. They were of John's sturdy type, simple-minded and clear-sighted; quickly understanding Buck's nature, they respected his boundaries without demanding false intimacy.

With John, however, Buck's capacity for service seemed limitless. No burden was too great when John commanded. One day, while sitting on a cliff overlooking a three-hundred-foot drop to bedrock, John impulsively challenged Buck. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, gesturing over the abyss. Before anyone could react, John and Buck were grappling on the precipice, pulled back to safety by Hans and Pete.

"It's uncanny," Pete remarked later.

Pete's fears materialized at Circle City. An evil-tempered man named "Black" Burton had provoked a fight at a saloon when John intervened. As Buck lay watching, Burton struck out blindly. John spun backward, saving himself only by grabbing the bar. Those present heard neither bark nor yelp, but a roar. Buck leaped into the air, launching himself at Burton's throat. Later that autumn, Buck saved John's life in a different manner. While the three men navigated a treacherous stretch of rapids on the Forty-Mile Creek, Hans and Pete managed the rope from the bank while John poled the boat. At a dangerous ledge, Hans checked the rope too abruptly. The boat flipped, dumping John into the raging current toward a section of water where no swimmer could survive.

Buck dove in instantly. After three hundred yards of desperate struggle, he caught John's tail and headed for shore. But the current was overwhelming. John scraped across rocks, bruising himself, and shouted, "Go, Buck! Go!" as he clung to a slippery stone.

Buck struggled but was swept downstream. Hearing John's command repeated, Buck reared up, throwing his head back in a final gesture of obedience, before turning toward the bank. He was dragged ashore by Hans and Pete just as the water became impassable. Knowing John hung on for mere moments, they raced up the bank, attaching a snubbing line to Buck's neck and shoulders. They launched him back into the torrent.

On the first attempt, Buck missed the mark, drifting helplessly past John. Hans snubbed the rope, jerking Buck underwater until he hit the bank. Drowned and gasping, Buck collapsed. The faint sound of John's voice in extremity shocked him awake. He scrambled up, ran to the launch point, and tried again. This time, he aimed true. Holding position directly above John, he turned and charged down the current like a battering ram. John met him mid-stream, wrapping his arms around Buck's shaggy neck. Hans secured the rope to a tree, and the pair were dragged under, tumbling over jagged rocks until they veered safely onto the bank.

John recovered, bruised and battered, to find Buck unconscious with three broken ribs. "That settles it," John declared. "We camp right here." They stayed until Buck's ribs knitted.

When Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon, he unlocked a door for his master. Thornton could now settle his debts and join his partners on a perilous expedition eastward, chasing a legend older than the land itself: a lost mine. History whispered of it, steeped in tragedy and mystery. Men had died searching for it; none had returned with proof of its existence except dying men, clutching nuggets of gold unlike any seen in the Northlands. Yet, no living soul had ever successfully looted the treasure. Now, Thornton, accompanied by Pete, Hans, Buck, and half a dozen other dogs, turned their faces east onto an unknown trail, hoping to succeed where others had failed.

Their sled tore seventy miles up the Yukon, swung left into the Stewart River, bypassed the Mayo and McQuestion creeks, and pressed onward until the river shrank to a brook winding through towering peaks that formed the continent's spine. Thornton cared little for the rigors of man or nature. Unafraid of the wild, armed only with salt and a rifle, he moved with the confidence of the Indigenous peoples. Hunting his own meals during the day's trek, he trusted that if hunger struck, the land would eventually yield. On this great journey, raw meat was the menu, and the cargo consisted mainly of ammunition and tools, while their schedule was dictated solely by the limitless future.

For Buck, this existence was pure bliss. Weeks passed in steady travel, followed by weeks of camping where the dogs lounged and the men dug through frozen muck to wash pans of dirt by the firelight. Their rhythm was erratic: sometimes starving, sometimes feasting, entirely dependent on the luck of the hunt. Seasons shifted around them. They rafted across blue mountain lakes, navigated unknown rivers in makeshift canoes, and endured summer blizzards crossing mountain divides. They huddled under the midnight sun on bare peaks between the timberline and eternal snows, then plunged into swarming summer valleys teeming with insects. In the shadow of glaciers, they picked strawberries as sweet as any from the south.

Autumn brought them to a strange, silent lake country where wildfowl had once thrived but now left only chilling winds and icy ripples on lonely shores. Through another winter, they tracked the obliterated paths of previous explorers. Once, they found an ancient blaze leading toward the Lost Cabin, but the trail began and ended in mystery, offering no clues to its maker. Later, amidst the rotting ruins of a hunting lodge, Thornton discovered a long-barreled flintlock—a relic of the Hudson Bay Company era, valuable enough to buy a man's height in beaver skins. That was all; the man who built the lodge and left the gun remained a ghost.

Finally, spring returned. They did not find the Lost Cabin, but they discovered a shallow placer in a broad valley where gold glittered like yellow butter across the pan bottoms. They stopped searching. Days turned into a relentless grind of labor, earning thousands in clean dust and nuggets. Gold was sacked into moose-hide bags, piled like firewood outside their spruce-bough lodge. Giants they were, working day after day, piling up wealth as if dreaming.

With the heavy lifting done, the dogs' role diminished to occasional meat hauling. Buck spent long hours by the fire, lost in thought. The vision of the short-legged, hairy man returned with renewed frequency. Blinking by the flames, Buck wandered mentally into that other world he remembered. Fear was the dominant emotion there. Watching the man sleep, head between his knees, Buck saw restless tossing, sudden awakenings, and fearful glances into the dark. Whether walking a beach gathering shellfish or creeping through the forest, the man was hyper-vigilant, ears twitching, nostrils quivering. He could swing through trees with impossible grace, a dozen feet between branches, never faltering. He roosted in the canopy, holding tight as he slept.

Closely tied to these visions was the Call, echoing from the deep forest. It filled Buck with a strange, sweet unrest and wild yearning. Sometimes he chased it, barking softly or defiantly, thrusting his nose into cool moss or crouching behind fallen logs, wide-eyed and alert. He didn't understand why he acted this way; he simply felt compelled. Irresistible impulses seized him. Dozing in the noon heat, he would suddenly jerk upright, ears pricked, and dash away for hours through the aisles of the forest. He loved running down dry watercourses, spying on birds, and lying in the underbrush to watch partridges strut. But most of all, he adored the dim twilight of summer midnights, listening to the forest's sleepy murmur, reading its signs like a book, seeking the mysterious voice that called him, waking or sleeping, always urging him forward.

One night, Buck woke with a start, nostrils quivering, mane bristling. From the forest came the Call—or perhaps one distinct note of it—clearer and more definite than ever before. It was a long-drawn howl, similar to, yet unlike, any sound a husky dog made. He recognized it instantly, as a sound from his past. Springing through the sleeping camp, he dashed silently into the woods. As he neared the cry, he slowed, cautious in every step, until he reached an open clearing. There, standing erect on haunches, nose pointed skyward, was a long, lean timber wolf.

Buck made no noise. The wolf paused, sensing his approach. Buck stalked forward, half-crouched, body compact, tail stiff, feet landing with unusual care. His movements mixed threat with tentative friendship, the truce of predators. But the wolf fled. Buck gave chase, leaping wildly in a frenzy to catch him. He drove the wolf into a blind channel blocked by a timber jam. The wolf spun around, pivoting on his hind legs like a cornered dog, snarling and snapping furiously.

Buck did not attack. Instead, he circled, closing in with friendly advances. Suspicious and terrified, the wolf realized Buck outweighed him significantly. Seizing a chance, the wolf darted away, and the chase resumed. Again and again, Buck cornered him, only for the wolf to dash free at the slightest opening. Eventually, Buck's persistence paid off. Seeing no malice, the wolf sniffed his nose. Friendship bloomed; they played nervously, masking their ferocity. Soon, the wolf led the way, loping easily toward somewhere specific. He signaled for Buck to follow, and they ran side by side through the somber twilight, up the creek bed, into the gorge, and across the bleak divide where the stream originated.

On the opposite slope, they entered a level landscape of vast forests and numerous streams. Hour after hour, they ran as the sun rose higher and the day warmed. Buck was ecstatic. He knew he was answering the Call, running beside his "wood brother" toward the source. Old memories surged, vivid and real. He had done this before in that dimly remembered world, running free with unpacked earth beneath his paws and the wide sky above.

Stopping by a running stream to drink, Buck suddenly remembered John Thornton. He sat down. The wolf started toward the Call's origin, then returned, nudging Buck encouragingly. But Buck turned and walked slowly backward. For nearly an hour, the wild brother ran alongside, whining softly. Finally, the wolf sat, pointed his nose upward, and howled. It was a mournful sound. As Buck continued his slow retreat, the howl faded into the distance until it vanished.

Back at camp, John Thornton was eating dinner when Buck crashed in, springing upon him in a frenzy of affection. He overturned his master, scrambled over him, licking his face and biting his hand—"playing the general tom-fool," as Thornton grumbled, shaking him back and forth with loving curses.

For two days and nights, Buck refused to leave Thornton's side, following him constantly, watching him eat, and guarding him through sleep and wakefulness. But after two days, the Call grew imperious. Restlessness returned, haunting him with images of the wild brother and the smiling lands beyond the divide. He ventured into the woods again, but the wolf did not appear, and the mournful howl never rose.
