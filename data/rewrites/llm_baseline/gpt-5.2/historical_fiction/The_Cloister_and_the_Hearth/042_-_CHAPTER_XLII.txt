Letters, the color of live coals, had asked their question on the church wall with a blunt, almost household curiosity—why, in such troubled weather of men’s souls, there was no mass for the duke. People who had gone to market for onions and eels found themselves instead standing with necks craned, mouths ajar, staring at scripture written by no human hand. It shone, it wavered, and it thinned like lard in a hot pan, fading slowly until only the lime-washed stones remained and the cold of daylight returned to its proper dominion.

Yet the question lingered as stubbornly as the smell of smoke in a cloak.

The magistrates, not wishing the heavens to outbid them in loyalty, began to buy masses with a generosity that had the clink of coin in it. The curé—an old acquaintance—took this sudden piety in hard cash, and with it indulged a very pretty taste. But miracles, like cheap wine, grow stale if you sip them too long; life moved on, as it always does, from wonder to bargaining.

Denys had begged for his arms.

“Why disgrace him as well as break his heart?” he pleaded, thinking of Gerard.

“Then swear,” came the answer, “on the cross of thy sword thou wilt not leave the bastard’s service until the sedition is put down.”

Need is a hard master. Denys poured out oaths in volleys—thick, quick, and bitter—till they were satisfied. His arms were restored; his liberty, too, in that narrow soldier’s sense which means a man may walk where he is ordered and die where he is paid.

They halted at an inn called The Three Fish, a place that smelled of damp straw, ale-sour boards, and frying fat that had done duty too many days. Marion, catching sight of him, cried out at once, as if she could keep fear at bay by making sport of it:

“I’m out of luck; who would have thought to see you again?”

Then she saw his face was not made for jesting and softened, the way a cook softens a tough scrap with patience and heat.

“What is amiss?”

He told her—of Gerard lost to him in this whirl of politics and steel, and of the promise that had bound him.

Marion’s mind, trained by hard streets and harder reckonings, took a bright view as naturally as it took a quick knife to an apple.

“Gerard is too handsome and too well-behaved to come to harm,” she said, as if those were protections like armour. “Women will always be on his side. Besides, it must go either well or ill with him. If it goes well, he will strike in with good company to Rome; if ill, he will come home again—perhaps there before you, for you have, by all accounts, a trifle of fighting to do in Flanders.”

She fetched the gold pieces he had entrusted to her. He urged her to keep one, then another, as a man presses bread on a hungry child. She refused steadily, with that honest obstinacy that is rarer than piety paid for by corporations. It eased him. It made him think a little better of the world—at least of the corners where a poor little friend walked alone and yet kept her hands clean.

Already the roads were dotted with foot soldiers in twos and threes, trudging lazily toward Flanders. Many were penniless, but bailiffs pushed them on from town to town, and the innkeepers—grumbling, sweating, and making do—were ordered to feed and lodge them. Anthony of Burgundy rode among these stragglers, gathering them as a man gathers loose ears of wheat into his apron, until he entered Flanders with six hundred men under his standard.

On the frontier Baldwyn, his brother, met him with arms, provisions, and more men. Their force was set in order and marched in battle array through several towns, not only unimpeded but acclaimed—shouts in the streets, bells in the air, the whole countryside sounding like a drum of loyalty. The noise of it, hearty and unanimous, sat ill on those who had been told they marched to chastise disaffection.

“This rebellion of ours is a bite,” growled a soldier named Simon, who had elected himself Denys’s comrade.

Denys said nothing. But inside him a vow hardened, like a crust set before the fire: if this rebellion that had cost him Gerard proved no rebellion at all, then, by St. Mars, he would shoot Anthony of Burgundy dead.

That afternoon they saw ahead a town that crouched behind walls thick as old bread and towers like clenched fists. A whisper ran through the ranks—this was a disaffected place. Yet as they approached, the great gate stood open, and each flanking tower showed only a single sentinel, as if the town had nothing to hide and no fear to show. Their formation loosened; men walked easier; harness creaked, and someone even laughed.

Then, when they were within a furlong, the drawbridge over the moat rose slowly, groaning on its chains until it stood upright against the fort. At that very instant the portcullis dropped with an iron rattle, and the walls bristled—lances, crossbows, sudden faces, the neat black mouths of slits alive with intent.

A stern hum rose from the front rank and shivered backward like a plucked string.

“Halt!” Anthony cried. The word ran down the line, and the whole body checked as if it had struck a wall of air.

“Herald to the gate!”

A pursuivant rode out and stopped twenty yards from the gate. He lifted his bugle; the herald’s flag drooped round it, and the summons rang bright, thin, and insolent against the stone. A tall figure in brazen armour appeared over the gate. Fiery words passed—inaudible, but their meaning plain in the way the herald’s shoulders stiffened. He blew one keen note, a threat sharpened by brass, and galloped back with war in his face.

Anthony bent his head to him for the space of two breaths, then wheeled and shouted, “Pioneers, to the van!”

Hedges were levelled; the force took the field and encamped just out of shot. Mounted officers rode south, east, and west to friendly towns for mangonels and palisades, mantelets and raw hides, tar-barrels and carpenters, provisions and all the stubborn furniture of a siege. The camp settled into a new kind of life—mechanical, busy, and incessantly loud: mallets on stakes, saws biting wood, chains clinking, carts rattling, and, above all, the high thin hiss of arrows sewing the air.

At the first clang of the portcullis Denys’s eyes had brightened. When the herald came back with battle in his gaze, Denys saw it and, for the first time in days, cried out, “Courage, tout le monde, le diable est mort.”

If the great warrior whose name he had spoken could have heard, he might have grinned.

The besiegers encamped a furlong from the walls and made roads. Pikemen waited ready for assault; sappers and pioneers crept forward; catapultiers and crossbowmen took their stations. The siege began in the old patient way—filling the moat, mining, breaching, and all the while working under a close rain of quarrels, bolts, stones, and little rocks. A siege was a kind of craft, a trade of wood and rope and leverage, and it demanded a hundred eyes and a mind awake in every limb. To those who watched from safety it might even seem amusing, like a fair; to those beneath the missiles it was a daylong reckoning.

Carpenters advanced behind rolling mantelets to raise a stockade at the very edge of the moat. Some lives were lost—men dropped as suddenly as sacks of meal—but not many, for crossbowmen, Denys among them, rolled up their shields and shot over the workmen’s heads at every besieged who showed so much as a nose, and at every slit or loophole that commanded the ground. Their bolts made a dull, satisfying thunk when they struck wood; a sharper, uglier sound when they found meat.

Under that condensed fire the palisade rose—fresh-cut timber smelling green and raw, sap bleeding where axes had opened it. It stood between the besiegers and the ordinary missiles of the walls.

The defenders answered at night by running out wooden penthouses atop the curtains, fitting beams into square holes built for the purpose. These hoards—true teeth of a medieval fort—gave defenders freer range than any fixed loophole. From them they could drop and shoot with a cruel liberty, as if the wall had grown hands.

The besiegers brought up mangonels and battered those woodworks with huge stones until boards shattered, and the air filled with splinters like flying bones. They built, too, a triangular wooden tower as high as the curtain, kept ready just out of shot, and the sight of it brooding there chilled the town like a bad omen. The defenders began mining beneath the part of the moat where that tower would come, preparing a warm reception. The besiegers mined elsewhere, aiming to get under the square barbican and throw it down.

All this time Denys worked behind his mantelet with another arbalestrier. He made excellent shots—clean, purposeful, the way a cook trims away waste with a sure knife. His skill earned him the peculiar esteem of an unseen archer within the walls, who now and then sent a winged compliment quivering into Denys’s board. One struck within an inch of the narrow slit through which Denys was squinting.

“Peste,” Denys cried, more angry than afraid. “You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as thine hide its head? Comrade, it is one of those cursed Englishmen with his half-ell shaft. I’ll not die till I’ve had a shot at London wall.”

On the defenders’ side a figure soon drew every eye—because he walked where any sensible man would crawl. A tall knight in complete brass paced the battlements under fire, carrying a light but prodigiously long lance with which he directed the defenders’ movements, as if he were conducting music. When disaster befell the besiegers, that knight and that lance were almost always part of it.

Why did not Denys shoot him? Denys did—every day. Other crossbowmen shot. Archers shot. Everybody shot; and the abomination was that he did not mind. Worse: he began to look as if he did not even know when he was hit. Bolts struck and glanced; arrows kissed the metal and fell. He kept pacing, glittering in the bright air, as calmly oblivious as a sailor in wool pacing his deck under harmless rain.

At last the besiegers, spiteful and practical, cursed him and his impervious coat and saved their good steel for softer targets.

The day was bright, clear, not quite frosty. The whole effort concentrated on about two hundred and fifty yards—two curtains and two towers. One tower was the square barbican; another had a pointed roof overlapping on a stone machicolation, so that a row of crenelets grinned between roof and masonry like a mouth of grinders. The curtains wore their hoards, battered by slinging engines.

Along the moat’s edge, on the besiegers’ side, the works sprawled like a gigantic arsenal—longer than broad, peopled by human ants. Levers groaned, winches squealed, pulleys creaked; capstans turned; carts came and went, rattling like bones in a box. Heaps of stone, piles of fascines, stacks of mantelets, rows of fire-barrels sat ready. And through that honest industry flew arrows, back and forth, stitching an invisible cloth. Here and there a man lay flatter than in any decent sleep with feathers stuck in him, and blood oozed darkly into the churned earth.

Opposite the wooden tower, a great penthouse called a “cat” crept toward the curtain, filling the moat with fascines and rubbish pushed out from its mouth. Ropes and pulleys and distant windlasses moved it with slow persistence. The tall knight burned the first cat with blazing tar-barrels. So the besiegers made the roof of this one very steep and covered it with raw hides; the tar slid off, spitting, and did no harm. The knight signalled, and a little trebuchet behind the wall began dropping stones into the moat. At last it found the range, and one stone punched through the cat’s roof, making an ugly wound.

Baldwyn saw, and his temper—always ready as flint—threw sparks. He ordered the great catapult that had been battering the hoards opposite to be turned and levelled, slantwise, at that invulnerable knight.

Denys and his Englishman went to dinner.

For all their murderous attentions, they had made a distant acquaintance, speaking by signs as men do who share a single, constant topic that keeps its importance in peace or war: food. Sometimes Denys would set a piece of bread atop his mantelet; sometimes the archer would dangle something from a string. Thus they dined together as if at the same board—each chewing, each watching, each listening to the battlefield’s rough orchestra: the thump of stones, the twang of bows, the low thunder of shouted orders.

Now the engineers took the unusual step of slinging fifty-pound stones at one man.

The catapult was a beautiful, brutal thing: a fir-tree trunk set to turn on an axis between uprights, not balanced—three-quarters of its length on one side. At the short thick end hung a weight like half a ton, butt toward the enemy. A winch forced the long end down to the earth and pinned it; the weight rose and strained aloft. A stone lay in a sling at the nose. Then the bolt was drawn, and gravity—old and patient—snapped the machine into motion: weight down, long arm up, sling whipping at the top, stone flying with a jerk that seemed to tear the very air.

The huge mass missed the knight but struck the hoard near him, went through roof and floor like paper, and made a gap as wide as a grave. Two things fell out—first the stone, then the mangled body of a besieger it had struck. Down the curtain they went, down, down, and struck the moat almost together. The water closed bubbling on them, swallowing both stone and bone.

“Aha! a good shot,” cried Baldwyn.

The tall knight retired, and the besiegers hooted. He reappeared on the barbican platform, helmet just visible above the parapet, busy as a man in a bakehouse. Soon an enormous Turkish catapult was hauled into place. From that height it flung a twenty-pound stone some two hundred and forty yards; it bounded and knocked dirt into Lord Anthony’s eye and made him swear. The next stone struck a horse drawing a cart of arrows, bowled it dead like a rabbit, and spilled the load.

Then the Turk turned on the besiegers’ wooden tower, supposed to be out of shot. He slung stones cut with sharp edges on purpose and struck it repeatedly, breaking it in several places. Two of the besiegers’ engines were turned on the barbican platform; they rained smaller stones and killed two engineers. But the Turk disdained to answer in kind. He sent a forty-pound stone crashing onto the besiegers’ great catapult near the axis and smashed the whole structure to pieces; the engineers fled, skipping and yelling like boys stung by bees.

In the afternoon Simon, running back to his mantelet from a palisade where he had been shooting, suddenly stared, held out his arms, and rolled face-first into the dirt with an arrow protruding from his back. The Englishman showed himself a moment to enjoy his work. Denys, already prepared, shot; his bolt struck, and the murderous archer staggered away wounded. Simon never moved again.

“I am unlucky in my comrades,” Denys said, and the words tasted like stale ale.

Next morning the besieged saw, with dismay, that the cat was now covered with mattresses and raw hides and was steadily filling the moat. The tall knight stoned it in vain; he flung burning tar-barrels in vain. Then, with his own hands, he lowered by a rope a bag of burning sulphur and pitch. The stench rolled out thick and choking—sulphur’s bitter bite, pitch’s oily reek—so that the men within were smoked like hams.

But Baldwyn, armoured like a lobster in shining plates, ran forward, bounded onto the cat’s roof, and cut the rope. The work went on while smoke curled and thinned, carried away by the indifferent wind.

Then the knight sent fresh engineers into the mine and underpinned the place with beams, smearing them thick with grease and tar, laying a trap in the earth as carefully as a cook lays kindling under a pot.

At break of day the moat was filled. The wooden tower began to move on its wheels toward the curtain where two catapults were already clearing the hoards. Its approach was awful, almost magical, for it moved without visible agency—driven by internal rollers worked by leverage. On its top platform stood the first assailing party, protected in front by the drawbridge of the turret, which stood upright until it would be lowered onto the wall; protected better still by full suits of armour.

The defenders slung stones at the tower and struck it often, but the hides and mattresses drank the blows. Presently it stood at the moat’s edge. The tall knight bade fire the mine beneath it.

At that moment the Turkish engine flung a stone of half a hundredweight right among the knights on the tower. Two were carried off it onto the plain. One lay and writhed; the other neither moved nor spoke.

Now the besieging catapults hurled blazing tar-barrels and set the hoards on both sides aflame. The assailants ran up ladders behind the tower; the drawbridge was lowered onto the battered curtain. In concert the engines fired adjoining works to drive defenders back. Armoured men sprang onto the bridge led by Baldwyn. The invulnerable knight and his men-at-arms met them, and a fearful combat began on that narrow plank of death. Men fell headlong, armour flashing, bodies tumbling like dropped pots.

Fresh besiegers swarmed up behind. The defenders were driven off the bridge. Another minute and the town might have been taken.

But the mine had been timed with a craft as exact as measuring spice: just then the underpinnings gave way. The tower sank suddenly away from the wall, tearing the drawbridge clear and pouring the soldiers off it—against the masonry and into the dry moat below, a heap of iron and limbs.

A fierce shout rose from the defenders. In a moment they surrounded Baldwyn and his fellows—and, strangely, offered quarter. While some disarmed and disposed of the prisoners, others fired the turret in fifty places with a kind of hand-grenades. The tall knight was busiest of all, loading fire-bags onto his long spear and thrusting them into the doomed structure that had lately been so terrible.

To do this he had to stand on a projecting beam of the shattered hoard, holding on by a pikeman’s hand to steady himself.

It provoked Denys. The confusion seemed a cloak. He ran out from behind his mantelet, levelled his crossbow, and shot—missing the knight clean, but driving the bolt into the pikeman’s brain. The hand let go. The tall knight fell heavily from the wall, lance and all.

Denys stared, wonder-struck. In that unlucky moment his arm burned hot, then went cold as if plunged into water. An English arrow had skewered it.

His small pain was swallowed by the larger drama. The knight, armour glittering in the morning sun, fell headlong, but turned as he struck the water and hit with a slap that sounded a mile off. No one thought to see him again. Yet he fell at the edge of the fascines where the turret stood cocked on one side. His spear stuck into them under water; by a mighty effort he worked to the side, but could not climb out.

Anthony sent a dozen knights with a white flag to take him prisoner. The fallen terror submitted like a lamb, saying nothing. They brought him dripping and mud-plastered to Anthony’s tent. At first Anthony laughed at the sight of that once-glittering armour fouled like a pot left too long in ashes. Then his face hardened.

“I marvel, sir,” he said, “that so good a knight as you should know his devoir so ill as turn rebel, and give us all this trouble.”

“I am n-n-n-n-no knight,” the prisoner stammered.

“What then?”

“A hosier.”

“A what? Then thy armour shall be stripped off, and thou shalt be tied to a stake in front of the works, and riddled with arrows for a warning to traitors.”

“N-n-n-no! Duda-duda-don’t do that.”

“Why not?”

“Tuta-tuta-townsfolk will h-h-h-hang t’other buba-buba-bastard.”

“What—whom?”

“Your bub-bub-brother Baldwyn.”

Anthony started as if a hot coal had fallen into his glove. “What, have you knaves ta’en him?”

The warlike hosier nodded.

“Hang the fool!” Anthony said peevishly, more angry at being checked than at the peril.

The hosier watched his eye, then doffed his helmet and drew from its lining a letter—intercepted. It was from the duke, bidding Anthony come to court immediately. He was to represent Burgundy at the court of England; to go over, receive the English king’s sister, and conduct her to her bridegroom, the Earl of Charolois.

The words had the power of perfume: they carried the scent of velvet halls, rich tables, music untroubled by screams, and a court famed as the gayest and most luxurious in Europe. The hosier saw desire rise in Anthony like hunger.

“We’ll gega-gega-give ye a thousand angels,” the hosier stammered, “to raise the siege.”

“And Baldwyn?”

“I’ll gega-gega-go and send him with the money.”

It was dinner-time. A flag of truce was hoisted on both sides. The sham knight and the true one dined together, and by the end of the meal—between bread broken and meat torn—came to a friendly understanding.

“But what is your grievance, my good friend?” Anthony asked, as if rebellion were merely a seasoning that had been overdone.

“Tuta-tuta-too much taxes,” said the hosier, with the plainness of a man who has counted every penny and felt each one taken.

Denys, discovering the arrow in his right arm, turned his back—protected by a long shield—and walked sulkily into camp, the noise of the siege dulled by the rush of blood in his ears. The wound throbbed with its own pulse, like a kettle beginning to simmer.

He met the Comte de Jarnac, who had seen his brilliant shot; finding him wounded into the bargain, the count dropped into his hand a handful of broad pieces, bright as freshly turned loaves.

“Hast got the better of thy grief, arbalestrier, methinks.”

“My grief, yes,” Denys replied, the words tight between his teeth. “But not my love. As soon as ever I have put down this rebellion, I go to Holland; and there I shall meet with him.”

The event was nearer than Denys thought. Next day he was relieved from service; and though his wound was no trifle, he set out with a stout heart to rejoin his friend in Holland, leaving behind him the siege’s clangour and its bitter smoke, and carrying with him the ache of iron in flesh and of a name that tasted, in his mouth, like home bread remembered in exile.
