Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man of cunning, and he approached the young novice not with direct accusation, but with a subtle feint. “The town records,” he began, “are written in cramped hand, and the ink has faded with time.” He offered Gerard the task of copying them into a clearer form—a duty that would take months. Gerard asked what he would be paid. Ghysbrecht named a sum barely enough to cover parchment, ink, and quills.

“But burgomaster,” Gerard replied, “what of my labor? This is no mere scribbling—it is skilled work, and a year’s worth of it.”

“Your labor?” Ghysbrecht scoffed. “Is marking parchment truly labor? It requires little sweat, I should think.”

“It is labor,” Gerard insisted, “and skilled at that. Such craft is valued more than brute toil, whether it sweats or not. And then there is my time.”

“Your time?” Ghysbrecht laughed. “What is time to a man of twenty-two?”

Then, fixing his gaze sharply upon the youth, he pressed on: “Say rather, you are idle. You are in love. Your body may be here among these chanting monks, but your heart is with Peter Brandt and his red-haired girl.”

“I know no Peter Brandt,” Gerard said flatly.

That denial only deepened Ghysbrecht’s suspicion. The boy had been playing his part well—too well. “You lie!” he burst out. “Did I not find you standing beside her on the road to Rotterdam?”

“Ahh!” Gerard murmured, though he said nothing more.

“Ahh!” Ghysbrecht echoed. “And you were seen at Sevenbergen just the other day.”

“Was I?” Gerard asked quietly.

“Ahh—and at Peter’s house.”

“At Sevenbergen?” Gerard repeated.

“Ay,” Ghysbrecht declared. “At Sevenbergen.”

It was a gambit—what today might be called a bluff—deliberately cast as fact to provoke a reaction. But the result startled even the crafty burgomaster. Gerard leapt to his feet, trembling, his eyes alight with sudden agitation.

“Burgomaster,” he said, voice shaking, “I have not set foot in Sevenbergen in three years. I do not know the names of those you claim to have seen me with, nor where they lived. But since you care so little for my time—though it is precious to me—I bid you good day.”

And with that, he turned and fled from the room, his eyes blazing.

Ghysbrecht rose in fury—but then sat back down, his expression darkening. “He does not fear me,” he muttered. “He knows something—perhaps all.”

Without delay, he summoned his trusted servant and nearly dragged him to the window. “See that man?” he hissed. “Go! Follow him—but keep hidden. He is young, yet seasoned in deceit. Watch him all day. Report every step he takes, every place he goes.”

Night fell before the servant returned.

“Well?” Ghysbrecht demanded, voice sharp with anticipation. “Well?”

“Master,” the servant reported, “the young man left you and went straight to Sevenbergen.”

Ghysbrecht groaned aloud.

“To the house of Peter the Magician.”
