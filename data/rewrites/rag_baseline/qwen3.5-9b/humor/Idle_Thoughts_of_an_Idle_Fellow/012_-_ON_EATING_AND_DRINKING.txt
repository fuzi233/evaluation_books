The aroma of roasted meat and steaming vegetables hung heavy in the air, a tangible promise of sustenance that seemed to vibrate against the very bones of the diner. I have always possessed a profound affection for the acts of eating and drinking, a predilection that was particularly acute during my childhood. In those early years, I was blessed with both a voracious appetite and a robust digestion. I recall vividly the arrival of a gentleman at our household, a figure distinguished by dull eyes and a complexion the color of a bruised plum. For approximately five minutes, he observed me consuming my meal with a fascination bordering on the hypnotic. Finally, turning his gaze to my father, he asked, "Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?"

"My son has never complained of such ailments," my father replied calmly. "Do you find yourself plagued by dyspepsia, Colly-wobbles?" (Though 'Colly-wobbles' was my nickname, it bore no resemblance to my actual name).

"No, sir," I answered promptly. Immediately following this declaration, curiosity compelled me to ask, "What precisely is dyspepsia, sir?"

The pallid gentleman regarded me with an expression of mingled astonishment and envy. Then, in a tone dripping with infinite pity, he delivered his verdict slowly: "You will know... some day."

My beloved mother, in her wisdom, often remarked that she derived genuine pleasure from watching me eat. It remains a source of enduring satisfaction to me that I must have provided her with considerable gratification in this regard. Indeed, a growing, healthy youth, engaged in ample physical activity and exercising restraint regarding excessive study, can generally satisfy even the most demanding expectations concerning his capacity for nourishment.

It is genuinely amusing to observe boys consume food when one need not pay for the privilege. Their conception of a proper, square meal involves a staggering array of provisions: a pound and a half of roast beef accompanied by five or six substantial potatoes (those coated in soap residue being preferred for their density), a generous portion of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, followed by a pair of currant dumplings, several green apples, a pennyworth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of ginger-beer. Upon completion of this gastronomic marathon, they proceed immediately to playing at horses.

One cannot help but wonder how they must disdain us men, who are required to remain sedentary for a couple of hours after dining on mere spoonfuls of clear soup and the wing of a chicken! Yet, the boys possess disadvantages as well. A boy never enjoys the luxury of total satiety; he never truly feels full. He can never stretch out his limbs, rest his hands behind his head, and close his eyes to sink into the ethereal bliss that envelops the well-dined adult. Food makes no significant difference to a boy. To a man, however, a satisfying dinner acts as a good fairy's potion, rendering the world thereafter brighter and more benevolent. A man who has eaten well develops a yearning love for all his fellow creatures. He strokes a cat with gentle tenderness, cooing "poor pussy" in tones of deepest emotion. He sympathizes with the musicians of a passing German band, wondering if they are chilled, and for a fleeting moment, he ceases to harbor hatred for his wife's relatives.

A good dinner reveals the softer dimensions of male character. Under its genial influence, the gloomy and morose transform into jovial conversationalists. Individuals who, throughout the day, move about with the sour demeanor of those subsisting solely on vinegar and Epsom salts, break into wreathed smiles after dinner, exhibiting a sudden urge to pat small children on the head and speak vaguely to them about sixpences. Serious men thaw into mild cheerfulness, while snobbish youths with heavy mustaches momentarily forget how to render themselves objectionable.

I invariably feel sentimental after dinner. It is the sole occasion when I can properly appreciate love stories. When the hero embraces his beloved in a final, wild clutch and stifles a sob, I feel as devastated as if I had played a disastrous hand at whist, revealing only a deuce. When the heroine meets her end, I weep openly. Were I to read the same narrative early in the morning, I would likely sneer at it. Digestion—or rather, indigestion—exerts a marvelous influence upon the heart. Should I wish to compose something profoundly pathetic, I consume a large plate of hot buttered muffins roughly an hour prior to commencing my work. By the time I sit down to write, an unutterable melancholy has descended upon me. I envision heartbroken lovers parting forever at lonely wayside stiles, surrounded by a deepening sad twilight, broken only by the tinkling of a distant sheep-bell. I imagine old men gazing at withered flowers until their sight blurs with tears; dainty maidens waiting at open casements, watching as "he cometh not," while the heavy years roll by and their golden hair turns white and thin. The infants they once dandled have become grown adults burdened by their own corporeal torments, and the playmates they once laughed with lie silent beneath the waving grass. Yet, they continue to wait and watch until the dark shadows of the unknown night encroach, gathering around them as the world, with its childish troubles, fades from their aching eyes.

In my imagination, I see pale corpses cast upon white-foamed waves, deathbeds stained with bitter tears, and graves scattered across trackless deserts. I hear the wild wailing of women, the low moaning of children, and the dry sobbing of strong men. It is all attributable to the muffins. I could not conjure a single melancholic fantasy upon a mutton chop and a glass of champagne.

A full stomach serves as a great aid to poetry, and indeed, no sentiment of any kind can endure upon an empty one. We lack neither the time nor the inclination to indulge in fanciful troubles until we have dispelled our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over deceased dicky-birds while a bailiff stands in the house, nor do we worry whether our mistress's smiles are cold, hot, lukewarm, or otherwise, when we are unsure of where to procure our next shilling.

When I refer to "foolish people" in this contemptuous manner, I mean those who entertain opinions differing from my own. If there is one individual I despise more than another, it is the man who does not think exactly as I do on all topics. Foolish people, therefore, are those who, having experienced little of either hunger or abundance, will assert that mental distress is far more agonizing than bodily pain. This is a romantic and touching theory, so comforting to the love-sick young sapling who looks down patronizingly upon a poor devil with a starved, white face, thinking, "Ah, how happy you are compared with me!" So soothing, too, for fat old gentlemen who cackle about the superiority of poverty over riches. But it is all nonsense—all cant. An aching head soon causes one to forget an aching heart. A broken finger drives away all recollections of an empty chair. And when a man feels truly hungry, he feels nothing else.

We sleek, well-fed folk can hardly comprehend the sensation of hunger. We know what it is to lack appetite and disregard the dainties placed before us, but we fail to understand what it means to sicken for food—to die for bread while others waste it—to gaze with famished eyes upon coarse fare steaming behind dingy windows, longing for a pennyworth of pea pudding yet lacking the coin to purchase it—to feel that a crust would be delicious and a bone a banquet.

For us, hunger is a luxury, a piquant, flavor-enhancing sauce. It is well worth experiencing hunger and thirst merely to discover the extent of gratification obtainable from eating and drinking. If you wish to thoroughly enjoy your dinner, undertake a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and refrain from consuming anything until your return. Your eyes will glisten at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes! With what a sigh of contentment you will lower the empty beer tankard and raise your knife and fork! And how comfortably you will feel afterward as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam upon everyone present.

However, ensure that when adopting this regimen, the promised good dinner is actually available at the conclusion, lest the resulting disappointment prove trying. I recall once a friend and I—dear old Joe, it was. Alas! How we lose one another in life's mist. It must have been eight years since I last saw Joseph Taboys. How pleasant it would be to encounter his jovial visage again, to clasp his strong hand, and to hear his cheery laughter once more! He also owes me fourteen shillings. Well, we were on holiday together, and one morning we broke fast early and embarked on a tremendous long walk. We had ordered a duck for dinner the previous evening. We instructed the cook, "Get a big one, because we shall come home awfully hungry." As we departed, our landlady approached in high spirits. "I have secured you gentlemen a duck, if you please," she announced. "If you manage to finish that, you will do well." She held aloft a bird approximately the size of a door-mat. We chuckled at the spectacle and declared we would attempt it, speaking with a self-conscious pride characteristic of men aware of their own power. Then we set forth.
