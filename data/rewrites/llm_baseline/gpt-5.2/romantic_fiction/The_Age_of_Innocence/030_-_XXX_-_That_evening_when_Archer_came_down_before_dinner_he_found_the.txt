That evening, when Archer came down before dinner, the drawing-room waited for him like a covered dish gone cold—orderly, polished, and mute, with not so much as a whisper of fire in it. Since Mrs. Manson Mingott had been struck down, the family’s dinners and visits had been postponed with the same solemnity with which a cook shutters a kitchen in mourning; and Archer and May, left to dine alone, moved through their quiet house as if through a well-run pantry where every jar was labeled and nothing unexpected ever fermented.

May was usually first—punctual as the dinner-bell itself—so his surprise sharpened into a small, persistent uneasiness. Upstairs, while he dressed, he had heard her footsteps and the soft drag of drawers, the faint metallic click of hairpins; proof she was at home, only delayed. He found himself clinging to such little proofs the way a man in a storm clings to the sound of a lighthouse bell, counting each pulse to keep his mind from drifting. Perhaps, he thought with a sudden sour sympathy, that was what had kept Mr. Welland so devoted to minor household details all these years: a careful husbandry of trifles to keep wilder visions from invading the domestic table.

When May finally entered, Archer saw at once that something in her had dimmed. She wore the low-necked, tightly-laced dinner dress that the Mingott ceremonial demanded even on the most “informal” evenings—an absurd insistence on trussing youth like poultry for a family feast. Her fair hair had been wound into its familiar accumulated coils with the patience of a hand that had done the same task in the same mirror for generations; but her face, in contrast, was wan, almost faded, as if the daylight had scalded it and left it pale. Yet she turned on him her practiced tenderness, and her eyes still held the blue dazzle of the day before.

“What became of you, dear?” she asked. “I was waiting at Granny’s, and Ellen came alone, and said she had dropped you on the way because you had to rush off on business. There’s nothing wrong?”

“Only some letters I’d forgotten,” he said, “and wanted to get off before dinner.”

“Ah—” May’s voice, after that little catch, steadied itself again. A moment later she added, too evenly: “I’m sorry you didn’t come to Granny’s—unless the letters were urgent.”

“They were,” Archer returned, surprised at the insistence, and suddenly irritated by the way she held to a small point as if it were a bone to be picked clean. “Besides, I don’t see why I should have gone to your grandmother’s. I didn’t know you were there.”

She turned away to the looking-glass above the mantel. Standing there, lifting her long arm to fasten a puff that had slipped in the intricate architecture of her hair, she looked to him oddly languid, as if the springiness had gone out of her, as if even her obedience to convention had begun to tire her. He wondered, with an uncomfortable pinch of conscience, whether the dead level of their days had laid its weight on her too.

And then he remembered: that morning, as he had gone out, she had called down from the landing that she would meet him at her grandmother’s so they might drive home together. He had answered lightly—too lightly—“Yes!” and then, his mind already elsewhere, had forgotten. The omission was small enough to be swallowed whole by any generous appetite; yet now it lay between them like an unacknowledged bitterness in a sauce.

Compunction pricked him, but irritation rose with it. Nearly two years of marriage, and still a trifling lapse could be stored up and served back at dinner like a cold leftover. He was weary of living in a perpetual tepid honeymoon—no heat of passion, yet all the obligations, all the careful attendance, all the expectation that he would be endlessly attentive to the minute cravings of a life arranged to exclude hunger.

To cover his annoyance, he asked after Mrs. Mingott. May said her grandmother was improving, but had been disturbed by the latest news about the Beauforts.

“What news?”

“They’re going to stay in New York. I believe he’s going into an insurance business, or something. They’re looking about for a small house.”

It was too preposterous for discussion—Beaufort reduced to hawking security like a peddler, the Beaufort name being packed into a “small house” like furniture in a cart. The absurdity sat on Archer’s tongue and would not be spoken, and they went in to dinner.

The meal was served with the Welland perfection—courses arriving as reliably as the chiming of a clock. There was soup with the faint, safe perfume of long-simmered stock; a fish course whose butter sauce shone without daring; game carved with reverence and no appetite; and the familiar sweets that ended the ritual as inevitably as a hymn. Yet even the most carefully managed table has its silences, and Archer noticed May made no allusion to Madame Olenska—neither to her presence at old Catherine’s nor to how she had been received.

He was grateful, and yet the omission seemed ominous, like a bird refusing to sing in a garden where it always sang before.

After dinner they went up to the library for coffee. Archer lit a cigar and took down Michelet, choosing history as he had taken to doing in the evenings—less from any devotion to France than from a wish to avoid poetry. If May saw him with verse she had lately begun to ask him to read aloud, and then to offer her own comments—opinions she had once borrowed obediently from him, but now produced with a conscientious freshness that spoiled for him what he loved. History, at least, could be held at arm’s length, and its dead voices could not be made to blush under her judgments.

May fetched her workbasket and drew up an armchair to the green-shaded student lamp. She uncovered a cushion she was embroidering for his sofa, stabbing at the canvas with slow diligence. Her hands—large, capable, meant for oars and reins and the clean resistance of outdoor life—were not made for such finicking labor, but since other wives embroidered cushions for their husbands, she would not omit this final stitch in the tapestry of devotion.

She sat so that, by merely lifting his eyes, Archer could see her bent over the frame; the ruffled elbow sleeves slipping back from firm round arms; the betrothal sapphire shining above the broad gold wedding ring; the right hand driving the needle down and drawing it up again, as if trying to force softness out of stubborn threads. The lamplight lay full on her clear brow, and with a secret dismay he told himself that he would always know the thoughts behind it. Never—through all the years ahead—would she startle him by an unexpected mood, a new idea, a weakness, a cruelty, an emotion. She had spent her romance in their brief courting as one spends the rarest spices: lavishly, and then—when the dish is served—there is no more to add. Now she was ripening into a copy of her mother, and by some obscure domestic alchemy trying to turn him into a Mr. Welland.

He laid down his book and stood up abruptly; at once she raised her head.

“What’s the matter?”

“The room is stifling. I want a little air.”

He had insisted that the library curtains should run on a rod, so they could actually be drawn, not nailed in place and looped over lace like a parlor’s dead drapery. He pulled them back and pushed up the sash, leaning out into the icy night.

The cold struck his face like a hard truth. Down the street the city made its winter music: the thin crunch of a late carriage wheel, the far-off iron ring of hoof on frozen ground, a muffled shout dissolving into distance. Above it all, the wind worried at cornices and chimneys with a steady hiss, a cleaner sound than the thick, upholstered hush indoors. The mere fact of not looking at May—seated beside his table, under his lamp, in the circle of domestic light—was a relief. Other houses rose in darkness, other roofs and chimneys, other lives behind other windows; and beyond them, other cities beyond New York, and a whole world beyond his world. The sense of that larger breathing space cleared his brain.

After a few minutes he heard May’s voice, drawn a little tight: “Newland! Do shut the window. You’ll catch your death.”

He pulled the sash down and turned back. “Catch my death!” he echoed, and felt the savage impulse to add: But I’ve caught it already. I am dead—I’ve been dead for months.

And then the word—death—leaped up in him, not as a metaphor but as a wild practical suggestion, as if a door he had never allowed himself to see had swung open on its hinges.

What if it were she who was dead?

If May were to die—soon—leave him free—

He stood there in the warm familiar room and looked at her and wished her dead; and the strangeness of the wish, the intoxicating release it promised, held him so completely that its horror did not strike him at once. He felt only that chance had offered him a new possibility to which his sick soul might cling. People did die: young people, healthy people. May might die, and set him free.

She glanced up; and he saw by the widening of her eyes that something in his own face had betrayed him.

“Newland! Are you ill?”

He shook his head quickly and turned toward his chair. She bent over her work again, and as he passed he laid his hand on her hair.

“Poor May,” he said.

“Poor? Why poor?” she echoed, with a strained little laugh.

“Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you,” he answered, and laughed too.

For a moment she was silent; then, very low, her head bowed over the canvas, she said: “I shall never worry if you’re happy.”

“Ah, my dear; and I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows.”

“In this weather?” she protested; and with a sigh he buried himself in Michelet, as if the dead past could drown out the living present.

Six or seven days passed. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska, and became aware that her name would not be spoken in his presence by any member of the family—as if it were a pungent spice banned from the household lest it awaken appetites. He did not try to see her; while she watched at old Catherine’s guarded bedside it would have been almost impossible. In the uncertainty he let himself drift, conscious somewhere below the surface of a resolve that had come to him at the open window. That resolve—dark, steady—made it easy to wait and make no sign.

Then one day May told him that Mrs. Manson Mingott had asked to see him.

There was nothing surprising in the request. The old lady was steadily recovering, and she had always declared openly that she preferred Archer to any other of her grandsons-in-law. May delivered the message with evident pleasure; she was proud of her grandmother’s approval of her husband, as if it were another course served up in honor of their marriage.

A pause, and then Archer said, as duty required: “All right. Shall we go together this afternoon?”

May’s face brightened at once, then she answered quickly: “Oh, you’d much better go alone. It bores Granny to see the same people too often.”

His heart began to beat violently as he rang old Mrs. Mingott’s bell. He had wanted above all things to go alone, for he felt sure the visit would give him a chance to speak a word in private to the Countess Olenska. He meant to wait until the chance presented itself naturally. Here it was; here he stood on the doorstep.

Behind the door, behind the curtains of the yellow damask room next to the hall, she was surely waiting. In another moment he should see her, speak to her before she led him to the sick-room. He wanted only to ask one question. What was the date of her return to Washington? She could hardly refuse to answer that.

But in the yellow sitting-room it was the mulatto maid who waited, her white teeth shining like a keyboard as she pushed back the sliding doors and ushered him in.

Old Catherine sat in a vast, throne-like armchair near her bed. Beside her stood a mahogany table bearing a cast bronze lamp with an engraved globe, topped by a green paper shade like a little canopy. There was no book within reach, no newspaper, no basket of knitting, no feminine pretense of busy fingers: conversation had always been Mrs. Mingott’s sole occupation, and she would have scorned to garnish it with fancywork. Archer saw no trace of the distortion left by her stroke. She looked paler, the shadows deeper in the folds and recesses of her obesity; and in her fluted mob-cap, tied with a starched bow between her first two chins, and the muslin kerchief crossed over her billowing purple dressing-gown, she had the air of some shrewd, kindly ancestress—one of those formidable farm-grandmothers who have always known where the best provisions are kept and who has eaten well enough to forgive most human folly.

She held out one of the little hands that nestled in her enormous lap like a pampered animal and called to the maid: “Don’t let in any one else. If my daughters call, say I’m asleep.”

When the maid vanished, the old woman turned her bright attention to Archer.

“My dear, am I perfectly hideous?” she asked gaily, shooting out a hand toward the inaccessible folds of muslin on her bosom. “My daughters tell me it doesn’t matter at my age—as if hideousness didn’t matter all the more the harder it gets to conceal!”

“My dear, you’re handsomer than ever,” Archer returned in her own tone; and she threw back her head and laughed.

“Ah, but not as handsome as Ellen!” she snapped, twinkling maliciously; and before he could answer she added: “Was she so awfully handsome the day you drove her up from the ferry?”

He laughed in spite of himself.

“Was it because you told her so that she had to put you out on the way? In my youth young men didn’t desert pretty women unless they were made to!”

She chuckled again, then cut it short to say almost querulously: “It’s a pity she didn’t marry you; I always told her so. It would have spared me all this worry. But who ever thinks of sparing their grandmother worry?”

For an instant Archer wondered if illness had blurred her discretion. But she broke out at once, brisk and resolute: “Well, it’s settled, anyhow. She’s going to stay with me, whatever the rest of the family say! She hadn’t been here five minutes before I’d have gone down on my knees to keep her—if only, for the last twenty years, I’d been able to see where the floor was!”

He listened, breath quickening, while she went on, her voice gaining force like a kettle coming to boil.

“They’d talked me over—you know that, no doubt. Persuaded me—Lovell and Letterblair and Augusta Welland and all the rest—that I must hold out and cut off her allowance till she was made to see it was her duty to go back to Olenski. They thought they’d convinced me when the secretary, or whatever he was, came out with the last proposals. Handsome proposals, I confess. After all, marriage is marriage, and money’s money—both useful things in their way—and I didn’t know what to answer—”

She paused to draw breath, and Archer heard in that pause the labor of her body, the heavy machinery of a great life refusing to stop.

“But the minute I laid eyes on her,” she resumed, “I said: ‘You sweet bird, you! Shut you up in that cage again? Never!’ And now it’s settled that she’s to stay here and nurse her Granny as long as there’s a Granny to nurse. It’s not a gay prospect, but she doesn’t mind; and of course I’ve told Letterblair she’s to be given her proper allowance.”

The news rushed through Archer with a heat he could not name—joy or pain, relief or fresh torment. He had so firmly decided on a course that for a moment he could not readjust his thoughts. Then, gradually, a delicious sense of difficulties deferred stole over him, of opportunities miraculously provided. If Ellen had consented to live with her grandmother, surely it was because she had recognized she could not give him up. It was her answer to his final appeal: if she would not take the extreme step he had urged, she had at last yielded to the half-measure that still kept her near.

He sank back into that thought with the involuntary relief of a man ready to gamble everything who suddenly tastes the dangerous sweetness of security.

“She couldn’t have gone back—it was impossible!” he exclaimed.

“Ah, my dear, I always knew you were on her side,” Mrs. Mingott said with satisfaction; “and that’s why I sent for you today, and why I said to your pretty wife, when she proposed to come with you: ‘No, my dear, I’m pining to see Newland, and I don’t want anybody to share our transports.’ For you see, my dear—” She drew her head back as far as her tethering chins allowed and fixed him with a look hard and keen as a cook’s carving-knife—“you see, we shall have a fight yet. The family don’t want her here. They’ll say it’s because I’ve been ill, because I’m a weak old woman, that she’s persuaded me. I’m not well enough yet to fight them one by one, and you’ve got to do it for me.”

“I?” Archer stammered.

“You. Why not?” Her round eyes sharpened further, and her hand fluttered from the chair-arm and lit on his with a clutch of little pale nails, bird-claws gripping. “Why not?” she repeated, probing.

Under her gaze Archer recovered himself. “Oh, I don’t count—I’m too insignificant.”

“Well, you’re Letterblair’s partner, ain’t you? You’ve got to get at them through Letterblair. Unless you’ve got a reason,” she insisted, and the word “reason” fell with a weight that made his skin prickle.

He met it with a steadiness he had learned from years of family dinners where the smallest false note was heard. “Oh, my dear, I back you to hold your own against them all without my help; but you shall have it if you need it,” he said.

“Then we’re safe!” she sighed; and smiling on him with all her ancient cunning, she added, settling her head among the cushions: “I always knew you’d back us up, because they never quote you when they talk about its being her duty to go home.”

He winced at her terrifying perspicacity and had a sudden, sharp desire to ask: And May—do they quote her? But he judged it safer to turn the question away from his wife’s mouth.

“And Madame Olenska? When am I to see her?” he asked.

The old lady chuckled, crumpled her lids, and performed archness like an actor who knows the audience will laugh. “Not today. One at a time, please. Madame Olenska’s gone out.”

He flushed with disappointment, and she went on, timing her effect as carefully as any hostess arranging courses.

“She’s gone out, my child—gone in my carriage to see Regina Beaufort.” She paused, letting the name settle, bitter as burnt sugar. “That’s what she’s reduced me to already. The day after she got here she put on her best bonnet and told me, cool as a cucumber, she was going to call on Regina Beaufort. ‘I don’t know her; who is she?’ says I. ‘She’s your grand-niece, and a most unhappy woman,’ she says. ‘She’s the wife of a scoundrel,’ I answered. ‘Well,’ she says, ‘and so am I, and yet all my family want me to go back to him.’ Well, that floored me, and I let her go. And finally one day she said it was raining too hard to go out on foot, and she wanted me to lend her my carriage. ‘What for?’ I asked. And she said: ‘To go and see cousin Regina’—cousin! Now, my dear, I looked out of the window and saw it wasn’t raining a drop; but I understood her, and I let her have the carriage. After all, Regina’s a brave woman, and so is she; and I’ve always liked courage above everything.”

Archer bent and pressed his lips to the little hand still resting on his, as if to seal his vow to her plot against the family.

“Eh—eh—eh!” Mrs. Mingott snapped out with her mocking cackle. “Whose hand did you think you were kissing, young man—your wife’s, I hope?”

As he rose to go she called after him, brisk again, dismissing him as if sending a messenger from her kitchen: “Give her her Granny’s love; but you’d better not say anything about our talk.”
