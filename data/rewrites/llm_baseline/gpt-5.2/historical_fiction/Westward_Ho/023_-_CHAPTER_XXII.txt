My next passage is a bitter one; I will make it no longer than the truth demands. Yet it must be set down, that those who read may judge, in fairness, what manner of enemies Englishmen had to meet in those iron years.

Three weeks have slipped away, and the world has shifted—as a pot is lifted from one hearth and set upon another—into a long, low corridor of cells in Cartagena, where the air is old, sour, and close, and every sound is magnified. The drip of moisture has a metronome’s patience; a rat’s scratch on stone is as loud as a file. Even the lamp’s small flame seems to make a noise in that darkness, with its thin, oily hiss and its smell of tallow and smoke.

The door of one cell stands open. Within are two cloaked figures. One we know: Eustace Leigh. The other is a familiar of the Holy Office, and in his hand he carries the lamp, holding it so that the light falls upon a bed of straw—flattened and fouled by long use—and upon the sleeping figure of a man.

The high white brow, the pale delicate features: we know them too. They are Frank’s.

Saved half-dead from the fury of savage negroes, he has been kept for cruelties more finely measured—cruelties practiced by civilized and Christian men, with pen, seal, and prayer. He had undergone the question that afternoon; and now Eustace, his betrayer, is come to persuade him—or to ensnare him. Eustace himself hardly knows which. And yet he would give his life to save his cousin.

His life? He has long since ceased to care for that. He has done what he has done because it was called duty; and now he is to do duty once more: to wake the sleeper, and argue, coax, threaten him into recantation while “his heart is still tender from the torture,” as Eustace’s employers phrase it, as coolly as if they spoke of softening beans in a pot, or kneading a stubborn dough.

And yet how calmly he is sleeping.

Is it only the lamp’s trick, trembling on Frank’s mouth, or is there a smile upon his lips? Eustace takes the lamp himself and bends over him to see; and as he bends he hears Frank whispering, in his dreams, his mother’s name—and then a name higher and holier still. The sound is so human, so homely, that for a moment the cell is not a cell, but a darkened room in England where a boy speaks in his sleep and the house listens, and the night wind outside is kind.

Eustace cannot find the heart to wake him.

“Let him rest,” he whispers to his companion. “After all, I fear my words will be of little use.”

“I fear so too, sir. Never did I behold a more obdurate heretic. He did not scruple to scoff openly at their holinesses.”

“Ah!” said Eustace; “great is the pravity of the human heart, and the power of Satan! Let us go for the present.”

“Where is she?”

“The elder sorceress, or the younger?”

“The younger—the—”

“The Señora de Soto? Ah, poor thing! One could be sorry for her, were she not a heretic.”

And the man eyed Eustace keenly, as if tasting his tone for any trace of softness, then added, quietly, “She is at present with the notary; to the benefit of her soul, I trust—”

Eustace half stopped, shuddering. He could hardly gather himself enough to gasp an “Amen!”

“Within there,” said the familiar, pointing carelessly to a door as they went down the corridor. “We can listen a moment, if you like; but don’t betray me, señor.”

Eustace knew well enough that the fellow was likely watching him even as he spoke, ready to report to his superiors the least sign of pity for a heretic. Still, a horrible curiosity overcame fear. He paused close to the fatal door.

His face burned; his knees knocked together; the ringing in his ears grew until it seemed the whole passage had become a shell held to the sea. His heart beat so violently that he felt it against the wall as he leaned there, hiding his convulsed face as best he could from his companion. In that place, even breath was loud. Even silence had a weight and a voice.

A man’s voice was plainly audible within—low, but distinct. The notary, patient as a butcher sharpening a blade, was trying that old charge of witchcraft, which inquisitors, whether to ease their consciences or to whiten their villainy in the eyes of the mob, so often laid upon their victims.

Then Eustace’s heart sank clean through him as he heard a woman’s voice reply—sharpened by indignation and agony, as a knife is honed on a stone:

“Witchcraft against Don Guzman? What need of that, oh God! what need?”

“You deny it then, señora? We are sorry for you; but—”

A confused, choking murmur from the victim, mingled with words that might mean anything or nothing, as if pain itself had broken language into scraps, into sounds that would not translate even to mercy.

“She has confessed!” whispered Eustace. “Saints, I thank you!—she—”

A wail rang out, and it seemed to strike the corridor like a blow, and rebound from stone to bone. It was not merely heard; it entered him—through ear, through teeth, through the very marrow—until his thoughts were nothing but that one sound.

He would have torn at the door to open it; but his companion forced him away. Another wail followed, and another, and the familiar hurried him on. Eustace stopped his ears with his hands in vain; those piercing cries chased him like avenging angels through the dreadful vaults.

At last he escaped—out into fragrant open air and golden tropic moonlight, into a garden that might have served as a model for Eden. Night insects kept their delicate percussion; palms whispered with a soft, continual brush; somewhere water fell, steady and bright, making a music fit to lull the sick. The scent of crushed leaf and bruised blossom rose as if the earth were offering comfort like warm bread.

But man’s hell followed into God’s heaven. The wails still rang through his ears, blotting out the garden’s gentler sounds, drowning the natural world’s clean song with a manufactured anguish.

“Oh, misery, misery, misery!” he murmured to himself through grinding teeth; “and I have brought her to this! I have had to bring her to it! What else could I? Who dare blame me? And yet what devilish sin can I have committed, that requires to be punished thus? Was there no one to be found but me? No one? And yet it may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance!”

“It may, indeed,” said the familiar, “for she is delicate, and cannot endure much. You ought to know as well as I, señor, the merciful disposition of the Holy Office.”

“I know it, I know it,” interrupted poor Eustace, trembling now for himself. “All in love—all in love.—A paternal chastisement—”

“And the proofs of heresy are patent, beside the strong suspicion of enchantment, and the known character of the elder sorceress. You yourself—you must remember, señor—told us that she had been a notorious witch in England, before the señora brought her hither as her attendant.”

“Of course she was; of course. Yes; there was no other course open. And though the flesh may be weak, sir, in my case, yet none can have proved better to the Holy Office how willing is the spirit!”

So Eustace departed; and ere another sun had set, he had gone to the principal of the Jesuits, and told him his whole heart—or as much of it, poor wretch, as he dared tell even to himself. He entreated to be allowed to finish his novitiate and enter the order, on the understanding that he should be sent at once back to Europe, or anywhere else.

“Otherwise,” he said frankly, “I shall go mad, even if I am not mad already.”

The Jesuit—kindly enough in manner, as men may be kindly while serving a pitiless machine—went to the Holy Office and settled all with the inquisitors, recounting to them, to set Eustace above suspicion, Eustace’s past valiant services to the Church. His testimony was no longer needed. He left Cartagena for Nombre that very night, and sailed the next week—I know not whither.

I say, I know not whither. Eustace Leigh vanishes henceforth from these pages. He may have ended as General of his Order. He may have worn out his years in some tropic forest “conquering the souls” (including, of course, the bodies) of Indians. He may have gone back to his old work in England, and been the very Ballard who was hanged and quartered three years afterwards for his share in Babington’s villainous conspiracy. I know not.

This book is a history of men—of men’s virtues and sins, victories and defeats; and Eustace is a man no longer. He is become a thing, a tool, a Jesuit: which goes only where it is sent, and does good or evil indifferently as it is bid; which, by an act of moral suicide, has lost its soul in the hope of saving it; without a will, a conscience, a responsibility (as it fancies) to God or man, but only to “The Society.”

In a word, Eustace, as he says himself, is “dead.”

Twice dead, I fear.

Let the dead bury their dead. We have no more concern with Eustace Leigh.
