The moon, just peeking over the crenellated roofline of Florence’s Old Palace barely an hour past midnight, could not paint its usual broad silver swathes across the piazza pavement that night. Not a hand’s breadth of stone was visible anywhere beneath a heaving, surging sea of bobbing heads: nearly every soul who counted themselves part of Florence’s public life was awake, packed tight within the piazza’s borders or fighting to force their way past the armed guards at the entrances. The soft, familiar hush of Florence’s midnight hours—where the patter of late footsteps, the distant twang of lutes, or the scamper of stray cats stood out sharp and clear—was gone, swallowed whole by a roar of mingled shouts, curses, stampeding feet, and the accidental clink of jostled weapons. Only a sudden, darting shriek or the deep, heavy toll of a distant bell cut through the din.

Inside the palace council chamber, all the chief magistrates, the eighty Senate members, and the city’s most prominent select citizens had raged at one another for hours, from bright daylight deep into guttering torchlight, torn over two choices: grant the condemned Medicean conspirators the right of appeal, or execute them immediately before a rescue attempt could unravel all their work. The debate had curdled into open quarrel so loud its echoes filtered out to the crowd outside. It was only in the last hour that the deadlock broke: four of the Signoria had refused to budge on the appeal even when rioters threatened to sack their homes, until Francesco Valori, the man the city called its Florentine Cato, spat out a brief, furious speech. He declared that if the Signoria refused to defend Florence’s liberty by putting the five perfidious traitors to death, other citizens would take the matter into their own hands, no matter the risk to anyone who stood in their way. Valori triumphed. When the votes were counted again, all nine bean tokens were the fatal black, condemning the five prisoners to instant death. The same vote sealed Francesco Valori’s own fate too, quietly and a great deal further down the line.

While the council’s judicial Eight rode for the Bargello to make arrangements for the execution, the five condemned men were led barefoot, their ankles heavy with irons, right through the midst of the still assembled council. Their friends had arranged this deliberate display of public shame, hoping Florentine hearts would soften at the sight: two venerable, respected old men like Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi, whose loyalty to the Medici had long predated Savonarola’s new order, paired with two brilliant, beloved young nobles like Tornabuoni and Pucci, whose absence would leave a hollow at every major gathering of Florence’s elite. It was useless. The pity that stirred now was no soft feeling that would move anyone to rescue, only a cold, hopeless kind that would fester into slow, deliberate revenge later.

Down in the palace courtyard, Romola leaned her weight against one of the massive weathered stone pillars, waiting for the moment her godfather Bernardo would be led out on his way to execution. She had pulled every favor at her disposal to win permission to visit him that evening, to stay at his side until the council’s verdict was announced. Now she waited beside his confessor, ready to follow the guard detail all the way to the Bargello, determined to cling to the childless old man to his very last breath, just as her own father would have wished. She had brushed off every remonstrance that she leave. At her side stood Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a devout follower of Savonarola, who would soon have to watch his own elder brother Niccolò die, and had sworn to keep her safe through all of it.

Tito was also somewhere inside the palace, but Romola had not seen him. They had avoided each other entirely since the night of the seventeenth, and Tito only knew from reports that Savonarola had refused to intervene on the prisoners’ behalf, that her pleas had failed. He mingled easily with the crowd of waiting Florentine officials and foreign envoys, putting on a quiet, somber air, as if the events of the night had torn him apart between public duty and private grief. When someone mentioned Romola in connection to the condemned men, he hinted that the shock of her godfather’s sentence had unbalanced her, that she had turned violently, irrationally hostile to him simply for continuing to serve the state that had condemned Bernardo. For her own sake, he claimed, he thought it wisest to keep his distance.

“Ah, that old hot-tempered Bardi blood,” Cennini shrugged. “I would not be surprised if this whole business yanks her free of the Frate’s following, same as a dozen others I could name.”
“Any woman who is as beautiful as Messer Tito’s wife, of course, may be forgiven for thinking her personal affections should overrule the good of the state,” the young French envoy smiled, bowing politely to Tito. “To her, no one ought to be beheaded who is related to someone she loves. But that is a view we cannot afford to indulge in men. Your Florentine politics are far too weakened by that softness.”
“True enough,” Niccolò Machiavelli nodded, his sharp mouth quirking. “But when personal loyalties run deep, the hostilities they spawn must be accounted for. Half these half-hearted severities are just blundering hot-headedness. The only safe blows to strike at your enemies are blows so heavy no one can rise up to avenge them.”
“Niccolò,” Cennini huffed, “there is a clever, wicked edge to your talk sometimes that makes me stare at your pleasant young face like it is Satan’s own mask.”
“Nonsense, old friend,” Machiavelli laughed, clapping a hand on the older man’s shoulder. “Satan was a blunderer, forever chasing new novelties, and he failed spectacularly. If he had won, we would all be worshipping him, and no one would paint his portrait as anything but noble.”
“Fine, fine,” Cennini grumbled. “I never said your doctrine was too stupid for Satan, only that it is wicked enough for him.”
“My doctrine is no more than what every man follows who sets his sights further than the end of his own nose,” Machiavelli replied. “Ask our Frate, our prophet, how he will bring about his universal renovation of the city. He will tell you first we must build a free and pure government. And since we cannot make all Florentines love one another, we must simply lop off every head that is stupidly obstinate enough to get in the way. The only mistake a man can make is to mete out a severity not thorough enough to be final, that leaves survivors alive to hate him. I suspect the Frate made exactly that mistake tonight. He could have won great glory by throwing his weight behind the appeal, instead he has lost all lustre and gained no new followers.”

Before anyone could reply, the long-awaited announcement came: the prisoners were leaving the council chamber. Most of the assembled crowd hurried for the doors, eager to claim a spot trailing the guard all the way to the Bargello, for the execution drew every kind of onlooker, from those burning with deepest personal grief to those driven only by cold, idle curiosity. Tito was one of the few who stayed behind. He had always had a natural revulsion for the sight of pain and death, and five days earlier, when he had only considered the execution a distant possibility, he had hoped the sentence would be commuted to exile: his own safety demanded no more. But now, the knowledge that Bernardo del Nero’s head would soon be severed from his shoulders felt like a welcome guarantee of security. Ever since Romola had returned and revealed she knew the truth of his past, he had lived in terror of her power to ruin his reputation. If she won Bernardo over to her side, no one in Florence would doubt her word about Baldassarre. But with Bernardo dead, Romola’s proud, shrinking nature would never let her drag herself into public scandal to oppose her husband. Tito felt a quiet weight lift from his chest when he learned the judicial Eight had already ordered the scaffold erected immediately. Four of his own close associates were to die alongside Bernardo, but he told himself a man’s own safety is a god that sometimes makes very grim demands. As he heard the shuffle of feet and clink of irons echo down the stairs, he answered the young French envoy’s casual questions without showing anything but a sad, resigned acceptance of harsh state necessities.

For Romola, every sound struck her with a sharp, almost supernatural clarity. She did not need anyone to hold her up, she shed no tears. She had reached that peak of feeling that transcends both grief and joy, where the mind feels kin to the old, primal forces that shaped the world before pleasure or pain were ever born. Since the verdict had been handed down, all her earlier struggle had faded: she walked in spirit beside Bernardo, asserting for him that whatever the state called treason, he was no traitor, only a man torn apart between two different kinds of faithfulness. He had not died for the noblest of causes, but he died because he was too noble to compromise his loyalties.

The procession to the Bargello was hemmed in on all sides by three hundred armed men posted by Francesco Valori, a strange, contradictory precaution that the authorities claimed was both to hold back the mob baying for the prisoners’ blood and to crush any last-minute attempt to rescue them. When they reached the Bargello courtyard, Romola was allowed a single moment of farewell with Bernardo and his confessor. Every eye turned to the pair, even in the heaving throng: the old man, forgetting his bound wrists, lifted his hands to touch her golden bowed head, then checked himself to lean forward and kiss her forehead. Romola seized his fettered, hanging hands and kissed them like they were sacred relics.

“My poor Romola,” Bernardo whispered, low enough only for her to hear, “I only have to die. You have to live. And I will not be there to help you.”
“Yes,” Romola said hurriedly, “you will help me, always. I will never forget you.”

She was led away then, up the steps to the loggia ringing the Bargello’s grand old courtyard, and took her place there, determined to watch until the moment her godfather laid his head on the block. As the prisoners were given a few last minutes alone with their confessors, spectators packed tight into the courtyard, pressing all around the black draped scaffold. Torches wedged in iron rings on the pillars flickered, casting strobing light first on cold, passionless stone carvings, then on a pale face twisted with suppressed rage or silent grief—the face of one of the dozens of close relatives of the condemned, waiting to carry their dead home. Romola’s face looked like carved marble against the dark archway as she stood, staring for the first glimpse of Bernardo at the foot of the scaffold. He was to be executed first. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, standing at her side, had promised to lead her out through a hidden rear door the second she had seen the last of the man who had been the only other person in the world to share her gentle love for her dead father. Even now, a faint, stubborn hope flickered far in the back of her mind that some last-minute reprieve would still come, that the scaffold would never be stained with blood.

For a long while, the courtyard seethed with movement: lights flickered, heads swayed, confused voices rose and fell, waves of distant roar crashing in from the streets outside. Romola felt as if she was bobbing in the middle of a storm-tossed sea, caring nothing for the storm, only determined to hold her signal high until the man she loved could see it for the last time.

Suddenly, silence fell. Even the flickering tapers seemed to tremble into stillness. The executioner stepped onto the scaffold, and Bernardo del Nero was seen climbing the steps, slow and unwavering, his white head held high. Romola did not move, did not make a sound. She stood firmer, holding fast to his courage as if it was her own. She saw him pause, glance slowly out over the crowd, and speak in a clear, ringing voice that carried to every corner of the court: “It is but a short space of life that my fellow-citizens have taken from me.” She felt his eyes land on her, saw herself stretching her arms out towards him, and then everything went dark.

When she came to, a long while later, she found Bernardo’s confessor standing beside her in a quiet room, where other grave, robed men talked in low, subdued tones. “My daughter,” he said gently, “all is peace now. I can conduct you home.”

Romola started to her feet at once. “I am ready,” she said. “Let us lose no time.” Every last old tie that had bound her to the life she had lived, to the husband she now loathed, was snapped. All her strength now bent to escaping the grasp that made her shudder.
