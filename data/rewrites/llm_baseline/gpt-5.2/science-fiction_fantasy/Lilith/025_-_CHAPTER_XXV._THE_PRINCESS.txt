I went the long way round the castle, letting my steps complete the circuit as if my feet needed to learn the place by repetition. The air in the outer passages had a hollow hush, the kind that holds your own breathing up to you like a cup: every bootfall sounded too loud, then died as though swallowed by stone. At last I came again to the open gates and crossed the moat—no water in it now, only a chasm of shadow, like a drained channel that remembered rivers. Beyond lay a paved court, its stones set close as a well-laid hearth, and planted at even intervals with tall, straight trees, poplar-like in their patience. One in the centre rose above the rest; near its crown its branches spread, not much, but enough to make it seem a palm trying to remember a warmer country.

Between the trunks I caught glimpses of the palace itself—long and low, with lofty corner towers and a huge dome in the middle that swelled up from the roof to half their height. Something in its curves and weight of line suggested an older southern craft, a style that belonged to heat and spice and slow evenings. The main entrance gaped in the centre: a low arch, half an ellipse, like a mouth that had forgotten how to close.

No sentries. No voices. No clink of metal, no domestic clatter, no kitchen-rattle of pans, none of the friendly chaos that tells you a house is inhabited by hunger and its satisfactions. The doors stood wide, and I went in as though expected.

The first hall was a long ellipse, dim and cool. Toward one side stood a cage. In it lay a leopardess—huge, white, patterned with dark oval spots as if someone had pressed ripe olives into snow. She was chained by a steel collar; her mouth was muzzled, her paws muffled, and yet she looked less restrained than the whole palace. Her green eyes were wide open, the pupils narrow as canoes; her gaze fixed, unblinking. She watched me, I think, but not a whisker moved, not an eyeball shifted, not the tip of her tail stirred. That tail lay stretched behind her, rigid as an iron bar. For a breath I could not tell whether she was alive or carved and set there as ornament—a monstrous trophy of elegance.

Two low passages led away. I took one. It branched into many, narrow and irregular, turning like a warren, the air changing by degrees: here faintly sour as of damp cloth, there dusty as old rushes, then suddenly a thread of warm scent that vanished before I could name it.

At a place where the walls pressed close, a page ran into me. He sprang back as if he had struck a ghost, then looked me over, found courage in my unarmed state, puffed himself out, and demanded my business.

“To see the princess,” I said.

“A likely thing!” he scoffed. “I have not seen her highness this morning myself!”

I caught him by the back of the neck, shook him—hard enough to rattle the impudence out of his bones—and said, “Take me to her at once, or I will drag you with me until I find her. She shall know how her servants receive her visitors.”

He darted a glance at my face, measuring whether I meant it. Then he began to pull and lead as a blind man’s dog might—resentful, yet obedient to necessity.

He brought me to a large kitchen.

It ought to have been the beating heart of the palace, full of knife-song and fire-breath, full of talk and the plain music of work. Instead it lay under a kind of languor. Many servants moved about feebly, as if each had been roused too early from a heavy sleep. The room held a faint warmth, but no true cheer. I smelled stale ash and lukewarm bread, a milkiness that had stood too long, and something else—something clean and distant, like crushed leaves carried on a draft from an unseen garden.

I expected them to shout, to seize me, to drive me out. They did none of it. They stared—wide-eyed—not at me but at something behind me. Their faces blanched in the same direction, as if a cold wind had slid into the room.

I turned my head.

The white leopardess stood there in the doorway, and in that instant I knew she had been no statue. She regarded the kitchen as if it were prey and she had leisure. Even muzzled, even chained in my memory, she carried the authority of claw and sinew. The servants’ fear thickened the air more than the heat ever could.

One of them, perhaps because he could not bear the stillness, began to recover himself. I let the page go.

“Take me to the princess,” I said to the man.

“She has not yet left her room, your lordship,” he answered, with the flat caution of one who speaks under threat, not from me but from what stood behind.

“Let her know that I am here, waiting audience of her.”

He hesitated. “Will your lordship please to give me your name?”

“Tell her,” I said, “that one who knows the white leech desires to see her.”

He shrank as if I had offered him poison. “She will kill me if I take such a message. I must not. I dare not.”

“You refuse?” I asked.

His eyes flicked again toward my attendant—the white terror with green eyes—then he turned and went without another word.

The others kept staring, too frightened to look away from the leopardess, as though any glance aside might invite her to spring. I went to her. She stood with her muzzle close to my heel, the whiteness of her coat carrying a warm splendour into that gloomy room like a bowl of milk set down among ashes. I stooped and patted her head.

She looked up at me. The simple movement scattered the servants as if a broom had swept them; they fled to corners, behind tables, through side doors, wherever their fear found shelter.

The leopardess rose on her hind legs and put her great paws on my shoulders. The muffling made the touch oddly gentle—like hands in thick gloves—yet the weight of her was enormous. I threw my arms around her neck, and for a moment I felt the heat of her body through her fur and the leather of the muzzle; the smell of her was like sun-warmed hide and clean dust, with a faint wild tang that made the mouth water and the skin tighten at once.

Her ears pricked. She broke from me and was out of sight in a moment, moving with that noiseless speed that belongs to creatures who do not waste sound.

The man I had sent returned, pale but compliant. “Please to come this way, my lord,” he said.

My heart throbbed once, as if it had braced itself against a blow.

He led me through passage after passage. The palace was built like a secret—corners within corners, turns that made you doubt you could ever find your way back. At last he drew aside a hanging and showed me into a room so large and so dark that its walls were invisible. The darkness had weight; it lay on the skin like cool silk.

A single spot on the floor reflected a little light. All around that spot the blackness was complete. I looked up and saw, far overhead, an oval aperture in the roof. Around its edge I could just make out the joints between blocks of black marble. The floor beneath my feet was slabbed in the same stone, fitted so close the seams were scarcely visible. Later I would understand that the walls too were black marble, drinking in what little light fell, as if the room fed on brightness and gave back nothing.

The roof was the long half of an ellipsoid. The opening sat over one focus of the floor’s ellipse, so that the little light gathered and fell in that lone patch as if the room were a lens for darkness.

I thought I caught sight of reddish lines—brief, like a memory of embers in a hearth. When I tried to look at them, they were gone.

Then, without warning, a radiant form stood in the centre of the dark.

Her robe was soft white. Her hair poured down like a cataract, black as the marble on which it fell. Her eyes were a luminous blackness—dark that shone. Her arms and feet were warm ivory. She smiled the innocent smile of a girl, yet her bearing had the poised assurance of one who had already commanded rooms far stranger than this. In face and figure and motion she seemed as if she had only just crossed the threshold of womanhood.

And I thought, with a sick turn inside me, Ill did I reckon my danger. Can this be the woman I rescued—she who struck me, scorned me, left me?

I stood, half swallowed by the darkness. She stood in her own light, gazing into the black as if searching for me. Then she vanished, as though the dark had shut over her.

She will not acknowledge me, I thought.

The next instant her eyes flashed out of the gloom straight into mine. She had found me and come to me—silent, swift, as if the darkness were her element.

“You have found me at last!” she said, and laid her hand upon my shoulder. The touch was light, but it carried heat through cloth and skin. “I knew you would!”

My body quivered with sensations I could not separate. Attraction and repulsion ran together until each seemed to be the other’s mask.

“You shiver,” she said. “This place is cold for you. Come.”

I could not speak. Her beauty had struck me dumb; her sweetness held me there as if I were a boy again, caught between reverence and anger.

She took my hand and drew me into the spot of light. There she flashed upon me anew.

An instant she studied my face. “You have grown brown since last I saw you,” she said, as if remarking on a loaf darkened in the oven.

“This is almost the first roof I have been under since you left me,” I answered.

“Whose was the other?” she asked quickly.

“I do not know the woman’s name.”

“I would gladly learn it,” she said, with a faint curl in her voice. “The instinct of hospitality is not strong in my people.”

Again she took my hand and led me through many steps of darkness to a curtain of black. Beyond it lay a white stair, its stone pale as flour. She brought me up, and at the top opened into a beautiful chamber.

“How you must miss the hot flowing river!” she said.

Her words brought back the smell of that stream—mineral and steamy, the air above it heavy as boiled roots. I did not answer.

“But there is a bath in the corner,” she went on, “with no white leeches in it. At the foot of your couch you will find a garment. When you come down, I shall be in the room to your left at the foot of the stair.”

She left me, and I stood for a moment accusing myself of presumption. How was I to treat this lovely woman as a thing of evil when she behaved to me like a sister—almost like a hostess who had been waiting with water and bread?

Whence the marvellous change in her? She left me with a blow; she received me with something like an embrace. She had reviled me; now she spoke as if my pursuit were not only expected but desired. Did she know my doubts—how much I needed explained? Could she explain it? And if she could, could I believe her?

Hospitality, at least, I had earned. I might accept it until I came to a judgment that would not shift like smoke.

Clear as crystal, the water in the great white bath sent a sparkling flash from its corner where it lay sunk in marble. The sight of it pierced me with longing. Except for the hot stream, two draughts in the cottage of the veiled woman, and pools along the track of the wounded leopardess, I had not seen water since leaving home. Here it looked celestial—like something preserved from a better world.

I plunged in.

At once my brain filled with an odour strange and delicate—herbal, floral, and faintly bitter, like bruised petals steeped too long. It was beautiful and yet it displeased me, as if it were meant to. My suspicion sprang up again: had she medicated it? enchanted it? was she working upon me unlawfully?

And how was there water in the palace when the city held not a drop? The question bit harder than the scent.

I remembered the crushed paw of the leopardess. I saw again the limping beast, heard again the howl that seemed to tear the air. I sprang from the bath, water streaming from me cold and bright.

What had I been bathing in?

For a moment I argued with myself as a thirsty man argues with scruple. What matter whence it flowed, if it was sweet? Water comes from heaven, does it not? What mattered the well where it gathered, the channel it had borrowed, the vessel that held it? Even the pitcher-plant secretes water and stores it in its heart for the weary traveller.

Yet I did not re-enter. I could not.

At the foot of the couch lay a robe of white wool, embroidered at neck and hem. It smelled faintly of sheep and smoke and some clean resin, as if it had been dried where pinewood burned. I put it on, and the warmth of it steadied me.

Then I went down the white stair to the room she had named.

It was round, all alabaster, without a single window. Light came through everywhere—not as beams, but as a soft pearly shimmer, as if the walls themselves had learned to glow. Over the low dome and along the walls drifted vague shadowy forms, like loose rain-clouds crossing a grey-blue sky.

The princess stood waiting.

She wore a robe embroidered with rings and discs, rectangles and lozenges, close together—argent shapes like a silver mail. It fell unbroken from her neck and hid her feet, but its long open sleeves left her arms bare, pale and alive against the metallic pattern.

An ivory table stood in the room. Upon it were cakes and fruit, an ivory jug of milk, a crystal jug of wine pale as rose-water, and a white loaf.

The arrangement struck me with the odd comfort of the domestic: food set out, drink ready, the old ritual of invitation. Yet even here the palace’s silence pressed in. There was no hearth-crackle, no kettle-song, no distant chopping. The quiet was so complete I could hear the faintest shift of cloth when she moved, and my own swallowing had the intimacy of a confession.

“Here we do not kill to eat,” she said. “But I think you will like what I can give you.”

I looked at the bread—white, fine-crumbed, the crust scarcely browned, as if baked in a gentle oven. The cakes had a sheen like honey set; the fruit gave off clean perfumes: citrus oil, bruised peach, something dark and winey like figs. The milk smelled sweet and animal, not long kept. For all my distrust, hunger rose in me with its plain truth.

“I could desire nothing better,” I said, and meant it.

She seated herself on a couch by the table and signed to me to sit beside her. She poured milk into a bowl and handed it to me, then offered the loaf.

“Break from it such a piece as you like,” she said.

The bread tore with a soft sigh. The crumb was tender; it tasted of grain and quiet warmth, and the milk cooled my mouth like a clean cloud.

Then she filled two silver goblets from the crystal jug. The workmanship was grotesquely graceful, as if vines and beasts had been twisted together into handles.

“You have never drunk wine like this,” she said.

I drank.

It was like flowers made liquid without becoming perfume—every bloom that bees had ever visited distilled into a single draught. It tasted of sun and slow hills, of thyme and wild honey, of something faintly saline that made the sweetness deeper. It did not strike like common wine; it unfolded, and in unfolding it made the air around my head feel thicker with scent, as though my own breath carried gardens.

“And now that you will be able to listen,” she said, “I must do what I can to make myself intelligible to you. Our natures are so different that this may not be easy.”

She spoke on, telling me that men and women live but to die, while she—she and a few like her—live to live on. Old age, she said, was horror to us, desire to her; that their perfection was a ceaseless ripening, while ours came soon and passed. She had lived thousands of my years, she said, and had not troubled to count them. The everlasting, she told me, will not be measured.

Her voice flowed like the wine—sweet, persuasive, meant to be taken in without chewing. Yet beneath it I heard something else: a way of choosing words that placed her above me, a lexicon of dominance wrapped in velvet. When she said “progress,” it was as if she had bought the word and stamped it with her seal. When she spoke of “perfection,” the meaning was hers alone.

“Many lovers have sought me,” she continued. “I have loved none of them. They sought but to enslave me; they sought me as the men of my city seek gems of price.”

Then she turned the story toward me.

“When you found me,” she said, “I found a man. I put you to the test; you stood it; your love was genuine. It was, however, far from ideal—far from such love as I would have.”

Her eyes held mine, dark and bright at once.

“You loved me truly,” she went on, “but not with true love. Pity has, but is not love. What woman of any world would return love for pity? Such love as yours was then is hateful to me.”

I felt heat rise in my face. The bread in my stomach seemed suddenly heavy, as if it had been kneaded with accusation.

“I knew,” she said, “that if you saw me as I am, you would love me—like the rest of them—to have and to hold. I would none of that. I would be otherwise loved. I would have a love that outlived hopelessness, outmeasured indifference, hate, scorn. Therefore did I put on cruelty, despite, ingratitude.

“When I left you, I had shown myself such as you could at least no longer follow from pity: I was no longer in need of you. But you must satisfy my desire or set me free—prove yourself priceless or worthless. To satisfy the hunger of my love, you must follow me, looking for nothing—not gratitude, not even pity in return—follow and find me, and be content with merest presence, with scantest forbearance.

“I, not you, have failed,” she said, and her voice softened. “I yield the contest.”

She looked at me tenderly and hid her face in her hands.

But I had caught a flash behind the tenderness—a sparkle like a blade briefly shown then put away. I did not believe her. It seemed to me she laid herself out to secure and enslave me; she fascinated rather than confessed.

“Beautiful princess,” I said, forcing steadiness into my voice, “let me understand how you came to be found in such evil plight.”

“There are things I cannot explain,” she replied, lowering her hands, “until you have become capable of understanding them—which can only be when love is grown perfect. There are many things so hidden from you that you cannot even wish to know them. But any question you can put, I can in some measure answer.”

She spoke then of her journey.

“I had set out to visit a part of my dominions occupied by a savage dwarf-people—strong and fierce, enemies to law and order, opposed to every kind of progress—an evil race.” The words came out as if they had been taught to her by a court. I heard again that ownership of language: “law,” “order,” “progress,” each a banner she waved to make conquest seem like virtue.

“I went alone,” she said, “fearing nothing, unaware of the least necessity for precaution. I did not know that upon the hot stream beside which you found me, a certain woman—not so powerful as myself, not being immortal—had cast what you call a spell.”

“Spell,” she said, and her mouth shaped the mortal word with a faint disdain, as if it were a crude utensil.

“It is merely the setting in motion of a force as natural as any other,” she continued, “but operating primarily in a region beyond the ken of the mortal who makes use of the force.

“I set out on my journey, reached the stream, bounded across it—”

A shadow of embarrassment rose in her cheek—quick as blush on fruit. I understood why and gave no sign.

“—you know what a step it is in parts,” she added, and hurried on. “But in the very act an indescribable cold invaded me. I recognised at once the nature of the assault and knew it could affect me but temporarily. By sheer force of will I dragged myself to the wood—nor knew anything more until I saw you asleep, and the horrible worm at your neck.

“I crept out, dragged the monster from you, and laid my lips to the wound. You began to wake; I buried myself among the leaves.”

As she spoke of laying her lips to my wound, the room’s scent seemed to thicken—milk, bread, wine, and that delicate bitterness I had smelled in the bath, all braided into one. I could not tell whether it came from the food, from her, or from my own suspicion.

She rose suddenly. Her eyes flashed as never human eyes flashed, and she threw her arms high over her head, the silver patterns on her robe catching the alabaster light and scattering it like scales.

“What you have made me is yours!” she cried. “I will repay you as never yet did woman. My power, my beauty, my love are your own: take them.”

She dropped kneeling beside me, laid her arms across my knees, and looked up into my face.

Then I saw her left hand.

On it was a large clumsy glove. Instantly my mind filled it with hair and claws. Yet I knew it was a hand shut hard—perhaps badly bruised, perhaps worse. I glanced at her other hand: it was lovely as a hand could be, long-fingered, capable of gentleness and command. I felt that if I did less than loathe her, I should love her.

Not to dally with emotions that would usurp my judgment, I turned my eyes aside.

She started to her feet.

I sat motionless, looking down.

To me she may be true, whispered vanity. For a moment I was tempted to love a lie because it offered itself warmed and perfumed, like bread handed out in a famine.

An odour—more than a breath, less than a wind—fanned my face. I glanced up.

She stood erect before me, waving her lovely arms in a fashion that looked mystic, as if she were shaping the air with her wrists.

A frightful roar tore through the palace.

My heart rebounded against my ribs as if trying to escape. The alabaster trembled; the very light seemed to quiver. The roar was not merely loud—it had the weight of a living thing, the kind of sound that belongs to muscle and rage, not to stone and echo.

The princess shuddered visibly.

“My wine was too strong for you,” she said, and her voice quavered. “I ought not to have let you take a full draught. Go and sleep now, and when you wake ask me what you please. I will go with you. Come.”

She led the way up the white stair. Her tone tried for ease, but the palace’s silence had been wounded; every step we took rang sharper in the renewed hush.

“I do not wonder that roar startled you,” she said as we climbed. “It startled me, I confess: for a moment I feared she had escaped. But that is impossible.”

Yet to me the roar seemed—how I could not tell—like the voice of the white leopardess. And it seemed meant for me, not for the princess: a warning sounded in an animal language older than courts and their polished words.

At my door she turned and smiled, and the smile was perfect as the crust of a cake glazed to deceive the eye into hunger. But as she turned away, I read anxiety on her beautiful face, as plain as salt on the tongue.
