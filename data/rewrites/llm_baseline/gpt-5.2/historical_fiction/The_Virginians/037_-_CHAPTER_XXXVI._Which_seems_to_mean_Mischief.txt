Though she had plainly been worsted in the skirmish of the previous day, the Baroness Bernstein met her niece as if no wound had been given and none received. Her voice came smooth as warmed wine, and her smile lay upon her face like a well-placed mask.

“Of course, my Lady Maria,” she said, “you can’t suppose that I, as Harry Warrington’s near relative, can be pleased at the thought of his marrying a woman as old as his mother, and with not a penny to her fortune. But if he chooses to do so silly a thing, the affair is none of mine; and I doubt whether I should have been much inclined to be taken au sérieux touching that offer of five thousand pounds which I made in the heat of our talk. So it was at Castlewood that this pretty affair was arranged? Had I known how far it had gone, my dear, I should have spared some needless opposition. When a pitcher is broken, what railing can mend it?”

“Madam!” Maria cut in, her colour rising.

“Pardon me—I mean nothing against your ladyship’s honour or character, which, no doubt, are quite safe. Harry says so, and you say so—what more can one ask?”

“You have talked to Mr. Warrington, madam?”

“And he has owned he made you a promise at Castlewood: that you have it in his writing.”

“Certainly I have, madam!” said Lady Maria, drawing herself up as if the paper were stitched into her very stays.

“Ah!” The elder lady did not wince. “And I own, too, that at first I put a wrong construction upon the tenor of your letters to him. They implicate other members of the family—”

“Who have spoken most wickedly of me, and endeavoured to prejudice me in every way in my dear Mr. Warrington’s eyes. Yes, madam, I own I have written against them, to justify myself.”

“But, of course, are pained to think that any wretch should get possession of stories to the disadvantage of our family, and make them public scandal. Hence your disquiet just now.”

“Exactly so,” Lady Maria said quickly. “From Mr. Warrington I could have nothing concealed henceforth, and spoke freely to him. But that is a very different thing from wishing all the world to know the disputes of a noble family.”

“Upon my word, Maria, I admire you, and have done you injustice. These—these twenty years, let us say.”

“I am very glad, madam, that you end by doing me justice at all,” said the niece, and her curtsey was the smallest a woman ever gave.

“When I saw you last night, opening the ball with my nephew, can you guess what I thought of, my dear?”

“I really have no idea what the Baroness de Bernstein thought of,” said Lady Maria, icily.

“I remembered that you performed to that very tune with the dancing-master at Kensington, my dear!”

“Madam, it was an infamous calumny.”

“By which the poor dancing-master got a cudgelling for nothing!”

“It is cruel and unkind, madam, to recall that calumny—and I shall beg to decline living any longer with any one who utters it,” Maria returned, with a spirit that made the words ring like a snapped fan.

“You wish to go home? I can fancy you won’t like Tunbridge. It will be very hot for you if those letters are found.”

“There was not a word against you in them, madam: about that I can make your mind easy.”

“So Harry said, and did your ladyship justice. Well, my dear, we are tired of one another, and shall be better apart for a while.”

“That is precisely my own opinion,” said Lady Maria again, dropping a deeper curtsey, as if she sealed the bargain with it.

“Mr. Sampson can escort you to Castlewood. You and your maid can take a postchaise.”

“We can take a postchaise, and Mr. Sampson can escort me,” echoed the younger lady. “You see, madam, I act like a dutiful niece.”

“Do you know, my dear, I have a notion that Sampson has got the letters?” said the Baroness, with a frankness that was its own sort of cruelty.

“I confess that such a notion has passed through my own mind,” Lady Maria answered; and her gaze did not flicker, only tightened, like thread drawn through cloth.

“And you want to go home in the chaise, and coax the letters from him! Delilah! Well, they can be no good to me, and I trust you may get them. When will you go? The sooner the better, you say? We are women of the world, Maria. We only call names when we are in a passion. We don’t want each other’s company; and we part on good terms. Shall we go to my Lady Yarmouth’s? ’Tis her night. There is nothing like a change of scene after one of those little nervous attacks you have had, and cards drive away unpleasant thoughts better than any doctor.”

Lady Maria, who could accept an insult as she accepted a compliment—without yielding an inch—agreed to go.

She was dressed and ready first, waiting in the drawing-room. When Madame Bernstein came down, she remarked, as if by accident, that Maria’s door was left open.

“She has the letters upon her,” thought the old lady; and the thought sat in her mind as steadily as a stone in a pocket.

They went off to their entertainment in their respective chairs, and the two of them, who had wounded each other so neatly, displayed that charming cordiality and respect which women can produce after, and even during, the bitterest quarrels. The city’s night-noise took them up—the hollow drum of sedan-poles, the clop and scrape of horses, the far-off bawl of a watchman, the near chatter at every lit doorway. Even the air seemed to have a sound, being thick with the rustle of silk and the click of fans, as if the town itself played at cards with them, shuffling and dealing.

Upon their return from the Countess’s drum, Mrs. Brett, Madame Bernstein’s maid, presented herself at Lady Maria’s call, when that lady rang her hand-bell on retiring. Betty, Mrs. Brett confessed with shame, was not in a condition to come before my lady. Betty had been a-junketing and merry-making with Mr. Warrington’s black gentleman, with my Lord Bamborough’s valet, and several more ladies and gentlemen of that station; and the liquor—Mrs. Brett was shocked to own it—had proved too much for Mrs. Betty.

Should Mrs. Brett undress my lady?

My lady said she would undress without a maid, and gave Mrs. Brett leave to withdraw.

“She has the letters in her stays,” thought Madame Bernstein.

They had bid each other an amicable good-night on the stairs.

Mrs. Betty had her scolding the next morning, when she came to wait on her mistress from the closet adjoining Lady Maria’s apartment in which Betty lay. She owned, with contrition, her partiality for rum-punch, which Mr. Gumbo had the knack of brewing most delicate. It was not mere spirit flung together with coarse haste, but a draught contrived with a cook’s patient cunning: the clean bite of lime, the burnt-sugar sweetness melted down to a dark edge, spice that rose after the first swallow and warmed the throat like a well-laid coal. Even now, speaking penitently, the girl’s mouth betrayed the memory of it, as if she still tasted the citrus on her tongue.

She took her scolding with meekness; and, having performed her usual duties about her lady’s person, retired.

Now Betty was one of the Castlewood girls who had been so fascinated by Gumbo, and was a very good-looking, blue-eyed lass, upon whom Mr. Case, Madame Bernstein’s confidential man, had also cast the eyes of affection. Hence, between Messrs. Gumbo and Case, there had been jealousies and even quarrels; which had caused Gumbo, who was of a peaceful disposition, to be rather shy of the Baroness’s gentlemen, the chief of whom vowed he would break the bones, or have the life of Gumbo, if he persisted in his attentions to Mrs. Betty.

But on the night of the rum-punch, though Mr. Case found Gumbo and Mrs. Betty whispering in the doorway in the cool breeze, and Gumbo would have turned pale with fear had he been able so to do, no one could be more gracious than Mr. Case. It was he who proposed the bowl, which was brewed and drunk in Mrs. Betty’s room, and which Gumbo concocted with exquisite skill. He complimented Gumbo on his music. Though a sober man ordinarily, he insisted upon more and more drinking, until poor Mrs. Betty was reduced to the state which occasioned her ladyship’s just censure.

As for Mr. Case himself—who lay out of the house—he was so ill with the punch that he kept his bed the whole of the next day, and did not get strength to make his appearance and wait on his ladies until supper-time; when his mistress good-naturedly rebuked him, saying it was not often he sinned in that way.

“Why, Case, I could have made oath it was you I saw on horseback this morning galloping on the London road,” said Mr. Warrington, who was supping with his relatives.

“Me! law bless you, sir! I was a-bed, and I thought my head would come off with the aching. I ate a bit at six o’clock, and drunk a deal of small beer, and I am almost my own man again now. But that Gumbo, saving your honour’s presence, I won’t taste none of his punch again.”

And the honest major-domo went on among the bottles and glasses, drawing corks with the neat snap of long practice, rinsing a glass to silence the last ghost of claret, setting decanters in their places as a gardener sets pots: domestic order against the day’s confusions.

As they sat after their meal, Madame Bernstein was friendly enough. She prescribed strong fortifying drinks for Maria against the recurrence of her fainting fits. The lady had such attacks not unfrequently; and the Baroness spoke of them with that worldly tenderness which is half care and half command.

She urged Maria to consult her London physician, and to send up an account of her case by Harry.

“By Harry?” asked the lady, and the question sounded too sharp for so smooth a room.

“Yes. Harry is going for two days on an errand for his aunt to London. I do not care to tell you, my dear, that it is on business which will do him good. I wish Mr. Draper to put him into my will, and as I am going travelling upon a round of visits when you and I part, I think, for security, I shall ask Mr. Warrington to take my trinket-box in his postchaise to London with him, for there have been robberies of late, and I have no fancy for being stopped by highwaymen.”

Maria looked blank at the notion of the young gentleman’s departure, but hoped she might have his escort back to Castlewood, whither her elder brother had now returned.

“Nay,” said his aunt, “the lad hath been tied to our apron-strings long enough. A day in London will do him no harm. He can perform my errand for me, and be back with you by Saturday.”

“I would offer to accompany Mr. Warrington, but I preach on Friday before her ladyship,” said Mr. Sampson; for he was anxious that my Lady Yarmouth should judge of his powers, and Madame Bernstein had exerted her influence with the king’s favourite to induce her to hear the chaplain.

Harry relished the notion of a rattling journey to London and a day or two of sport there. He promised his pistols were good, and that he would hand the diamonds over in safety to the banker’s strong-room. Would he occupy his aunt’s London house? No; that would be a dreary lodging with only a housemaid and groom in charge. He would go to the Star and Garter in Pall Mall, or to an inn in Covent Garden.

“Ah! I have often talked over that journey,” said Harry, and his face, which could brighten so boyishly, now clouded in a moment.

“And with whom, sir?” asked Lady Maria, who had an ear for every name that might be spoken.

“With one who promised to make it with me,” said the young man, thinking—as he always did—with extreme tenderness of the lost brother.

“He has more heart, my good Maria, than some of us!” said Harry’s aunt, witnessing his emotion.

Uncontrollable gusts of grief would still, not unfrequently, pass over the young man: the parting from his brother; the scene and circumstances of George’s fall last year; a remembered word, or some excursion at home which they had planned together—these would return with a force that made the present fall away like a loosened ribbon.

“I doubt, madam,” whispered the chaplain demurely to Madame Bernstein after one of these bursts, “whether some folks in England would suffer quite so much at the death of their elder brother.”

But sorrow is not a climate in which youth can breathe for ever; and we can fancy Mr. Warrington setting out eagerly enough, and very gay and happy, if it must be owned, to be rid—though he could scarce confess it even to himself—of his elderly attachment.

Yes. There was no help for it. At Castlewood, on one unlucky evening, he had made an offer of his heart and himself to his mature cousin, and she had accepted the foolish lad’s offer. But the marriage now was out of the question. He must consult his mother. She was mistress for life of the Virginian property. Of course she would refuse her consent to such a union. The thought of it was deferred to a late day. Meanwhile it hung like a weight about his neck, and caused him remorse and disquiet; no wonder that his spirits lifted as he came near London, and that he looked with a kind of delighted hunger from his postchaise windows upon the city as he advanced.

No highwayman stopped him on Blackheath. The road’s sounds—wheel-rim on flint, the leather creak of harness, the steady breath of the horses—seemed to thicken as the world grew fuller. Yonder were the gleaming domes of Greenwich, canopied with woods. There was the famous Thames, with its countless shipping; a forest of masts that chimed faintly in the wind like rigging-strung harps. There—there actually was the Tower of London.

“Look, Gumbo! There is the Tower!”

“Yes, master,” said Gumbo, who had never heard of the Tower; but Harry had, and remembered how he had read of it in Howell’s Medulla, and how he and his brother used to play at the Tower; and he thought with delight now of seeing the armour, the jewels, the lions—those old marvels which, told in a boy’s dialect and then heard again in a man’s, are never the same marvel, nor ever quite translatable from one age of the blood into another.

They passed through Southwark and over that famous London Bridge, which had been covered with houses like a street two years ago; now there was only a single gate left, and that too was coming down. Then the chaise rolled through the City; and—

“Look, Gumbo, that is Saint Paul’s!”

“Yes, master; Saint Paul’s,” said Gumbo, obediently, little struck by architecture, more struck by the smell of the town—the coal-smoke, hot tallow, river-mud, roasting meat, brewer’s yeast, and the sharp stink of narrow lanes that seemed never to air.

And so by the well-known course they reached the Temple, and Gumbo and his master looked up with awe at the rebel heads on Temple Bar. The crows made their own commentary on politics; the wind gave the poles a creaking note; and the city’s roar carried on as if it had forgotten, long ago, the individual voices it had swallowed.

The chaise drove to Mr. Draper’s chambers in Middle Temple Lane. Harry handed the precious box over to Mr. Draper, together with a letter from his aunt. The gentleman read it with some interest, carefully put it away, then consigned the trinket-box to his strong closet. He went into the adjoining room, taking his clerk with him, and then returned, at Mr. Warrington’s service to conduct him to an hotel.

An hotel in Covent Garden was fixed upon as best for his residence.

“I shall have to keep you for two or three days, Mr. Warrington,” the lawyer said. “I don’t think the papers which the Baroness wants can be ready until then. Meanwhile I am at your service to see the town. I live out of it myself, and have a little box at Camberwell, where I shall be proud to have the honour of entertaining Mr. Warrington; but a young man, I suppose, will like his inn and his liberty best, sir?”

Harry said yes: the inn would be best. So the postchaise, with a clerk of Mr. Draper’s inside, was despatched to the Bedford, whither the two gentlemen agreed to walk.

They sat and talked for a while first. The Drapers, father and son, had been lawyers time out of mind to the Esmond family; and the attorney told Mr. Warrington stories of the Castlewood ancestors, as if rummaging an old pantry for jars with labels half-faded—this one full of a quarrel preserved in vinegar; that one a feud sweetened with sugar and yet sharp on the tongue. Of the present Earl Mr. Draper was no longer agent: his father and his lordship had had differences, and his lordship’s business had been taken elsewhere; but the Baroness was still their honoured client, and very happy indeed was Mr. Draper to think her ladyship so well disposed towards her nephew.

As they were taking their hats to go, a young clerk of the house stopped his principal in the passage.

“If you please, sir, them papers of the Baroness was given to her ladyship’s man, Mr. Case, two days ago.”

“Just please to mind your own business, Mr. Brown,” said the lawyer sharply. “This way, Mr. Warrington. Our Temple stairs are rather dark. Allow me.”

Harry saw Mr. Draper throw a Parthian look of anger at Mr. Brown.

“So it was Case I saw on the London road two days ago,” Harry thought. “What business brought the old fox to London?”

But he was not curious to pry into other folk’s affairs, and dismissed it from his mind.

Whither first? Harry was for seeing the place where his grandfather and Lord Castlewood had fought their duel fifty-six years ago in Leicester Field. Mr. Draper knew the spot and the story; they might take Covent Garden on their way and see that Mr. Warrington was comfortably lodged.

“And order dinner,” said Harry; for even in pleasure he had a soldier’s method, and liked to have his provisions made sure.

No; Mr. Draper could not consent. Mr. Warrington must do him the honour to dine with him. In fact he had made so bold as to order a collation from the Cock: fowls trussed and roasted till the skin took a proper colour; cold ham shaved thin and glossy; a raised pie with its crust firm as a box; pickles and anchovies; bread crisp at the edge; and wine that set a warm note in the body as music sets a key. Mr. Warrington could not decline so pressing an invitation.

So they walked away gaily together, passing under that arch where the heads were. Harry took off his hat to them, much to the lawyer’s astonishment.

“They were gentlemen who died for their king, sir. My dear brother George and I always said we would salute ’em when we saw ’em,” Mr. Warrington said.

“You’ll have a mob at your heels if you do, sir,” said the alarmed lawyer.

“Confound the mob, sir,” said Mr. Harry loftily; but the passers-by, intent on their own errands, did not heed him, and he walked up the thronging Strand gazing with delight on all he saw, storing it away—sounds as much as sights: the rattle of carts, the hawkers’ cries, a distant hammering like a great heart beating in timber, the thin violin of a street-fiddler losing his tune beneath the heavier roar. Mr. Draper chattered ceaselessly; Harry heard little of it, not caring to let the lawyer know how much he was moved, nor the public perceive he was a stranger.

Nor was Mr. Draper displeased by the young Virginian’s silent and haughty reserve. A hundred years ago a gentleman was a gentleman, and his attorney his very humble servant.

At the Bedford the chamberlain showed Mr. Warrington his rooms, bowing with delightful obsequiousness; for Gumbo had already trumpeted his master’s greatness, and Mr. Draper’s clerk announced the new-comer as a “high fellar.” Then the rooms surveyed, the two gentlemen went to Leicester Field—Mr. Gumbo strutting behind—looked upon the scene of his grandsire’s wound and poor Lord Castlewood’s tragedy, and returned to the Temple to Mr. Draper’s chambers.

Who was that shabby-looking big man Mr. Warrington bowed to as they went out after dinner for a walk in the gardens? That was Mr. Johnson, an author he had met at Tunbridge Wells.

“Take the advice of a man of the world, sir,” said Mr. Draper, eyeing the shabby man of letters superciliously; “the less you have to do with that kind of person, the better. The business we have into our office about them literary men is not very pleasant, I can tell you.”

“Indeed!” said Mr. Warrington; and he liked his new friend the less as the latter grew more familiar and more ready with contempt, as if contempt were a sauce fit for all dishes.

The theatres were shut. Should they go to Sadler’s Wells? or Marybone Gardens? or Ranelagh? or how?

“Not Ranelagh,” said Mr. Draper, “because there’s none of the nobility in town.”

But seeing in the paper that at Sadler’s Wells, Islington, there would be a most singular diversion on eight hand-bells by Mr. Franklyn, as well as the surprising performances of Signora Catherina, Harry—who had already had his fill of city-bellow and wanted something not made of iron and wheels—wisely determined on Marybone Gardens; where there was a concert of music, a choice of tea, coffee, and all sorts of wines, and the benefit (or burden) of Mr. Draper’s ceaseless conversation. The clarinets and fiddles there seemed, for an hour, to cleanse the ear: a pocket of ordered sound against the grinding din beyond the gates.

Mr. Draper’s obsequiousness only ended at Harry’s bedroom door, where, with haughty grandeur, the young gentleman bade his talkative host good-night.

Next morning Mr. Warrington, in his brocade bedgown, took his breakfast, read the newspaper, and enjoyed his ease in his inn—hot rolls whose crusts broke with a small crackle, butter softening into their steaming crumb, and coffee strong enough to lay a steady hand upon the nerves. He read news from his own country; and when he saw the words, Williamsburg, Virginia, June 7th, his eyes grew dim somehow.

He had just had letters by that packet of June 7th, but his mother did not tell how—

“A great number of the principal gentry of the colony have associated themselves under the command of the Honourable Peyton Randolph, Esquire, to march to the relief of their distressed fellow-subjects, and revenge the cruelties of the French and their barbarous allies. They are in a uniform: viz., a plain blue frock, nanquin or brown waistcoats and breeches, and plain hats. They are armed each with a light firelock, a brace of pistols, and a cutting sword.”

“Ah, why ain’t we there, Gumbo?” cried Harry, and the words tasted bitter in his mouth, as if London’s richness had turned suddenly to ashes.

“Why ain’t we dar?” shouted Gumbo.

“Why am I here, dangling at women’s trains?” continued the Virginian; and the phrase itself—women’s trains—seemed to catch him like a hook, for it was the language of the town, neat and contemptuous, and it did not fit what he felt. What he felt had no clean English for it; it was a home-sound in him, a cadence of field and river, too thick with memory to be spoken in drawing-rooms.

“Think dangling at women’s trains very pleasant, Master Harry!” said the materialistic Gumbo, who was little affected by further home news which his master read—viz., that The Lovely Sally, Virginia ship, had been taken in sight of port by a French privateer.

And now, reading that the finest mare in England, and a pair of very genteel bay geldings, were to be sold at the Bull Inn, the lower end of Hatton Garden, Harry determined to go look at the animals, and asked his way.

He then and there bought the genteel bay geldings, and paid for them with easy generosity; for horseflesh spoke a universal tongue to him—muscle and wind, bright eye, sound leg—more honest than the smoothest phrases of law or love.

He never said what else he did that day, being shy of appearing a stranger; but it is believed he took a coach and went to Westminster Abbey, then to the Tower, then to Mrs. Salmon’s Waxwork, then to Hyde Park and Kensington Palace; and then, having given orders to go to the Royal Exchange, catching a glimpse of Covent Garden on his way, he bade Jehu take him back to his inn, and cut short the coachman’s enumeration of places by flinging the fellow a guinea.

Mr. Draper had called in his absence and said he would come again; but Mr. Warrington, having dined sumptuously by himself, went off nimbly to Marybone Gardens again, in the same noble company.

As he issued forth next day, the bells of St. Paul’s, Covent Garden, were ringing for morning prayers; and their measured peal—so different from the ragged racket of carts and cries—reminded him that friend Sampson was going to preach his sermon.

Harry smiled. He had begun to have a shrewd and just opinion of the value of Mr. Sampson’s sermons.
