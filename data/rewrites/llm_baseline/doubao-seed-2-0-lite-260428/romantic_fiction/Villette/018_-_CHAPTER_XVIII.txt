The first bright, soft-edged days of my stay at the Terrace stretched out in a hush of held breath. Every time Graham drifted to lean against the arm of my sofa, every time his long, pacing steps slowed near the corner of the parlor where I sat at my needlework, every time his brow furrowed into that preoccupied, uncharacteristic gravity, my mind flew straight to Miss Ginevra Fanshawe. I kept my ears pricked, my sympathy stored brim-full like a honey jar set out ready to pour, my patience armed and waiting for the tender, fumbling confession I was sure would soon fall from his lips.

At last, after a small, visible inward struggle I had the grace not to name aloud, he broached the subject gently, almost anonymously.
“Your friend is travelling through her vacation, I hear?”
“Friend, forsooth!” I thought to myself, but I held my tongue. There was no use contradicting him; I would bear the soft impeachment. Still, I could not help teasing a little, and asked which friend he meant.

He dragged a chair over to my work-table, reached out, and seized a full reel of silk thread, which he proceeded to unwind recklessly into a tangled, loose heap across the linen I was mending.
“Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe—left with the Cholmondeleys for a tour of the south of France, did she not?”
“She did.”
“Do the two of you correspond?”
“It will surprise you to hear I never once thought to beg that privilege.”
“You have seen samples of her writing, then?”
“Yes, several letters she sent to her uncle, M. de Bassompierre.”
“They cannot be lacking in wit and naïveté, surely? Her soul is all sparkle, no guile at all.”
“Her letters are plain enough for even the hasty reader to parse,” I told him. The truth was, Ginevra’s epistles to her wealthy kinsman were little more than blunt, unvarnished requests for more pocket money, no subtlety or sentiment about them.
“And her handwriting? It must be pretty, light, so ladylike, I should think.”
It was, and I said as much.
“I verily believe everything she does is done perfectly,” Dr. John said, and when I did not immediately leap to agree, he pressed, “You know her better than most. Can you name a single point in which she falls short?”
“She does a great many things very well,” I allowed, adding under my breath in my own head: Flirtation chief among them.
“When do you suppose she will return to town?” he asked next.

“Pardon me, Dr. John, I must set this straight. You do me too much honor to imagine I hold any intimate friendship with Miss Fanshawe. I have never been the keeper of her secrets or her plans. Her particular friends move in a sphere far above mine—among the Cholmondeleys, for example.”

The fool actually mistook my disclaimer for the pique of a jealous, discarded confidante! “Do not judge her too harshly,” he said, patting my hand lightly. “The glitter of fashion misleads her for now, but soon she will see how hollow those people are, and come back to you all the fonder, all the more trusting. I know the Cholmondeleys—superficial, showy, selfish. Rest assured, at heart Ginevra values you more than a dozen of her fine new friends.”

I only said, “You are very kind.” The urge to deny that I was pining for Ginevra’s attention burned hot on my tongue, but I swallowed it down. I submitted to being seen as the humiliated, cast-aside former favorite of the distinguished Miss Fanshawe—but I will own to you, it was a bitter, heavy submission.

“And you see,” Graham went on, “while I can comfort you, I cannot give myself that same solace. I cannot hope she will ever see how sincere my regard is. That worthless De Hamal, I fear he amuses her. What a wretched, foolish delusion she is caught in!”

That was the last straw. My patience, worn thin as it was by recent illness and weeks of held tongue, snapped all at once, without warning.
“Dr. Bretton,” I broke out sharply, “there is no delusion half so great as your own. On every subject but one you are frank, clear-sighted, good and true. On this one, you are no better than a slave. I swear, where Miss Fanshawe is concerned, you do not deserve respect. And you have none from me.”

I stood up so fast my sewing basket tipped, and I left the room trembling with anger, before he could speak a single word in reply.

That scene played out in the morning. When I had to meet him again that evening by the light of the parlor lamp, I saw at once I had done more harm than I meant. Graham was no common man, no vulgar, thin-skinned creature. His nature was broad, vigorous, shaped on generous lines, but laced through with a fine, almost feminine delicacy of feeling far keener than anyone who had not jostled his sharp, sensitive nerves would ever guess. He was not the sort to parade his hurts, nor was he unkind—if you laid your need before him, his purse was open, if you spoke your grief aloud, he never turned a deaf ear. But he was no mind-reader, no seer of unspoken feeling, and that night he carried the weight of my words quiet in his bones.

He entered the room, his face grave, almost cold even to his mother, but there was no malice, no petty resentment in the set of his handsome features. He was turning my accusation over in his head, wondering if I was right, if his foolish passion really was the root of all the disappointment that had gnawed at his peace for months. I scrambled to pull his chair out for him before the servant could reach it, handed him his tea with shaking, over-careful hands. “Thank you, Lucy,” he said, in that same warm, full, pleasant tone of his that my ear had always loved so well.

I knew there was only one course to take: I had to make amends for my hasty, cruel outburst, or I would never sleep a wink that night. The thought of being at odds with Dr. John, of that quiet rift stretching on between us, was worse than the cold silence of the school, worse than the stagnant stillness of the convent pensionnat. I would never again cross him over Ginevra, I swore to myself—let her fly as high as his fondest fancies could carry her, let her be set as a star among the constellations of his imagination, I would never argue the point again.

I tried again and again to catch his eye across the tea table, but every time our glances met he looked away, quiet, reading his book. I longed to go sit beside him, but feared if I did he would meet me with all the indignation I knew I deserved. When his mother left the room at last, I could bear the regret no longer, and murmured, soft as a breath, “Dr. Bretton.”

He looked up at once, his eyes not cold, not sharp, his mouth no cynical twist. His nature was too mellow, too generous to sour over one angry word.
“Dr. Bretton, forgive my hasty words. Do, please forgive them.”
He smiled, the soft, familiar smile I knew so well. “Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you do not respect me, it must be because I am not respectable. I fear I am a clumsy fool—whatever I do, it seems I cannot make the one I want to please care for me at all.”
“That is not something you can be sure of, and even if it were, it is not a flaw in your character, it is only a flaw in someone else’s perception. Let me unsay every angry word I spoke. In all things, great and small, I respect you deeply. If you think too little of yourself and too much of others? That is no flaw—it is a virtue.”
“Can I think too much of Ginevra?”
“I believe you can. You believe you cannot. Let us agree to differ. Only please say you pardon me. That is all I ask.”
“Do you think I would hold ill will against you for one honest, heated word?” he laughed softly. “I see you are not angry. But there is no need to nurse that heartache—you wounded me a little, yes, but the pain is gone now. I do not only forgive you, Lucy. I am grateful to you. You are the only sincere well-wisher I have, who will not lie to me to spare my feelings.”
“I am your sincere well-wisher,” I told him, and my voice was softer than I meant it to be.

And that was the end of our quarrel. If you find my opinion of Dr. John shifts as this story goes on, I beg you pardon the seeming inconsistency: I write only what I felt in the moment, what I saw of him as it revealed itself to me, day by day.

He proved the fineness of his nature by being kinder to me after that misunderstanding than he had ever been before. The thin, cold, translucent screen of ice that had glazed over all our interactions since we were children, that quiet reserve we had never quite pierced, dissolved in the heat of those angry, honest words. From that day on, he never spoke to me in ceremony, never stood on polite pretense. He knew I would always listen, always care, no matter what he chose to tell me.

Which meant, of course, that I heard a great many things about “Ginevra.” He spoke of her so fondly, so full of praise for her beauty, her sweetness, her supposed innocence, that even I, who knew exactly how vain and money-hungry she was, began to see her through the glow of his devotion, a soft, reflected light settling over even my plain, unvarnished impression of her. I knew he often spoke complete nonsense about her, but I made myself endlessly, unfailingly patient. I had learned my lesson: the pain of hurting him, of grieving him even a little, was worse than any irritation at his foolish fondness. I grew selfish, in a soft, new way: I could not deny myself the quiet pleasure of indulging his mood, of bending to his will, of being the only person he could speak freely to.

He still fretted, still despaired that he would never win her, and it made me stubbornly certain that Ginevra was only coquetting to tease him, that at heart she treasured every word and every look he gave her. One day, to soothe his fretting, I told him flat that I was positive she would accept him in the end.
“Positive? That is easy enough to say—but what grounds do you have for that certainty?” he pressed.
“The very best grounds.”
“Tell me what they are, Lucy. Tell me the full explanation, no evasions.”
“I will. You are a lavish, generous man. If Père Silas ever converted you, you would give alms to all his poor, hang tapers on every altar, and fill the shrine of your favorite saint with offerings fit for a king. Ginevra, Dr. John—”
“Hush,” he said, holding up a hand.
“I will not hush! I will speak plain. Ginevra’s hands have been filled with your gifts more times than I can count. You have tracked down the costliest, rarest flowers for her, thought up delicate, thoughtful presents that any woman would have been charmed by. She owns a whole set of fine jewelry, too, that you paid for—your generosity verged on outright extravagance to buy those for her.”

The bright flush that Ginevra herself had never once bothered to show when accepting his gifts, burned hot across his face. “Nonsense,” he said, snipping violently through a skein of my silk with my sewing scissors. “I offered those things to please myself. I felt she did me a favor just to accept them.”
“She did far more than a favor. She pledged her honor, almost, to return that regard. If she will not pay you back in affection, she ought to hand you back every last penny’s worth of those jewels in gold.”
“You do not understand her,” he protested. “She is far too disinterested to care for my gifts, too simple to even guess their value.”

I laughed out loud. I had heard Ginevra sit for hours, naming the exact price of every trinket she owned, scheming and plotting how to wheedle more money out of her uncle or her admirers—money had been the primary preoccupation of her thoughts for years, young as she was.
“Whenever I laid a small, trivial gift in her lap,” he went on, “she was always so cool, so unmoved. No eagerness to take it, no visible pleasure in looking at it. She would only keep the bouquet out of gentle reluctance to grieve me, or let me fasten a bracelet on her ivory arm without even glancing at the trinket, no matter how pretty it was. The glitter never dazzled her bright eyes. How can I take that as a sign she favors me? If I gave her every penny I owned and she took it, I still would not dare think it brought me a single step closer—she is far above sordid little considerations like that.”

“Dr. John,” I began, “Love is blind—”
And then a sharp, subtle, amused blue glint sped sideways from his eye. It reminded me of his old childhood portrait, of the boy I had known long ago. For a second, I half suspected that all his professed belief in Ginevra’s naivete was at least a little bit of an act. For all his passion for her beauty, maybe he was not nearly as fooled as he pretended, maybe he saw every one of her foibles clear as day, and loved her anyway.
Chance glance, or intentional, real or imagined—it was enough. The conversation ended there.
