About a week after this, the cyclical rhythm of the household had settled into a steady, sensory simmer. The two friends sat working together, though their acoustic and emotional footprints in the small studio could not have been more disparate. Pietro dashed fitfully at his canvas with the volatile energy of a frantic cook over-salting a broth, performing wonders in a few minutes only to abandon the work with a discordant groan; he would then resume in a fury, his brushstrokes slapping the easel in a harsh, staccato rhythm. Through all this jarring noise kept calmly working, calmly smiling, the canny Dutchman. Gerard possessed the meticulous reverence of a traditional agrarian elder sorting heritage seeds, a man who understood that sustenance—both spiritual and physical—demanded patience. He had put his erratic friend in harness.

They were painting playing cards to literally boil the pot. In this modest room, the olfactory landscape of their trade—raw egg yolks whisked to bind pigments, the earthy tang of crushed ochre, and the metallic bite of azurite—mingled seamlessly with the lingering aromas of yesterday’s garlic, wilted greens, and stewed lentils. When the batch was done, the indignant master took up his grand picture to make his daily tour in search of a patron. Gerard, treating the fruits of their labor like precious spices bound for the market, begged him to take the cards as well. Pietro looked at him with the sudden, venomous hiss of a rattlesnake, but eventually embraced Gerard in the effusive Italian fashion. He gathered the cards, first leaving the freshly painted queens and kings to dry in the heavy, baking heat of the Mediterranean sun, a daily mythic occurrence that baked the very stones of the ancient city.

Left alone, the sonic environment softened. Gerard executed a Greek letter or two on parchment, then took up a needle to mend a little rent in his hose. The quiet, rhythmic *snuff* of the thread pulling through wool offered a profound psychological comfort. He hummed a low, forgotten tune in his native Dutch, a dialect inextricably linked to his identity, its soft, guttural vowels a stark contrast to the sharp, dominant lexicons of the Italian merchants. His landlady, smelling faintly of roasted chestnuts, wild fennel, and the sharp bite of woodsmoke, found him thus employed. She inquired ironically whether there were no women in the house to tend to such domestic cycles. 

“When you have done that,” she said, her voice a warm, resonant hum in the quiet room, “come and talk to Teresa, my friend I spoke to thee of, that hath a husband not good for much, which brags his acquaintance with the great.”

Gerard went down to the kitchen, a space heavy with the rich, comforting aromas of simmering pork fat and bruised basil, only to find that Teresa was none other than the Roman matron. 

“Ah, madama,” said he, “is it you? The good dame told me not that. And the little fair-haired boy, is he well? Is he none the worse for his voyage in that strange boat?”

“He is well,” said the matron, her breath catching.

“Why, what are you two talking about?” said the landlady, staring at them both in turn over a bowl of shelled peas. “And why tremble you so, Teresa mia?”

“He saved my child's life,” said Teresa, making a supreme effort to compose the tremors in her voice. 

“What! my lodger? And he never told me a word of that. Art not ashamed to look me in the face?”

“Alas! speak not harshly to him,” said the matron. She then turned to her friend and poured out a glowing description of Gerard's conduct on the water. Her narrative flowed in a rich, impassioned Roman dialect, carrying the deep, untranslatable weight of maternal terror and gratitude—an emotional truth that the sterile, formalized Latin of the city’s elite could never hope to capture. During this, Gerard stood blushing like a girl, scarce recognizing his own performance in the mythic, heroic proportions her native tongue gave it.

“And to think thou shouldst ask me to serve thy lodger, of whom I knew nought but that he had thy good word, oh, Fiammina; and that was enough for me. Dear youth, in serving thee I serve myself.”

Then ensued an eager, conspiratorial discussion between the two women over the bubbling hearth. They spoke of what had been done, and what should be done, to penetrate the thick wall of fees, commissions, and chicanery which stood between the patrons of art and an unknown artist. It was a bitter critique of the city's power dynamics, where the pure, nourishing act of creation was starved by the superficial commercialization of the Eternal City. Teresa smiled sadly at Gerard's agrarian simplicity in leaving specimens of his skill at the doors of the great.

“What!” said she, “without promising the servants a share—without even feeing them, to let the signors see thy merchandise! As well have flung it into Tiber.”

“Well-a-day!” sighed Gerard. “Then how is an artist to find a patron? For artists are poor, not rich. We work for our daily bread.”

“By going to some city nobler and not so greedy as this,” said Teresa. Her face darkened as she uttered an old proverb, its regional syntax a testament to the erosion of cultural trust: “*La corte Romana non vuol' pecora senza lana.*” The Roman court wants no sheep without wool. The words hung in the air, a linguistic artifact of survival in a predatory landscape. She fell into thought, and said she would come again to-morrow. 

The landlady felicitated Gerard as she stirred a thick, fragrant pot of minestrone. “Teresa has got something in her head,” said she.

Teresa was scarce gone when Pietro returned. His heavy boots slammed against the stone stairs, a jarring, violent disruption to the house's acoustic ecology. He looked black as thunder. Gerard exchanged a knowing glance with the landlady, abandoned the soothing warmth of the kitchen, and followed him upstairs.

“What, have they let thee bring home thy masterpiece?”

“As heretofore.”

“More fools they, then.”

“That is not the worse.”

“Why, what is the matter?”

“They have bought the cards,” yelled Pietro, and hammered the air furiously right and left, his voice a ragged saw tearing through the room's quiet. 

“All the better,” said Gerard cheerfully, thinking of the saffron, the fresh marrow, and the sturdy roots those coins would procure.

“They flew at me for them! They were enraptured with them. They tried to conceal their longing for them, but could not. I saw, I feigned, I pillaged; curse the boobies.” 

Pietro flung down a dozen small silver coins on the floor. He jumped on them, dancing with basilisk eyes, his boots grinding the silver against the stone in a chaotic, ear-splitting clatter that mocked the shallow commercialization of their craft. He kicked them assiduously, sending them spinning and flying into the corners of the room. 

Down went Gerard on his knees. He followed the maltreated innocents directly, rescuing them from the dust and transferring them tenderly to his purse. To him, they were not insults, but the profound promise of a hearth-cooked meal, the very fuel of human endurance. 

“Shouldst rather smile at their ignorance, and put it to profit,” said Gerard, the steady food historian cataloging the bitter with the sweet.

“And so I will,” said Pietro, with concentrated indignation. “The brutes! We will paint a pack a day; we will set the whole city gambling and ruining itself, while we live like princes on its vices and stupidity. There was one of the queens, though, I had fain have kept back. 'Twas you limned her, brother. She had lovely red-brown hair and sapphire eyes, and above all, soul.”

“Pietro,” said Gerard softly, his voice dropping to a register of deep, ancestral longing, “I painted that one from my heart.” Into that small, mundane rectangle of paper, Gerard had woven a mythic occurrence—the untranslatable memory of a woman from a distant, cooler land, a secret love preserved against the erosion of time and distance.

The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling as he recognized the quiet power of Gerard's devotion. “You love her so well, yet leave her.”

“Pietro, it is because I love her so dear that I have wandered all this weary road.”

This tender colloquy was abruptly intersected by the landlady crying from below, her voice carrying the scent of wood-ash and roasted meat up the stairwell. “Come down, you are wanted.”

He went down, and there was Teresa again, her face resolute. 

“Come with me, Ser Gerard.”
