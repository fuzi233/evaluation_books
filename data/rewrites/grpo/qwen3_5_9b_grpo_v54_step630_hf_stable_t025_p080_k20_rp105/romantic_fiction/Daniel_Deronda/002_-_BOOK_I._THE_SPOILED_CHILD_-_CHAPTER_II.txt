The initial impact of this letter upon Gwendolen was half-stupefying. The
implicit confidence that her destiny awaited her in luxurious ease, where
any trouble would be elegantly clad and fully provided for, had been
stronger in her own mind than in her mother's, nourished by her youthful
blood and that sense of superior claims which occupied a large portion
of her consciousness. It was nearly as difficult for her to believe
suddenly that her position had shifted to one of poverty and humiliating
dependence as it would have been to accept the chilling realization that
her death was imminent. She stood motionless for several minutes, then
removed her hat and mechanically glanced into the mirror. The coils of
her smooth, light-brown hair remained perfect enough for a ballroom; and
as on other nights, Gwendolen might have lingered before her reflection
for pleasure (surely an allowable indulgence); but now she took no
conscious note of her beauty, staring fixedly ahead as if jarred by a
hateful sound, waiting for its source to reveal itself. By and by, she
threw herself into the corner of the red velvet sofa, picked up the
letter again, and read it twice deliberately, letting it finally fall to
the floor while she rested her clasped hands on her lap, sitting perfectly
still, shedding no tears. Her impulse was to survey and resist the
situation rather than to wail over it. There was no inward exclamation
of "Poor mamma!" Her mother had never seemed to derive much enjoyment
from life, and if Gwendolen felt pity at this moment, she would bestow
it upon herself—for was she not naturally and rightfully the chief
object of her mother's anxiety? But it was anger, it was resistance that
possessed her; it was bitter vexation that she had lost her gains at
roulette, whereas if her luck had continued through that single day, she
would have carried a handsome sum home, or might have played on until
she won enough to support them all. Even now, was it not possible? She
had only four napoleons remaining in her purse, yet she possessed
ornaments she could sell: a practice so common in stylish society at
German baths that there was no need for shame. Even without her mother's
letter, she would likely have decided to raise funds for an Etruscan
necklace she had not worn since her arrival; indeed, she might have done
so with an agreeable sense of living with intensity and escaping the
humdrum. With ten louis at her disposal and a return of her former luck,
which seemed probable, what could she do better than continue playing for
a few days? If her friends at home disapproved of how she acquired the
money, as they certainly would, the money would still be hers. Gwendolen's
imagination dwelt on this course, creating agreeable consequences, but
not with the unbroken confidence and rising certainty characteristic of
the gambler's mania. She had approached the roulette table not out of
passion, but in search of it: her mind remained sanely capable of
picturing balanced probabilities, and while the chance of winning
allured her, the chance of losing thrust itself upon her with alternate
strength, causing a vision from which her pride sank sensitively. For she
was resolved not to inform the Langens of any misfortune befalling her
family, nor to make herself indebted to their compassion; and if she were
to part with her jewelry to any observable extent, they would interfere
with inquiries and remonstrances. The path holding the least risk of
intolerable annoyance was to raise money on her necklace early in the
morning, tell the Langens that her mother desired her immediate return
without giving a reason, and take the train for Brussels that evening.
She had no maid with her, and the Langens might make difficulties about
her returning home, but her will was peremptory.

Instead of retiring to bed, she made the light as brilliant as possible
and began to pack, working diligently, though constantly visited by
scenes of the coming day—now by tiresome explanations and farewells, and
the whirlwind journey toward a changed home, now by the alternative of
staying just another day and standing once more at the roulette table.
But in this latter scene, there was always the presence of Deronda,
watching her with exasperating irony, and—the two keen experiences were
inevitably revived together—beholding her again forsaken by luck. This
importunate image certainly helped to sway her resolve toward immediate
departure and urged her packing to the point where a change of mind would
become inconvenient. It had struck twelve when she entered her room, and
by the time she ensured she had left out only what was necessary, the
faint dawn was stealing through the white blinds, dulling her candles.
What use was sleep? Her cold bath was refreshment enough, and she saw
that a slight trace of fatigue around the eyes only made her look the
more interesting. Before six o'clock, she was completely equipped in her
gray traveling dress, including her felt hat, intending to walk out as
soon as she could count on seeing other ladies heading to the springs.
Happening to sit sideways before the long strip of mirror between her
two windows, she turned to gaze at herself, leaning her elbow on the
chair's back in an attitude that might have been chosen for her portrait.
It is possible to possess a strong self-love without self-satisfaction,
rather with a self-discontent that is more intense because one's own
little core of egoistic sensibility is a supreme care; but Gwendolen knew
nothing of such inward strife. She had a *naïve* delight in her fortunate
self, which any but the harshest saintliness would indulge in a girl who
daily saw a pleasant reflection of that self in her friends' flattery as
well as in the looking-glass. And even in this beginning of troubles,
while for lack of anything else to do she sat gazing at her image in the
growing light, her face gathered a complacency gradual as the
cheerfulness of the morning. Her beautiful lips curled into a more and
more decided smile, until finally she removed her hat, leaned forward,
and kissed the cold glass which had looked so warm. How could she believe
in sorrow? If it attacked her, she felt the force to crush it, defy it,
or run away from it, as she had already done. Anything seemed more
possible than that she could continue bearing miseries, great or small.

Madame von Langen never went out before breakfast, allowing Gwendolen to
safely conclude her early walk by returning home through the Obere
Strasse, where the needed shop was certain to be open after seven. At
that hour, any observers she minded would be either strolling in the
region of the springs or still in their bedrooms; but certainly there
was one grand hotel, the *Czarina*, from which eyes might follow her up
to Mr. Wiener's door. This was a chance to be risked: might she not be
going in to purchase something that had struck her fancy? This implicit
falsehood passed through her mind as she recalled that the *Czarina* was
Deronda's hotel; but she was then already far up the Obere Strasse,
walking on with her usual floating movement, every line in her figure
and drapery falling in gentle curves attractive to all eyes except those
which discerned in them too close a resemblance to the serpent and
objected to the revival of serpent-worship. She looked neither right nor
left, transacting her business in the shop with a coolness that gave
little Mr. Wiener nothing to remark except her proud grace of manner and
the superior size and quality of the three central turquoises in the
necklace she offered him. They had belonged to a chain once her father's:
but she had never known her father; and the necklace was in all respects
the ornament she could most conveniently part with. Who supposes that it
is an impossible contradiction to be superstitious and rationalizing at
the same time? Roulette encourages a romantic superstition regarding the
chances of the game, and the most prosaic rationalism concerning human
sentiments that stand in the way of raising needful money. Gwendolen's
dominant regret was that after all, she had only nine louis to add to the
four in her purse; these Jewish dealers were so unscrupulous in taking
advantage of Christians unfortunate at play! But she was the Langens'
guest in their hired apartment and had nothing to pay there; thirteen
louis would suffice to take her home. Even if she determined to risk
three, the remaining ten would more than suffice, since she intended to
travel right on, day and night. As she turned homeward, nay, entered and
seated herself in the *salon* to await her friends and breakfast, she
still wavered regarding her immediate departure, or rather she had
concluded to tell the Langens simply that she had received a letter from
her mother desiring her return, leaving undecided when she should start.
It was already the usual breakfast time, and hearing someone enter as she
leaned back, tired and hungry with her eyes shut, she rose expecting to
see one or other of the Langens—the words that might determine her
lingering at least another day, ready-formed to pass her lips. But it was
the servant bringing in a small packet for Miss Harleth, which had at
that moment been left at the door. Gwendolen took it in her hand and
immediately hurried into her own room. She looked paler and more agitated
than when she had first read her mother's letter. Something—she never
quite knew what—revealed to her before she opened the packet that it
contained the necklace she had just parted with. Wrapped in a cambric
handkerchief underneath the paper was a scrap of torn-off note-paper, on
which was written with a pencil, in clear but rapid handwriting—"_A
stranger who has found Miss Harleth's necklace returns it to her with
the hope that she will not again risk the loss of it._"
