The second the deputation of disgruntled mill workers was off his property, Messrs. Helstone and Sykes swarmed around Robert Moore, giddy with congratulations, cracking jokes about how firmly he’d held his ground against the rabble. But Moore said barely a word in response. His face was as still and sunless as a overcast midwinter day, no trace of a smile, no spark of triumph, and the rector, after a long, shrewd look into his shadowed eyes, quickly buttoned up his jovial backpatting right along with his heavy coat. He turned to Sykes, who was too dull to notice he was the last person anyone wanted lingering that minute, and gestured for the door. “Come on, man, our roads run together for a stretch. Let’s be off and leave Moore to whatever quiet fancies he’s obviously set on entertaining.”

“Wait a second — where’s Sugden?” Moore called, lifting his head at last.

Helstone hooted a little, pleased with himself. “Oh, I wasn’t twiddling my thumbs while you were talking to that poor, downcast fellow Farren. I thought no time to waste. While you were parleying, I pried open the back counting-house window, shouted to Murgatroyd in the stables to bring Sykes’s gig around, and snuck Sugden and old Moses — wooden leg and all — out the sash before anyone could spot ‘em. Sugden’s got the reins, drives as fast as Jehu himself, and by now Barraclough’s halfway to the Stilbro’ jail, safe and sound.”

“Very good. Thank you,” Moore said, and rose politely to show them both to the threshold, waiting till their boots were far down the lane before he shut the door behind them.

The rest of the day he was quiet, heavy with thought, not even tossing a quick quip back and forth with his foreman Joe Scott. Joe, for his part, only spoke what was strictly necessary to keep the half-running mill turning, but kept darting sidelong glances at his master, popping into the counting-house every half hour to poke the fire bright for him, and when he locked up at dusk — trade was so slack they were on short hours already — he lingered to say the evening was fair, and he “could wish Mr. Moore’d take a bit of a walk up th’ Hollow. ‘Twould do ye a world of good, sir.”

Moore huffed a short, sharp laugh at that, teased Joe for being fussy as a old aunt, asking if he thought he was some weak child to be coddled, then snatched the keys from his hand and all but pushed him out the door. But before Joe could make it to the yard gate, he called him back.

“Joe, you know those Farrens, don’t you? They’re not making ends meet these days, are they?”

“Can’t be, sir. They’ve had no steady work near three months. Ye saw for yourself William’s half wasted away, pale as a ghost. They’ve sold nearly every stick of furniture they owned.”

“He was never a bad workman, though?”

“Never had a better man on the looms, sir, since ye first opened for trade.”

“And the whole family — decent folks?”

“Niver dacenter. Th’ wife’s a good, honest body, keeps their little cottage so clean ye could eat your porridge right off the flagstone floor. They’ve fallen so far it hurts to see. William knows gardening like the back of his own hand, too — apprenticed to a Scotsman as a lad who taught him every trick of the trade. Wish he could get a job tending some gentleman’s grounds instead of starving.”

“Right, you can go now. Stop hovering there staring at me like that.”

“No other orders, sir?”

“None but to get yourself home to your own supper.”
Joe nodded and vanished.

Spring evenings in Yorkshire often turn sharp and raw fast. The day had been bright, warm even in the midday sun, but the air bit cold the second the sun dipped below the horizon, the ground crusted crisp, and before full dark a thin, silvery hoar frost was creeping across the new grass and unfurling buds. It whitened the pavement out front of Briarmains, the old stone home of Mr. Yorke, nipping at the tender new blooms in his garden and dusting the mossy lawn. The great gnarled oak that guarded the road-facing gable, thick trunk and wide bare boughs, seemed to laugh off the light spring frost, same as the tall stand of leafless walnut trees rising behind the house.

It was a moonless but starry night, the windows of Briarmains glowing bright against the dark. This was no isolated, quiet spot: the house sat close to the highway, built long before that highway was even cut, back when the only path to the door was a winding lane through the fields. Briarfield village was barely a mile off, its distant hum and faint yellow glow clear to see. A hundred yards down the road, the big new raw red Wesleyan chapel, Briar Chapel, was holding a prayer meeting. Its lit windows cast a broad golden streak across the road, and a wildly exuberant hymn bounced off every nearby tree and cottage wall, so loud it felt even the staidest Quaker might feel moved to tap his foot. Snatches of the words drifted clear all the way to Briarmains:
> Oh who can explain this struggle for life,
> This travail and pain, this trembling and strife?
> Plague, earthquake, and famine, tumult and war,
> The wonderful coming of Jesus declare!

Then there was a lull of loud, heaving prayer and groans, followed by a shout echoing out the chapel doors: “I’ve found liberty! Doad o’ Bill’s has found liberty!” before the whole congregation burst back into song, faster and louder than before.
The roof never caved in, a testament to how well the slaters had done their work.

If the chapel hummed with fervent life, Briarmains hummed too, in a far quieter, softer way. The curtains were drawn across all the lower windows, hiding the interior and muffling most of the candlelight, but they didn’t muffle the faint sound of voices and quiet laughter filtering out through the panes. There were no guests, just the Yorke family all gathered in the back right parlour, their usual evening sitting room. The windows were made of rich stained glass, deep purple and amber, framing central medallions of Shakespeare’s soft, wise face and Milton’s serene one. On the walls hung prints of wild Canadian forests and blue cascading waterfalls, and blazing bright in their midst was a print of Mount Vesuvius erupting at night, the fiery reds and oranges glowing hot against the cool blue of the distant cataracts.

The fire on the hearth was the kind of roaring hot coal blaze southern folks rarely see in private homes, stacked high in the wide old chimney. Mr. Yorke insisted on fires this big even in the height of summer, and he sat beside it now with a book in his hand, not reading at all, just watching his half dozen kids play. Opposite him sat Mrs. Yorke, a large, solemn woman whose whole face and shoulders bore the weight of what she considered her duty to be grave, unsmiling, and suspicious of every last bit of lightness in the world. To her, being cheerful bordered on profanity, being merry was frivolity, no exceptions. She was a good wife and a careful, devoted mother, but she was convinced every one of her husband’s friends was a scoundrel at heart, and kept all his relatives at arm’s length.

Odd as it seemed, the two of them got along perfectly. Yorke was naturally social and warm, had always loved bright, lively women, but his own shadowy, cynical side had found a perfect match in his wife’s perpetually overcast, unforgiving view of the world. Their kids were all as unusual and sharp as you’d expect from two such strong-willed people. The youngest, a baby, sat on Mrs. Yorke’s knee — the only one she didn’t yet scrutinize and suspect, since it clung to her for every bit of its sustenance, and she was sure of its love.

The next two little girls, Rose and Jessy, huddled at their father’s knee, never going near their mother unless forced. Rose, twelve, was the spitting image of her father, but softened, her dark features gentled to ivory, round blooming cheeks, grey eyes lit with a quiet, serious soul far older than her years. Her mother was determined to force her to grow up just as grim and severe as she was, but Rose’s head was full of quiet, stubborn ideas no one had ever taught her, ideas she’d never been allowed to speak aloud. Little Jessy, younger by a year, was bright and chattering, sharp and full of mischief, fearless of her mother’s strict rules even when she got in trouble, soft and affectionate the second anyone showed her kindness. She was her father’s clear favorite, and though her face was the very copy of her mother’s, her bright, playful grin made her look nothing like the stern woman at the other end of the hearth. No one at Briarmains knew it then, but twenty years from that night, little Jessy would be gone, buried in a grave in a far foreign country, and Rose would be a lone emigrant sitting on the banks of an unknown southern river, thousands of miles from Yorkshire.

The three oldest, the boys Matthew, Mark, and Martin, were clustered in the far corner playing a boisterous board game. All three were dark-haired, dark-eyed, ruddy-cheeked, but their faces held very different natures. Matthew, the eldest, was the spitting image of the blazing Vesuvius print on the wall behind them, his whole soul made of flame and shadow, no soft sunlight anywhere in sight. His parents never dared cross him, placating him at every turn to avoid his rages, treating his younger brothers like they had to give way to him at all times, and the resentment was already simmering. Mark, the second, was calm, quiet, his smile sharp and cutting, already middle-aged in his soul at fourteen, already convinced all joy and laughter was foolish. Martin, the youngest of the three, was plain and awkward, a rough chrysalis just waiting for the day he’d shed his dry shell and bloom bright, hungry for all the pleasure and knowledge the world had to offer, no one quite sure if he’d turn out to be a great man, or something else entirely.

A soft tap at the door cut through the noise of the game. No one had heard the front door knocker over the hymn singing from the chapel and Jessy’s little Scotch song she’d been singing for her father. “Come in,” Mrs. Yorke called, her voice deliberately slow and grave, the same funereal tone she used even to give orders for the kitchen to bake a pudding.

In stepped Robert Moore. His habitual quiet gravity was just the sort of thing Mrs. Yorke approved of, and she hadn’t yet found a reason to whisper bad things about him behind his back the way she did all her husband’s other bachelor friends.

“Well, it’s you, is it?” she said, as he walked over to shake her hand. “What are you doing wandering about out at this time of night? You ought to be home.”

“Can a single man be said to have a home, madam?” he asked.

“Pooh,” she huffed, plainspoken as ever, no time for frills. “Don’t talk that nonsense to me. A single man can have a home if he wants. Doesn’t your sister Hortense make a home for you?”

“Hardly that,” Mr. Yorke cut in, laughing. “Hortense is a good honest lass, but when I was Robert’s age I had half a dozen sisters just as proper as her, and it never stopped me from going out and looking for a wife anyway.”

“And I’ve made him regret it every day since,” Mrs. Yorke joked dryly, nodding at the six kids swarming the room. “Young man, when you ever feel tempted to go and marry, look at these four rough boys and two girls, all to feed, clothe, rear, and set up in the world, and think twice before you leap.”

“I’m not tempted any time soon, at any rate,” Moore said. “These aren’t the sort of times for marrying or giving in marriage.”

That dark, grave sentiment won Mrs. Yorke over instantly. She nodded and groaned in full agreement, then waved him to a seat. The second he sat down, little Jessy jumped right off her father’s knee and ran straight into his outstretched arms, and he lifted her easily onto his lap.

“You’re talking to him like he needs to go find a wife,” she said indignantly to her mother, wiggling to get comfortable, “but he’s already got one, or as good as. He promised last summer, the first time he saw me in my new white frock and blue sash, that I’d be his wife. Didn’t he, father?”
