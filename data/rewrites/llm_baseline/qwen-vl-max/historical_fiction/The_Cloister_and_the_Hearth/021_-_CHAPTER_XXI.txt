Words are the common outlet for human thought, but there are feelings so raw and overwhelming that they burst forth not in speech, but in sounds—simple, powerful, unbidden. At such moments, man seems to shed his humanity, becoming little more than a creature of instinct; for even animals, when gripped by passion, cry out without language. Such was the scene now: the sudden roar of triumph from the hunters as they burst from the thicket, and the sharp, piercing cry of fear from those they chased—each sound stripped bare of reason, born of pure instinct.

The pursuers lunged forward, hands grasping at empty air—barely two feet behind the fleeing figures. For a heartbeat, confusion seized them, like beasts who miss their prey. Dierich and his men staggered, stunned by the nearness of failure. Then, with weapons raised, they surged after Gerard and the mule, certain of catching them. This was no new contest; they had raced before, and known each other’s pace. Earlier that day, in open ground, they had clearly gained on the trio. Now, at last, it was a true race—decided only by speed.

A hundred yards vanished in an instant. Yet still, ten yards remained between hunter and hunted. Dierich frowned. Something had changed since morning. The reason lay in the way three people run together: the pace is set by the slowest. From Peter’s house to the edge of the forest, Gerard had matched Margaret’s stride. But now he ran his own. The mule was swift—capable of outrunning them all. And beyond that, youth and a life untouched by vice were lending him strength. With each step, daylight crept between the fugitives and their pursuers.

Then Dierich made a desperate surge—gaining two yards. But within seconds, Gerard reclaimed them, effortlessly, silently. The hunters began to curse. Martin heard, and his face brightened. “Courage, Gerard! Courage, brave lad! They’re breaking apart!”

And so they were. Dierich, leading the chase, was now followed by one man, while another fell far behind. As they climbed a long, gentle rise, the advantage of youth, grit, and clean living grew stronger. By the time Gerard reached the crest, Dierich’s forty years pressed upon him like lead weights. “Our cake is dough,” he gasped. “Take him dead if you can’t take him alive.” He stopped running, and began to follow on foot.

Jorian Ketel lagged next, then another, until one by one, the hunters faltered. Only one kept going—steady as a bloodhound, though slowly losing ground. His name, if memory serves, was Eric Wouverman.

Together, they reached a second rise—shorter, but much steeper. “Hand on mane!” shouted Martin. Gerard obeyed. The mule, sensing the climb, leaned into the effort, helping him ascend faster than he had run before.

At the sight of this, Dierich’s man lost heart. Eighty yards behind Gerard, and well ahead of his nearest companion, he halted abruptly. Following orders, he drew his crossbow, took careful aim, and as the trio disappeared over the hilltop, loosed the bolt. It whistled through the air—and struck home.

A cry of horror rose from the group. Then, as if struck by lightning, they collapsed—mule and all—into the earth below.
