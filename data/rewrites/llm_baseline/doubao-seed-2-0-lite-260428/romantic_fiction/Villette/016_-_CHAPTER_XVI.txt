Where my soul wandered in that long, dark swoon I can never say. Whatever sights it drank in, whatever far shores it drifted to on that wild, storm-lashed night, it kept its secrets sealed tight: no whisper drifting back to memory, no trace left for imagination to chase. Perhaps it climbed upward, caught sight of its eternal home, and dared to hope it might rest at last, free of the aching, heavy weight of the frail mortal frame it had grown so tired of dragging around. Just as it lingered on heaven’s threshold, daring to dream of release, an angel may have touched its shoulder, wept to turn it away, and guided it back, all shuddering and unwilling, to that cold, wasted body it had almost escaped.

I know it re-entered that prison of flesh with a sharp, pained gasp, with a long, trembling shiver, dragged back against its will. Spirit and substance, those two parted mates, fought to rejoin rather than embraced: the first glimmer of returning sight bled red, as if my eyes swam in thick blood; sound rushed back in a roaring thunderclap, every distant creak and patter of rain loud enough to make me flinch; consciousness returned wrapped in raw, unnameable fear. I sat bolt upright, appalled, wondering what strange, unearthly realm I had woken into, what alien beings surrounded me. At first nothing I saw made sense: a wall was no wall, a lamp no lamp. I would have understood a ghost just as well as I understood the most ordinary household object—every shape my eyes fell on seemed spectral, something not quite of this world. But slowly, my frayed faculties settled back into their places, and the old tired human clock of my body began ticking at its usual, steady pace once more.

Even then, I had no clue where I was. Only slowly did I realize I had been carried far from the church steps where I had fainted: no portico stone pressed into my back now, the raging night and tempest shut out solidly by walls, windows, a ceiling overhead. Someone had brought me inside, to a house—but whose?

My first half-delirious thought was the Rue Fossette pensionnat. Still foggy with faintness, I tried to place which room they had laid me in—the great shared dormitory, or one of the tiny little single chambers? I was utterly puzzled, for none of the furniture peeking through my haze lined up with any room I knew there. No empty white iron beds, no long row of tall bare windows greeted me. “Surely they did not carry me to Madame Beck’s own private chamber,” I thought, and then my gaze fell on an easy chair draped in rich blue damask. Other matching cushioned seats swam into view, until I could make out the whole shape of a cozy parlour, a crackling wood fire glowing on a polished hearth, a carpet stitched with bright blue arabesques over a soft fawn ground. Pale walls were strung with an endless, tangled garland of azure forget-me-nots woven through thousands of tiny gold leaves and tendrils. A gilded mirror hung between two windows draped in the same blue damask, and in its glass I saw myself stretched out not in a bed, but on a sofa. I looked like a ghost: eyes too large and hollow, dark hair stark against my thin, ashen face. It was plain, from every piece of furniture, every placement of doors, windows and hearth, that I was in a stranger’s house, in a room I had never stepped foot in before.

Or—was it? That blue armchair looked familiar, the scroll-shaped couch, the round centre table covered in blue cloth bordered with autumn-hued foliage, and most of all, two little stools with embroidered covers, that tiny ebony chair whose seat and back were stitched with bright flower clusters on a dark background.

A jolt ran through me. Old, beloved acquaintances smiled out of every nook, echoes of auld lang syne rising soft and warm from every surface. Over the mantel hung two oval miniatures: I could recite every detail of their subjects by heart, the pearls strung in their high powdered wigs, the velvet ribbons circling their white throats, the soft swell of their full muslin neckerchiefs, the delicate pattern of lace ruffs at their cuffs. On the mantelshelf sat two china vases, the tiny fragments of a miniature tea set as smooth as enamel and thin as eggshell, a white alabaster classic group under a glass dome. I could name every tiny flaw, every faint crack on every last one of them, as clearly as any clairvoyant. Above all, there was a pair of hand-screens, covered in fine pencil drawings as sharp as line engravings. My eyes ached looking at them, recalling long hours of my girlhood, when I had guided that tedious, fussy schoolgirl pencil stroke by stroke, my thin young fingers moving over the page.

Where on earth was I? Not just in what corner of the world, but what year? All these objects belonged to a distant country, to days long past. I had said goodbye to them ten years prior. I had not set eyes on a single one since I was fourteen years old. I gasped out loud, “Where am I?”

A figure I had not noticed before stirred, rose, and stepped forward, a shape that clashed with the familiar old decor and tangled the riddle even further: a local bonne, in a plain print dress and common work cap. She spoke no French I knew, no English at all, and I could make no sense of her thick patois. But she bathed my forehead and temples with cool scented water, propped my cushions higher, gestured for me not to speak, then went back to her post at the foot of the sofa, knitting.

Too weak to unpick the mystery, I told myself it was a mistake, a fever dream, a hallucination—even as I knew I was not asleep, not delirious, not mistaken. All these things, the blue damask, the little hand-screens, the miniatures, were exact in every last detail to the drawing room in my godmother’s house in Bretton. It felt as if I had been spirited away like Bedreddin Hassan from the Arabian Nights, snatched from my doorstep in Villette and dropped thousands of miles away in the drawing room of the home I had loved as a girl, the home I had thought long gone. But that hearth had gone cold years ago, I knew, the household scattered to the winds.

The bonne noticed my wide, perturbed gaze, set down her knitting, poured water and measured drops from a small phial, and held the glass to my lips. I drank the strange sweet draught passively, and at once a soft tide of calm washed over my raw brain. The ache of weakness drained from my limbs, my muscles went loose and warm, and I drifted off into deep, dreamless sleep.

When I woke, everything was changed again. Bright autumn daylight filled the room—soft, leaden light of a raw, blustery October day, not warm summer sun. I thought for a moment I was back in the pensionnat, hearing rain lash the casement and wind howl through the garden trees, but when I pushed back the dimity curtains of the bed, I found myself in a tiny cabinet room, walls painted soft sea-green. The familiar furniture was all here too, the little green chintz armchair, the black carved foliated frame of the toilet mirror, the pale green china washbasin, the pincushion frilled with lace, stitched with my own hands. I turned it over, and there was the gold bead cipher: L.L.B. — Louisa Lucy Bretton, my godmother’s initials.

My heart hammered. Was I in England? At Bretton? I hauled myself up to yank up the blind, half expecting to see the old familiar grey stone buildings of St. Ann’s Street, the minster towers rising in the distance. Instead, I looked out over a grassy lawn terrace, tall old beech trees groaning in the gale, yellow leaves swirling across the empty avenue. The place was beautiful, and utterly unknown to me.

I sank back on the bed, turning my face away from the bewildering sights, until my gaze fell on a gilded framed portrait hanging on the wall: a watercolour sketch of a boy of sixteen, fair cheeked, hair glowing gold in the light, laughing, bright-eyed. It was the portrait of Graham Bretton, I realized at once, the one that used to hang over the breakfast room mantel. I remembered climbing up on a music stool as a girl to take it down, little Polly Home in my arms, both of us staring up at that bright face, until the little girl had begged me to put her down, too stirred to speak. “Graham,” I breathed the name out loud, half to myself.

“Graham?” echoed a soft, surprised voice at my bedside. “Do you want Graham?”

I turned, and my breath caught. There she stood, my godmother Mrs. Bretton, tall, stately, dressed in quiet widow’s silk, the same motherly warm face I remembered from my childhood, hardly aged a day. My pulse fluttered, blood draining from my cheeks, cold under the skin. “Madam, where am I?” I asked.

“In a safe, quiet house, my dear. You fainted on the church steps last night in the storm, and my son brought you back here. You must rest easy now, until you are well again,” she said, in that steady, no-nonsense English voice I knew so well. She told me she had just arrived in Villette from England, that her son, Dr. John, had recognized me as the English teacher from the Rue Fossette pensionnat.

I ate a morsel of dry toast and a sip of tea that morning, the first solid food I had tasted in nine days, and strength seeped slowly back into my bones. By evening, I was restless with confinement, sick of the tiny room, longing for firelight. I dressed myself, and Mrs. Bretton offered me her arm to walk downstairs, to the blue damask parlour I had first woken in. It was warm there, amber lamplight spilling over the rug, the fire glowing red, the familiar silver tea urn, the spiced seed cake Graham had always loved sitting on the table. I waited, watching Mrs. Bretton glance at her watch, listening for the sound of her son’s return through the storm. Soon enough, the front gate clanged shut, boots crunched on the gravel path, and Dr. John strode into the room, tall, broad-shouldered, the bright boy from the portrait all grown into a handsome, kind-faced man.

He rose courteously to greet me, smiling. “You must be feeling much better, then,” he said. “I was so alarmed when I found you last night, but I had to rush off to a dying patient, I am sorry I left you. Tell me, what brought on that faint in the storm?”

I met his eyes, steady and calm, and smiled softly.

“Much better,” I said. “Much better, I thank you, Dr. John.”
