The loggia crowning Bardo's house soared above the flanking structures, forming a secluded gallery around a quadrangular courtyard. While the street-facing side was supported by sturdy columns, the rear opened onto a series of arched windows piercing the wall. Through these arches, one could gaze past a jumble of haphazard dwellings toward the hill of Bogoli, offering Romola a private promenade shielded from prying eyes.

Near one of these arches, close to the entrance, Tito waited. Sunlight slanted harshly before him, mingling with the wreckage of his hopes. He had never truly believed that Romola's affection could withstand the repulsion born of his secret; he lacked the arrogance to imagine her love stemmed solely from faith in him. Yet, as he pictured her approaching in her radiant beauty, softened by her hazel eyes, a selfish wish arose: that she were merely human, lower in station, so he might clutch her and kiss her freely before their inevitable separation. He had rarely received genuine tenderness from her—only fleeting glances, occasional kisses, and pressed hands—and he had longed endlessly for solitude with her. Now, they were finally alone, yet she stood rigidly distant.

His heart hammered as the door shifted. Romola appeared. It struck him like lightning: he felt rather than saw the halo around her head, the tearful appeal in her eyes; he sensed rather than heard the cry of "Tito!" In that instant, she was in his arms, sobbing against his chest.

How desperately Romola had craved to see that bright face during the long nights! The terrifying vision of death, the bewildering doubts stirred by tales of a life alien to her understanding, the haunting specter she seemed to inhabit as her own dream—it all sharpened her realization that Tito was the first to bring hope and gladness into her existence, the first to blunt the sharp edges of grief regarding her brother. She needed to confide in him; no one else could hold her burden. Though she had restrained herself before her father all morning, the dam broke now. To the world, she was proud and controlled; in her love for Tito, she was as simple and unguarded as a child. Content with days spent merely gazing at one another, she now felt the urgent need to cling, with no hesitation holding her back.

"My Romola! My goddess!" Tito whispered, embracing her passionately, kissing the thick golden waves of her neck. He felt himself in paradise; disgrace, shame, and parting dissolved into insignificance. This happiness was too potent to be tainted by the knowledge that she loved a deceived man; indeed, he rejoiced in her delusion. After all, concealment had been wisdom. His only regret was his unnecessary dread, though perhaps that fear had been worth enduring for this sudden rapture.

The sobbing ceased, and Romola lifted her head. Silence stretched between them, filled with the sweet wonder of youthful love. Her long white hands rested on his dark-brown curls; his dark fingers were bathed in her streaming gold. Each was the most beautiful object in the other's world, experiencing an undisturbed mutual consciousness for the first time. A fresh wave of sadness pressed on Romola's heart, making her linger in this silent, soothing proximity. Tito dared not press his lips to hers, for that would alter the sacred stillness.

"Tito," she finally said, "it has been altogether painful, but I must tell you everything. Your strength will help me resist impressions that reason cannot shake."

"I know, Romola—I know he is dead," Tito replied. His long, lustrous eyes revealed none of the myriad wishes that would have summoned that death long ago had mere desire possessed such power. Romola read her own pure thoughts in their dark depths as clearly as one reads letters in a happy dream.

"So changed, Tito! It pierced me to think it was Dino. And so strangely hard: not a word to my father, nothing but a vision he wanted to tell me. Yet it was pitiful—the struggling breath, the eyes that seemed to look toward the crucifix and yet not see it. I shall never forget it; it feels as if it stands between me and everything I shall ever behold."

Her heart swelled anew, forcing her to pause, but the compulsion to unload her mind to Tito urged her to suppress the rising anguish. When she resumed, her thoughts had drifted slightly.

"It was strange, Tito. The vision concerned our marriage, yet he knew nothing of you."

"What was it, my Romola? Sit down and tell me," Tito said, guiding her to a nearby bench. A fear crossed him that the vision might somehow implicate Baldassarre; this sudden shift prompted him to change positions.

Romola recounted every detail from her entry into San Marco, omitting not a single word of her brother's speech, which burned itself into her memory. But as she reached the climax of the vision, she faltered. The remainder was too vivid to utter; she sat staring into the distance, momentarily unaware of Tito's presence.

*His* mind remained at ease; the vague vision had swept over him like white mist, leaving no trace. He waited silently for her to continue.

"I took it," she continued, as if he had read her thoughts. "I took the crucifix; it is down below in my bedroom."

"And now, my Romola," Tito entreated, "banish these ghastly thoughts. Such visions are bred of fasting and fanatical ideas in monks. Surely they carry no weight for you."

"No, Tito; no. But poor Dino... *he* believed it was a divine message. It is strange," she mused, "this life of men possessed by fervid beliefs that seem like madness to others. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic; and that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seems to penetrate me with a sense that there is some truth in what moves them: some truth of which I know nothing."

"It was only because your feelings were highly wrought, my Romola. Your brother's state of mind was no more than a form of theosophy, the common ailment of excitable, dreamy minds throughout history—the same ideas your father's old adversary, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonists. Only your brother's passionate nature drove him to act out what others merely discuss. As for Fra Girolamo, he is simply a narrow-minded monk gifted with instilling terror in the multitude. Any words or voice would have shaken you in that moment. Once your mind rests, you will judge such matters as you always have."

"Not about poor Dino," Romola insisted. "I was angry with him; my heart closed against him while he spoke. But since then, I have thought less of my own mind and more of his. Oh, Tito! It was terribly pitiful to watch his young life end thus. That yearning look at the crucifix as he gasped for breath—I can never forget it. Last night, I stared at the crucifix for a long time, trying to see how it could help him, until, by the lamplight, the suffering face seemed to shed pity."

"My Romola, promise me to resist such thoughts. They are fit for sickly nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who looks made to scatter all such twilight fantasies. Try not to think of them now; we shall not long be alone together."

His final words were delivered in a tone of tender pleading. He turned her face toward him with a gentle touch of his right hand. Romola had been gazing absently at the arched opening, missing the distant hill. She had been inside the chapter house, contemplating pale images of sorrow and death. Tito's touch and voice recalled her. Now, in the warm sunlight, she beheld a rich, dark beauty that seemed to gather images of joy around it: purple vines festooned between elms, strong corn ripening under the vibrating heat, bright-winged creatures darting among flowers, round limbs beating the earth in gladness with cymbals aloft, light melodies sung to the thrilling rhythm of strings—all signs of Nature reveling in her force.

A strange, bewildering transition occurred within her—from those pale specters of sorrow to this vibrant youthfulness, like a sun-god ignorant of night. What thought could reconcile the worn anguish on her brother's face, that straining reach for the invisible, with this satisfied strength and beauty, proving they belonged to the same world? Was there no reconciliation, only a blind worship of clashing deities, first in mad joy, then in wailing? For the first time, Romola felt this questioning need as a sudden, dizzying lack, a hunger for something to grasp. It was an experience shorter than a sigh, for the eager theorizing of ages compresses into the momentary void of a single mind. No answer met the need, and it vanished before the rushing tide of young sympathy for the glad, loving beauty beaming upon her in new radiance, like dawn after turning from the grey west.

"Your mind lingers apart from our love, my Romola," Tito murmured softly, reproachfully. "It seems a forgotten thing to you."

She looked at his beseeching eyes in silence until the sadness melted from her own countenance.

"My joy!" she exclaimed in her full, clear voice.

"Do you really care for me enough, then, to banish those chill fancies?" Tito asked, smiling brightly. "Or shall you always suspect me as the Great Tempter?"
