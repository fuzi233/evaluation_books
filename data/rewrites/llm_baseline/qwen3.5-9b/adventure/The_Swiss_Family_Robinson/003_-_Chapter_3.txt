"I shall spare you a detailed account of our first day's labors," my wife said gently. "Truth be told, I spent most of my time in anxious contemplation, keeping a watchful eye on your progress and our signals. I rose very early this morning; upon seeing your reassuring sign that all was well, my heart leaped with joy. Hastening to reply to you, I then sat while my sons still slept, pondering how we might improve our precarious situation.

'This place is untenable,' I murmured to myself. 'To survive much longer here is impossible. The sun beats down relentlessly upon this barren rock all day long. Our only shelter is this miserable tent, and beneath its canvas, the heat feels even more oppressive than on the open shore. Why should not I and my little boys contribute as much as my husband and Fritz? Why should we not strive to accomplish something useful?

'If we could merely trade this gloomy, unhealthy dwelling for a pleasant, shaded retreat, our health and spirits would surely improve. Among those delightful woods and groves where Fritz and his father discovered so many wonders, I am certain there exists a small sanctuary where we could establish ourselves comfortably. There must be one, and I shall find it.'

By this time, the boys had awakened. I observed Jack quietly and industriously working with his knife near the spot where Fritz's jackal had lain. Watching him, I saw him cut two long, narrow strips from the animal's skin, cleaning and scraping them with great care. He then took a handful of large nails from his pocket, driving them through the skin with the points facing outward. Next, he cut strips of canvas sailcloth twice as wide as the thongs, doubled them, and laid them over the raw side of the skin to cover the protruding nail heads.

At this stage, Master Jack approached me with a polite request that I kindly stitch the canvas and the moist skin together for him. I handed him needles and thread, though I hesitated to deprive him of the pleasure of performing the task himself. However, seeing how cheerfully he persisted with his clumsy, unskilled fingers, I felt compassion for him. Conquering my natural distaste, I finished lining the ingenious dog-collars he had devised. Soon after, I was asked to complete a fine belt of skin he had fashioned for himself. I advised him to devise a method to prevent the skin from shrinking.

Ernest, initially mocking Jack's creations, proposed a sensible plan which Jack immediately executed. He nailed the skin flat against a board and placed it in the sun to dry.

My plan for a journey was received with boundless enthusiasm by my young companions. Preparations were made instantly: weapons and provisions were gathered. The two older boys carried their guns, leaving me in charge of the water flask and a small hatchet. Leaving our camp in as good order as possible, we headed toward the stream, accompanied by the dogs. Turk, who had joined you on your first expedition, seemed to instantly grasp that we intended to follow the same route, proudly leading the way.

Gazing at my two young sons, each armed with a gun, I reflected deeply on how the safety of our entire party rested in their hands. This filled me with profound gratitude toward you, dear husband, for teaching them the use of firearms in their youth. Filling our water jar, we crossed the stream and ascended to the height from which, as you described, a magnificent view unfolds. At the sight of this landscape, a buoyant hope—a feeling I had long forgotten—awoke within my breast.

A charming little wood in the distance caught my particular attention, and thither we directed our steps. However, soon realizing it was impossible to force our way through the tall, luxuriant grass that grew denser than the children's heads, we turned toward the open beach on our left. Following the shoreline, we reached a point closer to the wood and moved inland.

We had not entirely avoided the tall grass, however. Struggling through the reeds with extreme fatigue, we were suddenly startled by a tremendous rushing noise that terrified us all. A very large and powerful bird sprang upward on its wings. Both boys attempted to take aim, but the bird was far away before they could fire.

"Oh dear, what a pity!" exclaimed Ernest. "Now, if I had only had my light gun, and if the bird had not flown quite so fast, I should have brought him down directly!"

"Oh yes," I replied, "no doubt you would be a capital sportsman if only your quarry always gave you time to prepare comfortably."

"But I had no notion that anything was going to fly up just at our feet like that!" he cried.

"A good shot," I corrected him, "must be prepared for surprises; neither wild birds nor wild beasts will warn you before they flee."

"What sort of bird could it have been?" inquired Jack.

"Oh, it certainly must have been an eagle," answered little Franz, "it was so very big!"

"Just as if every big bird must be an eagle!" retorted Ernest, in a tone of derision.

"Let us at least see where it was sitting," I suggested.

Jack sprang toward the spot, and instantly a second bird, slightly larger than the first, rushed upward with a startling roar. The boys stood staring upward, completely stupefied, while I laughed heartily. "Well, you are first-rate sportsmen, to be sure! You certainly will keep my larder famously supplied!"

At this, Ernest blushed and looked inclined to cry, while Jack put on a comical face, pulled off his cap, and bowed low, calling after the fleeing bird, "Adieu for the present, sir! I live in hopes of another meeting!"

Searching the ground carefully, we discovered a crude nest made untidily of dry grass. It was empty, though we noticed broken eggshells nearby. We concluded that the young brood had escaped into the surrounding grass, which we could indeed see waving as the tiny birds ran through it.

"Now look here, Franz," Ernest said shortly after, "consider how this bird could possibly have been an eagle. Eagles never build nests on the ground, nor can their young leave the nest and run immediately after hatching. That is a peculiarity unique to the gallinaceous tribe of birds. Therefore, these must belong to that family. The species, I believe, is indicated by the white belly and the dull red color of the wing coverts I observed. I am convinced they are bustards, especially since I noted in the largest specimen the fine, moustache-like feathers over the beak, characteristic of the Great Bustard."

"My dear boy!" I said, "your eyes were actively employed, I must confess, even if your fingers were slow with the gun. And after all, it is just as well, perhaps, that we have not thrown the bustard family into mourning."

Thus chatting, we finally approached my beloved wood. Numerous birds fluttered and sang among the high branches, but I discouraged the boys' desire to shoot any of the happy creatures. We were lost in admiration of the trees in this grove. I cannot describe to you how wonderful they are, nor can you imagine their enormous size without seeing them. What we had called a wood turned out to be a group of only about a dozen trees. What was truly strange was that their roots supported the massive trunks elevated in the air, forming strong arches and props around each stem, which remained firmly rooted in the center.

I gave Jack some twine. Scrambling up one of the curious open-air roots, he measured the circumference of the trunk itself, determining it to be about eighteen yards. I saw no fruit, but the foliage was thick and abundant, casting a delicious shade over the ground below, which was carpeted with soft green herbage entirely free from thorns, briars, or bushes. It was the most charming resting place imaginable. Myself and the boys enjoyed our midday meal immensely in this glorious palace of the woods, so grateful to our senses after the glare and heat of our journey.

After a while, the dogs joined us. They had lingered behind on the seashore, and I was surprised to see them lie down and fall asleep comfortably without begging for food, as they usually do when we eat.

The longer we stayed in this enchanting place, the more it captivated my fancy. If we could manage to dwell in some sort of structure up among the branches of those grand, noble trees, I would feel perfectly safe and happy. It seemed absurd to suppose we would ever find another place half so lovely, so I decided to stop searching and return to the beach to check if anything from the wreck had washed ashore that we could carry away.

Before departing, Jack persuaded me to sit a little longer to finish making his belt and the spiked collars for the dogs. As you know, the child had been carrying the board on which these were stretched the entire time to ensure they received the full benefit of the sun. Once they were dry, I completed them easily. Jack girded on the belt with great pride, placing his pistols inside, and marched about in a most self-important style, while Ernest fitted the collars onto the two dogs.

Upon reaching the shore, we found it strewn with many articles, doubtless of value, but all too heavy for us to lift. We rolled some casks beyond high-water mark and dragged a couple of chests higher onto the beach. During this effort, we noticed our dogs busy among the rocks. They were carefully inspecting crevices and pools, occasionally pouncing downward to seize something they swallowed with evident relish.
