As he came out into the lobby, the last muffled bars of the orchestra still clinging to the plush and gilt, Archer ran into Ned Winsett. Winsett stood a little apart from the well-fed ripple of the crowd, his shoulders rounded under a coat that had known too many weathers, and his thin face turned, not toward the departing ladies, but back toward the Beaufort box, as if a single glance could keep what he had seen from slipping away into the general churn of Fifth Avenue talk.

They shook hands; Winsett’s palm was dry and ink-stained, the hand of a man who lived by words and yet could never afford to waste them. He nodded toward the street with the air of proposing something both consoling and cheap.

“Bock?” he suggested. “Little German place round the corner. They draw it properly—cold as a cellar stone, with that clean yeast-breath on it.”

Archer, who had the taste of theatre dust in his throat and an impatience for Winsett’s corner of the world just then, declined on the plea that he had work to do at home. Winsett’s mouth twisted in a half-smile.

“Oh, well—so have I, for that matter. I’ll be the Industrious Apprentice too.”

They went out together. Broadway sounded raw after the house: wheels on paving-stones, a newsboy’s cry, the clack of hoofs, the thin metallic laughter of a street-organ struggling against the traffic. Archer noticed, as he always did, how the city’s noise changed a man’s thoughts—how it pressed them flatter, made them harder to hold. Winsett walked as if he were used to being jostled by sound and circumstance, and presently said, as if the matter had been brewing in him all evening:

“Look here—what I’m really after is the name of the dark lady in that swell box of yours—with the Beauforts, wasn’t she? The one your friend Lefferts seems so smitten by.”

Archer could not have said why, but annoyance pricked him—quick and irrational. What did Ned Winsett want with Ellen Olenska’s name? And why, above all, drag Julius Beaufort’s box and Lawrence Lefferts’s glib admiration into it? Winsett was not usually curious in that way; then Archer remembered: journalist. The habit of needing names, needing labels, needing the proper spelling for a thing that, once named, could be circulated till it lost its savor.

“It’s not for an interview, I hope?” Archer laughed.

“Well—not for the press; just for myself,” Winsett returned. “The fact is, she’s a neighbour of mine—queer quarter for such a beauty to settle in—and she’s been awfully kind to my little boy.”

Archer’s annoyance loosened, and something warmer dilated in him—an intimate glow, as if a shutter had been lifted and a small domestic scene allowed him, suddenly, into her life.

“He fell down her area chasing his kitten,” Winsett went on, “and gave himself a nasty cut. She rushed in bareheaded, carrying him in her arms, with his knee all beautifully bandaged—clean work, mind you; she’d have made a decent nurse in a campaign—so sympathetic and beautiful that my wife was too dazzled to ask her name.”

There was nothing extraordinary in the tale; any woman could have tied up a child’s knee. But Archer saw it as if he had been there: Ellen without hat or cloak, hair loosened by haste, hands steady with that particular competence which came not from training but from pity that did not pause to consider itself. He could almost smell the sharp clean bite of spirits, the faint sweet of old linen, the sting of winter air brought into a narrow staircase.

“That is the Countess Olenska,” he said. “A granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott’s.”

“Whew—a Countess!” Winsett whistled. “Well, I didn’t know Countesses were so neighbourly. Mingotts ain’t.”

“They would be, if you’d let them,” Archer answered—knowing even as he spoke that it was their old, worn argument: the refusal of the “clever people” to frequent the fashionable; the fashionable’s conviction that cleverness was merely a contrivance for being shabby with a good conscience. They had crossed that ground too many times to find anything new in it.

Winsett broke off, his eyes narrowing, that sudden flicker of penetration lighting his tired face.

“I wonder how a Countess happens to live in our slum?”

“Because she doesn’t care a hang about where she lives,” Archer said, and heard in his own tone a secret pride in her freedom, “or about any of our little social sign-posts.”

“H’m—been in bigger places, I suppose,” Winsett commented, and then nodded toward his corner. “Well—here’s my turning.”

He slouched away across Broadway, swallowed by the jangle of vehicles and the shout of men. Archer stood and watched him go, thinking of the way Winsett’s mind flashed—brief illuminations over a landscape he seemed content to tramp through in twilight. Those flashes were the most interesting thing about him, and always made Archer wonder why they had allowed him, at an age when most men were still struggling, to accept failure so stolidly.

Archer had known that Winsett had a wife and child, but he had never seen them. He met Winsett at the Century, or in the haunts where journalists and theatrical people ate and drank cheaply among loud voices and perpetual smoke. The German restaurant Winsett had mentioned came back to him with sensory exactness: the sour-sweet smell of cabbage boiled too long, the honest savor of sausage split open to the heat, the beer’s cold foam leaving rings on thick glass, and above all the clamor—chairs scraped, plates slapped down, a piano ground out of tune—sound used like fuel to keep a room from thinking.

Winsett had once given Archer to understand that his wife was an invalid; it might be true, or might merely mean she lacked the social gifts, the evening clothes, or both. Winsett himself had a savage abhorrence of social observances. Archer, who dressed in the evening because he thought it cleaner and more comfortable to do so, had never paused to reckon that cleanliness and comfort were among the costliest items in a modest budget. He had always taken Winsett’s attitude for part of the dull “Bohemian” posture that amused and irritated people who changed their clothes without remarking on it and did not harangue about servants and simplicity. Yet Winsett always stimulated him; whenever Archer caught sight of the lean bearded face and melancholy eyes, he found himself routing the man out and carrying him off for long talk.

Winsett was not a journalist by choice. He was a pure man of letters, untimely born in a world that had no need of letters—no patience for the slow reduction of life into exact words. After publishing one volume of brief, exquisite literary appreciations—of which one hundred and twenty copies were sold, thirty given away, and the rest destroyed by the publishers (as per contract) to make room for more marketable matter—he had abandoned his real calling and taken a sub-editorial job on a women’s weekly. Fashion-plates and paper patterns alternated with New England love-stories and advertisements of temperance drinks. On the subject of “Hearth-fires” (as the paper was called) he was inexhaustibly entertaining; but under his jokes lay the sterile bitterness of a still-young man who had tried and given up.

His talk always made Archer take the measure of his own life and feel how little it contained; but Winsett’s, after all, contained still less. Their common stock of intellectual curiosity made their conversations exhilarating, and yet their exchange of views usually remained within the limits of a pensive dilettantism—as if they were forever tasting dishes they could not afford to order.

“The fact is, life isn’t much a fit for either of us,” Winsett had once said. “I’m down and out; nothing to be done about it. I’ve got only one ware to produce, and there’s no market for it here, and won’t be in my time. But you—you’re free and you’re well-off. Why don’t you get into touch? There’s only one way to do it: go into politics.”

Archer had thrown his head back and laughed. In that laugh was the whole unbridgeable difference between men like Winsett and men like Archer. Every one in polite circles knew that, in America, a gentleman couldn’t go into politics. But since he could hardly put it in those words, he had answered evasively:

“Look at the career of the honest man in American politics! They don’t want us.”

“Who’s ‘they’? Why don’t you all get together and be ‘they’ yourselves?”

Archer’s smile had lingered—slightly condescending. It was useless to prolong it. Everybody knew the fate of the few gentlemen who had risked their clean linen in municipal or state politics in New York. The day was past when it was possible; the country was in possession of bosses and the emigrant, and decent people had to fall back on sport or culture.

“Culture!” Winsett had cried. “Yes—if we had it. But there are just a few little local patches, dying out here and there for lack of—well, hoeing and cross-fertilising: the last remnants of the old European tradition that your forebears brought with them. But you’re in a pitiful little minority: you’ve got no centre, no competition, no audience. You’re like the pictures on the walls of a deserted house: ‘The Portrait of a Gentleman.’ You’ll never amount to anything—any of you—till you roll up your sleeves and get right down into the muck. That, or emigrate… God! If I could emigrate…”

Archer, mentally shrugging, had always turned the talk back to books—where Winsett, even when uncertain, was interesting. Emigrate! As if a gentleman could abandon his country. One could no more do that than roll up one’s sleeves and go down into the muck. A gentleman stayed at home and abstained. But you couldn’t make a man like Winsett hear the unspoken rules; perhaps because rules that were never spoken were like dialect words—understood only inside the little circle that used them, untranslatable outside it without losing their force.

And it was this, Archer thought, that made the New York of literary clubs and exotic restaurants—though at first shake it looked like a kaleidoscope—turn out, in the end, to be a smaller box with a more monotonous pattern than the assembled atoms of Fifth Avenue. The same phrases, the same approvals and condemnations, clattered through every room like the ceaseless street-noise outside: a din that drowned the finer registers of feeling before they could become articulate.

The next morning Archer scoured the town in vain for more yellow roses. He went from florist to florist with a hunger that had, absurdly, the keenness of appetite: as if the right shade of gold, the right clustered scent—honeyed and green, with that faint peppery undernote in the petals—could be found and carried, like a message, to the one person who would understand what it meant. The shops were bright with winter flowers forced into bloom, their moisture and leaf-smell rising under glass; but nowhere did he see that particular warm yellow he wanted—yellow like a small domestic sun held in the hand.

In consequence of this search he arrived late at the office, perceived at once that his lateness made no difference whatever to anyone, and was filled with sudden exasperation at the elaborate futility of his life. Why should he not, at that moment, be on the sands of St. Augustine with May Welland? No one was deceived by his pretense of professional activity.

In old-fashioned legal firms like that of which Mr. Letterblair was the head—firms mainly engaged in the management of large estates and “conservative” investments—there were always two or three young men, fairly well-off and without professional ambition, who for a certain number of hours each day sat at their desks accomplishing trivial tasks or simply reading newspapers. It was supposed proper that they should have an occupation; the crude fact of money-making was still regarded as derogatory; and the law, being a profession, was accounted a more gentlemanly pursuit than business. But none of these young men had much hope of advancing, or any earnest desire to do so. Over many of them the green mould of the perfunctory was already perceptibly spreading.

It made Archer shiver to think that it might be spreading over him too.

He had, to be sure, other tastes and interests: he spent his vacations in European travel, cultivated the “clever people” May spoke of, and generally tried to “keep up,” as he had somewhat wistfully put it to Madame Olenska. But once he was married, what would become of this narrow margin of life in which his real experiences were lived? He had seen enough of other young men who had dreamed his dream—perhaps less ardently—who had gradually sunk into the placid, luxurious routine of their elders. Their days became like dinners repeated with slight variations: the same courses, the same sauces, the same talk, until even pleasure lost its flavor from being served too often and too predictably.

From the office he sent a note by messenger to Madame Olenska, asking if he might call that afternoon, and begging her to let him find a reply at his club. At the club he found nothing; nor did he receive any letter the following day.

This unexpected silence mortified him beyond reason. It was not only the thwarting of a plan; it was as though the one voice he had begun to distinguish from the general city-din had suddenly fallen silent, leaving him with nothing but the loud, familiar hum of his own world. And though the next morning he saw, behind a florist’s window-pane, a glorious cluster of yellow roses—tight-hearted buds and a few full-blown flowers exhaling their rich, almost buttery perfume into the heated shop—he left it there, turning away as if the sight of them could bruise him.

Only on the third morning did he receive a line by post from the Countess Olenska. To his surprise it was dated from Skuytercliff, whither the van der Luydens had promptly retreated after putting the Duke on board his steamer.

“I ran away,” the writer began abruptly, without the usual preliminaries, “the day after I saw you at the play, and these kind friends have taken me in. I wanted to be quiet, and think things over. You were right in telling me how kind they were; I feel myself so safe here. I wish that you were with us.”

She ended with a conventional “Yours sincerely,” and without any allusion to the date of her return.

The tone surprised Archer. What was Madame Olenska running away from, and why did she need to be safe? His first thought, as it always was with her, went to some dark menace from abroad—an invisible hand reaching across the ocean. Then he reflected that he did not know her epistolary style, and that it might run to picturesque exaggeration. Women always exaggerated; and moreover she was not wholly at her ease in English, which she often spoke as if she were translating from the French. He could hear, behind her words, the cadence of another tongue—the way meaning shifted when poured from one language into another, losing one flavor to gain a different sharpness.

“Je me suis évadée…” Put in that way, the opening sentence immediately suggested that she might merely have wanted to escape a boring round of engagements; which was very likely true, for he judged her to be capricious and easily wearied of the pleasure of the moment.

It amused him to think of the van der Luydens’ having carried her off to Skuytercliff on a second visit—and this time for an indefinite period. The doors of Skuytercliff were rarely and grudgingly opened to visitors; a chilly week-end was the most ever offered to the few thus privileged. But Archer had seen, on his last visit to Paris, the delicious play of Labiche, “Le Voyage de M. Perrichon,” and he remembered M. Perrichon’s dogged, undiscouraged attachment to the young man he had pulled out of the glacier. The van der Luydens had rescued Madame Olenska from a doom almost as icy; and though there were many other reasons for being attracted to her, Archer knew that beneath them all lay his own gentle and obstinate determination to go on rescuing her.

He felt a distinct disappointment on learning she was away; and almost immediately remembered that only the day before he had refused an invitation to spend the following Sunday with the Reggie Chiverses at their house on the Hudson, a few miles below Skuytercliff. He had had his fill long ago of the noisy friendly parties at Highbank—coasting, ice-boating, sleighing, long tramps in the snow, and a general flavour of mild flirting and milder practical jokes. He had just received a box of new books from his London bookseller—fresh paper, crisp cut pages, the faint dusty sweetness of cloth bindings—and had preferred the prospect of a quiet Sunday at home with his spoils.

Now he went into the club writing-room, wrote a hurried telegram, and told the servant to send it immediately. He knew that Mrs. Reggie did not object to her visitors suddenly changing their minds, and that there was always a room to spare in her elastic house.
