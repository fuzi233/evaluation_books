On the far bank of the Arno, where the chill grey twilight deepened, another figure emerged from the shadows, bound for a desolate stretch of the riverbank. Unlike the armed Compagnacci moving elsewhere, whose errands were violent and communal, this man sought isolation. He made his way to a spot overlooked by no dwellings, hidden behind warehouses and granaries that turned their backs upon the water. Here, a sloping expanse of long grass and rushes grew damp, fed by broad gutters emptying their murky contents directly into the swift, channeled flow of the river.

These gutters and the profound silence drew him. He sat down among the reeds, bending over the rushing water, waiting for a chance to feed. He remembered times when a kind "runlet" had slipped him a large piece of bread, or a raw carrot and apple parings. In a world where visibility meant danger, this secluded corner offered a sanctuary for survival. Often, in his restless wakefulness, he returned here before dawn, hoping to catch another scrap and thereby avoid the humiliating, silent begging on the church-steps beyond the Porta San Frediano.

Baldassarre hated the thought of begging more than anything else; he would have chosen death over the act. Yet, he desired to live. This desire was no longer anchored in hope, but in a persistent, haunting possibility that clung to every obsession that completely possesses the mind—a delirious certainty akin to a woman watching a headland for a ship known long ago to be a wreck. After leaving the convent hospital of San Miniato, where monks had cared for him in his helpless state, he had waited in vain for the Wife who was to aid him. She had vanished, likely claimed by the pestilence that seemed to consume everything since the night he parted from her. He had been unable to conceive how sacred vengeance could be satisfied through his arm alone. His knife was lost, and his body too frail to earn another weapon through labor; his mind, shattered and bewildered, too weak to devise a plan even if he possessed the blade. He was an old man, broken and lonely, yet he waited. He awaited something dim and formless, a vague spark that startled him and caused strong pulsations within his chest, much like the phantom objects we seek when jolted awake from sleep by no voice or touch. He lived for this undefined future, creeping out into the grey light to sit in the long grass and watch the waters, which held a faint, elusive promise.

While Baldassarre waited in silence, the Compagnacci were busy with their work. These formidable bands of armed men, left to their own devices by an embarrassed, perhaps complicit, Signoria, had split into two masses but converged swiftly toward the Arno. The smaller group headed for the Ponte Rubaconte, the larger for the Ponte Vecchio. A single phrase had passed from mouth to mouth like a signal, uniting the rabble: they were going to the Via de' Bardi to sack a specific house. If they knew no other reason, what better justification could exist? The leaders of these streaming mobs understood something more: a brief command flew between them, ensuring that both streams would meet before a particular house near the eastern end of the street, where the master was expected to be asleep and caught unawares.

However, the master of that house was neither sleeping nor in bed. Tito's anxiety regarding his departure from Florence, spurred by the investigations of the previous day, had kept him awake. He feared that any delay would invite further inquiry, and in stalling, he sensed only impending danger. Falsehood had flourished and grown strong, but it had nurtured its twin, Fear. Though he no longer wore armor and was no longer terrified of Baldassarre specifically, a spirit had risen from the corpse of that dead fear—the undying habit of fear. He felt unsafe anywhere within this fierce, turbid Florence until he could escape. Now, he was ready. Maso was to hand over his house to a new tenant; his horses and mules awaited him in San Gallo; Tessa and the children were lodged for the night in the Borgo outside the gate, dressed and ready to mount and join him.

Tito descended the stone steps into the courtyard, passing through the great doorway. He was not the same man who had first entered this house and made the fatal mistake of falling in love with Romola; that mistake was remedied, and the old life was soon to be far behind him. He turned with rapid steps toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, intending to cross the Ponte Rubaconte. However, certain sounds reached his ears, causing him to wheel around and walk faster in the opposite direction. Was the mob approaching from Oltrarno? It was a vexation, as he would have preferred a private road. He decided he must now take the Ponte Vecchio. Unpleasant sensations made him draw his mantle tight around him, forcing him to walk at his utmost speed. Though no one saw him in the grey twilight, before he reached the end of the Via de' Bardi, the sounds fell upon his ear again, much louder and nearer. Had he been deceived before? The mob must be crossing the Ponte Vecchio. Driven by an impulse of fear stronger than reflection, he turned again, only to confirm that the crowd was indeed entering the street from the opposite end. He chose not to return to his house; surely they would not attack *him*. Still, he carried valuables, and with a mob, all things except reason and order became possible. Necessity, however, performed the work of courage. He proceeded toward the Ponte Vecchio, the rush, trample, and confused voices growing so loud ahead that he could no longer hear them behind.

Having reached the end of the street, the crowd pouring from the bridge hemmed him in. He had no time to wonder at a sudden shout before he felt himself surrounded—not initially by unarmed rabble, but by armed Compagnacci. The next sensation was his cap falling from his head as he was thrust violently forward into the throng, driven along the narrow passage of the bridge. Then the shouts erupted: "Piagnone! Medicean! Throw him over the bridge!" His mantle was torn from him with strong pulls that would have throttled him if the fibula holding it together had not given way. Someone snatched at his *scarsella* (purse), but amidst the hustle and drag, the snatch failed, and it remained at his side. Shouting, yelling, and half-motiveless execrations rang stunningly in his ears, spreading even among those who had not yet seen him and only knew there was a man to be reviled. Tito's horrible dread was that he would be struck down or trampled before reaching the open arches spanning the center of the bridge. His sole hope lay in being thrown over before they wounded him or beat the strength out of him; his entire soul was absorbed in this hope and its terrifying reverse.

Yes, they were at the arches. In that critical moment, Tito, with a bloodless face and dilated eyes, experienced one of those self-preservation inspirations that arrive in extremity. With a sudden, desperate effort, he mastered the clasp of his belt and flung the belt and *scarsella* forward toward a yard of clear space against the parapet, crying in a ringing voice, "There are diamonds! There is gold!"

In the instant the hold on him relaxed, a rush surged toward the purse. Tito threw himself onto the parapet with a desperate leap, and the next moment he plunged—plunged with a great splash—into the dark river far below.

It was his chance of salvation, and a good one. His life had been saved once before by his fine swimming, and as he rose to the surface after a long dive, he felt a sense of deliverance. He struck out with all the energy of his strong youth, aided by the current. If he could swim beyond the Ponte alla Carraia, he might land in a remote part of the city and eventually reach San Gallo. Life was still before him, and the idiot mob shouting and bellowing on the bridge would undoubtedly think him drowned.

They did think so. Peering over the parapet along the dark stream, they could not see the moving blackness of floating hair or the velvet sleeves of his tunic drifting downstream.

It was only from the other side that a pale olive face could be seen looking white above the dark water—a face not easy even for the indifferent to forget, marked by a square forehead, long low arches of eyebrows, and long, lustrous, agate-like eyes. Onward the face drifted on the dark current, with inflated, quivering nostrils and blue veins distended on the temples. One bridge was passed—the Bridge of Santa Trinita. Should he risk landing now rather than trust to his strength? No. He heard, or fancied he heard, yells and cries pursuing him. Terror pressed him most from the side of his fellow-men; he was less afraid of indefinite chances and swam on, panting and straining, not as fresh as he would have been had he slept the night away.

Yet the next bridge—the last—was passed. He was conscious of it, but in the tumult of his blood, he could only feel vaguely that he was safe and might land. But where? The current was doing its work upon him; he hardly knew where he was. Exhaustion was bringing on the dreamy state that precedes unconsciousness.
