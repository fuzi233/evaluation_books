The silence of the refectory was broken not by the clatter of spoons, but by a hushed reference to the sudden passing of the scribe, the monk tasked with transcribing the monastery's charter and rules. Gerard, seated at the table, felt the weight of the news settle upon him. Seizing the moment with a trembling humility, he offered his services. "Nay, not for hire, my lords," he stammered, his voice barely rising above the murmur, "but for love, and as a humble token of gratitude for the countless nights of shelter the brethren of your order have granted to a poor wayfarer."

A monk, his face softening with approval, shook his head gently. "The late brother was a master of the pen," he murmured, "and his work cannot be continued save by a master."

Gerard's heart hammered against his ribs. With a gesture of trepidation, he reached into his wallet and withdrew a vellum deed. He had spent hours cleaning its back and practicing upon it, creating a specimen of his finest hand. As the monk's eyes fell upon the script, he gave a sharp intake of breath. In a flurry of motion, he rose and hurried up the long hall to the high table. Bending his knee just enough to graze the fifth and tenth steps, he presented the deed to the Prior, his voice trembling with excitement.

Instantly, a dozen knowing eyes locked onto the page. A low buzz of conversation rippled through the hall. Gerard watched as the Prior pointed repeatedly, his expression shifting from scrutiny to delight. Soon, the monk returned, his face flushed with pride, bearing a savory *crustade royale*—a game pie rich with gravy and spices—for Gerard, alongside a silver grace cup brimming with *pimentum*.

Gerard accepted the cup, bowed low first to the distant Prior, then to his companions, and raised it in a toast before circulating it among the brothers. To his astonishment, the entire table erupted in greeting. "Art convent-bred? Deny it not!" they called out.

He acknowledged their words with a surge of relief, thanking Heaven profusely. Without this recognition, he would have remained as rough and ignorant as his former comrades, Sybrandt and Cornelis.

"But 'tis passing strange how you could know," one monk asked, leaning forward.

"You drank with the cup in both hands," two others chimed in simultaneously.

For some time, a raucous laughter had echoed from a table at the far end of the hall, but now it burst forth with such obstreperous volume that the Prior dispatched the Sub-Prior to quell the disturbance and assign penance to the offenders. Gerard's cheeks burned with shame; amidst the unruly merriment, his ear had caught the word "courage!" and the distinct, trumpet-like tones of Denys of Burgundy.

Later, Gerard was settled into Feu Werter's cell, illuminated by wax lights and equipped with a writing frame adjustable to any angle, surrounded by the finest caligraphic materials. Yet, the work proved too taxing for a single evening. The question arose: how could he ask Denys, the self-proclaimed monk-hater, to remain longer?

"Tell me," Gerard urged gently, "and I shall abide by your decision."

To his delight, Denys replied with gracious ease, "A day's rest will harm neither of us. Write thou, and I shall pass the time as I may."

Gerard's labor was met with vast admiration. They agreed that the monastery's records had been grievously diminished by poor Werter's death. The Sub-Prior pressed a *rix-dollar* into Gerard's hand, along with several brushes and pigments from the convent's ample stock. Warm-hearted and buoyed by this good omen, Gerard resumed his march, for he relied upon his pen to secure his fortune and win back his beloved.

"Come, Denys," he said with a cheerful smile, displaying his gifts. "See what the good monks have bestowed upon me. Now, do try to be fairer to them; for, to be blunt, it chilled my friendship for a moment to hear even you call my benefactors 'hypocrites.'"

"I recant," Denys declared instantly.

"Thank you! Thank you, good Denys!" Gerard exclaimed.

"I was a scurrilous vagabond," the soldier admitted.

"Nay, nay, say not so!" Gerard protested warmly.

"But we soldiers are rude and hasty," Denys continued, his tone sincere. "I give myself the lie and offer my highest esteem to those I misunderstood. 'Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few."

"Now you speak reasonably," Gerard said, pleased. "Have you pondered what I said?"

"Nay, it is their own doing," Denys replied.

Gerard crowed softly; all men delight in being proven right. He listened intently as Denys recounted the tale of his conversion.

"Well then," Denys began, his voice animated, "at dinner on the first day, a young monk beside me opened his jaws and laughed right out, most musically. 'Good,' said I, 'at last I have fallen upon a man and not a shorn ape.' To test him further, I slapped his broad back and administered my consigne. 'Heaven forbid!' he cried. I stared. For the dog looked as sad as Solomon; a better mime you never saw, even at a Mystery play. 'I see war is no sharpener of the wits,' he said. 'What are the clergy for but to fight the foul fiend? And what else are the monks for?'

'You may plough up the convents,' he continued, 'and we poor monks shall have naught to do—but turn soldiers, and so bring him to life again.' Then there was a great laugh at my expense. 'Well, you are the monk for me,' said I. 'And you are the crossbowman for me,' he retorted. 'And I'll be bound you could tell us tales of the war that would make our hair stand on end.' 'Excusez! The barber has put that out of the question,' I quipped, and then I had the laugh."

"What wretched ribaldry!" Gerard observed, his brow furrowing pensively.

The candid Denys immediately admitted he had witnessed merrier jests hatched with less cackle. "'Twas a great matter to have got rid of hypocrisy," he said. "'So,' said I, 'I can give you the *chaire de poule*, if that may content ye.' 'That we will see,' was the cry, and a signal went round."

Denys then burst into a gleeful description of his bedtime ordeal. He had been taken to a cell instead of the great dormitory and strictly forbidden to sleep. To aid his vigil, a book of pictures depicting a hundred merry adventures of monks pursuing the female laity was lent to him. In due course, he was led barefoot to the parlour, where a supper fit for a duke awaited him, served by twelve jolly friars—the roaringest boys he had ever met in peace or war.

The story, the toast, the jest, and the wine-cup circulated freely. Some played cards with a gorgeous pack where Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, bedizened with gold, stood for the four queens, while black, white, grey, and crutched friars represented the four knaves. They staked their very rosaries, swearing like troopers when they lost. At midnight, a sly monk stole out, only to be cunningly followed into the garden, where he was seen thrusting his hand into the ivy and pulling out a rope-ladder. He scaled the ten-foot wall with surprising nimbleness, and a russet kirtle popped up from the outer world as quickly as he. Thus began a scene of billing and cooing that struck Denys as rather feline than ecclesiastical, drawing from him the appropriate comment of a "mew!" The monks joined the musical chorus, and the lay visitor shrieked in discomfort. But Abelard merely cried, "What, are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? Ye shall caterwaul to some tune tomorrow night. I'll fit every man-jack of ye with a fardingale." This brutal threat had reconciled him to stay another day—at Gerard's request.

Gerard groaned.

Meanwhile, unable to disconcert such a brazen monk, and the demoiselle beginning to whimper, they had danced caterwauling in a circle, bestowed a solemn benediction on the two wall-flowers, and retired to the parlour. There, they found a pair lying dead drunk and two others affectionate to tears. They had straightway carried off the inanimate, dragged the weeping ones away, and kicked them all merrily into their respective cells.

"And so shut up in measureless content," Denys concluded.

Gerard was disgusted and voiced his displeasure.

Denys chuckled and proceeded to describe the next day's exploits. He and the young monks had drained the fish-ponds, secreting much pike, carp, tench, and eel for their own use. In the dead of night, he had been taken shoeless by crooked paths into the chapel—a ghost-like place, dark and eerie—and then down steps into a crypt below the chapel floor, where suddenly paradise had burst upon him.

"'Tis there the holy fathers retire to pray," Gerard interjected solemnly.

"Not always," Denys corrected. "Wax candles by the dozen were lighted, and princely cheer. Fifteen soups *maigre*, with marvelous twangs of venison, grouse, and hare, and twenty different fishes—being Friday—cooked with wondrous art. Each dish was served between two buxom lasses, and each lass between two lads with a cowl; all but me: and to think I had to woo by interpreter. I doubt the knave put in three words for himself and one for me; if he didn't, hang him for a fool. And some of the weaker vessels were novices, not wont to hold good wine; had to be coaxed ere they would put it to their white teeth; *mais elles s'y faisaient*; and the story, the jest, and the cup went round (by-the-by, they had flagons made to simulate breviaries). A monk touched the cittern and sang ditties with a voice tunable as a lark in spring. The posies did turn the faces of the women folk bright red at first: but *elles s'y faisaient*."

Here Gerard exploded.

"Miserable wretches! Corrupters of youth! Perverters of innocence! But for your being there, Denys, who have been taught no better, oh, would God the church had fallen on the whole gang. Impious, abominable hypocrites!"

"Hypocrites?" Denys cried, with unfeigned surprise. "Why, that is what I clept them ere I knew them: and you withstood me. Nay, they are sinners; all good fellows are that; but, by St. Denys his helmeted skull, no hypocrites, but right jolly roaring blades."

"Denys," Gerard said solemnly, "you little know the peril you ran that night. That church you defiled amongst you is haunted; I had it from one of the elder monks. The dead walk there; their light feet have been heard to patter o'er the stones."

"Merci-corde!" Denys whispered.

"Ay, more," Gerard lowered his voice almost to a whisper. "Celestial sounds have issued from the purlieus of that very crypt you turned into a tavern. Voices of the dead holding unearthly communion have chilled the ear of midnight. And at times, Denys, the faithful in their nightly watches have even heard music from dead lips; and chords, made by no mortal finger, swept by no mortal hand, have rung faintly, like echoes, deep among the dead in those sacred vaults."

Denys wore a look of dismay. "Ugh! If I had known, mules and wain-ropes had not hauled me thither; and so" (with a sigh) "I had lost a merry time."

Whether further discussion might have thrown any more light upon these ghostly sounds, who can tell? For up came a "bearded brother" from the monastery, spurring his mule and waving a piece of vellum in his hand. It was the deed between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt. Gerard valued it deeply as a remembrance of home; he turned pale at first, fearing he had so nearly lost it. To Denys's infinite amusement, he not only gave a piece of money to the lay brother but kissed the mule's nose.

"I'll read you now," Gerard said, "were you twice as ill written; and—to make sure of never losing you"—here he sat down, taking out needle and thread, and sewed it with feminine dexterity to his doublet. His mind, and heart, and soul were away to Sevenbergen.

They reached the promised land, and Denys, who was in high spirits, doffed his bonnet to all the females, who curtsied and smiled in return; he fired his consigne at most of the men, at which some stared, some grinned, and some did both; and finally, he landed his friend at one of the long-promised Burgundian inns.

"It is a little one," he said, "but I know it of old for a good one; *Les Trois Poissons*." But what is this writ up? I mind not this," he added, pointing to an inscription running across the whole building in a single line of huge letters. "Oh, I see. *Ici on loge à pied et à cheval*," Denys said, going minutely through the inscription and looking bumptious when he had effected it.

Gerard did look, and the sentence in question ran thus:

"ON NE LOGE CEANS A CREDIT; CE BONHOMME EST MORT, LES MAUVAIS PAIEURS L'ONT TUE."
