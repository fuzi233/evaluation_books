The atmosphere in the hallway was thick with the residue of officialdom and the stale scent of fear, a far cry from the crisp, investigative clarity Mr. Gryce usually commanded. He felt a peculiar disorientation, a sense of being adrift in a sea of anomalies that threatened to drown his deductive faculties. The victim, Mr. Adams, had been a man existing in a vacuum: a solitary figure with no domestic circle save for a silent, mute attendant, residing in a district where proximity to neighbors was an impossibility. Even his social orbit seemed deliberately pruned; despite months of visibility in this quarter, the silence following his death had been absolute. No neighbors, no friends, no inquisitive crowds had materialized at the threshold. Yet, this very absence of the mundane chorus of grief and curiosity sharpened the edges of the mystery, transforming a local tragedy into a puzzle of isolated variables.

As the clock neared midnight, the tide of the coroner's procession finally receded. Reporters, physicians, and clerks filed out like tides retreating from a shore, leaving Mr. Gryce alone once more, the master of a situation that demanded immediate consolidation. Two critical threads remained loose, demanding his attention before the night claimed him. His first stop was the hall, where a small boy sat slumped in a chair, succumbing to exhaustion.

"Come, Jake," Gryce roused him, his voice cutting through the boy's lethargy. "Time to look lively. We need to retrace the steps of the lady who encountered you today."

Jake stirred, blinking against the dim light, his gaze drifting to the missing hat on the floor. "I'd like to see my mother first," he pleaded, his voice raspy. "She must be devastated. I've never been gone so long."

Gryce moved closer, his demeanor softening just enough to bridge the gap between investigator and child. "Your mother knows exactly where you are, Jake. I dispatched a message hours ago. Her report was exemplary; she speaks of you as an obedient lad who has never lied to her."

"She's a good mother," Jake declared, the warmth returning to his eyes. "I'd be as bad as my father if I didn't treat her well." He reached for his cap, securing it firmly on his head. "I'm ready."

Stepping out into the cool night, they navigated the sparse arteries of the city, where only scattered pockets of life pulsed beneath the moonlight. They halted before a darkened shop window, where Jake's attention snapped to a tableau of two stuffed frogs locked in mortal combat with miniature swords—a scene he had watched intently moments before the lady's arrival. Gryce kept his eyes on the boy, though the frog duel played out with a vividness that suggested the child's focus was undiminished.

"Was she a pretty lady?" Gryce asked.

Jake scratched his head, confusion clouding his features. "She made me afraid. She wore... oh, gorgeous clothes!" he exclaimed, the word hanging in the air with awe. "And she was young, carrying a bunch of flowers, looking troubled?"

Gryce frowned internally. *Young? Flowers? Troubled?* The narrative Jake provided clashed sharply with the memory of the woman he had seen—a figure older, devoid of blooms, radiating anxiety rather than sorrow. "You think she was troubled?"

"I think so," Jake admitted, shaking his head. "But she wasn't crying. When she spoke, her grip was tighter than her voice. She just dragged me to the drugstore. If she hadn't paid me first, I would have wriggled free. I like money, sir; I don't get enough of it."

"Not young," Gryce muttered to himself, the realization settling cold in his gut. "An old flame, then. Dangerous, invariably more dangerous than the youthful infatuation."

At the corner of the drugstore, Gryce stopped. "Show me where she stood."

Jake indicated the precise spot with a finger stained with ink. "And was she there when you emerged?"

"No, sir. She vanished while I was inside."

"Did you witness her departure? Direction?"

"One eye was on her," Jake explained, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I feared she might follow me into the shop, and my arm was too sore to endure another clutch. As she began to move, I stepped forward, catching a glimpse of her heading toward the curb, raising a hand. But it wasn't a carriage; none arrived for several minutes."

Gryce's brow smoothed, the tension easing slightly. "Then a cab or a hack. Did you notice any empty vehicles nearby?"

The boy shook his head, having reached the limit of his observation. Gryce handed him over to a waiting assistant and turned back toward the ill-omened house, the weight of the discrepancy pressing heavier with every step.

Inside, Styles met him at the door. "Someone is waiting, sir. A young man named Hines. Claims to be an electrician."

"That is precisely who I require," Gryce replied. "Where is he?"

"In the parlor, sir. Should I ascend or guard the entrance?"

"You take the stairs. I shall watch the door."

Styles nodded and vanished upward. Gryce entered the parlor, where a dapper youth with an intelligent, alert gaze rose to greet him. After a brief exchange confirming Hines' credentials, Gryce guided him into Mr. Adams's study. The body had been removed to an upper chamber, leaving the room bathed in a single, mild candlelight positioned strategically near the exit.

However, Gryce's intent was not to rely on the flickering flame. He walked to the central table, placed a hand upon a specific button, and instructed Hines to extinguish the candle. Darkness swallowed the room instantly. Then, with a sudden, theatrical burst, a deep violet luminescence flooded the space, illuminating the faces of the two men with equal parts wonder and bewilderment.

"That is merely one slide," Gryce noted calmly. "Press another, and the hue shifts to pink. This yields green, this white, and this a bilious yellow—not flattering to either of us, I assure you. Examine the connections; is there anything anomalous?"

Hines immediately began his inspection. Beyond the obvious conclusion that the device was the product of an amateur's hands, he detected nothing irregular, save for its flawless operation.

"He constructed it himself?" Gryce asked, a note of disappointment coloring his tone.

"Undoubtedly, or someone equally unfamiliar with modern wiring techniques," Hines confirmed.

Gryce gestured to a shelf lined with technical manuals. "Review these volumes. Could an amateur, armed with such resources, replicate this arrangement?"

Without removing a book, Hines replied succinctly, "A man with a deft touch and a scientific inclination could achieve—and exceed—all of this using those texts. The aptitude is present."

"Then Mr. Adams possessed that aptitude," Gryce stated dryly. The implication hung heavy in the air. "One prone to mechanical contrivances often squanders considerable time and capital on frivolities suited only for children. Observe this birdcage, perched at a height inaccessible to anyone without a ladder. Its utility is evident—it houses a lively occupant—but how is it raised and lowered?"

Hines scanned the room, eventually locating a concealed button nestled within the wall tapestry. Upon pressing it, a mechanism disengaged, and the cage descended smoothly into Gryce's grasp.

"We shall not release this poor creature again," Gryce said gently, holding the cage. "An English starling is a rarity here. Listen—he is about to speak."

The bird, seemingly attuned to the gravity of the moment—or perhaps sensing the stern scrutiny of the detective—whisked about briefly before settling into a feigned slumber, lulled by the fascinated gaze of the elderly gentleman holding it. Gryce set the cage on the floor and, driven by a lingering curiosity or perhaps a desire for amusement, pressed another button he had previously ignored. The room remained bathed in the same yellow glare. The previous testing had evidently disrupted the circuit. Shrugging, Gryce prepared to depart when a sudden, urgent motion arrested him.

He directed Hines's attention to the doorway leading to the antechamber, the only exit from the suite. Slowly, inexorably, the aperture was sealing. Within seconds, the gap shrank from yards to feet, then inches, until it was nearly closed.

"Well, that is undoubtedly the invention of a lazy individual," Hines chuckled nervously. "Sitting in his chair, he can seal his fate without shouting at a deaf servant or tripping over rugs. I suppose I approve of the convenience, though..." He trailed off, noticing the grim set of Gryce's jaw. "...the mechanism itself is peculiar. Not wood, as a sensible door should be, but—"

"Steel," Gryce finished, his voice oddly flat.

"This is the most bizarre development yet," Hines whispered. "It appears Mr. Adams was obsessed with electrical contrivances."

"And that we are effectively imprisoned," Hines added, his bravado evaporating. "I detect no means to retract this slide."

"There is surely another button for that," Gryce dismissed casually, though his eyes betrayed a flicker of unease.

After five fruitless minutes of searching, Gryce sighed. "If you have no objections, I shall restore a more congenial illumination. Yellow suits neither the occasion nor our spirits."
