At the foot of the stair the street lay like a strip of hammered silver, moonlight bruised into every cobble. Wind prowled there—thin, sour, and inhospitable—whistling between doorways like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath. Yet not a thread of it reached the nook where I had made my poor lodging. The air above the steps held its own temper: stale stone, old dust, and the faint, teasing memory of bread that had once risen somewhere in this quarter, before Bulika forgot how to feed itself with anything but fear.

I had set my thoughts in order as one sets a table with no guests expected, and was lowering myself to rest when my eyes opened of their own accord—wide, as if some sound too small for the ear had touched them.

There—lifting above the uppermost step—was the head of the shining creature I had seen at the Shadow’s heel. White as milk under moon, but not soft-looking: a sheen like clean fat on chilled broth, beautiful and dangerous both. The moment her gaze met mine, she froze. Then, as if the stair were a narrow pantry passage and she feared to turn and clatter a pot, she began to withdraw—tail foremost, careful, almost polite.

I sprang up. With no room to pivot, she flung herself backward, head over tail, scrambled upright, and in a heartbeat was down the stair and gone. I followed to the bottom and looked up and down the street. Nothing moved but that unwholesome wind, combing the empty way with cold fingers.

I went back to my hard couch.

So there were two prowlers in this city—one with spots, one without. The thought had the sourness of a stew turned in the pot. I was not inclined to risk much for man or woman in Bulika; here even kindness seemed bartered and measured. But the life of a child—warm, breathing, still capable of learning the taste of its mother’s tongue—might be worth a poor life like mine. I resolved to keep watch at the door the rest of the night.

Presently I heard the latch move—slow, slow—like a careful hand easing a knife from a block. I looked up; the door stood half open. I rose, slid softly in, and found behind it not the woman I had befriended but the muffled woman of the desert, wrapped and soundless, as if she had swallowed her own language and would not risk spilling it.

Without a word she led me a few steps to an empty, stone-paved chamber and pointed to a rug on the floor. The rug smelled of old wool, smoke, and something that might once have been cumin ground between callused palms. I wrapped myself in it and lay down again, like dough set aside to rise in a cold room.

She shut the door. Then I heard the outer door open and close once more.

There was no light save what drifted in from the moonlit air, pale as skimmed milk. I lay sleepless, listening to the city’s silence—not the clean silence of fields at night where you can hear the separate insistence of cricket and owl and wind through grasses, but the muffled hush of stone that has been long denied its proper living noises: the scrape of bowls, the laughter around a hearth, the creak of a cart bearing vegetables in from morning ground. This silence had been engineered, tended, like a cruel art; it pressed on the ear until the mind itself began to ring.

In that ringing, a stifled moaning began. It went on a good while, squeezed through walls as if through cloth. Then came the cry of a child—sharp and new, like the first crackle when fire takes—and immediately after it a terrible shriek.

I sprang up and darted into the passage.

From another door in it came the white leopardess, a new-born baby in her mouth, carrying it as she would a cub of her own. The infant’s skin shone damply in the moonlight; its small life beat and trembled in the careless grip of teeth meant for tearing.

I threw myself upon her. In the narrow passage we struggled—my hands in her fur, my weight forcing her jaw to open. I compelled her to drop the infant. It fell upon the stone slabs with a piteous wail that struck me like a pan dropped on flagstones—sudden, ringing pain.

At the cry appeared the muffled woman. She stepped over us—beast and man tangled together—without a pause, as if stepping over a spilled sack, lifted the child with swift certainty, and carried it away. Returning, she seized me by the shoulders, hauled me off the animal, opened the door, and pushed me gently out, as one pushes a troublesome dog from the kitchen before it upsets the pail.

At my heels followed the leopardess.

“She too has failed me!” I thought. “Given me up to the beast to be settled with at her leisure! But we shall have a tussle for it!”

I ran down the stair, expecting the weight of her spring on my back, the hot iron of her claws. But she followed quietly, pads soundless on stone, a silence with a body.

At the foot I turned to lay hold of her. She sprang over my head. When again I faced her, she was crouching at my feet.

I stooped and stroked her lovely white skin. It was cool where moon had touched it, warm beneath where life kept moving—like the surface of a loaf that seems cold until you break it and the steam tells the truth. She responded by licking my bare feet with her hard, dry tongue—rough as a rasp, yet oddly tender in its insistence.

Then I patted and fondled her, and a well of tenderness overflowed in me. She might be treacherous too; this city trained everything living to betray. Yet if I turned from every show of love lest it be feigned, how was I ever to find the real love which must be somewhere in every world—even one whose common speech seemed to have lost the words for mercy, or to have had them stolen and repurposed for commands?

I stood up. She rose and stood beside me, close enough that I heard her breathing: not a snarl now, but a steady, domestic sound, like bellows when the fire is kept at a low, useful heat.

A bulky object fell with a heavy squelch in the middle of the street a few yards from us.

I ran to it. It was a pulpy mass, with just form enough left to show it had been the body of a woman. Something in me tried to name what I saw, to fit it into known categories—flesh, bone, death—but the naming failed. There are horrors no word will carry whole; the mouth refuses, as if to speak them would be to season them and make them fit for consumption.

It must have been thrown from some neighboring window.

I looked around.

On the other side of the way, the Shadow was walking along—calm, deliberate—and the white leopardess was again at his heel.

I followed, gaining upon them. In my heart I pleaded for the leopardess: perhaps she was not a free agent. Perhaps her will had been taken and tied to his like a tethered beast’s rope.

When I drew near, however, she turned and flew at me with a hideous snarl. The sound tore at the street’s enforced quiet—an ugly tear, like ripping cloth. Instinctively I drew back. Instantly she resumed her place behind the Shadow.

Again I drew near. Again she sprang, eyes flaming like live emeralds, her mouth a bright, wet threat.

Once more I made the experiment. She snapped at me like a dog and bit me.

My heart gave way and I cried out. At the sound she looked round with a glance that plainly meant, Why would you make me do it?

I turned away, angry with myself, as if I had kept trying to taste from the same bowl knowing it had been poisoned. I had been losing my time ever since I entered this place. Night as it was, I would go straight to the palace.

From the square I had seen it—high above the heart of the city, compassed with many defenses, more fortress than palace. Yet when I reached it, I found the fortifications, like those of the city, much neglected and partly ruinous. For centuries, clearly, they had been of no account. Even stone forgets its purpose if no living hand remembers how to mend it.

Great gates stood there, strong enough once to have denied an army. There was something like a drawbridge over a rocky chasm, but the gates stood open, and it was hard to believe water had ever occupied the hollow before them. The place had the drained look of a cistern long dry: built for one element, abandoned to another.

All was so still that sleep seemed to have seeped into the masonry, making the very moonlight look discordantly awake—as if light itself had become an intruder, too loud for such quiet. I must either enter like a thief or break a silence that made the mere thought of sound frightful. In a world where noise had been stripped away, a footfall felt like an act of violence.

Like an outcast dog I walked about the walls until I came to a little recess with a stone bench. I took refuge there from the wind. The bench held the day’s cold as a platter holds chill, but I lay down, and in spite of it fell fast asleep.

Something leapt upon me. A rough tongue licked my face.

“It is the white leopardess,” I thought, half-dreaming. “She has come to suck my blood—and why should she not have it? It would cost me more to defend than to yield it.”

So I lay still, waiting for the shoot of pain.

But the pang did not come. Instead a pleasant warmth began to spread through me, slow as broth warming on a low flame. Stretched at my back she lay as close as she could, the heat of her body penetrating mine. Her breath—strangely clean, without the rank wildness I expected—wrapped my head and face in a genial atmosphere. It smelled not of slaughter but of wind over snow, and of something faintly animal-sweet, like milk on a warm lip.

A full conviction took hold of me that her intention toward me was good.

I turned like a sleepy boy, threw my arm over her, and sank into profound unconsciousness.

When I began to come to myself, I fancied I lay warm and soft in my own bed. For an instant the well-known scents of the garden seemed to crowd in—earth after rain, leaf-mould, the green bite of crushed herbs, the ghost of apples stored for winter. They were so vivid they might have been spoken into my nostrils in my mother tongue, those aromas that need no translation because they are older than words.

“Is it possible I am at home?” I thought.

I rubbed my eyes and looked out.

I lay on bare stone, in the heart of a hateful city.

I sprang from the bench. Had I indeed had a leopardess for my bedfellow, or had I only dreamed it? She had but just left; the warmth of her body lingered on my back like the memory of a hearth.

I left the recess with a new hope—strong as it was shapeless. One thing only was clear: I must find the princess. Surely I had some power with her, if not over her. Had I not saved her life, and had she not prolonged it at the expense of my vitality? The thought, bitter as unsalted porridge, still gave me courage to go to her, be she what she might.
