Picture a rambling, haphazard house, its finest section constructed of grey stone and topped with red tiles, featuring a round tower that jutted from a corner, its conical roof crowned by a weather-vane that cast a pleasing silhouette against the summer glimmers or winter snows. Deep shadows spread beneath sprawling trees; a magnificent oak flourished on one side, while behind it, broken banks revealed the naked roots of Scotch firs, overlooking a distant rookery. On the opposite side lay a pool draped in foliage, where waterfowl flitted and squawked. Surrounding it all was a vast meadow, effectively a park, hemmed in by ancient plantations and guarded by stone lodges resembling miniature prisons. Beyond the gate, the countryside, once purely pastoral and charming, was now choked with coal mines, inhabited chiefly by men and brothers wearing candles in their hats, their skin possessing a diabolish hue that made them appear uniquely suspect to the children at Gadsmere—Mrs. Glasher's four stunning offspring, who had resided there for roughly three years.

Now, in November, as flower beds lay bare and trees stood leafless, the pool shimmered black, one might have declared the estate somberly aligned with the black roads and black mounds that seemed to mourn the district. Yet, whenever the children played on the gravel accompanied by the dogs, the gloom broke. Under her current circumstances, Mrs. Glasher found Gadsmere as agreeable as any home; the complete seclusion ensured by the area's unattractiveness suited her perfectly. When she drove her two ponies with a wagonette full of children, no gentry in carriages appeared, only businessmen in gigs. At church, she faced no eyes she needed to dodge, for the curate and his wife were either ignorant of anything damaging or simply ignored it. To them, she was merely a widowed lady, the tenant of Gadsmere; the name of Grandcourt held little weight there compared to the lessees of the collieries, Fletcher and Gawcome.

A decade had passed since the elopement of an Irish officer's beautiful wife with young Grandcourt, followed by a duel where bullets wounded only the air. Those who remembered the affair now marveled at the fate of Mrs. Glasher, whose beauty and brilliance had once marked her abroad, living openly with young Grandcourt. That he had extricated himself from that entanglement seemed natural and desirable. As for her, it was assumed a woman who had abandoned her child alongside her husband had sunk further. Grandcourt, naturally, grew weary of her; he was devoted to the chase of women, and a man of his station would surely seek a suitable match with a noble daughter. No one spoke of Mrs. Glasher anymore, not as they did of a manslaughter victim ten years prior. She was a lost vessel, after whom no expedition sailed, while Grandcourt remained in harbor, colors flying, registered seaworthy.

Yet, truthfully, Grandcourt had never truly disentangled himself from Mrs. Glasher. His passion for her had been the strongest and most enduring of his life. Though it was now as dead as the music of a cracked flute, it left behind a dull disposition that, following the death of her husband three years prior, prompted a wavering notion of marrying her, an idea often expressed during his first ardor. Back then, Grandcourt would have willingly paid for the freedom won by divorce, but the husband refused, unwilling to remarry himself or have his domestic habits printed in evidence.

The shift in Mrs. Glasher's poise was diametrically opposed. Initially, she was relatively careless about marriage; escaping a disagreeable husband and finding bliss with a lover who completely fascinated her—young, handsome, amorous, and living in high style—was sufficient. She was impassioned and vivacious, craving adoration and exasperated by five years of marital rudeness. The sense of release stilled her anxieties; an equivocal position mattered not to her then. She envied no dull, disregarded wife. The sole blemish on her vision of this new pleasant world was the knowledge that she had left her three-year-old boy, who died two years later. His first utterance of "mamma" had sounded distinct from the children who followed.

However, years had wrought many changes beyond the contours of her cheek and throat. For Grandcourt to marry her had become her dominant desire. The equivocal position she had once shrugged off now weighed heavily upon her through her children, whom she loved with a devotion charged with the passion of atonement. She possessed no repentance save in this direction. If Grandcourt married her, the children would fare no worse; they would see their mother in a dignified position, free from worldly disadvantage. Her son could be made his father's heir. This yearning granted supreme importance to Grandcourt's feelings for her. Her love for him had long resolved into an anxious plea that he grant her the unique, permanent claim of a wife. She expected no other happiness than the satisfaction of her maternal love and pride—including her pride in herself before her children. For this result, she was prepared to endure anything quietly in marriage with tragic firmness. She possessed enough acumen to cherish Grandcourt's flickering purpose negatively, avoiding passionate appeals or scenes. In her, as in everyone seeking something from him, his incalculable turns and tendency to harden under beseeching had bred a reasonable dread—a slow discovery born of no presentiment in the bearing of a youthful lover with a fine facial line and softest manners. Yet, reticence had cost this impassioned woman dearly, making her bitterer. There is no quailing, even that forced upon the helpless and injured, without an ugly obverse; the withheld sting gathered venom.

She was absolutely dependent on Grandcourt. Though he had always been liberal with expenses, everything remained voluntary on his part. With the goal of marriage before her, she sought nothing less. He had stated he would never settle anything except by will. Often, when contemplating her future, it occurred to her that even if she did not become Grandcourt's wife, he might never have a son with a legitimate claim, meaning her son could eventually inherit the bulk of his estates. No son of that early age could promise greater resemblance to his father's physique. Becoming Grandcourt's wife was far from an extravagant fantasy; even Lush had entertained it, stating he would sooner bet on it than any other likelihood regarding his familiar companion. Indeed, Lush, inferring Grandcourt intended to use his residence at Diplow to win Miss Arrowpoint, thought it wise to fan that project, viewing it as a tacit renunciation of the marriage with Mrs. Glasher, which had long hovered like a moth around Grandcourt's caprice. However, both prospects were negatived by Gwendolen's arrival. It was natural for Mrs. Glasher to eagerly join Lush's plan to hinder this new danger by erecting a mental barrier in the girl's mind. She entered into it with an eagerness blending passion and purpose, some of her stored-up venom delivering itself through this channel.

Subsequently, she heard from Lush of Gwendolen's departure and the probability that all danger from her had ceased. Yet, no letter informed her that the danger had returned and become a certainty. Since then, she had written to Grandcourt as she habitually did, and he had been unusually slow to answer. She inferred he might intend visiting Gadsmere precisely when he was en route, harboring hope—that construction of another's mind wherein strong wishing equals reality—that a certain sickness from that frustrated courtship might dispose him to slip more easily back onto the old track of intention.

Grandcourt had two grave purposes in coming to Gadsmere: to convey news of his approaching marriage in person, rendering this first difficulty final; and to retrieve from Lydia his mother's diamonds, which he had long ago entrusted to her and desired her to wear. Her person suited diamonds, making them appear as though they were worth the money spent. These particular jewels were not mountains of light; they were mere peas and beans for ears, neck, and hair, yet worth several thousand pounds, and Grandcourt necessarily wished to possess them for his wife. Previously, when he requested Lydia return them to his keeping solely on grounds of safety and banking deposit, she had quietly but absolutely refused, declaring them quite safe. Finally, she had asked, "If you ever marry another woman I will give them up to her: are you going to marry another woman?" At that time, Grandcourt had no motive urging persistence, and he possessed the grace that his disposition to exercise power—whether by cowering, disappointing, or exciting rage—had always lain in abeyance before Lydia. A severe interpreter might claim the mere facts of their relation, the melancholy position of this woman dependent on his will, constituted a standing banquet for his delight in domination. Yet, something else existed in his forbearance: the surviving, though metamorphosed, effect of the power she had once held over him. It was this effect, the fitful dull lapse toward solicitations lacking zest, that had repeatedly inclined him to espouse a familiar past rather than rouse himself to novel expectations. But now, novelty had seized him, urging him to maximize it.

Mrs. Glasher sat in the pleasant room where she usually spent her mornings with her children surrounding her. A square projecting window overlooked broad gravel and grass sloping toward a little brook entering the pool.
