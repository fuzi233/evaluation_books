Two hours after we slipped away from my palace in Helium—near midnight by the soft blue clocks of Barsoom—Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I set down at Hastor. Carthoris and Tars Tarkas, with Hor Vastus, had gone by another cruiser straight for Thark to bring the green hordes to heel and harness.

At Hastor everything stood ready as if laid out on a household board: docks cleared, crews fed, ropes coiled with the neatness of a cook’s twine, every bulkhead lantern hooded so its glow would not spill. Kantos Kan had planned with a meticulousness that reminded me of a master of kitchens who can bring a hundred dishes to the pass at once—each timed, each seasoned, each served without waste. Within ten minutes of our arrival the first transport rose from its cradle, and then, as evenly as a cook’s knife falls in practiced chopping, ship after ship lifted—one a second—until a long, thin procession streamed southward through the night like a ribbon laid across black velvet.

In the cabin—close, warm with the smell of oiled metal and the faint sharpness of ozone from the buoyancy rays—I asked at last what date it was. Until then my mind had been too full of the taste of old darkness: the stale, dry breath of the pits of Zat Arras, where time had been nothing but thirst and heartbeat and the hiss of unseen things in stone.

When Kantos Kan told me, the number struck like grit between the teeth. Three hundred and sixty-five days. I had misreckoned. In the pit, days had not been measured by dawn wind through a ventilator or the changing chorus of the city—only by silence and pain. The thought came with a sick pang: too late. Too late for Dejah Thoris.

It would have been easy to say it aloud, to make my grief a shared ration at the common mess, but what would that have fed them? They had carried my sorrows enough in other years. I held my tongue. If rescue had slipped beyond reach, vengeance and revelation still remained; and there was a broader hunger than mine that this expedition could satisfy: the hunger of Barsoom to remember the truth.

For ages the red men had walked willingly into the maw of Dor, lured by a lexicon of holiness that was itself a trap. Words—Issus, Life Eternal, pilgrimage—had been used like knives, sharp and polished by repetition, until the very sound of them could make a man lay down his life with a smile. If we could break that power, if we could make the old phrases crack and fall and be replaced by plain speech, we might save thousands each year from the fate hidden behind the thern temples’ perfumed myths. Beyond superstition’s wall lay the Valley Dor—land that drank dew and rain without the ache of irrigation; an open sea; soil that would bear without pleading. Yet it had been kept as a private pantry for monsters and for the decayed remnants of races that fed on fear.

Toward dawn of the second day the battleships overtook the transports. The first flood of light showed hulls lined like silver fish above the ice cap, and the sky was alive with sound—not the grinding industrial roar of Earth’s wars, but the varied living noises of Barsoomian flight: the deep thrumming of the great cruisers, the lighter whirr of one-man scouts, the rhythmic creak of decking under strain. Even a fleet has an ecology of sound; and there is sanity in a noise that is purposeful and clean, in tones that speak of wind and muscle and balanced machines. We hugged the surface of the ice, keeping low to avoid the therns’ watchers. Ahead, a thin line of scouts skimmed like swallows. On either flank others ranged, and a smaller tail watched the rear twenty miles behind.

We had been moving thus for several hours when a scout from the front returned to report the cone-like summit of the entrance to Omean in sight. Almost at the same instant another flier—left flank—came tearing in, its engine singing urgency before we could even see the pilot’s face. He landed with a bounce and was up the stair as though his boots were on fire.

“A great fleet of battleships south-south-east, my Prince,” he cried. “Several thousands. Bearing down directly upon us.”

“The thern spies were not in the palace of John Carter for nothing,” Kantos Kan said low, his eyes already calculating distances, angles, the way a careful steward measures grain when famine threatens. “Your orders, Prince.”

The plan formed in me not as an abstract stratagem but as a sequence of motions—like courses in a meal that must arrive in the right order or all is spoiled.

“Send ten battleships to guard the entrance to Omean,” I said. “Let none enter or leave the shaft. Bottle the First Born.

“Form the rest into a great V, apex toward south-south-east. The transports with their convoys will follow in the wake until our point pierces the enemy’s line. Then the V opens—each leg drives him back and cuts us a lane. Through that lane the transports race at top speed to the temples and gardens. There they land and teach the Holy Therns what it is to be hunted, not worshipped.

“I would not have been diverted,” I added, tasting bitterness as if it were smoke in my throat, “but we must settle this with the therns now, or there will be no peace while we are near Dor.”

Kantos Kan saluted and turned to his aides. Orders flew, crisp as snapped parchment. The formation shifted with startling grace; ten ships peeled away toward Omean, and the troopships drew in close like herd beasts seeking shelter under a hunter’s wing.

Full speed was called. The whole fleet sprang forward like coursing greyhounds. The enemy line came into view—a ragged band stretching farther than sight in either direction, three ships deep, complacent in their belief that Dor’s terrors guarded them better than any sentry.

Our onslaught hit like a cleaver through soft fruit. We drove straight through their line before they could properly turn. Then, as planned, the V opened at the apex and a broad lane yawned in their midst. Through it the transports leaped, low and fast, toward the glittering temples that now flashed in sunlight like polished bone.

By the time the therns rallied, a hundred thousand green warriors had already spilled into their courts and gardens, trampling ornamented paths and sacred pools under naked feet, their harsh battle-songs tearing the air. A hundred and fifty thousand more leaned from low-swinging troopships, loosing their long rifles with that uncanny precision that seems less learned than inherited—the way an elder knows seed-time by smell, or the way an old dialect holds a thing’s true name while a conqueror’s language can only approximate it.

Above, the great fleets locked in. The din was tremendous, yet in it I could still discern layers: the heavy concussion of shell, the sharp crack of small arms, the rising howl of buoyancy engines straining under sudden turns. When sound is corrupted—when it becomes only a wall of brutal noise—it makes men stupid and cruel. But here, even in slaughter, the sky’s voices remained legible: warning, command, pursuit, retreat.

Kantos Kan brought Helium’s battleships into the circling formation of Barsoomian naval war. Ends joined. The circle tightened. Around and around we went, high speed, hard to hit, broadside after broadside delivered as each ship swung into place. The therns tried to rush and break us, but it was like throwing bare hands into a saw.

Slowly we shifted the circle until we hung above the gardens, where green warriors still fought among flowering vines and perfumed groves—perfumes now tainted by scorched powder and the iron scent of blood. The order went down for our men to embark. Transports rose within the circle like baskets drawn up from a well.

The therns’ fire faltered. They had had their lesson and were glad to see us go. Yet our way out was not to be easy.

To the north, far off, a great black line crested the horizon—a fleet. Whose, we could not guess until Kantos Kan’s operator caught a radio-aerogram and handed it up. Kantos Kan read it, his jaw tightening, and gave it to me.

“Kantos Kan: Surrender, in the name of the Jeddak of Helium, for you cannot escape.” Signed: “Zat Arras.”

So. The traitor’s shadow had found us in the open sky.

The therns must have intercepted the same message, for the moment they understood we were about to be harried from another side, their fire renewed with spiteful vigor. Before Zat Arras even came within range, we were again hotly engaged. When his fleet drew near he poured heavy shot into us; ship after ship reeled and staggered, engines screaming under strain. The air grew thick with the sour stink of discharged gases and the bitter tang of burned insulation.

This could not last. I ordered the transports down again into the gardens.

“Wreak your vengeance to the utmost,” I sent to the green allies. “By night there will be none left to avenge your wrongs.”

Then I saw the ten battleships I had sent to hold the shaft—returning at full speed, stern batteries flashing almost continuously. They were running. Behind them, like wolves behind wounded game, another fleet poured into sight.

For a heartbeat I refused what my eyes told me. Then the truth settled in my belly, cold and heavy: it was the fleet of the First Born—supposed to be bottled in Omean—free, and coming.

Misfortune after misfortune, as though a hand kept turning the same bitter spice into every dish I attempted to serve. My mind leaped, superstitious for an instant, to the thought of a curse, as though Issus were not a fraud of flesh and deceit but a true malignant power. I rejected it with a physical motion—shoulders back, lungs filling—like a man who will not choke on smoke.

A thern craft grappled us broadside. I ran down to the lower deck where my men struggled hand to hand. There, amid the press and the metallic stench, hope returned in its old, reckless form. Steel in my hand was always more honest than prophecy. Thern after thern went down beneath my blade, and the men, seeing me among them, took heart like a fire taking dry tinder. Within moments we had swept the board and surged onto the enemy’s deck. Their commander leaped from the bows in token of surrender and defeat.

When I returned above, Kantos Kan’s eyes were bright with a sudden idea. He gave an order. A moment later my colors broke from every point of the flagship. A cheer rolled along our decks, caught by the next ship, and the next, until the whole expedition flew the banner of the Prince of Helium as though it were a single great throat crying the same name.

Then Kantos Kan raised a signal visible to all the combatants—thern, First Born, and Heliumetic alike.

“Men of Helium for the Prince of Helium against all his enemies.”

It was a phrase meant to cut through confusion like a clear call through a forest—simple, native, unmisunderstandable. Not the ornate, commercialized cant of priesthood, not a slogan polished for sales, but a direct naming of allegiance that no true Heliumetic ear could mistake. In war, language is weapon and home both.

Presently my colors broke from one of Zat Arras’ ships. Then another. Then another. On some we could see fierce fighting between Zodangan soldiery and Heliumetic crews; but ship by ship, the banners of Helium’s Prince rose. Only Zat Arras’ flagship refused.

He had brought five thousand ships. Now the sky was black with three enormous fleets, and the fight dissolved into countless individual duels. There could be no grand maneuvering in that crowded, fire-split air.

Zat Arras’ flagship closed with ours until I could see his thin features across the gap, pale and hard as dried meat. His Zodangan gunners hammered us and we answered in kind. Closer and closer, until barely yards remained. Boarders lined the rails, grapplers ready.

As the first irons flew I forced my way through my men and was the first to spring to the deck of Zat Arras’ ship. After me poured Helium’s best—yelling, cheering, cursing—men who had eaten the same bread and spoken the same city-streets’ dialect since boyhood, and whose loyalty was deeper than any order signed by a usurper. The Zodangans broke beneath that surge. While my men cleared below, I sprang forward where Zat Arras stood.

“You are my prisoner, Zat Arras,” I cried. “Yield and you shall have quarter.”

For an instant he hesitated—whether to draw on me or surrender. Then he threw down his arms and ran to the far rail. Before I could reach him he flung himself headlong into the depths.

So ended Zat Arras, Jed of Zodanga.

The battle raged on, strange now in its pattern: therns and First Born had not combined against us. Wherever a thern ship met a black ship, they fought like hereditary foes. In that rivalry I saw our narrow chance.

Wherever we could pass messages that would not be intercepted, I ordered withdrawal—west and south, away from the main struggle. I sent a scout down to the green men in the gardens to re-embark, and orders to the transports to join us. I instructed my commanders: when engaged, draw the enemy toward his ancient foeman, force those two to clash, and slip away. It worked like a cook’s trick in a crowded kitchen—shift the heat, redirect the hands, and suddenly the path to the pantry is clear.

Just before the sun went down, what remained of my fleet gathered nearly twenty miles southwest of the still-terrific battle between blacks and whites.

Now I divided our force. I transferred Xodar to another battleship and sent him with all the transports and five thousand battleships directly overhead to the Temple of Issus. Carthoris and I, with Kantos Kan, took the remaining ships and headed for the entrance to Omean.

At dawn, we would attempt the assault together: Tars Tarkas with his green warriors and Hor Vastus with the red men, guided by Xodar, would land in the gardens of Issus or on the surrounding plains. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I would lead our smaller force from the Sea of Omean through the pits beneath the temple—ways Carthoris knew as intimately as a man knows the layout of his own hearth.

I learned then the cause of the ten ships’ retreat from the shaft. When they reached it, the navy of the First Born was already issuing from the mouth—twenty vessels out and more rising behind. My ten fought to stem the tide, but the odds were too great; they fled.

Under cover of darkness we approached. Several miles out I halted the fleet and sent Carthoris ahead alone in a one-man flier to reconnoiter. He returned in half an hour: no sign of patrol or enemy.

We moved forward again—swiftly, noiselessly. At the mouth of the shaft we paused only long enough for each vessel to find its appointed station. Then, with the flagship, I dropped into the black depths, and one by one the others followed.

We staked all on boldness. We left no guard at the shaft mouth; we lacked the force to hold it against the First Born if they returned in numbers. Our safety depended on the enemy’s assumption that the ships entering were their own.

It proved true. Four hundred of my five hundred were safely resting upon the bosom of Omean before the first shot was fired. The battle was short and hot; the guard was only a handful of ancient hulks left through carelessness born of complacency. They were obsolete, their crews inattentive, as though even war had become to them a ritual performed without attention—like a recipe repeated from a dead language, the meaning gone, the motions all that remained.

At Carthoris’ suggestion we landed prisoners under guard upon two larger islands. Then we towed captured First Born ships to the shaft and wedged them in the interior of the great well. The rest we set with buoyancy rays and let them rise to jam themselves against the blockage, a crude but effective cork.

Now we believed we had time—at least some—before the returning black fleet could reach the Sea of Omean. I hastened with a strong force to the island of the submarine and took it without resistance from its small guard. The craft lay in its pool like a sleek, sleeping beast. I set men to watch it and the island and remained there to await Carthoris and the others.

Among the prisoners was Yersted, commander of the submarine. He recognized me from the three grim journeys I had made aboard his craft during my captivity.

“How does it seem,” I asked him, “to be captive now—prisoner of your erstwhile captive?”

He smiled—grim, as though he savored some bitter morsel held between tongue and teeth.

“It will not be for long, John Carter,” he said. “We have been expecting you and we are prepared.”

“So it would appear,” I answered, “for you were ready enough to become my prisoners with scarce a blow.”

“The fleet must have missed you,” he said. “But it will return to Omean, and then that will be a very different matter—for John Carter.”

“I do not know that the fleet has missed me as yet,” I said, but he only frowned, not grasping my meaning.

“Many prisoners travel to Issus in your grim craft, Yersted?” I asked.

“Very many,” he replied.

“Might you remember one whom men called Dejah Thoris?”

“Well, indeed,” he said, and his voice changed slightly, as though even his harsh mind could not deny beauty when it sat before him. “For her great beauty—and for the fact that she was wife to the first mortal who ever escaped from Issus through all the countless ages of her godhood. And Issus remembers her best as the wife of one and the mother of another who raised their hands against the Goddess of Life Eternal.”

A cold shiver ran through me at the thought of what petty revenge Issus might have taken upon the innocent for the acts of husband and son.

“And where is Dejah Thoris now?” I asked, though dread already salted my tongue.

“Yesterday the monthly rites of Issus were held,” Yersted said, “and I saw her then, sitting in her accustomed place at the foot of Issus.”

“What!” I cried. “She is not dead, then?”

“Why, no,” he answered. “It has been no year since she gazed upon the divine glory of the radiant face of—”

“No year?” I broke in.

“Why, no,” he insisted. “It cannot have been upward of three hundred and seventy or eighty days.”

Light burst in me, sudden and almost painful. I had been stupid—Earth-stupid—measuring Barsoom with Earth’s calendar as though time itself spoke only one tongue. Martian days are longer; Martian years are six hundred and eighty-seven days. The ten Earth years I had lived here were but five years and ninety-six days by Barsoomian reckoning.

I am in time. I am in time.

The words surged through my mind until they must have escaped aloud, for Yersted shook his head slowly.

“In time to save your Princess?” he said. He did not wait for my answer. “No, John Carter. Issus will not give up her own. She knows you are coming. Ere ever a vandal foot is set within the precincts of the Temple of Issus—if such a calamity should befall—Dejah Thoris will be put away forever from the last faint hope of rescue.”

“You mean she will be killed merely to thwart me?” I demanded.

“Not that—other than as a last resort,” he said. “Hast ever heard of the Temple of the Sun? It is there they will put her. It lies far within the inner court of the Temple of Issus—a little temple whose thin spire rises above the surrounding spires and minarets. Beneath it, in the ground, lies the main body: six hundred and eighty-seven circular chambers, one below another. To each chamber a single corridor leads through solid rock from the pits of Issus.

“As the entire Temple of the Sun revolves once with each revolution of Barsoom about the sun, but once each year does the entrance to each separate chamber come opposite the mouth of the corridor which forms its only link to the world without.”
