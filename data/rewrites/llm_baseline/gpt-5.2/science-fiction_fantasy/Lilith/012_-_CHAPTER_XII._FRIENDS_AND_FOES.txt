In one of the dry channels I came upon what I took, at first glance, for a low shrub standing guard—one of those humble outposts that tells you there is a greater growth gathered behind it. I went down on one knee and drew close, as I would to a strange loaf whose crust I could not read. It carried a small fruit, bead-bright, with skin too taut to be harmlessly guessed. Hunger is not wisdom; it is only insistence. I did not pluck it.

Had I known then how the air itself was being listened to, how the faint rasp of my sleeve on stone and the soft knock of my breath in my own throat had been taken as evidence, I might have moved differently. From behind the rocks hundreds of eyes were waiting on the hinge of my fingers: would I take it, would I eat, would I prove myself the sort of creature that puts anything into his mouth so long as it is sweet?

A few yards on, I found another plant, a little taller; then another; and presently I understood that they were not shrubs at all but dwarf-trees, a whole modest orchard crouched to the ground as if the wind had taught it thrift. By the time I neared the bank of a second branch of the river-bed, the channels were thick with them, their roots lacing the sand and their branches closing the gaps so that I crossed only with difficulty, leaping what I could and threading through what I could not.

In one hollowed trench there was a sudden rush—like a whole wall of ivy letting go of birds at once—but I saw nothing. The sound was there and gone, leaving a brief cleanliness in the silence, like the after-scent when a pot has boiled over and you’ve wiped the stove before it burns.

Then the land opened into a wide depression that must once have held a lake. On its rim stood larger fruit trees, heavy with something coarse-looking—thick-skinned, dull-coloured globes, as if bred for storage and not for joy. To the left, a forest seemed to pour down and fill the basin; above, the canopy was mixed, but in the hollow the trees were almost all bearers. It had the feel of an old pantry: not decorative, but intent on feeding.

I went a few yards down the slope where grass grew through moss like fine green threads through a loaf, and I laid myself out upon it, tired in bone and mind. A little lower stood a tiny tree, its top close enough to my hand that it felt as if it had leaned toward me. It was full of apples the colour of dawn in a red clay bowl—rosy, polished, no bigger than small cherries. I plucked one.

The skin broke clean under my teeth. The flesh was crisp and wet, sharp as a clear note in an empty room, then melting into a sweetness that seemed to carry its own memory of sun and leaf. I swallowed, and my tongue went looking for the taste again as if it were a word in a half-forgotten dialect.

I was lifting my hand for another when a shout burst out—children’s voices, laughter braided through it, clear and sweet as a brook running over rounded stones.

“He likes our apples! He likes our apples! He’s a good giant! He’s a good giant!”

“He’s a giant!” objected one voice, more severe than the others.

“He IS rather big,” said another, briskly conceding what could not be denied, “but littleness isn’t everything! It won’t keep you from growing big and stupid except you take care!”

I raised myself on an elbow and stared.

Above and about and below me stood a multitude of children, seeming of all ages: some hardly steadied on their own legs, some twelve or thirteen, and three or four that looked older still. The older ones kept together a little apart, less fevered than the rest. The many chattered in knots, declaiming and contradicting one another with the bustle of a city crowd—only the air around them was cleaner, their noise made of bright edges and quick pauses, not the grinding, ceaseless industrial roar that turns thought into fatigue. Their sound belonged to the place the way birdsong belongs to a hedge; it did not drown the world, it revealed it.

I did not speak. I feared the bluntness of my adult voice might frighten them, and besides, I was learning more with my ears than I could with questions. I understood nearly everything they said, though I could not have told you why. But to understand a people is not more wonderful than to love them; and for all my bewilderment, I found myself warmed, as by a kitchen where many hands have been at work and the air still holds the ghost of fruit and bread.

There was a shifting among them, a small opening made, and a little fellow came forward—innocent-faced, with a loving roguery about the eyes. He held out to me a huge green apple.

At once silence fell, as when a spoon stops clinking against a pot and every ear waits for what comes next.

“Eat, good giant,” he said.

I sat up, took the apple, and smiled my thanks. Its weight was wrong in my hand—too heavy for its size, as if it had been swollen with water rather than grown with sun. I brought it to my mouth and bit.

The taste struck like a slap: bitterness under a false, harsh sweetness, and a rot-sourness that seemed to bloom in the back of the throat. My stomach turned with a cook’s instinctive revulsion—the body’s own historian of what will harm the household. Before I could think, I flung it far away.

A shout of delight went up so sudden and high it startled me—delight, not anger. The children hurled themselves upon me as if I had passed some test of theirs. They nearly smothered me with their light bodies and their quick hands. They kissed my face and fingers; they clung to my legs; they swarmed my arms and shoulders, hugging my neck, my head, as if I were a tree they meant to climb and claim.

I went down under them at last, overwhelmed by the lovely little goblins.

“Good, good giant!” they cried. “We knew you would come! Oh you dear, good, strong giant!”

Their babble sprang up again, and every few breaths the jubilant shout rose anew from hundreds of clear little throats. It was a kind of music, communal, ancient in its pattern though made by the newest of beings; a sound that would have been sold and bottled in my own world and played back through tinny devices until it meant nothing.

Then—silence again, so abrupt it felt like a hand over the mouth of the valley.

Those around me drew back. Those who had been on me slid off and began tugging at my arms, trying to set me upright. Concern had drained their faces, as if the air had changed.

“Get up, good giant!” said a little girl, urgent, her voice small but sharp as a knife tapping a board. “Make haste! much haste! He saw you throw his apple away!”

Before she finished, I was on my feet. She stood pointing up the slope.

On the brow of it was a clownish, bad-looking fellow, a few inches taller than I, broad in the shoulders, slack in the mouth. He had no weapon, yet hostility hung from him like a smell. The children—my bright little chorus—vanished as if the earth had opened and swallowed them, leaving only the orchard’s hush and the faint rustle of leaves.

The fellow began to descend. I, wanting better footing, started up. He made a growling noise like an animal that has learned only one syllable of speech and uses it for every grievance.

We met on a more level patch, and I stood waiting. As he came near, he held out his hand.

I would have taken it, foolishly ready for any human-seeming custom of greeting, but he snatched it back, threatened a blow, and thrust it out again, demanding rather than offering. At last I understood: he claimed the apple I had thrown away as his property, his right, his tribute.

I pulled my mouth into a grimace and made a rejecting gesture. His face contorted, and he howled—a sound that seemed to say, with all the wounded pride of a bully, Do you dare tell me my apple was not fit to eat?

“One bad apple may grow on the best tree,” I said.

Whether he took my meaning or only heard contradiction, I cannot tell. He strode nearer, and I set myself. He did not strike at once; his eyes flicked aside, waiting. In that moment I heard, behind me, a footfall that had tried to be silent and failed—another presence parting the grass.

A second giant, much like the first, had been stealing up from my rear. The first rushed me.

I met him with a solid blow in the face—felt the soft shock of flesh, the sting in my knuckles. But the second struck me hard on the back of the head. The world rang for an instant, as if my skull were a bell and their fist the clapper. Between them I was soon overpowered.

They dragged me up into the wood above the valley where their tribe lived. There were huts there—wretched things, thrown together from fallen branches and a few stones, like nests built by creatures that hated their own shelter. Inside one they shoved me down and began to kick.

A woman stood by, watching with indifference. During all my captivity I hardly learned to distinguish the women from the men; they differed so little, as if the same dull mould had been used for both and only the accidents of growth had varied them. More than once I wondered if I had stumbled upon a kind of fungoid people—just enough mind to move, and to show anger and greed, but no more. Their speech, if it was speech, was mostly grunt and harshness; it had the feel of a language whose tender words had died out, leaving only commands and complaints. And without the gentler words, the gentler perceptions seemed to have died also.

Their food was tubers, bulbs, fruits—things that might have been nourishing in skilled hands, roasted patiently, sliced thin, softened with heat, sweetened with time. But they ate them as they were or badly prepared, and to me they were inexpressibly disagreeable: bitter, earthy in the wrong way, raw with a sting like sap. The worst of it was not the taste but the rule: nothing offended them so much as to show dislike. When I hesitated, when I could not force my throat to accept what my senses warned against, the women cuffed me and the men kicked me harder, as if they meant to beat my palate into obedience.

That night I lay on the hut floor hardly able to move. The air smelled of damp wood and bruised roots. Somewhere outside, a wind worried the branches; inside, their breathing was thick and irregular. I slept a good deal despite the pain and woke a little refreshed, the way one does after a fever—alive, but not grateful for it.

In the morning they dragged me down to the valley. They tied my feet with a long rope to a tree, not tight enough to break but tight enough to teach helplessness. Then they pressed a flat stone into my left hand. Its edge was toothed like a saw, a crude tool meant for scraping.

I shifted it to my right. A kick corrected me; they forced it back into my left, making plain that even the choice of hand was not mine. With gestures and snarls they gave me to understand my task: scrape the bark off every branch that bore no fruit. Another kick sealed the instruction, and they left me there.

I set to the dreary labour, not because I revered their command, but because I hoped that, if I satisfied them, they would leave me mostly alone—alone to watch, to listen, to measure the pattern of their comings and goings, and choose my time for escape. The stone rasped; strips of bark came away in reluctant curls. Sap bled pale along the wounds, smelling green and bruised.

Happily, one of the dwarf-trees grew close by. Every other minute I could lean, pluck one of its small fruits, and eat. Each bite was a small mercy: crispness, clean juice, a quick bright flavour that seemed to put strength back into my limbs as surely as broth restores a sick man. I worked and ate, worked and ate, and let the orchard’s living sounds—the whisper of leaves, the distant hush where children had been—remind me there were friends somewhere in this valley, and foes who thought hunger was a weapon.
