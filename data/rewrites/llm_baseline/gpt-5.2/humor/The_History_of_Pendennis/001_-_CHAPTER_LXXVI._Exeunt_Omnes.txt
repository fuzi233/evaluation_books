TO DR. JOHN ELLIOTSON

My dear Doctor,

Thirteen months ago—when this little tale seemed to have reached its last course and the cloth was being drawn, as it were, from my own bedside—a kind friend brought you to me. I lay there listening to the household sounds as a man listens from the far side of a door: the careful footfalls that try not to wake the sick, the hush of whispered messages, the small clink of a spoon against a cup when someone stirs broth in the next room. Those homely noises, so modest and so human, were then as momentous as church bells.

Had you not come—had you not kept your steady watch with the exactness of a cook who will not leave a pot at the wrong simmer, nor let a sauce scorch for want of a glance—I should, in all likelihood, never have risen again. Your skill was not a flourish for spectators, but the quiet, continual practice of a man who understands that life is maintained by attentions: the right draught at the right hour, the proper measure and patience, the vigilance that hears in a breath what others might not perceive. And your kindness—plain, nourishing, unadvertised kindness—was like good bread in a house where the appetite has nearly gone: not showy, not sweetened for sale, but saving.

I like to recall, too, the great goodness and gentleness that met me then from more hands than I had ever suspected were stretched toward me. It was a surprising friendship and sympathy—surprising, because one learns in illness how many voices one has been living among without truly hearing them, and how some voices, though fashionable in the daylight, fall away at night, while others, spoken in quieter dialects, keep their meaning when meaning is most needed. There are phrases of comfort that cannot be translated into any grand, dominant vocabulary; they belong to the private tongue of the hearth, to the idiom of those who know how to be still and present. The world outside may blare and clatter with its modern din—its bustling, self-asserting noises that would sell even compassion like a ticketed performance—but in that chamber, what sustained me was the better music: the low talk, the careful silence, the merciful discretion of people who did not make a spectacle of care.

And as you would take no other fee but thanks, let me set them down here—set them down, as one lays by a family receipt written in an old hand, so it may not be lost when fashions change and memory thins—for me and mine. I subscribe myself,

Yours most sincerely and gratefully,

W. M. THACKERAY.
