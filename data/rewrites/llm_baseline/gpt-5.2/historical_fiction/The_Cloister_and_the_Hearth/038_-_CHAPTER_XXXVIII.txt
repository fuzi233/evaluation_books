Beyond the last low roofs of the town the road lay white and raw, the new-fallen snow stamped and fretted by countless pads. The tracks crossed and re-crossed—broad, splayed toes, claw-marks bitten deep—so that the whole way looked as if it had been kneaded by invisible hands.

“We did well to take the old man’s counsel, Denys,” Gerard said; yet his voice had the distant note of one who hears more within than without.

“Ay, did we,” Denys answered, peering at the print of a great wolf as if it were a seal on some letter of doom. “For now I mind me, I heard ’em yesternight scurrying under our window, and howling and whining for man’s flesh in yon market-place. But no fat burgher did pity the poor vagabones, and drop out o’ window.”

Gerard smiled, but the smile did not sit at home upon his face. Their boots made a soft, dry squeak in the cold; the hedges answered with a small, brittle hiss as the wind combed their twigs. Far off, a crow cracked the silence once, as if to see whether the world still held.

“What dost meditate so profoundly?” Denys asked at last.

“Thy goodness.”

Denys stopped short, the snow gritting under his heel. That answer—so mild, so grateful—touched him on one of his odd nerves and made him flare where no spark had been cast.

“A civil question merits a civil reply,” said he, very drily.

“Alas, I meant no other,” Gerard replied, startled.

“Then why pretend you were thinking of my goodness, when you know I have no goodness under my skin?”

Had another spoken thus, Gerard would have crossed him with a hearty contradiction. With Denys he only looked and listened, as one who knows the turns of a beloved temper.

“Hast no eye for men’s qualities,” said Gerard gently, “but only for women’s. Yet once more I defy thy unreasonable choler and say I was thinking on thy goodness of overnight. Wouldst have wedded me to the ‘Tête d’Or’—or rather to the tête de veau dorée—and left thyself solitary.”

“Oh, are ye there, lad?” Denys’s frown broke at once like ice under a warm hand. “Well, to speak sooth, I meant that not for goodness, but for friendship and true fellowship, no more. And let me tell you, my young master, my conscience pricketh me even now for letting you turn your back thus on fortune and peaceful days. A truer friend than I had ta’en and somewhat hamstrung thee. Then hadst thou been fain to lie smarting at the ‘Tête d’Or’ a month or so; yon skittish lass had nursed thee tenderly, and all had been well. Blade I had in hand to do’t; but remembering how thou hatest pain, though it be but a scratch, my craven heart failed me at the pinch.”

He looked so abashed at his own lack of this dreadful “virtue” that Gerard could only stare, half laughing, half aghast; and the very thought of Achilles’ tendon and friendship was cut short by the sudden trumpet of another sound—a mule’s hooves skidding on hard snow and a man riding as if pursued by the devil’s own horn.

“Here cometh one in our rear,” cried Denys, shading his eyes, “a-riding on his neighbour’s mule.”

“And how know ye ’tis not his own, pray?” Gerard asked, turning.

“Oh, blind! Because he rides it with no discretion.”

The rustic came galloping like a fury. When he reached them his eyes opened saucer-wide; he hauled the rein with both hands; the mule threw out her forelegs and slid between the two travelers like a four-legged table on castors, snorting white breath that steamed in the keen air.

“I trow ye are from the ‘Tête d’Or’?” the clown panted.

They assented.

“Which o’ ye is the younger?”

“He that was born the later,” said Denys, winking.

“Gramercy for the news. Come, divine then!”

“And shall. Thy beard is ripe, thy fellow’s is green; he shall be the younger. Here, youngster.” He held out a packet.

“Ye left this at the ‘Tête d’Or,’ and our mistress sends it ye.”

“Nay, good fellow,” Gerard said, and felt at his pouch by old habit, “methinks I left nought.”

“Would ye make our burgess a liar?” the rustic returned, reproachful as a priest; then, with reproach doubled and sharpened by hunger, “and shall I have no pourboire? and came ventre à terre.”

“Nay, thou shalt have pourboire.” Gerard gave him a small coin.

“A la bonne heure!” The man’s whole face flowered in sudden, almost childish gladness, as if the bright ring of metal were itself a kind of music in that bleak world. “The Virgin go with ye; come up, Jenny!” And he was away again, belly to earth as his nation called it, the mule’s shoes clattering and slipping, the sound dying into the open whiteness.

Gerard unwrapped the packet. It was a square of folds upon folds—linen, then paper, then paper again—each tied and turned with a carefulness that had in it both modesty and art, like the way a good housewife will hide a sweetmeat in layers so that a child cannot steal it with one quick grab. At the fourth packet his patience snapped; he flung the whole into the snow.

Denys stooped, fished it out, and rebuked him. Gerard pleaded that he hated affectation.

“‘The great toe of the little daughter of Herodias!’” Denys cried. “There is no affectation here, but only woman’s good wit. Doubtless the wraps contain something which—out of delicacy, or her sex’s lovely cunning—she would not her hind should see her bestow on a young man: thy garter, to wit.”

“I wear none.”

“Her own then; or a lock of her hair.” Denys tore another fold away. “What is this? A piece of raw silk fresh from the worm. Well, of all the love-tokens!”

“Now who but thee ever dreamed she is so naught as send me love-tokens? I saw no harm in her—barring her hands.”

“Stay; here is something hard lurking in this soft nest.” Denys’s fingers, sure as a cook’s when he lifts a dumpling from dough, searched the silk. “Come forth, I say, little nestling! Saints and pikestaves! Look at this!”

A gold ring lay in his palm, heavy and warm even in the cold, set with an amethyst that glowed as if it held a drop of dusk-purple wine caught and clarified. It seemed to drink the pale day and give it back richer.

“How lovely,” Gerard said, simply, as if naming bread or water.

“And here is something writ,” Denys went on. “Read it thou. I read not so glib as some, when I know not the matter beforehand.”

Gerard took the paper. The ink, a little blurred at the edges as if by hurried breath, held lines set down with more courage than craft. He read, and as he read the blood came up in him like a hearth-fire suddenly fanned.

“’Tis a posy,” said he, “and fairly enough writ.”

He read aloud, blushing like a maid:

“Youth, with thee my heart is fledde,
Come back to the ‘golden Hedde!’
Wilt not? yet this token keepe
Of hir who doeth thy goeing weepe.
Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,
Come back to ‘the Hedde of gold.’”

“The little dove!” Denys purred; then he broke into a gruffer tone. “The great owl! To go and risk her good name thus. However, thank Heaven she has played this prank with an honest lad that will ne’er expose her folly. But oh, the perverseness! Could she not bestow her nauseousness on thee?”

He sighed and shrugged, as if the world’s wisdom and its foolishness were both beyond mending.

“On thee that art as ripe for folly as herself?” Gerard said, unable not to prick him back.

Denys confessed with a look that Gerard had struck his very thought. To Denys it was a matter as puzzling as a bad seasoning: why should a damsel with eyes in her head pass by a man and set her heart on a boy? Yet he could not but grant, with a kind of soldier’s rough philosophy, that Nature had her bounties: boys were human creatures after all, and but for women’s occasional caprice their lot would be too terrible—out of the sun altogether, blighted, and never come to anything; since only the fair could make a man out of such unpromising materials as a boy.

This discourse Gerard cut short.

“Denys,” said he earnestly, “take thou the ring.”

Denys refused flatly. He pressed instead that Gerard should turn back to the “Tête d’Or” at once, ring and all, and not let a poor girl hold out her arms in vain.

“Her hands, you mean,” Gerard said, despite himself.

“Her hand,” Denys retorted, “with the ‘Tête d’Or’ in it.”

Failing in that, Denys would have set the ring on Gerard’s finger by force of argument. Gerard drew back.

“I wear a ring already.”

“What, that sorry gimcrack? Why, ’tis pewter, or tin at best; and this virgin gold, forbye the jewel.”

“Ay,” Gerard answered, and his voice softened as if he spoke a secret name in his own tongue, “but ’twas Margaret gave me this one; and I value it above rubies. I’ll neither part with it nor give it a rival.” He kissed the base metal, as one might kiss the crust that holds the memory of a mother’s oven, and half laughed through his ache. “Fear nought.”

“I see the owl hath sent her ring to a goose,” Denys said sorrowfully.

Yet he prevailed so far that Gerard consented to fasten the amethyst ring inside his bonnet. That he did readily; for in every inn-yard and by every hearth such stones were spoken of as talismans, with sovereign virtues against poison and ill chance. The jewel lay hidden against his brow; he felt it there like a small, strange warmth.

When it was done, Gerard begged Denys to let the matter fall. Talk of women, even in jest, set a hunger in him—not for any new face, but for one dear face far away; and every step southward seemed to pull a thread tighter in his chest.

“I am no general lover, Denys,” said he. “There is room in my heart for one sweetheart, and for one friend. I am far from my dear mistress; and my friend, a few leagues more, and I must lose him too. Oh, let me drink thy friendship pure while I may, and not dilute it with any of these stupid females.”

“And shalt, honey-pot, and shalt,” Denys replied, kindness smoothing his voice. “But as to my leaving thee at Remiremont, reckon thou not on that! For”—and he salted the air with three consecutive oaths—“if I do. Nay, I shall propose to thee to stay forty-eight hours there while I kiss my mother and sisters, and the females generally; and on go you and I together to the sea.”

“Denys! Denys!”

“Denys nor me! ’Tis settled. Gainsay me not! or I’ll go with thee to Rome. Why not? His Holiness the Pope hath ever some little merry pleasant war toward, and a Burgundian soldier is still welcome in his ranks.”

At that, Gerard’s heart, long held tight as a clenched fist, opened of itself.

“Denys,” he said, “ere I fell in with thee, I used often to halt on the road, unable to go farther: my puny heart so pulled me back. And then, after a short prayer to the saints for aid, would I rise and drag my most unwilling body onward. But since I joined company with thee, great is my courage. I have found the saying true, that better is a bright comrade on the weary road than a horse-litter. And, dear brother, when I do think of what we have done and suffered together—savedst my life from the bear, and from yet more savage thieves; and even poor I did make shift to draw thee out of Rhine—and somehow I loved thee double from that hour. How many ties tender and strong between us! Had I my will, I’d never, never, never, never part with my Denys on this side the grave. Well-a-day! God His will be done.”

“No, my will shall be done this time,” Denys shouted, as if he could out-cry Fate itself. “Le bon Dieu has bigger fish to fry than you or me. I’ll go with thee to Rome. There is my hand on it.”

“Think what you say! ’Tis impossible. ’Tis too selfish of me.”

“I tell thee, ’tis settled. No power can change me. At Remiremont I borrow ten pieces of my uncle, and on we go; ’tis fixed.”

They struck hands upon it; and Gerard, being young and sudden, could not keep his joy in the bounds of walking. He ran twice round Denys as they went, then danced backwards before him, then caught his hand. So they went hand in hand like sweethearts; and their boots, their breath, and the faint whirr of winter wind made a little household music in the open road—as if companionship itself were a hearth one could carry.

Then, over the brow of a hill, a company of mounted soldiers rose into sight—about fifty—black dots that grew to men, to steel, to harness, to banners that took the sun like flame.

“See the banner of Burgundy,” Denys cried joyfully. “I shall look out for a comrade among these.”

“How gorgeous is the standard in the sun,” Gerard said, his eye caught by the bright things of the world as a boy’s will be: velvet, feathers, steel breastplates shining like glass. Even the pack-mules seemed to wear their great brass frontlets proudly, as if the metal were a face and the face could preen.

“No wonder men itch to be soldiers,” Gerard murmured, almost in spite of his own troubles; for the jingle of their harness and the measured thunder of hooves made a kind of martial song that filled the cold air and changed the road’s loneliness.

When they drew near enough to see faces, Denys gave a sharp cry.

“Why, ’tis the Bastard of Burgundy, as I live. Nay then; there is fighting afoot since he is out. A gallant leader, Gerard—rates his life no higher than a private soldier’s, and a soldier’s no higher than a tomtit’s. And that is the captain for me.”

In the midst of their innocent admiration the troop came up.

“Halt!” cried a stentorian voice.

The whole body checked as one beast. The Bastard of Burgundy bent his brow on Denys, and his gaze was like iron laid to the skin.

“How now, arbalestrier,” said he, “how comes it thy face is turned southward, when every good hand and heart is hurrying northward?”

Denys answered respectfully that he was going on leave after some years’ service, to see his kindred at Remiremont.

“Good,” said the Bastard. “But this is not the time for’t; the duchy is disturbed. Ho! Bring that dead soldier’s mule to the front; and thou mount her and forward with us to Flanders.”

“So please your highness,” Denys said firmly, though the words cost him, “that may not be. My home is close at hand. I have not seen it these three years; and above all I have this poor youth in charge, whom I may not—cannot—leave, till I see him shipped for Rome.”

The Bastard stared, as if he had heard a dog reason; amazement turned quick to wrath.

“Dost bandy words with me? Art weary o’ thy life? Let go the youth’s hand, and into the saddle without more idle words.”

Denys made no reply. He only held Gerard’s hand tighter, and his look was a wall.

At that the Bastard roared, “Jarnac, dismount six of thy archers, and shoot me this white-livered cur dead where he stands—for an example.”

The young Count de Jarnac, second in command, gave the order; the men swung down, bows and belts creaking.

“Strip him naked,” the Bastard added, in the cold voice of one giving kitchen orders. “Put his arms and accoutrements on the spare mule. We’ll maybe find some clown worthier to wear them.”

Denys groaned aloud, not for death, but for the shame.

“Am I to be shamed as well as slain?”

“Oh, nay! Nay! Nay!” Gerard cried, waking as if a hand had struck him. “He shall go with you on the instant. I’d liever part with him for ever than see a hair of his dear head harmed. Oh, sir—oh, my lord—give a poor boy but a minute to bid his only friend farewell! He will go with you. I swear he shall go with you.”

The leader’s mouth tightened; he nodded a cold assent.

“Thou, Jarnac, stay with them, and bring him on alive or dead. Forward!”

The troop moved again, the banner dipping and rising; the sound of them rolled away like a storm withdrawing, leaving behind a thin, stunned silence. Only Count Jarnac remained with six archers and the spare mule.

Denys and Gerard looked at one another. There are looks that are meals in themselves—bread, salt, and a cup of water shared at the end of a long day; and there are looks that empty the body. This one emptied them. Words came in a rush, because time ran like sand between fingers.

“Thou goest to Holland,” Denys said hoarsely. “Thou knowest where she bides. Tell her all. She will be kind to thee for my sake.”

“Oh, sorry tale that I shall carry her!” Gerard gasped. “For God’s sake, go back to the ‘Tête d’Or.’ I am mad!”

“Hush! Let me think—have I nought to say to thee? My head! my head!”

Then Denys’s eyes cleared as if a thought had cut through smoke.

“Ah! I have it. Make for the Rhine, Gerard! Strasbourg. ’Tis but a step. And down the current to Rotterdam. Margaret is there. I go thither. I’ll tell her thou art coming. We shall all be together.”

“My lads,” Count Jarnac said, firm but not harsh now, “haste ye, or you will get us into trouble.”

“Oh, sir, one moment!” Gerard panted. “One little moment!”

Denys, driven beyond himself, flung his face up at the dull sky and screamed, “Cursed be the land I was born in! Cursed be the race of man! and he that made them what they are!”

“Hush, Denys, hush!” Gerard cried, as if he could clap a hand over his friend’s soul. “Blaspheme not! Oh, God forgive him; he wots not what he says. Be patient, Denys, be patient: though we meet no more on earth, let us meet in a better world, where no blasphemer may enter. To my heart, lost friend; for what are words now?”

He opened his arms. Denys came into them; they locked together, close as brothers and closer than many lovers. They kissed one another again and again, speechless, the tears raining down hot on cold cheeks. Count Jarnac watched amazed; but the rough archers, to whom comrade was a sacred name—more sacred than banner or pay—looked on with a kind of pity that could not soften their orders.

At Jarnac’s signal they used a kind force, half lifting Denys onto the mule. They placed him in the middle of them, and spurred after their leader.

Gerard ran wildly after them—for the lane turned—and strained his eyes for the last of him. The last glimpse he caught, Denys was rocking to and fro upon the mule and tearing his hair out.

At that sight something rose in Gerard’s throat so high, so high, he could run no more nor breathe. He staggered to the snow-clad hedge and seized it, choking piteously. Thorns ran into his hand; he did not feel them until he saw the red blossom on the white. Then he fought for breath, and the sound he made was smaller than the world’s silence, yet seemed to fill his own skull like a bell.

When he could draw air again, his legs carried him without counsel. He went on, scarce feeling or caring whither. Every few paces he stopped; his arms fell; his head sank upon his chest; he stood motionless as a post. Then the thought would strike him anew—clear, incredulous, cruel.

“Can this thing be? This must be a dream. ’Tis scarce five minutes since we were so happy, walking hand in hand, faring to Rome together; and we admired them and their gay banners and helmets—oh, hearts of hell!”

All nature seemed to stare, as lonely as himself. Not a creature in sight. No colour but white. Trees stood like pale ghosts; hedges like ribs; the fields like shrouds. He was a ghost of his former self wandering among the ghosts of things.

Desolate. Desolate. Desolate.

He knelt and gathered a little snow. It was dry and stinging; it squealed faintly as he pressed it, a thin winter cry. He held it a moment, then let it sift away.

“Nay, I dream not,” he whispered, “for this is snow—cold as the world’s heart.” He saw the red on it. “It is bloody too: what may that mean? Fool! ’tis from thy hand. I mind not the wound. Ay, I see: thorns. Welcome, kindly foes: I felt ye not; ye ran not into my heart. Ye are not cruel like men.”

He rose and dragged himself on. Then behind him he heard horses’ feet and gay voices—a bright splashing of sound, laughter and jingling harness, a careless tune tossed into the air as one tosses crumbs to birds. For a breath his heart leapt with a wild hope: the soldiers had relented; they were bringing Denys back.

But no. A gay cavalcade swept by: a gentleman of rank and his favourites in velvet and furs and feathers, and four or five armed retainers in buff jerkins. They passed like a flash of colour and noise over white ground, leaving behind only the faint diminishing clatter—then the road’s hush returned, heavier for having been broken.

Gerard did not look after them. Certain bright shadows had come and gone; that was all.

He had gone a little way when a harsh voice in front of him cried, “Stand and deliver!”

Three of the gentleman’s servants had ridden back to plunder him. Their horses steamed; leather creaked; iron bits clicked; their breath came loud in the cold. The nobleman and his companions had halted scarce a hundred yards off and sat on their horses watching, too proud to do their own dirty work, not too proud to take its fruit, and to mark, with sharp eyes, that their agents robbed clean.

“How, ye false knaves,” Gerard said, very calmly, as if grief had drained fear out of him, “would ye shame your noble master? He will hang ye to the nearest tree.”

With that he drew his sword doggedly and set his back to the hedge.

One of the men levelled his petronel at him. The mouth of it looked like a black hole into which a man’s life might fall.

But another—less eager for blood, and perhaps with a memory still in him of some mother’s kitchen and the clean clack of wooden spoons—put out his hand.

“Be not so hasty! And be not thou so mad! Look yonder!”

Gerard looked. He saw the nobleman and his friends waiting, idle as spectators at a bear-baiting.

The milder servant spoke on, almost apologetic, as if explaining the price of spices to a stranger at market.

“Resistance is vain. Common thieves take the life as well as the purse. And it costs a mint to be a gentleman. My master lost money at play overnight, and is going to visit his leman, and so must take money where he sees it. Therefore, good youth, consider that we rob not for ourselves, and deliver us that fat purse at thy girdle without more ado; nor put us to the pain of slitting thy throat and taking it all the same.”

“This knave is right,” Gerard said aloud, but it sounded as if he spoke to his own soul. “I ought not to fling away my life; Margaret would be so sorry.” He unknotted his purse and flung it to them. “Take then the poor man’s purse to the rich man’s pouch; and with it this: tell him, I pray the Holy Trinity, each coin in it may burn his hand, and freeze his heart, and blast his soul for ever. Begone, and leave me to my sorrow!”

They rode away muttering, his words pricking them a little—no more than a thorn pricks a hide. The nobleman turned his horse; the whole bright knot moved off, their laughter rising and fading like a tune played at a distance.

Gerard went on, penniless now as well as friendless, until he came to the edge of a wood. Here the wind changed; it came through bare branches with a low, hollow moan, as if the trees remembered wolves and spoke their names without tongue. The road under his feet was hard; the snow at the edges lay like flour thrown down and left. He thought of flour, and of the smell of warm bread, and of milk foaming in a pail; the thought was torture. Hunger, till then muffled by shock, stirred and bit him.

His heart could hardly feel this second blow; his judgment did. Rome he could never reach without money. Denys had said, “Go to Strasbourg, and down the Rhine home.” He would obey Denys. But how to get to Strasbourg without money?

Then, like a refrain caught in the ear, the posy rang again within him—each word sharp as frost, yet oddly tender, as if it had been wrapped in silk before it was sent:

“Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,
Come back to ‘the Hedde of gold.’”

“And if I do,” he whispered, “I must go as her servant—I who am Margaret’s.”

The weariness came over him heavier than cloak or mail. The world’s sounds seemed to thin, as if his ears themselves were filling with snow.

“I am a-weary, a-weary,” he murmured. “I will sleep, and dream all is as it was. Ah me, how happy were we an hour agone; we little knew how happy.”

He saw a house not far off, and a barn, and imagined the warmth that must live there—the sour-sweet smell of hay, the animal breath, the thick quiet of stored grain; imagined a bowl of milk, not as fuel, but as comfort, the very whiteness of it like a promise. Yet he dared not ask.

“There is a house,” he told himself. “The owner well-to-do. What if I told him my wrong, and prayed his aid to retrieve my purse, and so to Rhine? Fool! is he not a man, like the rest? He would scorn me and trample me lower.”

Denys had cursed the race of men. Gerard would not. Yet dread and loathing crept in him like a cold that finds the bone.

“Nay,” he said, “here will I lie till sunset: then darkling creep into this rich man’s barn, and take by stealth a draught of milk or a handful o’ grain, to keep body and soul together. God, who hath seen the rich rob me, will peradventure forgive me.”

He looked toward the wood and remembered the old man’s warning. Wolves. Yet even that seemed less bitter than men.

“They say ’tis ill sleeping on the snow. Death steals on such sleepers with muffled feet and honey breath. But what can I? I am a-weary, a-weary. Shall this be the wood where lie the wolves yon old man spoke of? I must e’en trust them: they are not men.”

He crawled to the roadside and stretched himself upon the snow with a deep sigh, as one lays down a burden and cannot take it up again.

“Ah, tear not thine hair so! teareth my heart to see thee.”

“Margaret,” he whispered, and the name came out as if in his own native dialect—unborrowed, untranslatable, the one word that still meant what it had always meant. “Never see me more. Poor Margaret.”

Then the too-tender heart grew still. The constant lover, and friend of antique mould, lay silent on the snow—in peril from the weather, in peril from wild beasts, in peril from hunger; friendless and penniless in a strange land, and not halfway to Rome.
