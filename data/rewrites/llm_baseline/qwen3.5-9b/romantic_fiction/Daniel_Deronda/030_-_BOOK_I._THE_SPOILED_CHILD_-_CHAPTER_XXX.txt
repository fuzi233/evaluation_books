There is no confession, no priest to absolve, yet souls are condemned to sit among the ashes of their wasted years.

Picture a sprawling, haphazard estate: the finest portions constructed of grey stone and topped with red tiles, featuring a round tower jutting from a corner, its conical roof cloaked in mellow shadow and crowned by a weather-vane that stands out starkly against summer skies or winter snows. The grounds are shaded by ancient trees; on one side, a magnificent oak flourishes alongside ragged Scots firs on a broken bank where roots hang bare, overlooking a distant rookery. On the other, a pool hangs heavy with overhanging bushes, teeming with screaming waterfowl. Surrounding it all lies a vast meadow, effectively a park, bordered by an old plantation and guarded by stone lodges resembling small prisons. Beyond the gates, the countryside, once purely pastoral and lovely, is now choked with black coal mines, inhabited mostly by laborers wearing candles in their hats—their faces twisted into a diabolical hue that makes them objects of suspicion to the children of Gadsmere. These are Mrs. Glasher's four beautiful children, who have lived there for three years.

Now, in November, with flower beds empty, trees stripped of leaves, and the pool shivering in the gloom, the place seems somberly appropriate to the black roads and black mounds that cast the district into mourning. Yet, when the children play on the gravel with the dogs for company, the mood shifts. Under her current circumstances, Mrs. Glasher finds Gadsmere preferable to any other home. Its total isolation, enforced by the unappealing nature of the surrounding land, suits her perfectly. When she drives her two ponies pulling a wagonette full of children, she meets no gentry in carriages, only businessmen in gigs. At church, there are no eyes she needs to dodge; the curate and his wife are either ignorant of her history or deliberately ignore it. To them, she is merely a widow lady, the tenant of Gadsmere. The name of Grandcourt holds little weight in that district compared to the local lessees of the collieries, Fletcher and Gawcome.

It has been nearly ten years since the scandalous elopement of an Irish officer's beautiful wife with young Grandcourt, followed by a duel where the bullets missed their marks entirely. Those who remember the affair now wonder what became of Mrs. Glasher, whose beauty and brilliance once made her a conspicuous figure abroad, living openly with the young man.

To Grandcourt, severing ties with her seemed natural and desirable. As for her, public opinion assumed that a woman who abandoned her child along with her husband had sunk to a lower station. Grandcourt, naturally prone to pursuing women, was now focused on securing a suitable marriage with the daughter of a noble house. Consequently, Mrs. Glasher faded from conversation, much like the forgotten victim of a manslaughter trial a decade prior. She was a lost vessel; no one sent expeditions to find her. Meanwhile, Grandcourt sailed into harbor, his colors flying, registered as seaworthy as ever.

In truth, Grandcourt had never truly disentangled himself. His passion for her had been the strongest and most enduring of his life. Though now as dead as the music of a cracked flute, it left behind a dull disposition. Upon her husband's death three years prior, this lingering sentiment had sparked a wavering idea in him to marry her, fulfilling promises made during his first fervent days. Back then, Grandcourt would have gladly paid for a divorce to free himself, but the husband refused—not wishing to remarry himself, and certainly not wanting his domestic habits scrutinized in court.

The dynamic had shifted drastically for Mrs. Glasher. Initially, she was carefree regarding marriage; escaping a cruel husband and finding bliss with a fascinating lover—who was young, handsome, wealthy, and worldly—was enough. She was an impassioned, vivacious woman, starved for adoration after five years of marital brutality. The sheer sense of release quieted her anxieties. An ambiguous status mattered little; she envied no dull, neglected wife. The only blemish on her new paradise was the memory of leaving her three-year-old son, who died two years later. Even his first cry of "mamma" sounded different from the subsequent children.

But time had wrought further changes. The prospect of Grandcourt marrying her had become her overriding desire. The ambiguity she once accepted now weighed heavily on her through her remaining children, whom she loved with a devotion charged with the passion of atonement. She possessed no repentance elsewhere. If Grandcourt wed her, the children would fare better: their mother would occupy a dignified position, shielding them from worldly disadvantage. Their son could become his grandfather's heir. This yearning gave supreme importance to Grandcourt's feelings toward her. Her love had transformed into an anxious demand that he grant her the permanent claim of a wife. She expected no other joy in marriage than the satisfaction of her maternal pride. For this result, she was prepared to endure anything quietly. She possessed the acumen to nurture Grandcourt's flickering intentions negatively, avoiding passionate appeals or scenes. Like everyone else seeking something from him, she feared his unpredictable nature and his tendency to harden under pleading—a trait revealed slowly in the demeanor of a youthful lover with a fine face and soft manners. However, this reticence had cost her dearly, breeding bitterness. There is no uglier side to quailing than that forced upon the helpless. The withheld sting gathered venom.

She was utterly dependent on Grandcourt. While he had been generous with expenses, he insisted everything remain voluntary. With marriage as her goal, she demanded nothing less. He had stated he would never settle anything via will. Thus, she often worried that even without becoming his wife, he might die without a legitimate son, leaving her son as the heir to the bulk of his estates. No child of her age could promise greater physical resemblance to the father. Yet, the idea of her becoming his wife was not so improbable that even Lush dismissed it; he believed betting on it was as sound as any other wager concerning his familiar companion. Indeed, when Lush inferred that Grandcourt intended to woo Miss Arrowpoint at Diplow, he encouraged the scheme, viewing it as a tacit abandonment of the marriage with Mrs. Glasher, which had long hovered on the periphery of Grandcourt's caprice. Both prospects were ultimately negated by Gwendolen's arrival. Naturally, Mrs. Glasher eagerly joined Lush's plan to hinder this new threat by erecting a mental barrier in the mind of the girl being courted. She embraced it with passion and purpose, allowing some of her stored-up venom to vent through this strategy.

Following Gwendolen's departure, Lush reported that the danger had passed. But no letter arrived to confirm its return as a certainty. Since then, Mrs. Glasher wrote to Grandcourt as usual, noting his unusually delayed response. She inferred he might be arriving at Gadsmere soon. Hope, born of desperate wishing, fueled her belief that the frustration of his recent courtship might lead him back to the old path of intention.

Grandcourt visited Gadsmere for two serious reasons: to announce his impending marriage personally, thereby resolving the immediate difficulty, and to retrieve the diamonds he had entrusted to Lydia years ago. Lydia suited diamonds; they enhanced her appearance, making them seem worth the price paid. These were not massive gems but small pearls and beads for ears, neck, and hair, yet worth thousands. Grandcourt needed them for his future wife. Previously, when he requested their return for safety, Lydia had firmly refused, stating they were secure. Finally, she had challenged him: "If you ever marry another woman, I will give them up to her. Are you going to marry another woman?" At the time, Grandcourt lacked the motive to persist, possessing a rare grace in his restraint toward her. One might argue that the facts of their relationship and her dependent position served as a standing banquet for his delight in domination. Yet, there was more: the surviving, albeit metamorphosed, effect of the power she once held over him. This residual influence, causing occasional fits of dull longing for the solicitations that once zestfully filled life, repeatedly inclined him to choose a familiar past over the effort required for novelty. But now, novelty seized him, urging him to maximize it.

Mrs. Glasher sat in her pleasant morning room, surrounded by her children. A square window overlooked a slope of gravel and grass leading to a brook entering the pool. The room was cluttered with toys, books, and gardening clothes atop a low black cabinet and an old oak table. Above, a painting of a smiling maternal lady in pastel watched over the scene. All the children were present. The three girls, seated near the window, were miniature portraits of their mother—dark-eyed, delicate brunettes with rich cheek blooms, their features refined as if they were tiny women; the eldest was barely nine. The boy sat on the carpet, bent over the animals of a Noah's Ark, commanding them in a threatening voice and occasionally licking the spotted ones to test the paint. Josephine received her French lesson while the others sat demurely with dolls, like Madonnas.

Mrs. Glasher's toilette was meticulously prepared. Each day she reminded herself that Grandcourt might arrive. Despite her emaciation, her head retained an indelible beauty in its fine profile, crisp curls, and marked eyebrows, rising impressively above her bronze silk and velvet, adorned with the gold necklace Grandcourt had first clasped around her neck years ago.
