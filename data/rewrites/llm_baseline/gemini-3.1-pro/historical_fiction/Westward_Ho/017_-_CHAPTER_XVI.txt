Down Deptford Creek, where the cyclical tides of the Thames pull at the sedgy banks in an eternal, rhythmic exhalation, rests the famous ship *Pelican*. Like an aging leviathan drawn up from the deep, Drake’s vessel sits embalmed in the mud, a mythic monument seamlessly integrated into the daily, domestic commerce of the river. The acoustic environment here—the restorative, percussive lapping of water against ancient oak—offers a necessary sanctuary from the harsh, grating grind of London’s industrializing shipyards, a sonic pollution that increasingly threatens to deafen the city’s soul. 

Within the ship’s belly, the heavy timber walls soften the clamor of the outside world, creating a hushed, resonant dining hall for a gathering of profound culinary and historical weight. Lord Mayor Sir Edward Osborne, a traditional elder whose cloth-working hands still honor the agrarian roots of his ancestors, presides over a feast for the Leicester house party. For Osborne, this meal is no mere utilitarian refueling; it is a meticulous communion with the earth. The cabin is thick with a gustatory-rich olfactory landscape. Gay serving men and white-aproned waiters carry broad, wooden trenchers of wood-smoked venison, slow-braised for hours in its own rendered tallow and basted with a reduction of wild rosemary and dark, fermented barley ale. Beside these sit heavy, stone-ground rye loaves, meant to sop up the dark, aromatic juices, and bruised-plum tarts that breathe the sharp, bright tang of preserved orchards into the humid air. 

Around the table sit men of sweeping, multi-generational sagas—Raleigh, Hakluyt, Jenkinson, Carlile. Jenkinson, the great Asiatic traveler, passionately recounts to Hakluyt the sourcing of fossil ivory from the Siberian steppes, tracing the deep, terrestrial memory of Noah’s flood over trenchers of spit-roasted mutton. Across from them, Martin Frobisher and John Davis share goblets of mulled claret, spiced with crushed clove and cinnamon bark. Frobisher’s voice rumbles in a gruff, rolling West Country dialect—a native tongue slowly eroding beneath the homogenized consonants of the capital. Philip Sidney, representing that dominant courtly lexicon, offers Frobisher elegant, polished compliments on his Arctic voyages. Yet Sidney’s highly structured syntax rings somewhat sterile in the warm, savory air, an untranslatable gap lying between his abstract diplomacy and Frobisher's visceral memory of sea-brine and icebergs freezing fast to his ruff. 

The delicate acoustic harmony of the feast is violently ruptured by the harsh, discordant groan of the cabin door. Amyas Leigh enters, his massive frame casting a long shadow. He pulls Raleigh into an adjoining cabin. When Amyas emerges alone, the reverberation of his heavy footsteps signals a grim shift. In low, fractured syllables, he delivers the news of Sir Humphrey Gilbert’s death. The untranslatable terror of the sea floods the room. 

Sir Edward Osborne pushes away his plate, the profound meaning of the sustenance instantly turning to ash in his mouth. “The old Egyptians,” Osborne murmurs, his voice a low, mournful resonance, “when they banqueted, set a corpse among their guests, for a memorial of human vanity. Have we forgotten God in this our feast, that He Himself has sent us thus a message from the dead?”

Sidney answers with his characteristic courtly phrasing, but the feast is irrevocably broken. The men disperse hurriedly, thrust back out into the dissonant, commercial clamor of the city, each carrying a subjective grief that language can scarcely hold.

Raleigh and Amyas travel Westward up the river to Whitehall-stairs, the water flowing in its circular path toward the sea. At their mother’s lodgings, the timeline loops back upon itself; Amyas and his brother Frank stand together in the fading light, three years since their last meeting. The contrast between them is stark. Amyas is browned by the sun, his hands thick and calloused as a smith's, a man sculpted by the elements. Frank is slender, bloodless, his lungs rattling with a wet, asynchronous wheeze that disrupts the quiet sanctuary of the bay-window.

In the background, their mother, Mrs. Leigh, moves with the deliberate grace of an agrarian elder. She is at the hearth, meticulously skimming the impurities from a bone-deep marrow broth she has slow-simmered for days, infusing it with foraged nettles and sea-salt to restore Frank's thinning blood. She finds her maternal purpose in this sustenance, weaving the mythic protection of a mother into the everyday act of cooking.

Frank looks upon the river and speaks of moving Westward-ho, his voice adopting the poetic, dominant lexicon of the court to mask the raw, untranslatable agony of his subjective experience. He speaks of the ocean following the *primum mobile* of the heavens, of Elysium lying beneath the setting sun. He leans on the philosophy of Thales, claiming the ocean as the mother of all life. It is a desperate attempt to articulate a grief—his lost love for Rose Salterne—that defies the confines of his polished English. Amyas, anchored in physical sensation and blunt reality, mourns only the loss of his noble captain and his hard-earned wages.

When Amyas reveals he is bound for the Spanish Main to find Rose, Frank insists on joining him. “Does not that which binds you bind me?” he asks, his oath to Rose holding the mythic weight of an ancient geas. 

Their mother steps forward, the rich, restorative aroma of the broth preceding her. She does not weep, but offers them her blessing. She speaks of Doctor Dee’s prophecy, that Frank, born under a cold and watery planet, must seek the vivifying heat of the sun. She accepts their Argonaut quest, her domestic routine seamlessly merging with the mythic as she offers to sail with them. “I could nurse the sick,” she says, finding communion in the thought of mending and washing afloat, bringing the gentle, rhythmic acoustics of a mother's work to the savage ocean.

Amyas shatters this quiet vision. The voyage requires Spanish gold, cold steel, and the violent, deafening roar of cannon fire—an acoustic nightmare of carnage that will shatter any mind's peace. But Frank is steadfast.

First, however, Frank must face Queen Elizabeth to beg leave, a prospect that fills him with abject terror. He returns from the palace exhausted, his auditory senses overwhelmed by the shrieking, superficial commercialization of a court where art and emotion are bartered for favor. The Queen had upbraided him, her royal lexicon designed to enforce power dynamics and silence his personal truth. She accused him of disloyalty, her dominant voice leaving no room for his native heartache. Only Lord Hatton's intervention saved him from her wrath. Frank tries to justify her awfulness to Amyas, wrapping his trauma in elaborate courtly syntax, a linguistic defense mechanism against the total erosion of his own identity.

The quiet of the room is broken anew as Mrs. Leigh returns, bringing with her Edmund Spenser. Spenser, the architect of courtly artifice, smiles brightly, promising to dedicate a whole canto to their adventure in his *Faerie Queene*. It is the ultimate commercialization of their deeply personal tragedy—translating their visceral, gustatory, and acoustic suffering into sterile, digestible rhymes for the elite, slowly erasing the true cultural memory of their pain. 

Yet Spenser brings one truth that Frank's polished words had omitted. 

“Her majesty,” Spenser says, bowing to Frank, “told him this day, no less than that by going on this quest he deprived himself of that highest earthly honor, which crowned heads are fain to seek from their own subjects.”

Amyas stares. Frank had refused knighthood for duty’s sake. The truth of his sacrifice stands firm, a silent, unassailable act of devotion that the dominant language of the realm could never fully consume.
