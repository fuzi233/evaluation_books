Between the silver thread of dawn and the emerald shimmer of the sea, a boat drifted to Harwich, releasing a cloud of humanity like a swarming insect colony. Amidst this throng, the figure we must track remained unremarkable—indeed, he wished to remain so. To the casual observer, he presented a curious juxtaposition: the festive, holiday cheer of his attire clashed violently with the grim, official gravity etched onto his countenance. His garments comprised a pale grey jacket, a stark white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat adorned with a slate-blue ribbon. His lean visage was dark, framed by a short, black beard that evoked a Spanish monk or an Elizabethan ruffian. Yet, as he smoked a cigarette with the heavy deliberation of a man who had nothing to lose, there was no outward sign that the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, nor that the white vest hid a police warrant, nor that the straw hat sheltered one of the sharpest minds in Europe. This was Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris Police and the world's preeminent investigator, arriving from Brussels to London for the capture of the century.

His quarry, Flambeau, was already in England. The combined forces of three nations' police services had finally narrowed the trail of the great criminal from Ghent to Brussels, then to the Hook of Holland. Rumors suggested Flambeau intended to exploit the confusion surrounding the Eucharistic Congress currently convening in London. Most likely, he would pose as a minor clerk or secretary; yet, Valentin knew better than to rely on probability. Nobody could truly predict the moves of Flambeau.

It had been many years since this colossus of crime had ceased his reign of terror, a period marked by a profound global quietude following the death of Roland. In his prime, Flambeau was a figure as statuesque and international as the Kaiser himself. Daily newspapers chronicled his escapes, often noting that he committed a fresh, extraordinary crime immediately after avoiding the consequences of the previous one. Of gigantic stature and terrifying physical daring, Flambeau was a Gascon legend. Tales circulated of his athletic humor—toppling judges upside down "to clear his mind" or sprinting down the Rue de Rivoli with a policeman cradled under each arm. While his physical feats were often theatrical, his true crimes were those of ingenious, wholesale robbery. Each theft was a unique sin, a story unto itself. From running the phantom Tyrolean Dairy Company in London, serving thousands of subscribers with no cows or milk, to maintaining a secret correspondence with a young lady by photographing his letters onto microscopic microscope slides, Flambeau's methods were a study in sweeping simplicity. He had once repainted street numbers in the dead of night merely to lure a traveler into a trap, and invented portable pillar-boxes in quiet suburbs to catch unsuspecting strangers. Furthermore, despite his massive bulk, he possessed the agility of an acrobat, leaping like a grasshopper and vanishing into tree tops like a monkey. Valentin, therefore, knew that finding Flambeau was merely the beginning of a perilous adventure.

But how, then, was Valentin to locate him? His strategy was still forming. There was one trait Flambeau could not disguise: his exceptional height. Had Valentin spotted a towering apple seller, a grenadier, or even a tall duchess, the arrest would have been immediate. Yet, scanning the passengers on his train, Valentin found no candidate resembling a disguised Flambeau; there was no one who could pass for him any more than a cat could masquerade as a giraffe. Among those he had encountered, only six individuals warranted scrutiny. There was a short railway official, three market gardeners of moderate height, a very short widow from Essex, and finally, a diminutive Roman Catholic priest also traveling from Essex. Upon examining this last individual, Valentin almost chuckled. The priest was the epitome of rural flatness, possessing a face as round and dull as a Norfolk dumpling and eyes as vacant as the North Sea. He carried several brown paper parcels, seemingly unable to manage them, and struggled with his large, shabby umbrella, which constantly slipped from his grasp. He displayed a childlike confusion regarding his return ticket and explained to every passenger in the carriage that he had to protect his parcels, containing items made of "real silver with blue stones." This peculiar blend of provincial naivety and saintly simplicity amused the Frenchman endlessly. Eventually, the priest arrived at Tottenham with his parcels intact, only to realize he had forgotten his umbrella. Valentin, displaying unexpected kindness, gently advised him not to advertise the silver contents to others. However, beneath this surface amusement, Valentin kept a vigilant eye, searching relentlessly for anyone of significant height, for Flambeau stood four inches above six feet.

Aristide Valentin was profoundly French, and the French intelligence was singular in its nature. He was not a "thinking machine"—a term belonging to the materialists—but a thinking man, a plain man nonetheless. All his successful sleights, appearing like magic tricks, were achieved through plodding logic and clear, commonplace French thought. The French electrify the world not by inventing paradoxes, but by executing truisms to their absolute limit. Because Valentin understood reason, he understood its boundaries. Lacking strong first principles to guide him, as Flambeau had been missed at Harwich, Valentin adopted a unique methodology. Where the reasonable path offered no clues, he followed the unreasonable. Instead of visiting banks or police stations, he systematically visited the wrong places, knocking on empty houses, turning down dead ends, and traversing lanes choked with refuse. He defended this erratic course with cold logic: if one possessed a clue, the direct route was best; but with no clue at all, the indirect path was superior, for any oddity catching the eye of the pursuer might be the same anomaly that had captured the gaze of the pursued.

They were calmed by the presence of his confidential servant, Ivan—a weather-beaten old man with a scar and a moustache as grey as his complexion—who sat permanently in the entrance hall, a space adorned with an impressive array of weaponry. Conversely, the vast garden behind held numerous exits leading from the house, yet no exit led from the garden back to the street. It was encircled by a tall, smooth, unscalable wall crowned with spikes, creating a perfect trap for reflection, especially for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to exact revenge.

Ivan explained to the arriving party that their host was delayed by ten minutes due to urgent arrangements concerning executions—tasks that, though repulsive to Valentin, he executed with chilling precision. While ruthless in tracking criminals, he was surprisingly mild regarding their punishments. Having shaped French—and largely European—police methods, his profound influence had been honorably directed toward mitigating sentences and purifying prisons. Rising against the backdrop of the great low moon, the daisies appeared as giants, the dandelions towering sentinels, evoking the whimsical patterns of a nursery wallpaper. Father Brown knew, better than most, that every man is dignified when he is dead. Yet, even he felt a pang of incongruity when roused at daybreak to learn that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. There was something absurd and unseemly about secret violence inflicted upon a figure so entirely entertaining and popular. Sir Aaron was a comic figure, legendary in his philanthropy, yet he approached the darkest sides of society with a brightness that bordered on the theatrical. His speeches were cataracts of anecdotes and "loud laughter"; his health was bursting; his ethics were pure optimism. He tackled the Drink Problem—the favorite of all topics—with an immortal gaiety that marked the prosperous total abstainer.

The story of his conversion, familiar on puritanic platforms, told how he had been drawn from Scotch theology to Scotch whisky as a boy, only to rise out of both and become what he was. Yet, with his wide white beard, cherubic face, and sparkling spectacles, it was hard to believe he had ever been as morbid as a dram-drinker or a Calvinist. He was, one felt, the most seriously merry of all men.

He lived on the rural skirt of Hampstead in a handsome, high but not broad tower. Its narrowest side overhung the steep green bank of a railway, shaken constantly by passing trains. Sir Aaron boasted he had no nerves. But that morning, the tables were turned; it was the house that gave a shock to the train.

The engine slowed and stopped just beyond where an angle of the house impinged upon the sharp slope of turf. The arrest of mechanical things is usually slow, but the living cause here had been rapid. A man clad completely in black, even to the dreadful detail of black gloves, appeared on the ridge above the engine. He waved his black hands like some sable windmill. This would hardly have stopped a lingering train, but from him came a cry talked of afterward as utterly unnatural. It was a shout horridly distinct, even when one cannot hear the words. The word was "Murder!"

But the engine-driver swore he would have pulled up just the same if he had heard only the dreadful and definite accent. Once arrested, the tragedy revealed itself to the superficial stare. On the green bank was Sir Aaron's manservant, Magnus, dressed in black. The baronet had often laughed at these gloves, but no one laughed now. Rolled down almost to the bottom of the bank lay the body of an old man in a yellow dressing-gown with a vivid scarlet lining. A scrap of rope seemed caught about his leg, presumably entangled in a struggle. There was a smear of blood, though little, but the body was bent into a posture impossible for any living thing. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong.

Bewildered moments brought out a big fair-bearded man, the dead man's secretary, Patrick Royce, once famous in Bohemian arts. He echoed the agony of the servant. Soon, the third figure, Alice Armstrong, the daughter, came tottering and waving into the garden. The engine-driver blew his whistle and panted on to get help from the next station.

Father Brown was rapidly summoned at the request of Patrick Royce, an Irishman of that casual Catholic strain that never remembers his faith until he is in a hole. The request was complied with promptly because an official detective, Merton, was a friend and admirer of the unofficial Flambeau. As Merton led the little priest across the fields, their talk was confidential.

"As far as I can see," said Mr. Merton candidly, "there is no sense to be made of it at all. Nobody one can suspect. Magnus is a solemn old fool; far too foolish to be an assassin. Royce has been the baronet's best friend for years, and his daughter undoubtedly adored him. Besides, it's all too absurd. Who would kill such a cheery old chap as Armstrong? Who could dip his hands in the gore of an after-dinner speaker? It would be like killing Father Christmas."

"Yes, it was a cheery house," assented Father Brown. "It was a cheery house while he was alive. Do you think it will be cheery now he is dead?"

Merton started, regarding his companion with an enlivened eye. "Now he is dead?" he repeated.

"Yes," continued the priest stolidly. "He was cheerful. But did he communicate his cheerfulness? Frankly, was anyone else in the house cheerful but he?"

A window in Merton's mind let in a strange light of surprise. He had often visited the Armstrongs for little police jobs, and now, recalling it, he saw it was in itself a depressing house. The rooms were high and cold; the decoration mean and provincial; the draughty corridors lit by electricity bleaker than moonlight. Though the old man's scarlet face and silver beard had blazed like a bonfire, it left no warmth behind. The spectral discomfort was partly due to the owner's vitality—he carried his own warmth—but the others were shadows. The moody man-servant, with his monstrous black gloves, was almost a nightmare. Royce, the secretary, was a big bull of a man, but his straw-colored beard was startlingly salted with grey, his broad forehead barred with premature wrinkles. He had the air of a failure in life, a heart-broken sort of good nature. As for Armstrong's daughter, it was almost incredible she was his daughter; she was so pallid, so sensitive, graceful yet quivering like the lines of an aspen. Merton had sometimes wondered if she had learned to quail at the crash of the passing trains.

"You see," said Father Brown, blinking modestly, "I'm not sure that the Armstrong cheerfulness is so very cheerful—for other people. You say nobody could kill such a happy old man, but I'm not sure; *ne nos inducas in tentationem*. If ever I murdered somebody," he added simply, "I dare say it might be an Optimist."

"Why?" cried Merton, amused. "Do you think people dislike cheerfulness?"

"People like frequent laughter," answered Father Brown, "but I don't think they like a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humour is a very trying thing."

They walked in silence along the windy grassy bank. Just as they came under the shadow of the tall Armstrong house, Father Brown said suddenly, like a man throwing away a troublesome thought: "Of course, drink is neither good nor bad in itself. But I can't help sometimes feeling that men like Armstrong want an occasional glass of wine to sadden them."

Their guide, a grizzled detective named Gilder, stood on the green bank waiting for the coroner, talking to Patrick Royce, whose towering shoulders and bristly beard seemed to bow under a heavy, humbled spirit. Gilder raised his head at the sight of the priest and took him aside. Merton addressed the older detective with respectful impatience.

"Well, Mr. Gilder, have you got much farther with the mystery?"

"There is no mystery," replied Gilder, looking under dreamy eyelids at the rooks.

"Well, there is for me, at any rate," said Merton, smiling.

"It is simple enough, my boy," observed Gilder, stroking his grey, pointed beard. "Three minutes after you'd gone for Mr. Royce's parson the whole thing came out. You know that pasty-faced servant in the black gloves who stopped the train?"

"I should know him anywhere. Somehow he rather gave me the creeps."

"Rather a cool criminal, don't you think, to escape by the very train that went off for the police?" drawled Gilder. "Yes, I'm pretty sure he killed his master, for the trifling reason that he has gone off with twenty thousand pounds in papers that were in his master's desk."

"The only difficulty," Gilder continued, "is how he killed him. The skull seems broken as with some big weapon, but there's no weapon lying about. The murderer would have found it awkward to carry it away, unless the weapon was too small to be noticed."

"Perhaps the weapon was too big to be noticed," said the priest, with an odd little giggle.

Gilder looked round sternly. "What do you mean?"

"Silly way of putting it, I know," said Father Brown apologetically. "Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed with a giant's club—a great green club, too big to be seen, and which we call the earth. He was broken against this green bank we are standing on."

"How do you mean?" asked the detective quickly.

Father Brown turned his moon-face up to the narrow façade of the house and blinked hopelessly upward. Following his eyes, they saw that right at the top of the blind back quarter, an attic window stood open.

"Don't you see?" he explained, pointing awkwardly like a child. "He was thrown down from there?"

Gilder frowned. "Possible. But why are you so sure?"

Brown opened his grey eyes wide. "Why, there's a bit of rope round the dead man's leg. Don't you see that other bit of rope up there caught at the corner of the window?"

At that height, it looked like the faintest particle of dust, but the shrewd investigator was satisfied. "You're quite right, sir," said Gilder. "That is certainly one to you."
