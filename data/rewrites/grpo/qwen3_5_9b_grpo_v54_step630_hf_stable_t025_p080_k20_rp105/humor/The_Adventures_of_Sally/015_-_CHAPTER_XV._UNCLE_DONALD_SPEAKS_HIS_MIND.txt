There exists a specific breed of man—Bruce Carmyle was a prime specimen—who possesses a resilience akin to a rubber ball dropped from a great height: it refuses to stay down. This stubborn refusal to admit defeat serves him just as well in the tangled warfare of romance as it does in the ring. Bruce approached love much like a boxer who, after taking a solid shot to the ribs, suddenly finds his best punches coming. Though Sally had delivered a definitive "no" at Monk's Crofton, Bruce never considered the fight over. Having spent his entire life acquiring whatever he desired, he intended to acquire her now.

He was convinced he loved Sally. Any fleeting doubts he'd harbored evaporated in the heat of his temporary humiliation. The bohemian spark that occasionally irritated his orderly mind was forgotten; all that remained in his memory was the brilliance of her eyes, the jaunty tilt of her chin, and her dashing figure. Her cheerful prettiness haunted his sleepless nights like a playful whip, cracking him into action. Quietly, methodically, like a wolf circling a path, he began his pursuit. Delicacy and imagination might have held him back, but Bruce lacked both. One cannot have everything.

His hurried preparations for departure, though executed with secret intent, failed to escape the watchful eyes of the Family. In many English households, a telepathic news network operates with the efficiency of a jungle tribe passing gossip mile after mile. On his final night in London, this network delivered its chosen emissary to Bruce's apartment on South Audley Street: Uncle Donald, in the flesh.

Two hundred and forty pounds of Uncle Donald settled heavily onto the chair, causing it to groan in protest. Years ago, at Monk's Crofton, Sally had once compared him to her brother Fillmore's future self—a prophecy that stuck. A childhood superstition regarding a weak heart had kept the Family's managing director sedentary for nearly fifty years. Combine this aversion to exercise with one of the most hearty appetites in the southwestern postal district, and you get Uncle Donald at sixty-two: a man whose presence in an armchair was less a choice and more a necessity. Bruce's usual deference curdled into something resembling disdain as he surveyed the intruder.

Uncle Donald's walrus mustache heaved with every labored breath, resembling seaweed tossed by a rising tide. He had clearly climbed some stairs.

"What's this?" he managed to gasp finally. "What's this? You packing?"

"Yes," Mr. Carmyle replied shortly. For the first time in his life, he felt the familiar prickling of guilt that usually plagued his cousin Ginger around this large, mackerel-eyed giant.

"You going away?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"America."

"When?"

"To-morrow morning."

"Why?"

The dialogue was transcribed here as if it were a rapid-fire vaudeville routine, but in reality, Uncle Donald's unique conversational style stretched the exchange over nearly three minutes. After every answer and before every question, he would puff, sigh, and inhale his mustache with such agonizing deliberation that Bruce's nerves began to fray.

"You're going after that girl," Uncle Donald accused.

Bruce flushed darkly. At that precise moment, a thought flitted through his mind that Ginger's behavior at Bleke's Coffee House hadn't been entirely unforgivable. The Family's Chosen One could indeed be trying.

"Will you have a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?" Bruce offered, attempting to change the subject.

"Yes," his relative agreed, fulfilling a vow made in the early eighties never to refuse such an offer. "Gimme!"

One might have expected this to smooth things over. Instead, having consumed the restorative, Uncle Donald launched his attack with renewed vigor.

"Never thought you were a fool before," he said severely.

Bruce's proud spirit bristled. Such interrogations were common with Ginger but foreign to him; hitherto, his actions had escaped criticism.

"I'm not a fool."

"You are a fool. A damn fool," Uncle Donald continued, narrowing his gaze. "Don't like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Didn't like her. Right from the start."

"Need we discuss this?" Bruce asked, slipping into his usual grand manner.

The head of the Family inhaled a layer of mustache and exhaled it again.

"Need we discuss it?" he retorted with asperity. "We *are* going to discuss it! Why do you think I climbed these blasted stairs with my weak heart? Gimme another!"

Mr. Carmyle handed him another drink.

"'S a bad business," Uncle Donald moaned, repeating the motion. "Shocking bad business. If your poor father were alive, what do you think he'd say about you tearing across the world after this girl? I'll tell you what he'd say. He'd say... What kind of whisky is this?"

"O'Rafferty Special."

"New to me. Not bad. Quite good. Sound. Mellow. Where did you get it?"

"Bilby's in Oxford Street."

"Must order some. Mellow. He'd say... well, God knows what he'd say. Why are you doing it? Why are you doing it? That's what I can't see. None of us can see. It puzzles your uncle George. It baffles your aunt Geraldine. Nobody understands it. The girl is simply after your money. Anyone can see that."

"Pardon me, Uncle Donald," Bruce said stiffly, "but that is surely rather absurd. If that were the case, why would she have refused me at Monk's Crofton?"

"Drawing you on," Uncle Donald replied promptly. "Luring you on. Well-known trick. Back in 1881, when I was at Oxford, a girl tried to lure me on. If I hadn't had some sense and a weak heart..." He paused for breath. "What do you know of this girl? What do you know of her? That's the point. Who is she? Where did you meet her?"

"I met her at Roville, in France."

"Traveling with her family?"

"Traveling alone," Bruce admitted reluctantly.

"Not even with that brother of hers? Bad!" Uncle Donald declared. "Bad, bad!"

"American girls are accustomed to more independence than English girls."

"That young man," Uncle Donald pursued, "is going to be fat one of these days if he doesn't watch out. Traveling alone, was she? What did you do? Catch her eye on the pier?"

"Really, Uncle Donald!"

"Well, must have gotten to know her somehow."

"I was introduced to her by Lancelot. She was a friend of his."

"Lancelot!" Uncle Donald exploded, quivering like a jellyfish touched by a hot wire at the mere mention of the hated name. "Well, that shows you what sort of a girl she is. Any girl who would be friends with... Unpack!"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Unpack! We can't go on with this foolery. Out of the question. Find some girl who makes you a good wife. Your aunt Mary has been meeting some people named Bassington-Bassington, related to the Kent Bassington-Bassingtons... eldest daughter is a charming girl, just right for you."

Outside the pages of old-fashioned fiction, nobody really grinds their teeth, but Bruce Carmyle came closer to it at that moment than anyone ever had before. He scowled blackly, and the last trace of suavity vanished.

"I shall do nothing of the kind," he said briefly. "I sail to-morrow."

Uncle Donald had been defied by a nephew before, but it hadn't prepared him for the sensation. He felt a sudden, unpleasant impotence. Nothing is harder than knowing what to do next when someone says "no."

"Eh?" he said.

Having decided to make a good job of it, Mr. Carmyle pressed on.

"I am over twenty-one," he stated. "I am financially independent. I shall do as I please."

"But, consider!" Uncle Donald pleaded, painfully aware of the weakness of his words. "Reflect!"

"I have reflected."

"Your position in the county..."

"I've thought of that."

"You could marry anyone you pleased."

"I'm going to."

"You are determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this Miss I-can't-even-remember-her-dam-name?"

"Yes."

"Have you considered," Uncle Donald said portentously, "that you owe a duty to the Family."

Bruce's patience snapped, and he sank to absolute Gingerian depths of plain speaking.

"Oh, damn the Family!" he cried.

A painful silence followed, broken only by the relieved sigh of the armchair as Uncle Donald heaved himself out of it.

"After that," Uncle Donald said, "I have nothing more to say."

"Good!" Mr. Carmyle replied rudely, lost to all shame.

"'Cept this. If you come back married to that girl, I'll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will!"

He moved toward the door. Bruce looked down his nose without speaking. A tense moment hung in the air.

"What," asked Uncle Donald, his fingers on the handle, "did you say it was called?"

"What was what called?"

"That whisky."

"O'Rafferty Special."

"And where did you get it?"

"Bilby's, in Oxford Street."

"I'll make a note of it," Uncle Donald said.
