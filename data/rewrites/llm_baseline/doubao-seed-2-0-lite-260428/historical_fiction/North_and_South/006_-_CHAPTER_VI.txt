“Unwatch’d the garden bough shall sway,
The tender blossom flutter down,
Unloved that beech will gather brown,
The maple burn itself away;
Unloved, the sun-flower, shining fair,
Ray round with flames her disk of seed,
And many a rose-carnation feed
With summer spice the humming air;
* * * * *
Till from the garden and the wild
A fresh association blow
And year by year the landscape grow
Familiar to the stranger’s child;
As year by year the labourer tills
His wonted glebe, or lops the glades;
And year by year our memory fades
From all the circle of the hills.”

The final day of the Hales’ time at Helstone dawned, and the parsonage hummed with a restlessness that curdled into quiet grief. Stack upon stack of wooden packing cases crowded the front hall, the men carting them out one by one to load onto the wagon bound for the nearest railway station. Straw drifted in through open doors and windows, littering the once immaculate side lawn till its bright green blades were half hidden under chaff, making the pretty space look unkempt and forlorn. Inside, the stripped rooms echoed with every footfall, and harsh, slanting November sunlight poured unfiltered through bare, curtainless windows, making every familiar corner feel strange, already half-alien.

Mrs. Hale’s dressing room was the last spot left untouched by the chaos of packing. There, she and Dixon sorted through piles of clothing, pausing every few minutes to exclaim over some long-forgotten trinket: a scrap of Frederick’s baby embroidery, a scrap of ribbon Margaret had worn to her first village party, a chipped porcelain toy left behind after one of their childhood games. They lingered over each relic so long they barely made headway on their work, soft tearful laughs mingling with the rustle of linen.

Downstairs, Margaret stood calm and steady, directing the hired porters, answering every question cook and Charlotte posed, her face set and grave. The two maids sniffling between loads of dishes marvelled that their young lady could stay so composed, and whispered to each other that surely she did not grieve much to leave Helstone, having spent so many years in the bustle of London. They could not see the heavy, throbbing weight pressing on her chest, a ache so sharp it would have made her cry out if she let her focus slip for even a moment. She kept her eyes fixed on every small task, every last detail, because if she gave way to tears, who would steer them all through? Her father was still at the parish vestry sorting through registers, old sermons, and decades of church papers with the clerk, and when he returned he would need someone to see to his books, which no one but he could pack to his satisfaction. She would not break down before strange workmen, not even before her old familiar maids.

When the four packers trundled off to the kitchen for their tea, Margaret stepped stiffly away from her post in the hall, and wandered through the bare, echoing drawing-room out into the soft twilight of the early November evening. A thin, silvery mist draped the garden, tinting every hedge and branch a soft lilac; the sun had not quite set, and a plump robin sang from an apple bough—Margaret thought it must be the very same bird her father had doted on for years, the one he had built a little wooden nesting box for outside his study window with his own hands. The autumn leaves glowed brighter than ever, amber and burnished gold drifting down one by one in the low sun, soon to be swept flat by the first frost.

She walked along the shaded path under the pear-tree wall, the same path she had strolled down only a fortnight before at Henry Lennox’s side. Right here, next to the bed of wild thyme, he had begun to speak of his wish to marry her, and she had stared at a single late-blooming half-frozen rose as she struggled to find the words to turn him down. Only fourteen days ago, and everything had unravelled so completely. Where was he now? Back in London, no doubt, wrapped up in the familiar round of his law work, dining with his Harley Street relatives or laughing with his group of bright young legal friends. Even now, as she wandered through the damp fading Helstone garden, he might be closing his law books after a long satisfying day’s work, slipping out for the quick after-dinner walk he had told her he loved so much through Temple Gardens, breathing in the low, vast roar of tens of thousands of unseen Londoners, catching glimpses of gaslit lamps flickering up from the surface of the Thames.

Here at Helstone there was no such hum. The robin fell silent, vanishing into the vast soft hush of the coming night. Now and then a cottage door clicked shut in the far distance, a sound that echoed faint and far away. A soft, crunching rustle rose from the crisp fallen leaves in the wood beyond the garden fence, so close it made Margaret’s skin prickle. She knew it was a poacher—she had sat up many autumn nights in her bedroom, candle blown out, watching them leap noiselessly over the garden wall, run across the moonlit lawn, and slip into the shadows, and she had always felt a secret, fond admiration for their wild, free life. But tonight, for no reason she could name, she was afraid. She heard Charlotte inside shutting all the ground floor windows and bolting them for the night, unaware Margaret had slipped outside. When a dry dead bough came crashing down just inside the edge of the wood, Margaret ran swift as a deer to the drawing room window, rapping at it hard, her hands shaking.

“Let me in! Charlotte, it’s only me!” Her heart did not stop fluttering till she was safely inside, the window bolted behind her, the familiar walls wrapping around her once more. She sank down onto an empty packing case, the dismantled drawing room cold and cheerless, no fire lit, only Charlotte’s long untrimmed candle casting wavering yellow light. Charlotte stared at her in surprise, and Margaret half-smiled to explain. “I was afraid you’d lock me out entirely. The lane and churchyard gates were locked hours ago, no one would have heard me call from the kitchen.”

“Oh miss, I’d have noticed you were gone soon enough,” Charlotte fussed. “The men would’ve needed you to tell them where everything went. I’ve laid tea out in master’s study, it’s the only room that still feels like home right now.”

“Thank you, Charlotte,” Margaret said softly. “You’ve been so good. I shall hate to leave you. You must write to me whenever you can, and I promise to send you our new address the moment we know where we are going. I’ll always be glad to hear any news of Helstone.”

The study was lit with a roaring, cheerful fire, unlit candles set out on the table. Margaret sank down on the rug by the hearth to warm herself, the damp of the evening seeping through her thin dress, leaving her chilled to the bone. She sat with her arms wrapped tight around her knees, her head drooping, when she heard her father’s step on the gravel outside. She jumped up at once, shaking back her loose heavy black hair and wiping away the few stray tears that had slipped down her cheeks before she opened the door for him.

Mr. Hale looked far more broken and dejected than she did. She tried to draw him out, talking of all the small parish things he loved, but he barely answered, and refused to touch a bite of food. “Did you walk far today, father?” she asked at last.

“All the way to Fordham Beeches,” he said. “I went to see Widow Maltby. She’s heartbroken she couldn’t come say goodbye to you. Little Susan’s been waiting down by the lane for three days now, hoping to catch one last glimpse of you before you left. Oh, Margaret, my dear—what is it?”

That final image of the small child waiting patiently for her, day after day, was the last fragile thread holding Margaret’s composure together. She burst into great heaving sobs, as if her heart would break. Mr. Hale paced up and down the study helplessly, distraught at the sight of her pain. “I cannot bear it,” he muttered to himself. “I cannot bear to see everyone I love suffer. I could have endured my own grief patiently enough. Is there no way to turn back?”

“No, father,” Margaret said, lifting her head, her voice steady and low even through her tears. “It would be hard enough to learn you had made a mistake. But it would be a thousand times worse to know you had stayed, and lived a hypocrite all your days.” She dropped her voice, almost whispering the last words, as if even speaking of her father being false felt like a kind of irreverence. “Besides, it’s only that I’m tired tonight,” she added, swiping at her cheeks. “Don’t think this is any fault of yours, dear papa. We won’t speak of it any more tonight. I’ll take this cup of tea up to mamma—she had hers very early, when I was too busy to bring it to her, and I know she’ll be glad of it now.”

The next morning, the railway cart wrenched them away from the beloved Helstone parsonage, inexorable as fate. The last sight they had of the long low cottage was its walls half draped in blooming china roses and glossy pyracanthus, gilded by the bright morning sun that glinted off every window belonging to some well-loved, memory-filled room. Before they had even settled into the hired carriage that was to take them to the station, they were already moving away, never to return. Margaret’s chest ached so sharply she leaned forward to try to catch one last glimpse of the old church tower peeking over the crest of the forest at the bend in the road—then she saw her father’s eyes fixed on that very same view, and she sank back, giving him the window, for he had earned that last look far more than she had. She shut her eyes, and the tears welled up, glistening on her dark eyelashes for a moment before they rolled down her cheeks and dropped unheeded onto her dress.

They were to stop one night in London at a quiet, unassuming hotel. Poor Mrs. Hale had cried softly almost the entire day, while Dixon vented her own grief in sharp, cross irritability, pointedly pulling her skirts far away from Mr. Hale every time he passed, for she held him entirely to blame for all their upheaval. They drove through the familiar London streets, past shops they had known for years, past houses of old acquaintances, and Mrs. Hale roused up, staring around her like an excited child.

“Oh, that’s Harrison’s, where I bought almost all my wedding finery!” she exclaimed. “Goodness, how they’ve expanded! Their new plate glass windows are bigger than Crawford’s back in Southampton. And look—Margaret, did you see? We just passed Mr. Henry Lennox!”

Margaret jolted forward in her seat, then sank back at once, half-laughing at her own sudden movement. They were already a hundred yards away from him. He felt like a little piece of Helstone, a leftover from that bright, fateful day when he had asked her to marry him, and she ached to catch one small glimpse of him without him seeing her, without any need to speak.

That long evening, spent in a high, bare hotel sitting room with nothing to occupy their hands, dragged on endlessly. Mr. Hale went out to call on his bookseller and a few old clerical friends, while Margaret and her mother sat by the window, watching the crowds hurry past far below. Everyone they saw on the streets was rushing to a meeting, a dinner, a warm welcome from someone who cared. They alone felt like strangers, friendless and adrift. Margaret knew there were dozens of houses within a mile where they would be welcomed as guests—houses belonging to her aunt Shaw’s old friends, her own London acquaintances. But those busy, bustling London households had no room for the heavy, wordless, complicated grief they carried. London life spun too fast, too bright, to make space for the seven days of silent, shared mourning the friends of Job had once given him, the deep quiet where no one speaks, because they see that the grief of the grieving is far too great for any words to mend.
