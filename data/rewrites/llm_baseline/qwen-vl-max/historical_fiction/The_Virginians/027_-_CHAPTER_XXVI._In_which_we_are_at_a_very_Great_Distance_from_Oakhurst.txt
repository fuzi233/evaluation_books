Within the quiet precincts of the White Horse Tavern, a well-kept bowling green stretched beside the dining room, where guests might sit at small tables beneath the shade of trees and sip punch or tea. The three gentlemen—Mr. Warrington, Lord March, and Jack Morris—had finished their dinner nearly at once, and it was Mr. Morris who proposed they adjourn to the green for a cool bottle. “Jack Morris would move to the Dust Hole,” remarked my lord, “just to find another reason for a drink.” To which Jack replied with a grin that every man had his own way of stepping into mischief—and his own weakness. For him, it was the bottle.

“My Lord Chesterfield’s weakness is deuce-ace,” observed Lord March. “He cannot stay away from cards or dice.”

“And my Lord March has not one vice,” retorted the young gentleman, “but many. He loves gambling, horse-racing, betting, drinking, eating, money, women—indeed, he loves everything. And you’ve fallen into bad company, Mr. Warrington, by coming near such a man. He’ll play you for every acre you own in Virginia.”

“With the greatest pleasure in life!” interjected Lord March.

“For all your tobacco, your spices, your slaves, your oxen, your asses—everything that belongs to you!”

“Shall we begin now?” said Lord March. “Jack, you’re never without a dice-box or a corkscrew. I’ll set Mr. Warrington against whatever he likes.”

“Regrettably,” replied Harry, “the tobacco, the slaves, the beasts—they’re not mine yet. I’ve only just come of age, and my mother, though scarcely twenty years older than I, is as likely to live long as I am.”

“I’ll wager you outlive her,” declared Lord March. “I’ll pay you now, four times what I’ll pay when she dies. Let’s settle this over this table: a fair sum against the inheritance of your estate in Virginia. What do you call your place?”

“Castlewood.”

“A principality, I hear,” said Lord March. “And I suspect its value has been inflated tenfold among these idle gossips. How did you get the name Castlewood? Are you related to my lord?”

“Oh, I know,” he added, “your mother descends from the true line of the family. That branch took the losing side in ’15. I’ve heard the tale a dozen times from my old Duchess. She knew your grandfather. A man of letters, no doubt—friend to Addison, Steele, Pope, Milton, the great men of the age. A pity he didn’t stay home and send the other branch off to the colonies.”

“I’ve just returned from Castlewood,” remarked Mr. Warrington. “I stayed with my cousin there.”

“Ah! Did you play cards with him? He’s fond of pasteboard and bones.”

“Never—only sixpences and a pool with the ladies.”

“So much the better for both of you. But did you play with Will Esmond, if he was home? I’ll lay ten to one you did.”

Harry blushed slightly and admitted that in the evenings, he and his cousin had played a few hands.

“And Tom Sampson, the chaplain?” cried Jack Morris. “Was he part of the party? I swear Tom made a third. God save you from Tom and Will Esmond together!”

“Truth be told,” said Harry, “I won from both of them.”

“And they paid you? Well, miracles never cease!”

“I didn’t say anything about miracles,” Harry remarked, smiling over his wine.

“And you don’t tell tales out of school—the volto sciolto—eh, Mr. Warrington?” asked Lord March.

“I beg your pardon,” said Harry bluntly. “French is the only language besides my own that I understand.”

“My Lord March learned Italian at the Opera,” remarked Jack Morris. “And it cost him a pretty penny.”

“We must show him the Opera,” he added. “Mustn’t we, March?”

“Must we, Morris?” replied Lord March, as if mildly offended by the familiarity.

Both men were dressed alike: small scratch wigs without powder, blue frocks with plate buttons, buckskins and riding boots, little hats adorned with narrow lace cords—no outward sign of fashion, but practical for the road.

“I don’t care much for the Opera,” said Harry, warming with the Burgundy. “But I’d like to see Newmarket. I long to watch a real English hunting field.”

“We’ll take you to Newmarket and the hunt,” said Lord March. “Can you ride well?”

“I think so,” answered Harry. “And I can shoot fairly, and jump a bit.”

“What’s your weight? I’ll bet we’re even—or I weigh more. I’ll bet Jack Morris beats you at birds or marks at twenty-five paces. I’ll bet I jump farther than you on this grass.”

“I don’t know Mr. Morris’s shooting—I’ve never seen either of you before—but I’ll take your bets, my lord, at any odds,” said Harry, now quite flushed with wine.

“Ponies on each!” shouted Lord March.

“Done and done!” echoed both men. Harry felt it was a matter of honor to accept any challenge laid before him.

“We can try the last bet now, if your feet are steady,” said Lord March, springing up and stretching his limbs. He removed his boots, then his coat and waistcoat, fastening his belt tightly around his waist and tossing his clothes to the ground.

Harry, however, held more respect for his garments. It was his best suit. He carefully removed his velvet coat and waistcoat, folded them neatly, and, seeing the tables nearby were splashed with drink, carried them inside to place them on a dry table near the open window.

At that moment, a new guest entered—the Colonel Wolfe, soberly enjoying a chicken and salad with a modest pint of wine. Harry, full of spirits, approached him. “Colonel,” he said, “I’ve got a bet with Lord March—would you stand me half?”

The Colonel smiled. “Too poor to bet, sir. But I’ll come out and see fair play.”

So he set down his glass and followed his young friend through the open window.

“Who is that tallow-faced fellow with the red hair?” asked Jack Morris, whose wine had begun to affect him.

Mr. Warrington explained: “That’s Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe, of the 20th Regiment.”

“Your humble servant, gentlemen!” said the Colonel, offering a stiff military salute.

“Never saw such a figure in my life!” exclaimed Jack Morris. “Did you, March?”

“I beg your pardon,” said the Colonel, looking surprised. “You said March?”

“I am the Earl of March, sir,” said the nobleman, bowing. “My friend, Mr. Morris, is so close to me that after dinner, we’re like brothers.”

Why isn’t all Tunbridge Wells here to hear this? thought Morris, delighted. He could not resist shouting, “Two to one on my lord!”

“Done!” called Mr. Warrington. Jack, caught up in the moment, had to cry “Done!” too.

“Take him, Colonel,” whispered Harry.

But the Colonel shook his head. “I can’t afford to lose, so I can’t hope to win.”

“I see you’ve already won one of our bets, Mr. Warrington,” said Lord March. “I’m taller by an inch or two, but you’re broader across the shoulders.”

“Pooh, my dear Will!” cried Jack. “I bet you weigh twice as much as he does!”

“Done, Jack!” laughed Lord March. “All bets are ponies. Will you take him, Mr. Warrington?”

“No, my dear fellow—one’s enough,” said Jack.

“Very good,” replied Lord March. “Now let’s settle the next wager.”

Harry, already dressed in his finest silk stockings, black satin breeches, and neat pumps, refused to remove his shoes, unlike his opponent, whose heavy riding boots and spurs were ill-suited for jumping. Before them lay a smooth stretch of grass, thirty yards long—perfect for a run and a leap. A gravel path ran around it, beyond which stood a wall with a gate bearing the Hanoverian arms: a white horse rampant between two skittles, with the landlord’s name and motto inscribed below.

Lord March placed a handkerchief on the ground as the starting point. Mr. Wolfe stood at the far end to mark where each jumper landed.

“My lord went first,” wrote Mr. Warrington later in a letter to Mrs. Mountain at Castlewood, Virginia—a letter still preserved. “He wanted me to go first, but remembering the story of Fontenoy that my dear George used to tell, I said, ‘Monseigneur le Comte, tirez le premier, s’il vous plaît.’ So he ran barefoot, and for the honor of Old Virginia, I beat him by more than two feet—two feet nine inches, to be exact. I jumped twenty-one feet three inches, measured by the tavern keeper’s tape, while his lordship managed only eighteen feet six. I’d already guessed his weight the moment I saw him. So he and Mr. Jack paid me both bets. With deepest respect to my mother—she’ll not be displeased. I bet for the honor of the Old Dominion, and my opponent was a nobleman of the highest rank, holding two earldoms and heir to a dukedom. Betting is the rage here—every young man of fashion is betting from dawn till dusk.

“I told them—perhaps that was my mistake—that there was a man back home who could beat me by a full foot. When they asked who, I said Colonel G. Washington of Mount Vernon—because he can, and he’s the only man in either our counties who can. Mr. Wolfe questioned me at length about Colonel Washington, showed he’d heard of him, and spoke of last year’s unfortunate campaign as if he knew every inch of the ground. He knew all our rivers—though he called the Potomac ‘Pottamac,’ which made us laugh. My Lord March and Ruglen were in no way upset by losing; they handed me notes from their pocketbooks, filling my own, which had grown quite empty thanks to payments to servants at my cousin’s house and the purchase of a horse at Oakhurst. I may need to draw again on my mother or her agents in London or Bristol.”

After their feats, the four men strolled from the garden into the public walk, where a crowd had gathered. Jack Morris, frank and loud, began making remarks—some of them less than polite. When Lord March made a joke (and he made many), Jack roared, “Oh, ho, ho! Oh, good Gad! Oh, my dear earl! Oh, my dear lord, you’ll be the death of me!” “It seemed,” wrote Harry sagely to Mrs. Mountain, “as if he wished everyone to know his companion was an earl!”

Indeed, the walk was filled with a wide array of characters. M. Poellnitz passed by, no more elegantly dressed than at dinner, grinning and tipping his large laced hat with tarnished feathers. Then came Lord Chesterfield, in a pearl-colored suit, blue ribbon and star, saluting the young men in turn.

“I’ll back the old boy for taking his hat off against the whole kingdom, and France too,” said Lord March. “He hasn’t changed his hat’s shape in twenty years. Look—there it goes again! Do you see that big, awkward, pock-marked man with the snuff-colored face? He barely touches his clumsy beaver in return. D—— his impudence—do you know who that is?”

“No, curse him! Who is it, March?” asked Jack, swearing.

“It’s Johnson, the Dictionary-maker. Lord Chesterfield wrote some brilliant articles to promote him when his dictionary came out. I know they were brilliant—Horry Walpole said so, and he knows these things. Confound the impudent schoolmaster!”

“Hang him—he should stand in the pillory!” cried Jack.

“That fat man walking with him is another writer—a printer named Richardson. He wrote *Clarissa*, you know.”

“Great heavens, my lord—is that the great Richardson? Is that the man who wrote *Clarissa*?” exclaimed Colonel Wolfe and Mr. Warrington together.

Harry rushed forward to look at the elderly gentleman, slowly walking with a train of admiring ladies.

“Indeed, my dear sir,” one woman said, “you are too great and good to live in such a world. But surely you were sent to teach it virtue!”

“Ah, Miss Mulso,” replied the kind, round-faced man, raising his eyes upward, “who shall teach the teacher? Even he has faults and errors. Even his age and experience do not prevent him from stumbl—Heaven bless my soul, Mr. Johnson! I beg your pardon if I trod on your corn.”

“You have done both, sir—you’ve trod on the corn and received the pardon,” said Mr. Johnson, muttering verses as he walked, eyes fixed downward, hands behind him, occasionally threatening the honest eyes of his companion with his cane.

“They don’t see well, my dear Mulso,” he said. “But even so, I would keep my lash from Mr. Johnson’s cudgel. Your servant, sir.” He bowed low and tipped his hat to Mr. Warrington, who stepped back, blushing deeply, after returning the greeting. The great author was accustomed to adoration. No gentler wind ever stirred mortal vanity. Enraptured spinsters scattered tea leaves at his feet, offered him coffee, kissed the slippers they had knitted for him. A halo of virtue surrounded his nightcap. All Europe had trembled, wept, admired over the pages of this kindly, honest man with the round belly. Harry returned glowing with pride at having received a bow from him. “Ah!” he said, “my lord, I am glad to have seen him!”

“Seen him? Why, dammy, you can see him any day in his shop, I suppose?” laughed Jack.

“My brother insisted that he and Mr. Fielding—wasn’t that his name?—were the greatest geniuses in England. He often said that when we came to Europe, his first pilgrimage would be to Mr. Richardson,” said Harry, always passionate, honest, and tender when speaking of his dear friend.

“Your brother spoke like a man,” said Colonel Wolfe, his pale face flushing. “I would rather be a man of genius than a peer of the realm.”

“Every man to his taste, Colonel,” said Lord March, amused. “Your enthusiasm—nothing personal—I assure you—it refreshes me.”

“So it does me—by gad—perfectly refreshes me,” said Jack.

“So it does Jack—you see—it actually refreshes Jack! Now, Jack, which would you rather be? A fat old printer who wrote a story about a ruined girl, or a peer of Parliament with ten thousand a year?”

“March—my Lord March, do you take me for a fool?” asked Jack, voice trembling. “Have I done anything to deserve such words from you?”

“I would rather win honor than honours. I would rather have genius than wealth. I would rather make my name than inherit it, though my father’s, thank God, is an honest one,” said the young Colonel. “But forgive me, gentlemen,” and with a quick bow, he crossed the parade toward a young lady, an older woman, and a gentleman approaching.

“It’s the beautiful Miss Lowther. I remember now,” said Lord March. “Look—he takes her arm! They say he’s engaged to her.”

“You don’t mean such a fellow is engaged to one of the Lowthers of the North?” cried Jack. “Curse me, what has become of the world—with printers, half-pay ensigns, schoolmasters, and this infernal nonsense?”

The Dictionary-maker, who had shown so little desire to bow to Lord Chesterfield earlier, now removed his beaver and bowed almost to the ground before a florid figure in a large round hat, bands, and gown. This was the Bishop of Salisbury, wearing proudly the blue riband and badge of the Garter, of which he was prelate.

Mr. Johnson stood hat in hand throughout the conversation with Dr. Gilbert, who praised Mr. Richardson extravagantly—calling him the defender of virtue, the preacher of sound morals, the pillar of religion—all of which the printer believed wholeheartedly.

Let no young lady rush to her grandfather’s bookshelf because of this praise and rashly pull down *Clarissa*. She would not care to read the volumes that once made her ancestors weep and tremble. Once celebrated from pulpits to palaces, now they gather dust. I wonder—have our women become more virtuous, or merely more sensitive? If the former, then Miss Smith of New York is certainly more modest than Miss Smith of London, who still says tables, pianos, and animals have legs. Oh, faithful Samuel Richardson! Has the news reached you in Hades that your sublime novels are now hidden away, unread, as if *Clarissa* were as forbidden as *Tom Jones*? Rise, Samuel, and reconcile yourself with your hated brother-scribe. I wonder—if a century hence, today’s novels will be locked behind wires and make maidens blush?

“Who is that strange woman in the high headdress of my grandmother’s time, speaking to Mr. Richardson?” asked Harry, as a fantastically dressed lady approached and curtseyed to the printer.

Jack Morris nervously struck Harry with the butt of his whip. Lord March laughed.

“That queer person is my gracious kinswoman, Katharine, Duchess of Dover and Queensberry, at your service, Mr. Warrington. She was once a beauty! Changed now, isn’t she? What an old Gorgon! She’s a great patron of authors. When that old frump was young, poets wrote verses about her.”

Lord March briefly left his friends to greet the duchess, while Jack explained to Harry how, upon the Duke’s death, Lord March would inherit the dukedoms.

“I suppose,” said Harry simply, “his lordship is here attending the old lady?”

Jack burst into laughter.

“Oh yes! Very much so! Exactly!” he said. “Why, my dear fellow, haven’t you heard about the little Opera-dancer?”

“I’ve only just arrived in England, Mr. Morris,” said Harry with a smile. “And in Virginia, we’ve heard little of the little Opera-dancer.”

Fortunately, the secret of the little Opera-dancer remained unspoken, for their conversation was interrupted by a lady in a cardinal cape and a hat resembling those now popular a century later. She made a deep curtsey to the two gentlemen and received their greetings.

She stopped before Harry and held out her hand, surprising him:

“Have you so soon forgotten me, Mr. Warrington?”

Off went Harry’s hat instantly. He started, blushed, stammered, and cried out, “Good heavens!” as if a celestial event had occurred. It was Lady Maria, come out for a walk. He had not been thinking of her. She had, for the moment, completely slipped from his mind—so utterly that her sudden reappearance startled him, flooding his cheeks with guilt.

No—he wasn’t even thinking of her! A week ago—perhaps a year, a hundred years—it seemed—he would not have been surprised to meet her anywhere. She had haunted his dreams, glided across garden terraces, lingered in corridors, appeared in shadows. A week ago, his heart had beaten for her. A week ago, even as he woke, her image smiled upon him. And it was only last Tuesday that his love had been wounded and died—so thoroughly that he had ceased mourning her, and indeed, forgotten her!

“You will walk with me a little?” she asked. “Or would you prefer the music? I dare say you’d prefer the music.”

“You know,” said Harry, “I don’t care much for music—except”—he hesitated, recalling the evening hymn—“except your playing.” He turned crimson as he spoke, feeling he was betraying himself terribly.

The lady herself was agitated by the flutter in her young companion. Could that tremor mean she was mistaken? That he still loved her? “Give me your arm,” she said, waving a curtsey to the others. “My aunt is asleep after dinner.” Harry could not refuse. He offered his arm and pressed the hand resting against his heart. Maria made another graceful curtsey to Harry’s companions and walked away with her prize.

In grief, rage, pain, and anguish, how a woman remembers to smile, curtsey, caress, dissemble! How resolutely she fulfills her social duties—how she offers a word, a hand, a kind phrase to the passing acquaintance who unknowingly crosses the path of tragedy, drops a light remark, and moves on. He passes, thinking, “She seemed pleased with what I said. That joke was neat. I think Lady Maria looks favorably on me—she’s a devilishly fine woman, by Gad!” O wiseacre! Such was Jack Morris’s thought as he walked away, leaning on Lord March’s arm, convinced the entire society of Tunbridge Wells was watching him. He had made clever remarks at Lady Flushington’s card game the night before, and Lady Maria had replied gracefully. And so Jack walked off, perfectly happy.
