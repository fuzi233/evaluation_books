The truce between the sisters didn't hold. Laura, ever the peacemaker, was quick to forgive, while Miss Blanche's grudges were notoriously short-lived anyway. After the recent row, Blanche wasn't even truly offended; accusations of wickedness rarely sting a vain woman's pride. In fact, she found her friend's righteous fury rather entertaining, especially since both knew exactly why Laura was so indignant, even if neither dared admit it aloud.

So, Laura sighed, conceding that the romantic phase of their friendship had expired. She realized her beloved Blanche was merely deserving of polite, ordinary regard. Meanwhile, Blanche, feeling deserted, promptly penned a series of tear-jerking verses lamenting her "cold" treatment and "neglected" fidelity. Just as the old clichés go, love met ice, and loyalty met indifference.

Coincidentally, some new neighbors arrived from London—daughters included. Seizing the moment, Miss Amory adopted a fresh confidante, pouring out her sorrows to this new sister. The flow of love letters to Laura dried up; the pony carriage stopped visiting Fairoaks regularly. When Laura did drop by, Blanche wore a mask of saintly suffering, while Laura simply laughed at her friend's melodramatic mood, treating it with cheerful disrespect.

However, Blanche's circle expanded beyond women. Any young man she encountered—a stroll in the garden, a chat through a drawing-room window, or a pause during a waltz—became the recipient of her dramatic monologues. She would bat her lashes, speak in a trembling, tender tone, and leave him breathless, only to repeat the performance with the next available suitor.

At first, when the Claverings moved to the Park, Blanche had few suitors to practice on, leaving Pen to monopolize her glances and confessions. But the local male population was sparse: mostly a couple of curates, a rustic squire with oversized shoes and shabby clothes, and the local dragoon regiment, which the Baronet avoided due to past squabbles over horses, gambling debts, and a "white feather" incident.

Things changed with the autumn session and the end of the London season. County families trickled down to Baymouth, Mr. Bingley's theater opened in Chatteris, and the Assizes and race-meets drew crowds. Previously, the established locals—the Fogies of Drummington, the Squares of Tozely Park, and the Welbores of The Barrow—had kept their distance, whispering scandalous rumors about the Claverings' origins, Sir Francis's wife, and Captain Strong. It took three months of silence before the elite finally extended invitations.

The turning point came when the Earl of Trehawk visited Eyrie Castle and the Dowager Countess of Rockminster settled at Baymouth. Their arrival in state forced the lesser nobility to follow suit, filling the avenues of Clavering Park.

This influx gave Mirobolant (the artist) a chance to forget his heartbreaks amidst his craft, and freed the footmen from delivering notes to Fairoaks. Blanche found new admirers, and Pen found himself alone by the river, whipping the stream in vain. The "fish" refused to bite, and the elusive "Peri" never surfaced.

Strictly speaking, let's address the elephant in the room: Pen had turned a hollow tree near the stream into a secret post office. For years, he'd slipped poems and letters under a moss-covered stone, addressed to an imaginary "Undine" or "Naiad." Occasionally, a pink note or a single French word would replace his missive. Miss Amory, who loved pink scented paper, likely walked past this spot often. But once the grand carriages started arriving nightly, the ritual ended. No one fetched Pen's mail anymore. The white paper remained untouched beneath the stone while the river roared by.

Perhaps the letters weren't deep anyway—just playful banter between two young people pretending at romance. But when the game stops working, tempers flare. Pen grew serious, desperate for an explanation.

He tried several times. Once, the house was packed with guests. Another time, Blanche was asleep, preparing for a ball. Pen cursed the balls and his poverty. On a third attempt, he found her in the garden, walking in high procession with the Bishop and Bishopess of Chatteris. The clergyman, recognizing Pen from a previous encounter in the Dean's garden, scowled.

"The Bishop says you're a sad young man," Lady Clavering whispered kindly. "Have you done something to vex your dear Mar? How is she? Why doesn't she come round? We haven't seen her in ages. We've been traveling so much lately. Send our love to her and Laura, and join us for dinner tomorrow."

Mrs. Pendennis was too ill to attend, but Laura and Pen came. Amidst the chatter, Pen managed a hurried exchange with Blanche.

"You never come to the river now," he said.

"I can't," Blanche replied. "The house is full of people."

"Undine has left the stream," Pen continued, trying to maintain his poetic facade.

"She shouldn't have gone there," Blanche corrected sharply. "She won't return. It was foolish, wrong. It was just play. Besides, you have other comforts at home," she added, locking eyes with him for a split second before lowering her gaze.

If he wanted her, why didn't he speak then? She could have said yes. Instead, hearing her mention "comforts at home," he thought of Laura—so pure, so devoted—and his mother, who had dreamed of marrying him to her adopted daughter.

"Blanche!" he started, annoyed. "Miss Amory!"

"Laura is looking at us, Mr. Pendennis," Blanche said smoothly. "I must return to the group." She fled, leaving Pen biting his nails in confusion, staring blankly at the moonlit garden.

Meanwhile, Laura watched Pen. She was conversing with Mr. Pynsent, the son of Lord Rockminster, a tall, grave figure seated in honor. Pynsent and Pen had attended Oxford together; back then, Pen, in his prime of wealth and style, had looked down on the young patrician. Now, they nodded politely across the table—a classic university gesture meaning, *"Confound you, what are you doing here?"*

"I knew that man at Oxford," Pynsent told Miss Bell. "A Mr. Pendennis, I believe."

"Yes," Miss Bell confirmed.

"He seems quite taken with Miss Amory," Pynsent observed. Laura glanced at them but said nothing.

"A wealthy county man, isn't he? Used to talk about running for Parliament. Where are his estates?"

Laura smiled. "On the other side of the river, near the lodge gate. He's my cousin, and I live there."

"Where?" Pynsent laughed.

"Why, on the other side of the river, at Fairoaks," Miss Bell answered.

"Many pheasants there? The cover looks promising," the naive gentleman remarked.

"We have nine hens, a rooster, a pig, and an old pointer," Laura quipped, amused by the idea that her Pen was a major sporting landlord.

"Pendennis doesn't shoot, then?" Pynsent pressed.

"You should come and see him," Laura laughed, clearly enjoying the joke that her cousin pretended to be a grand country squire.

"Indeed, I'd love to renew our acquaintance," Pynsent said gallantly, his eyes fixed on Laura.

Just then, Blanche approached with a dazzling smile, inviting Laura to sing a duet. Laura obligingly went to the piano. Pynsent listened intently until Blanche took the lead, then strode away.

"What a lovely, frank, well-bred girl that is," Pynsent told a friend from Baymouth, gesturing to Laura. "Tall, with ringlets and terribly red lips."

"And the mistress of the house?" his friend asked.

"I think she's a lean, scraggy fraud," Pynsent admitted candidly. "She strains against her dress, never rests her eyes, and simper-ogles like a French maid."

"Pynsent, be civil! Someone might hear," his friend warned.

"Oh, it's Pendennis of Boniface," Pynsent called out cheerfully. "Lovely evening, Mr. Pendennis. We were discussing your charming cousin."

"Related to my old friend, Major Pendennis?" asked Mr. Wagg, joining them.

"Nephew. Met you at Gaunt House," Pen replied with practiced elegance. The two men bonded instantly.

The next afternoon, Pen returned from a fruitless fishing trip to find Pynsent and Wagg chatting comfortably in his mother's drawing room with the widow and her ward. Pynsent, tall and gaunt with red whiskers, loomed over Laura, who found his simple, slightly slang-heavy humor amusing. This was Laura's first exposure to a true London dandy; previously, she'd only known Mr. Foker, a boyish schoolboy.

Mr. Wagg, observing everything with the eye of a detective, noted the old gardener, the laundry drying on gooseberry bushes, the blue aprons, and the white duck trousers. "Rather a shy spot for a debut MP, eh, Pynsent?" he mused.

"A snug little crib," Pynsent agreed. "Cosy lawn."
