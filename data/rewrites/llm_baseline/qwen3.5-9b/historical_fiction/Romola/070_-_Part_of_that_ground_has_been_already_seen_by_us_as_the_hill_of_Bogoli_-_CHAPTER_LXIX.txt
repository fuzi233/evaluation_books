In the hushed silence of those winter hours, as Romola rested from her exhaustion, her mind drifted backward through the past and gazed across the foggy horizon of the future. Everything appeared shifted, viewed from a vantage point entirely new. Since awakening in the boat, her recent experiences had struck her with the force of a fresh seal pressing upon dissolving wax. She had felt untethered, devoid of purpose, sinking into a selfish lament that life offered her nothing but emptiness. A desperate cry had risen within her—"I am weary of life; I wish to die"—as she succumbed to sleep. Yet, from the moment she woke to the sound of that cry, reflection ceased. Unlike in Florence, where she had often rationalized her survival by thinking how she might alleviate sorrow, she now simply *lived*. With an urgent, driving impulse, she sought to share the existence around her, answering every call of need and performing the labor that screamed to be done. The reasons for enduring, working, and breathing no longer required logical argument; they were instinctive.

This transformation felt like a rebirth. In Florence, the simple connections between human beings had become tangled for her with the heavy knots of marriage, state duty, and religious devotion. When these structures failed her, the blow had severed her from life and numbed her compassion. Now, however, she declared, "It was sheer baseness in me to crave death. If all else is uncertain, this suffering I can ease is absolute truth; if the glory of the cross is a phantom, then sorrow is merely the reality." As long as strength remained in her arms, she would extend them to the fainting; as long as light visited her eyes, she would seek out the forsaken.

Then, the past surged forward with renewed urgency. Her work in this verdant valley was finished, and the emotions previously held captive by those around her flooded back into the deep channels of utility and love. This rare opportunity for self-reflection, born of total separation from her accustomed life, allowed her to judge herself as never before. The guilt inherent in a sensitive nature, acutely aware of others' potential pain, stirred within her with growing intensity. She questioned the justice of her own judgments and actions: she had been rash, arrogant, perpetually dissatisfied that others were not virtuous enough, while failing to honor the highest ideals her soul once recognized. She condemned her departure as a cowardly act of self-preservation. After all, the reasons Savonarola had given for bringing her back were truer, deeper than the excuses for her second flight. How could she claim to feel the needs of strangers while ignoring the needs of those closest to her?

Yet, resistance rose against this harsh self-reproach. Memories of her life with Tito surfaced—the circumstances that rendered their true union impossible, while their outward marriage imposed false duties that concealed and sanctioned what her mind rebelled against. These memories told her that flight had indeed been her only recourse. Every mind, save perhaps those dulled by insensibility, must endure this recurring conflict where life's complex twists forbid the fulfillment of sacred bonds. Strictly speaking, relationships cannot be replaced; the arrival of the new does not erase the failure or breach of the old. Life has lost its perfection; it is maimed. Until the wounds fully scar, conscience constantly casts backward, casting doubtful glances.

Romola shrank in terror at the prospect of renewing her proximity to Tito, yet unease gnawed at her regarding his fate. She feared the moment might arrive when he suffered and needed her. A thread of pain remained within her, echoing Fra Girolamo's warning: she could not cease to be a wife. Could anything truly end for her that had once mingled with the very current of her heart's blood? Florence, and all her life there, returned to her like a sudden hunger. Her feelings refused to wander toward the abstract and vague; their living fiber was sustained by the memory of familiar things. The thought that she had severed herself from them forever grew increasingly insistent during these idle hours.

*What if Fra Girolamo was wrong?* *What if the life of Florence is a tapestry woven from contradictions?* Was she, then, something superior, entitled to shake the dust from her feet and declare, "This world is not good enough for me"? If she were truly higher, she would not have so readily lost all her trust. Her indignant grief for her godfather no longer held complete sway; her sense of debt to Savonarola was regaining dominance. Nothing that had occurred or would occur could negate the fact that he had inspired a new life within her. Who, in all her experience, could demand equal gratitude? His errors—could they not bring calamity? She could not rest. She barely knew whether her renewed vigor stemmed from the budding spring or an eager longing to draw nearer to Florence. Though she did not dare imagine entering the city, the desire to be close enough to learn what transpired there pressed upon her with a strength that excluded all other aims.

One March morning, the villagers gathered in the valley to witness the Blessed Lady's departure. Jacopo had procured a mule for her and accompanied her over the mountains. The Padre also traveled with her to the nearest town, intending to assist her in finding the safest route to Pistoja. Her hoard of trinkets and coin, untouched since arriving in the valley, was ample for her journey. Had Romola been less consumed by the longing pulling her away, leaving the village for the final time would have been a difficult moment. She stood on the village street, while the Padre and Jacopo waited near the well with the mule. Her progress was slowed by the weeping crowd, who knelt to kiss her hands, then clung to her skirts, kissing the grey fabric as they cried, "Ah, why will you leave, when the good season begins and the crops promise plenty?"

"Do not mourn," Romola replied gently. "You are well now, and I shall remember you. I must go to see if my own people require me."

"Ah, yes—if they have the plague!" someone called out.

"Look upon us again, Madonna!" another pleaded.

"Yes, yes, we will be good to little Benedetto!"

Finally, Romola mounted the mule. However, a vigorous scream from Benedetto, seeing her turn away in this new capacity, gave everyone an excuse to follow her, insisting that the child must ride on the mule's neck down to the bottom of the slope. The parting was inevitable, yet as Romola kept turning before disappearing from view, she watched the small flock linger, striving to catch the last wave of her hand.
