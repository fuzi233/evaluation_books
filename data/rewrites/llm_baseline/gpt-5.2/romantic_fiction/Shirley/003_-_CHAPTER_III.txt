Cheerfulness, it seems, is kneaded as much from what a man carries in his own breast as from what the weather pours upon his shoulders. That night, the drizzle fell with a cold pertinacity, and the road off Stilbro’ Moor lay half lost in darkness and wet; yet Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore went forward from the mill-yard gates with a kind of fierce enjoyment, as if danger were a spice they had long missed at table.

Each of the three pedestrians behind them bore a lantern; and whenever the light struck Moore’s face, it found—unusual there—animation, a quick glitter in the eye like fire caught in a black bottle, and a liveliness that made the stern line of his mouth look young. When it shone on the rector, it showed hard features set, not in prayer or pity, but in exultation: grin and gleam together, like a blade that likes its own edge. The night was raw, the expedition not without peril; any lurking hand among the men who had been at work on the moor might have taken pleasure in shooting either leader from behind a wall. Both leaders knew it. The knowledge, to two natures of steady pulse and tempered nerve, was a cordial.

It is a grave thing—so you will say—to see a parson warlike. A clergyman’s errand among men is peace; his pattern is not a captain’s. I do not deny it. Yet it is not always possible to cut a man out, as you cut a pattern for a garment, and then fit him without wrinkle to the profession hung upon him. Helstone had missed his vocation. He should have been a soldier; circumstance had made him priest. He was conscientious, brave, stern, rigid; faithful to what he called principle, and faithful also to prejudices that hardened into bone. Sympathy was scant in him, gentleness scant; but sincerity was there, and an honour that would not bend. Such men are commonly cursed by one half the people and adored by the other; he had both portions.

United, for the present, in one cause, and elated by the same risk, you might expect cordial talk between them as they rode side by side. It never came. Their temperaments—hard and bilious, each in his own fashion—met like flint and steel, not to warm but to strike. Their old subject rose as inevitably as smoke from damp turf: the war.

Helstone was high Tory; Moore, by interest and by mordant disposition, took the Whig side at least so far as opposition to war went. Of British politics beyond what touched his mill and purse he professed little; but in this one matter he delighted to fret the rector. He would talk of Bonaparte with a cool audacity, as though the man were an inevitable storm and Europe a field of ripe grain. He would taunt England, hint at her impotence, advance—quietly, obstinately—that it might be wiser to yield soon than late, since, as he believed, Napoleon must in the end crush every antagonist and reign supreme.

Helstone could not bear such sentiments. Only by remembering that Moore was half alien—born abroad, with foreign gall in his veins—could the rector refrain from the impulse to lift his cane. Yet even while disgusted he had a sort of harsh respect for Moore’s consistency: the dogged way he stood to his opinion, as a man stands to a post in a gale.

They turned into the Stilbro’ road and met what little wind the night possessed. Rain slanted into their faces like fine thrown grit; the sound of it on cloaks and hat-brims made a restless, incessant hush—nature’s own percussion, clean and steady, unlike the harsh metal clatter of machinery that had lately begun to rule men’s nerves by day. The moor itself answered with darker tones: the sucking of wheels in mire, the thin cry of some bird startled from a furze-bush, the far murmur of water swollen in a gully. It was a soundscape to bring a man either to calm or to challenge; to these two it brought challenge.

Moore, braced by the rawness and perhaps sharpened by the stinging drizzle, began again to goad.

“Does your Peninsular news please you still?” he asked.

“What do you mean?” returned Helstone, surly.

“I mean—have you still faith in that Baal of a Lord Wellington?”

“And what do you mean now?”

“Do you still believe that wooden-faced, pebble-hearted idol of England has power to send fire down from heaven to consume the French holocaust you want to offer up?”

“I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte’s marshals into the sea the day it pleases him to lift his arm.”

“But you can’t be serious. Bonaparte’s marshals are great men, acting under the guidance of an omnipotent master-spirit. Your Wellington is the most humdrum of commonplace martinets—slow, mechanical—hampered, too, by an ignorant home government.”

“Wellington is the soul of England,” Helstone said, and his voice took that ring which in a pulpit would have been called fervour, and in a camp, spirit. “He is the right champion of a good cause—the representative of a powerful, resolute, sensible, honest nation.”

“Your good cause—so far as I understand it—is the restoration of that filthy, feeble Ferdinand to a throne he disgraced. Your representative of an honest people is a dull-witted drover acting for a duller farmer; and against these are arrayed victorious supremacy and invincible genius.”

“Against legitimacy is arrayed usurpation; against modest, single-minded, righteous, brave resistance to encroachment is arrayed boastful, double-tongued, selfish, treacherous ambition to possess. God defend the right!”

“God often defends the powerful.”

Helstone’s whole rigid little frame seemed to tighten, as if the wet air had turned to iron in his lungs.

“What! Then the handful of Israelites standing dryshod on the Asiatic side of the Red Sea was more powerful than Pharaoh’s host drawn up on the African side? Were they more numerous? better appointed? more mighty? Don’t speak, Moore, or you’ll tell a lie—and you know you will. They were poor, overwrought bondsmen. Tyrants had oppressed them four hundred years; women and children weakened their thin ranks. Their masters were pampered—armed, horsed, charioted; the wanderers were afoot. Few had better weapons than shepherds’ crooks or masons’ tools; their leader had only his rod. But right was with them. The God of battles was on their side. Crime and the lost archangel generalled Pharaoh—and which triumphed? We know. ‘The Lord saved Israel that day… the depths covered them; they sank to the bottom as a stone.’ The right hand of the Lord dashed in pieces the enemy!”

Moore, with a kind of cold enjoyment, answered, “You are all right—only you forget the true parallel. France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses. Europe—with her old overgorged empires and rotten dynasties—is corrupt Egypt. Gallant France is the Twelve Tribes, and her fresh vigorous Usurper the Shepherd of Horeb.”

“I scorn to answer you,” said Helstone, and set his jaw as if that were an act of piety.

Moore, as though talking partly to himself now, let his voice fall. “Oh—in Italy he was as great as any Moses. Fit to head and organize measures for the regeneration of nations. It puzzles me still how the conqueror of Lodi condescended to become an emperor—a vulgar, stupid humbug; and still more how a people that once called itself republican sank again to the grade of mere slaves. I despise France! If England had marched as far on the road of civilization as France did, she would hardly have retreated so shamelessly.”

“You don’t mean to say that besotted imperial France is worse than bloody republican France?” demanded Helstone.

“I mean to say nothing,” Moore returned, with that air of keeping the knife in while smiling, “but I can think what I please—about France and England; revolutions, regicides, restorations; the divine right of kings you stickle for in sermons; the duty of non-resistance; the sanity of war—and—”

His sentence was cut clean through, not by argument but by wheels. A gig came rolling up fast, lamp glimmering; it stopped suddenly in the middle of the road. Moore and Helstone, absorbed in their quarrel, had scarcely marked its approach.

“Nah, maister; did th’ wagons hit home?” demanded a voice from the vehicle—broad, keen, with a relish in it, as if the speaker tasted every word before he let it out.

“Can that be Joe Scott?” Moore exclaimed.

“Ay, ay!” answered another voice. The gig held two persons, seen dimly by its own light. The lantern-bearers had dropped back; the mounted men had outpaced their little company.

“Ay, Mr. Moore, it’s Joe Scott,” said the first voice again; and now its owner leaned forward, showing a sharp-featured man, vigorous, alert, his expression half humorous, half combative. “I’m bringing him back to you in a bonny pickle. I fand him on top o’ th’ moor yonder—him and three others. What will you give me for restoring him to you?”

“Why, my thanks, I believe,” Moore replied; “for I could better afford to lose a better man. That is you, I suppose, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?”

“Ay, lad, it’s me. Coming home from Stilbro’ market; and just as I got to th’ middle o’ th’ moor, and was whipping on as swift as wind—(for these, they say, are not safe times, thanks to a bad government!)—I heard a groan. I pulled up. Some would ha’ whipt on faster; but I’ve naught to fear that I know on. I don’t believe there’s a lad i’ these parts would harm me—leastways, I’d give ’em as good as I got if they tried. I says, ‘Is there aught wrong anywhere?’ ‘Deed is there,’ says somebody, speaking out o’ th’ ground, like. ‘What’s to do? Be sharp and tell me,’ I ordered. ‘Nobbut four on us ligging i’ a ditch,’ says Joe, as quiet as could be. I telled ’em more shame to ’em, and bid ’em get up and move on, or I’d lend ’em a lick o’ th’ gig-whip; for my notion was they were all fresh. ‘We’d ha’ done that an hour sin’, but we’re teed wi’ a bit o’ band,’ says Joe. So i’ a while I got down and loosed ’em wi’ my penknife; and Scott would ride wi’ me, to tell me how it happened; and t’ others are coming on as fast as their feet will bring ’em.”

“Well, I am greatly obliged to you, Mr. Yorke,” Moore said.

“Are you, my lad? You know you’re not.” The man’s tone made the rebuke almost friendly. “However, here are the rest approaching. And here, by th’ Lord, is another set with lights in their pitchers, like Gideon’s army; and as we’ve th’ parson wi’ us—good-evening, Mr. Helstone—we’se do.”

Helstone returned the salutation stiffly, as though politeness were a coin he hated to part with.

Mr. Yorke ran on, warming to his own picture. “We’re eleven strong men, and there’s both horses and chariots amang us. If we could only fall in wi’ some o’ these starved ragamuffins o’ frame-breakers we could win a grand victory. We could iv’ry one be a Wellington—that would please ye, Mr. Helstone—and sich paragraphs as we could contrive for t’ papers! Briarfield suld be famous. But we’se hev a column and a half i’ th’ Stilbro’ Courier ower this job, as it is, I dare say. I’se expect no less.”

“And I’ll promise you no less, Mr. Yorke,” returned the rector, the war in him suddenly turning journalist with relish, “for I’ll write the article myself.”

“To be sure—sartainly! And mind ye recommend weel that them ’at brake t’ bits o’ frames, and teed Joe Scott’s legs wi’ band, suld be hung without benefit o’ clergy. It’s a hanging matter—or suld be. No doubt o’ that.”

“If I judged them I’d give them short shrift!” cried Moore.

“But I mean to let them quite alone this bout,” he added, cooling instantly, “to give them rope enough—certain that in the end they will hang themselves.”

“Let them alone, will ye, Moore? Do you promise that?”

“Promise? No. All I mean is, I shall give myself no particular trouble to catch them; but if one falls in my way—”

“You’ll snap him up, of course,” said Yorke. “Only you’d rather they did summat worse than merely stop a wagon afore you reckon with ’em. Well—we’ll say no more on’t at present. Here we are at my door, gentlemen, and I hope you and the men will step in. None o’ ye will be the worse o’ a little refreshment.”

Moore and Helstone objected—unnecessary, they said; they must return. But the night, with its thin, penetrating rain, pressed persuasion on the skin; and the house before which they halted shone through muslin-curtained windows with that particular domestic invitation which seems to promise not splendour but warmth, not ceremony but shelter. At last they yielded.

Mr. Yorke alighted, left his gig to a man who came out from an outbuilding, and led the way in. It was soon plain that his speech was like a cupboard with two sets of crockery: now broad Yorkshire, now pure English; his manner also shifted—polite and affable one moment, blunt and rough the next—so that you could not at once label his station by his accents.

He sent the men round by the kitchen way, saying he would “see them served wi’ summat to taste presently.” The gentlemen were admitted by the front.

They entered a matted hall lined almost to the ceiling with pictures. It had that smell old houses keep—waxed wood, woollen matting warmed, a faint tang of smoke long since settled into plaster—overlaid now by the more immediate aroma of a great fire drawing well. From the hall they were shown into a large parlour where a broad blaze in the grate made the wet clothes steam gently, and made every raindrop on hat-brims and sleeves shine before it dried. The room was cheerful in a way that went deeper than light. There was no gaudy splendour; there was taste—uncommon taste—the taste of a travelled man who had looked at beauty without buying mere show.

Italian views covered the walls: true art, not bright daubs. Even by candle-light the clear skies opened, the soft distances receded with blue air trembling between eye and hill, the lights and shadows sat in their places like well-ordered flavours in a dish—each distinct, none crude. The subjects were pastoral, sunny, as if this Yorkshire room insisted on remembering warmth. A guitar lay with some music on a sofa; cameos and delicate miniatures hinted at hands that had chosen, not snatched. On the mantelpiece stood vases of a Grecian look; and books—well arranged—in two elegant cases. There was, too, a quiet in the room that belonged to it: the fire’s low, steady roar; the soft tick of some clock not obtrusive; outside, rain still whispering at the panes. It was an acoustic refuge, a pocket of ordered sound set against the harsher world of mills and mobs—one could feel the mind ease in such a place, as the body eases when it smells bread rather than oil and iron.

Mr. Yorke offered chairs, then rang for wine. To the servant who brought it he gave hearty orders for refreshment to be carried to the men in the kitchen. Helstone remained standing; he looked as if he disliked his quarters. When the decanter was pressed towards him, he refused, and would not touch it.

“E’en as you will,” observed Yorke. “I reckon you’re thinking o’ Eastern customs, Mr. Helstone, and you’ll not eat nor drink under my roof, feared we suld be forced to be friends; but I’m not so particular nor superstitious. You might sup that decanter, and you might give me a bottle o’ th’ best in your own cellar, and I’d hold myself free to oppose you at every turn still—in every vestry-meeting and justice-meeting where we meet.”

“It is just what I should expect of you, Mr. Yorke,” Helstone returned, coldly.

“Does it agree wi’ ye, now, Mr. Helstone, to be riding out after rioters o’ a wet night, at your age?”

“It always agrees with me to be doing my duty; and in this case my duty is a thorough pleasure. To hunt down vermin is a noble occupation, fit for an archbishop.”

“Fit for ye, at ony rate.” Yorke’s eyes glinted. “But where’s t’ curate? He’s happen gone to visit some poor body i’ a sick gird—or he’s happen hunting down vermin i’ another direction.”

“He is doing garrison-duty at Hollow’s Mill.”

“You left him a sup o’ wine, I hope, Bob,” Yorke said, turning briskly on Moore, “to keep his courage up?”

He did not wait for an answer. His glance had fixed meanwhile upon the old-fashioned chair by the fireside, the one with carved arms and a three-cornered back, where Moore, pleased to be warm, had flung himself with lazy assurance.

“Move it, Robert! Get up, my lad! That place is mine. Take the sofa, or three other chairs, if you will—but not this. It belangs to me, and nob’dy else.”

Moore rose at once—he obeyed, but with a look that said he noted the command and stored it. “Why are you so particular to that chair, Mr. Yorke?”

“My father war afore me, and that’s all t’ answer I sall gie thee; and it’s as good a reason as Mr. Helstone can give for th’ main feck o’ his notions.”

Helstone, who had been watching as if he mistrusted the whole hospitality, broke in impatiently. “Moore, are you ready to go?”

“Nay; Robert’s not ready,” Yorke said, with quick possession, “or rather, I’m not ready to part wi’ him. He’s an ill lad, and wants correcting.”

“Why, sir? What have I done?” Moore asked, and there was a sharpness under the smoothness—as if the warmth of the hearth could not wholly thaw the chill he carried.

“Made thyself enemies on every hand.”

“What do I care for that? What difference does it make to me whether your Yorkshire louts hate me or like me?”

“Ay—there it is.” Yorke leaned back in his chair as if it were a pulpit of his own. “The lad is a mak’ of an alien amang us. His father would never ha’ talked i’ that way. Go back to Antwerp, where you were born and bred, mauvaise tête!”

“Mauvaise tête vous-même; je ne fais que mon devoir; quant à vos lourdauds de paysans, je m’en moque!” Moore shot back; the French came out of him like a native taste, sudden and strong, as if English were sometimes too blunt a vessel for certain bitterness.

“En revanche, mon garçon, nos lourdauds de paysans se moqueront de toi; sois-en certain,” Yorke replied, answering in French with an accent nearly as pure as Moore’s own, as though he too had kept languages stored like preserves, each with its own scent and sting.

“C’est bon! c’est bon! Et puisque cela m’est égal, que mes amis ne s’en inquiètent pas.”

“Tes amis! Où sont-ils, tes amis?”

“Je fais écho, où sont-ils? et je suis fort aise que l’écho seul y répond. Au diable les amis! Je me souviens encore du moment où mon père et mes oncles Gérard appelèrent autour d’eux leurs amis, et Dieu sait si les amis se sont empressés d’accourir à leur secours! Tenez, M. Yorke, ce mot, ami, m’irrite trop; ne m’en parlez plus.”

“Comme tu voudras,” said Yorke; and he held his peace.

He sat leaning back in his carved oak chair, his face lit by fire and candle in alternating strokes. Moore, warmed outside and dark within, stared into the blaze as if it were a thing he might one day master or be mastered by. Helstone stood rigid, as though even comfort were a compromise. And in that room—so full of chosen images and guarded quiet—the very shift of tongues seemed to show how words can belong like land, how certain syllables open the old cellars of memory while others shut them, how one man’s “friend” can be another man’s wound.
