The silence of the lock-up was not empty; it was heavy, a physical weight pressing against the ribs of Harry Warrington. Remorse for past follies coiled in his gut, sharp and familiar, but beneath it lay a hotter, more immediate irritation: the indignity of arrest itself. Yet, he dismissed the present misery as transient. His mind, trained to calculate probabilities rather than feel despair, swept through a roster of potential saviors. There was Mr. Draper, the obsequious businessman; his uncle the Baronet, who had once offered his estate as a sanctuary; Cousin Castlewood, the gambler who had drained his pockets; the noble friends of the Chocolate House; even Aunt Bernstein. Any of them could intervene, though some might append a lecture on imprudence to their aid. The primary objective, however, remained singular: secrecy. Mr. Warrington insisted that the public knowledge of a gentleman's arrest was a vulgar stain, a detail to be scrubbed from the narrative of society. He reasoned with a detached cynicism that the world, ultimately, cared for nothing. Scandal was merely noise, a temporary irritation that the masses endured with a weary, blasé indifference. Whether one was imprisoned, bankrupt, or caught in a foolish bargain, the outcome was identical: vanity. The world would continue its rhythm of dinners, laughter, and yawning, indifferent to the private collapse of individuals. To assume otherwise was a conceit of the ego.

Yet, Harry Warrington chafed. He imagined the atmosphere of the club, the sharp edges of Mr. Selwyn's mockery, the trembling of March regarding their wager, the raucous congratulations at the Savage Arms. He could not face the world again. He resolved against contacting his relations or his peers at White's. Instead, he summoned Sampson, the chaplain, a man who understood the mechanics of crisis. Dawn broke slowly over Cursitor Street, delayed as if the sun had forgotten its rounds. Once the inmates stirred, Harry dispatched a messenger to Long Acre, entrusting the chaplain with his plight and seeking his counsel.

Unaware that sending for Sampson was akin to inviting a lion into a pit, Harry believed his ally was free to act. The message sent, the captive's tension eased fractionally. Breakfast arrived, served by an attendant who inquired if he wished to dine alone or join the other gentlemen at Mrs. Bailiff's. Harry declined. He intended to leave before dinner, informing the chamberlain of his imminent departure. The man, skeptical and experienced, noted the frequency of such announcements among young gentlemen and closed the door, leaving Harry to his solitude.

Time dragged, measured in agonizing increments. Ten o'clock passed, then eleven, then noon. Still, no Sampson. Just after midday, a figure entered: Gumbo, carrying a portmanteau of his master's clothes. The boy threw himself at Harry's feet, roaring with grief, offering his life, his freedom, anything to restore his beloved master. Harry, touched by the display, commanded him to rise and dress him, strictly ordering silence. "Not a word," Harry warned, his voice severe.

"Oh no, sir, never to nobody!" Gumbo declared, his face a mask of solemn devotion. He proceeded to groom his master with meticulous care, applying powder and arranging the finest attire, transforming Harry into a figure of splendor fit for St. James's. Gumbo performed every act of love and servitude within his power, yet he possessed limitations. He could not reverse the past, nor could he suppress the truth when pressed. The language of slaves, whether black or white, is built on subterfuge; they hide like hunted animals, retreating behind veils of fiction. These strange relics of feudalism persist because society demands hypocrisy from its dependents. We expect them to lie, to withhold the whole truth, and when they fail, we are indignant.

But Gumbo's oath of silence was already broken. Before he could utter a single word, he had broadcast the catastrophe to a prodigious audience: gentlemen of the shoulder-knot and worsted lace, members of the footman's club, patrons of Sir Miles Warrington's butler, and finally, the household of Lord Castlewood. By the time he knelt before his master, promising eternal silence, the news had already rippled through the corridors of power.

While Gumbo finished the toilette, the hours stretched endlessly. Finally, a letter arrived from Sampson, sealed with a damp wafer, the ink barely dry. The chaplain wrote that he had done all he could, unaware of the peril he faced. He revealed that he had been seized by villains holding a writ against him and was currently incarcerated in a cell three doors away, separated only by garden walls. He confessed he had much to say but refused to commit his patron's affairs to paper.

Now, as Sampson remained silent, the truth emerged from the shadows of his omission. The chaplain's loquacity had not yet reached Long Acre; he was ignorant of the full extent of Harry's disaster until receiving the messenger. Driven by gratitude and desperation, he sought out Lord Castlewood, hoping to arouse pity. He delivered an eloquent plea, speaking with genuine sympathy of his cousin's generosity. My Lord Castlewood, however, cut him short with peevish brevity. "Did I not tell you two days ago," he snapped, "that I was as poor as a beggar? Has anyone left me a fortune since? The little I won from my cousin was swallowed by others. Not only cannot I help Mr. Warrington, but I had just written to him asking for assistance." Indeed, a letter to this effect had reached Harry's lodgings via the penny post.

"I must get him money, my lord," Sampson cried, desperate. "If I pawn my cassock and bands..."

"Amen," Castlewood replied, dismissing him with a wave of his hand. "Go and pawn whatever you please. Your enthusiasm does you credit." He returned to his paper while the chaplain departed in deep despondency.

Meanwhile, Lady Maria had sensed the gravity of the situation. Seizing the chaplain as he exited his futile meeting with her brother, she drew him into the dining room, her face etched with grief. "What has happened?" she demanded. "I have been in mortal anxiety since his servant brought the news last night." She claimed to have learned of the arrest from Lady Yarmouth's box at the opera.

"Then my lord knew?" Sampson asked, confused.

"Benson told us at tea," Lady Maria confirmed.

Patience evaporating, the chaplain swore and recounted the cruel irony of his encounter with Lord Castlewood—the nobleman's refusal to aid his cousin despite having won large sums from him. Enraged by the duplicity, Lady Maria launched into a furious tirade against her own family. Though rarely truthful, when familial bonds fracture, the floodgates of honesty open wide. With tears and imprecations, she denounced her brothers, sisters, and parents, questioning why God had fashioned such creatures. Emboldened by wrath, she stormed toward her brother's library door, her shrieks filling the hall.

Castlewood looked up from his book, surprised by her ferocity, and prepared a strong oath to check her advance. But Maria, usually afraid of him, was now fearless.

"So, my lord!" she shouted. "You sit down with him in private to cards and pigeon him! You get the poor boy's last shilling, and you won't give him a guinea out of his own winnings now he is penniless!"

"That infernal chaplain has been telling tales!" Castlewood retorted.

"Dismiss him! Pay him his wages and let him go—he will be glad enough!" Maria cried.

"I keep him to marry one of my sisters, in case he is needed," Castlewood glared.

"What can the women be in a family where there are such men?" she asked.

"Effectivement!" he shrugged.

"What can we be, when our fathers and brothers are what they are? We are bad enough, but what are you? I say, you neither have courage, nor honour, nor common feeling. As your equals won't play with you, my Lord Castlewood, you must take this poor lad out of Virginia, your own kinsman, and pigeon him! Oh, it's a shame—a shame!"
