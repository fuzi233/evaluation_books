The signing of the Will was a far more hurried affair than I had anticipated, conducted with a haste that bordered on indecency. Samuel, the footman, was summoned to serve as the second witness, and the pen was placed directly into my aunt's trembling hand. I felt a strong urge to offer a few appropriate words on this solemn occasion, yet Mr. Bruff's demeanor convinced me that silence was the wiser course while he remained in the room. Within two minutes, the ceremony was concluded, and Samuel, receiving no spiritual benefit from my potential sermon, descended the stairs once more.

Mr. Bruff folded the document and glanced my way, seemingly uncertain whether to leave me alone with my aunt. I carried my mission of mercy upon my heart and my bag of sacred publications ready on my lap. He might as well have expected to topple St. Paul's Cathedral by mere inspection as to move me. Yet, he possessed one merit, likely due to his worldly training: he was quick to perceive things. He appeared to receive from me nearly the same impression as the cabman had; he too uttered a profane exclamation and retreated in a violent hurry, leaving me mistress of the field.

Immediately we were alone, my aunt reclined upon the sofa and alluded, with an air of confusion, to the subject of her Will.

"I hope you won't think yourself neglected, Drusilla," she said. "I mean to give you your little legacy, my dear, with my own hand."

Here lay a golden opportunity! Seizing it instantly, I opened my bag and extracted the top publication. It was an early edition—number twenty-five—of the famous anonymous work, believed to be by the virtuous Miss Bellows, titled *The Serpent at Home*. Its design, perhaps unfamiliar to the worldly reader, is to demonstrate how the Evil One lurks within the most innocuous actions of daily life. The chapters best suited for female perusal include "Satan in the Hair Brush," "Satan behind the Looking Glass," "Satan under the Tea Table," and "Satan out of the Window," among others.

"Give your attention, dear aunt, to this precious book," I said, handing it to her open at a marked passage—a continuous burst of burning eloquence. The subject was "Satan among the Sofa Cushions."

Poor Lady Verinder, reclining thoughtlessly upon her own cushions, glanced at the volume and handed it back, looking more bewildered than before.

"I'm afraid, Drusilla," she replied, "I must wait until I am a little better before I can read that. The doctor—"

The moment she mentioned the physician's name, I knew the obstacle. In my past experience among my perishing fellow-creatures, the members of that notoriously infidel profession had repeatedly stepped between me and my mission of mercy, claiming under the miserable pretext that the patient required quiet and that the disturbing influence of all others was that of Miss Clack and her books. This same blinded materialism, working treacherously behind my back, now sought to rob me of the only right of property my poverty could claim: my right of spiritual inheritance in my dying aunt.

"The doctor tells me," my poor misguided relative continued, "that I am not well today. He forbids me to see any strangers; and he orders me, if I read at all, only the lightest and most amusing books. 'Do nothing, Lady Verinder, to weary your head, or to quicken your pulse'—those were his last words, Drusilla, when he left me."

There was no choice but to yield—for the moment only. Any open assertion of the infinitely superior importance of my ministry compared to that of the medical man would only provoke the doctor to exploit the human weakness of his patient and threaten to abandon the case. Fortunately, there are more ways than one of sowing the good seed, and few are better versed in these methods than myself.

"You might feel stronger, dear, in an hour or two," I said. "Or you might wake tomorrow morning with a sense of something wanting, and even this unpretending volume might supply it. Will you let me leave the book, aunt? The doctor can hardly object to that!"

I slipped it under the sofa cushions, half in and half out, close by her handkerchief and smelling-bottle. Every time her hand searched for either item, it would brush against the book; sooner or later, the book might touch her. Having made this arrangement, I deemed it wise to withdraw. "Let me leave you to repose, dear aunt; I will call again tomorrow." As I spoke, I glanced accidentally toward the window. It was filled with flowers in boxes and pots. Lady Verinder was extravagantly fond of these perishable treasures and had a habit of rising to admire and smell them. A new idea flashed across my mind. "Oh! may I take a flower?" I said, moving unsuspectedly to the window. Instead of removing a flower, I added one—in the shape of another book from my bag—which I left to surprise my aunt among the geraniums and roses. The happy thought followed: "Why not do the same for her, poor dear, in every other room she enters?" I immediately bid farewell and crossed the hall, slipping into the library. Samuel, coming up to let me out and assuming I had departed, went downstairs again. On the library table, I noticed two of the "amusing books" recommended by the infidel doctor. Instantly, I covered them from sight with two of my own precious publications. In the breakfast-room, I found my aunt's favorite canary singing in his cage; she always fed the bird herself. Some groundsel was strewn on a table standing directly beneath the cage. I placed a book among the weeds. In the drawing-room, I discovered further opportunities to empty my bag. My aunt's favorite musical pieces lay on the piano; I slipped two more books among the sheet music. I disposed of another in the back drawing-room, hidden under some unfinished embroidery known to be Lady Verinder's work. A third small room opened from the back drawing-room, separated by curtains rather than a door. My aunt's plain, old-fashioned fan rested on the chimney-piece. I opened my ninth book at a particularly special passage and inserted the fan as a marker to keep the place. Then the question arose: Should I ascend higher still, risking the insult of being discovered by the person with the cap-ribbons in the upper regions of the house? But oh, what of that? It is a poor Christian who fears insult. I went upstairs, prepared to bear anything.

The house was silent and solitary; it was likely the servants' tea-time. My aunt's room was at the front. The miniature of my late dear uncle, Sir John, hung on the wall opposite the bed. It seemed to smile at me, whispering, "Drusilla! deposit a book." Tables stood on either side of the bed. She was a bad sleeper and often desired—or thought she desired—many things at night. I placed a book near the matches on one side and another under the box of chocolate drops on the other. Whether she sought a light or a drop, there was a precious publication to meet her eye or hand, silently urging, "Come, try me! try me!" One book remained in my bag, and but one apartment was unexplored: the bathroom, opening from the bedroom. I peeped in, and the holy inner voice that never deceives whispered, "You have met her, Drusilla, everywhere else; meet her at the bath, and the work is done." I observed a dressing-gown thrown across a chair; it had a pocket, and into that pocket I placed my final book. Can words express my exquisite sense of duty fulfilled when I slipped out of the house, unsuspected by anyone, and found myself in the street with my empty bag under my arm? Oh, my worldly friends, pursuing the phantom of Pleasure through the guilty mazes of Dissipation, how easy it is to be happy if you will only be good!

That night, when I folded up my things and reflected on the true riches I had scattered with such a lavish hand from the top to the bottom of my wealthy aunt's house, I declared I felt as free from anxiety as a child. I was so light-hearted that I sang a verse of the Evening Hymn, and so buoyant that I fell asleep before I could sing another. Quite like a child again! Quite like a child again!

Thus I passed that blissful night. Rising the next morning, I felt how young I was! I might add how young I looked, if I were capable of dwelling on the concerns of my own perishable body. But I am not capable, and I add nothing.

Towards luncheon time—not for the sake of creature comforts, but for the certainty of finding my dear aunt—I donned my bonnet to go to Montagu Square. Just as I was ready, the maid at my lodgings looked in and announced, "Lady Verinder's servant, to see Miss Clack."

I occupied the parlour-floor during my residence in London. The front parlour served as my sitting-room. It was very small, very low-ceilinged, and poorly furnished—but oh, so neat! I looked into the passage to identify which of Lady Verinder's servants had requested me. It was the young footman, Samuel—a civil, fresh-colored person with a teachable look and an obliging manner. I had always felt a spiritual interest in Samuel and a desire to try him with a few serious words. On this occasion, I invited him into my sitting-room.

He entered carrying a large parcel under his arm. When he set it down, it appeared to frighten him. "My lady's love, Miss; and I was to say that you would find a letter inside." Having delivered this message, the fresh-faced young footman surprised me by looking as though he wished to flee.

I detained him to make a few kind inquiries. Could I see my aunt at Montagu Square? No; she had gone out for a drive. Miss Rachel had accompanied her, and Mr. Ablewhite had taken a seat in the carriage as well. Knowing how sadly dear Mr. Godfrey's charitable work was in arrears, I found it odd that he should be going out driving like an idle man. I stopped Samuel at the door to make further inquiries. Miss Rachel was attending a ball that night, and Mr. Ablewhite had arranged to come for coffee and accompany her. A morning concert was advertised for the following day, and Samuel was ordered to secure places for a large party, including one for Mr. Ablewhite. "All the tickets may be gone, Miss," said this innocent youth, "if I don't run and get them at once!" He ran as he spoke, and I found myself alone again, burdened with anxious thoughts.

We had a special meeting of the Mothers'-Small-Clothes-Conversion Society that night, summoned expressly to obtain Mr. Godfrey's advice and assistance. Instead of sustaining our sisterhood under an overwhelming flow of trousers that prostrated our little community, he had arranged to take coffee in Montagu Square and attend a ball afterward! The afternoon of the next day had been selected for the Festival of the British-Ladies'-Servants'-Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision Society. Instead of being present, the life and soul of that struggling institution, he had engaged to join a party of worldlings at a morning concert! I asked myself what this meant. Alas, it meant that our Christian Hero was to reveal himself to me in a new character and become associated in my mind with one of the most awful backslidings of modern times.

Returning to the history of the passing day, upon finding myself alone in my room, I naturally turned my attention to the parcel that had so strangely intimidated the fresh-colored young footman. Had my aunt sent me my promised legacy? Would it take the form of cast-off clothes, worn-out silver spoons, unfashionable jewelry, or something similar? Prepared to accept all and resent nothing, I opened the parcel—and what met my view? The twelve precious publications I had scattered through the house the previous day, all returned to me by the doctor's orders! Well might the youthful Samuel shrink when bringing his parcel into my room! Well might he run after performing his miserable errand! As for my aunt's letter, it simply amounted, poor soul, to this—that she dared not disobey her medical man.

What was to be done now? With my training and principles, I never had a moment's doubt.

Once self-supported by conscience and embarked on a career of manifest usefulness, the true Christian never yields. Neither public nor private influences produce the slightest effect on us once we have secured our mission. Taxation may be the consequence of a mission; riots may follow; wars may ensue: we proceed with our work, irrespective of every human consideration that moves the world outside us. We are above reason; we are beyond ridicule; we see with nobody's eyes, hear with nobody's ears, feel with nobody's hearts, but our own. Glorious, glorious privilege! And how is it earned? Ah, my friends, you may spare yourselves the useless inquiry! We are the only people who can earn it—for we are the only people who are always right.

In the case of my misguided aunt, the form that pious perseverance was next to take revealed itself plainly.

Preparation by clerical friends had failed due to Lady Verinder's own reluctance. Preparation by books had failed owing to the doctor's infidel obstinacy. So be it! What was the next step? The next thing to try was—Preparation by Little Notes. In other words, since the books themselves had been sent back, select extracts from them, copied by various hands and addressed as letters to my aunt, were to be sent by post or distributed throughout the house according to the plan I had adopted the previous day. As letters, they would excite no suspicion; as letters, they would be opened—and once opened, they might be read. Some I wrote myself: "Dear aunt, may I ask your attention to a few lines?" "Dear aunt, I was reading last night and chanced upon the following passage," etc. Other letters were written for me by my valued fellow-workers, the sisterhood at the Mothers'-Small-Clothes: "Dear madam, pardon the interest taken in you by a true, though humble, friend."
