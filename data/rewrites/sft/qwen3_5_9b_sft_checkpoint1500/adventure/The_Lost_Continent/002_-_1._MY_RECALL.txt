The public ceremony had concluded. The verdict was pronounced, the name of Phorenice, the Empress, acclaimed, and the new Viceroy enthroned amidst a display of vast, ponderous ceremonial that had accumulated its grandeur over centuries. By right of form, I had surrendered the reins of government; by right of form, Tatho had seated himself upon the snake-throne and donned the gem-studded chain symbolizing supreme authority. Then, as the drums and trumpets blared their clamorous proclamation, he rose to his feet to begin his first state progress around the gilded council chamber as Viceroy of the Province of Yucatan.

With arms folded and head bowed, I followed him through lines of glittering soldiers and a brilliant throng of courtiers, chiefs, and statesmen. The roof-beams vibrated with the cries, "Long Live Tatho!" "Flourish the Empress!" uttered as duty bound, and the new ruler acknowledged the welcome with stately bows. In turn, he approached the three lesser thrones of the governors in the East, North, and South, receiving homage from each as ritual dictated. I, the man his arrival had deposed, followed in his train with the prescribed meekness.

It was a difficult burden, yet those who hold high office learn to wear a passionless mask before the people. Twenty years prior, these same gracious obeisances had been rendered to me. Now, the Gods had deemed it fitting to turn Fortune's wheel. Yet, as I walked humbly behind the heels of Tatho, though etiquette forbade loud salutations to myself, I could not suppress the gentle glances directed at me. Kind eyes swept over me from every soldier, courtier, and chief standing in that gilded hall, falling upon me with surprising gratitude. It is rare for the fallen to meet such tenderness.

Custom dictates that on such great days of changing rulers, those present may petition, accuse the retiring head with immunity, or propose theories for better governance. I confess, perhaps vainly, that not a single voice was raised against me, nor against any item of my twenty-year rule. Nor did anyone speak for future alterations. Even as we circled the chamber three times, all present offered their approval in generous silence.

Then, one behind the other, the new Viceroy and the old marched with formal steps over the golden tiles of the council hall beneath the pyramid. The great officers of state left their stations to join our train, and at the far wall, we reached the door of the private chambers—an hour ago, my own domain.

Ah, well! I had no home now among the wondrous cities of Yucatan. A bitterness welled within me, though truly, I ought to have been thankful to return to the Continent of Atlantis with my head still firmly attached.

Tatho issued his formal summons, "Open ye to the Viceroy," commanded by ritual, and the slaves within swung the massive stone valves of the door wide open. Tatho entered, I at his heels; the others halted, sending valedictions from the threshold, as the valves clanged shut behind us. We passed into the chamber beyond, and when, for the first time, we were alone together and the forced etiquette of courts lay behind us, the new Viceroy turned with meekly folded arms and bowed low before me.

"Deucalion," he said, "believe me, I have not sought this office. It was thrust upon me. Had I refused, my head would have paid the forfeit, and another man—your enemy—would have been sent out as Viceroy in your stead. The Empress permits no questioning of Her will."

"My friend," I answered, "my brother in all but blood, there lives no man in all Atlantis or her territories to whom I would rather entrust my government. For twenty years I have ruled this country of Yucatan and Mexico beyond, first under the old King, then as minister to this new Empress. I know my colony like a book. I am intimate with all her wonderful cities, their palaces, pyramids, and people. I have hunted beasts and savages in the forests, built roads, and altered the rivers to carry shipping. I have fostered arts and crafts like a merchant, preached the cult of the Gods three times daily with my own lips. Through evil and good years I have ruled here, striving solely for the prosperity of the land and the strength of Atlantis, growing to love these peoples like a father. To you, Tatho, I bequeath them, with tender supplication for their welfare."

"It is not I who can carry on Deucalion's work with Deucalion's power," he replied. "But rest assured, my friend, that I shall do my humble best to follow exactly in your footsteps. Believe me, I came to this government with a thousand regrets, yet I would have died sooner than take your place had I known how vigorously the displacement would trouble you."

"We are alone here," I said, "removed from the formalities of assemblies, where a man may give vent to his natural self without tarnishing ceremony. Your arrival was sudden. Until an hour ago, when you demanded audience, I intended to rule longer; and even now, I know not the cause for which I am deposed."

"The proclamation stated: 'We relieve our well-beloved Deucalion of his present service, because we have great need of his powers at home in our kingdom of Atlantis.'"

"A mere formality."

Tatho glanced uneasily around the hangings of the chamber, drawing me to its center and lowering his voice.

"I doubt that," he whispered. "I believe she needs you. Troubles brew on the horizon, and Phorenice requires the ablest men in the kingdom ready to her call."

"You may speak openly," I said, "without fear of eavesdroppers. We stand in the heart of the pyramid, built of solid stone laid by a man's hand. I myself oversaw every course. Besides, here in Yucatan, we lack the niceties of your old-world diplomacy; we do not listen, for we count it shameful to do so."

Tatho shrugged. "I acted according to my education. At home, a loose tongue makes a loose head, and there are those whose trade it is to carry tales. Nevertheless, the throne shakes, and Phorenice sees the need for sturdy props. Hence this proclamation."

"But why summon me? It has been twenty years since I sailed to this colony, and I have not returned to Atlantis once. I know little of the old country's politics. Whatever news drifts across the ocean reads with slender interest here. Yucatan is another world, my dear Tatho, as you will learn in your government—a realm of new interests, new people, everything new. To us here, Atlantis remains only a figment, a shadow far across the waters. I have striven for this new world of Yucatan through all these years."

"If Deucalion has scant time to brood over his fatherland, at least Atlantis has found leisure to admire the deeds of her brilliant son. Over yonder, your name carries magic. When we were lads, the colleges taught that men of the past were the greatest the world has ever seen; today, that teaching has changed. It is Deucalion who stands as the model and example. Mothers name their sons Deucalion, considering it the most precious gift. Deucalion is a household word. Indeed, there is only one name nearly equal to it in familiarity."

"You trouble me," I said, frowning. "I have strived to do my duty for its own sake and the country's, not for the patsy of the vulgar. Moreover, if names must fill everyone's mouth, they should be the names of the Gods."

Tatho shrugged again. "The Gods? They occupy us very little these latter years. With our modern science, we have grown past the tether of the older Gods, and no new deity has emerged. No, my Lord Deucalion, if it were merely the Gods competing for men's lips, your name would be a thousand times better known."

"Of mere human names," I countered, "the title of this new Empress should rank first in Atlantis, our old Lord King having now passed."

"She certainly would have it so," replied Tatho, and a certain weight in his tone suggested more lay behind the words. Drawing him to a marble seat, I leaned familiarly toward him. "I speak," I said, "not to the new Viceroy of Yucatan, but to my old friend Tatho, a member of the Priests' Clan, like myself, with whom I worked side by side in scores of minor home governments—from hamlet to town—as we gained experience in war and knowledge in the art of ruling, winning our promotions tediously. I speak in your private abode, which was mine but two hours hence, and I demand an answer with that plainness we once shared."

The new Viceroy sighed whimsically. "I almost forget how to speak plainly now," he admitted. "We have grown so polished in these later days that bare truth would be hissed as indelicate. But for the memory of those early years, when we expended as much law and thought over the ownership of a haystack as we now do over the fate of a rebellious city, I will strive to speak plainly to you, Deucalion. Tell me, old friend, what is it?"
