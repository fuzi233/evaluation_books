Twelve long, slow years had drifted by since I sealed the casket of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, away from mortal eyes in that uncanny, purpose-built mausoleum tucked in the shadow of Richmond’s oldest cemetery. All that time, his bizarre, unorthodox instructions for the tomb gnawed at the edge of my thoughts: rules that demanded he be interred in an open casket, that the colossal bolt mechanism securing the vault’s ton-heavy granite door could only ever be operated from the inside. No earthly logic I could name justified such a design.

Twelve years, too, had I pored over the tattered, dog-eared manuscript that man had left me – the manuscript of a man who could not recall a single childhood memory, who could not even hazard a guess as to his own age, who stayed as sharp and lithe as a thirty-year-old fighting man for as long as I had known him, and who had once bounced my great-great-grandfather on his knee. A man who spent ten full years on the dying red planet of Barsoom, fighting alongside the six-limbed green Thark hordes and against them, riding the great shaggy thoats across the crumbly rust-colored dead sea bottoms, clashing with the noble red city-states one day and allying with them the next, and who had won the heart of the incomparable Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, to become a beloved prince in the court of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.

Twelve years since his body was found sprawled on the wind-scoured bluff outside his Hudson-side cottage, no mark of violence on him, no trace of where he’d gone in the hours he vanished. Through every one of those years I’d wondered: was John Carter truly dead? Or was he already tramping those red dust-choked sea bottoms, racing to pry open the gates of Barsoom’s mighty global atmosphere plant – the plant he had been hurled forty-eight million miles back to Earth mere minutes from saving, on that terrible day millions of Barsoomians had choked on thinning air? I wondered if he’d found his black-haired princess waiting for him in the perfumed royal gardens of Helium, if the slender, red-skinned son he’d dreamed of while stranded on Earth was there clinging to Dejah Thoris’ hand. Or had he arrived too late? Trapped in a living death on a world already slipping into its final, cold grave? Or was he truly gone forever, never to set foot again on either his mother Earth or the red planet that had claimed all of his heart?

I was adrift in that exact useless speculation one sweltering, magnolia-scented August evening, when my old body servant Ben handed me a crumpled telegram. I tore it open at once, my fingers trembling:
> Meet me to-morrow hotel Raleigh Richmond.

I caught the very first train out for Richmond at dawn the next day, and two hours later was shown into his sunlit hotel suite. He stood to greet me the second I crossed the threshold, that familiar, warm, battle-worn smile spreading across his sharp, handsome face. He had not aged a single day. Straight, broad-shouldered, clean-limbed, the exact same fighting man of thirty I had first met decades before. His keen grey eyes were as bright and sharp as a Martian radium blade, the only lines on his face the deep-set marks of iron courage and unbreakable determination I had known there for thirty-five years.

“Well, nephew,” he laughed, clapping me on the shoulder, “do you reckon you’re staring at a ghost, or that old Ben slipped too much bourbon in your last mint julep?”
“Julep, I think,” I laughed back, “because I’ve never felt better. But it’s more the sight of you. You’ve been back to Barsoom, haven’t you? Tell me – is Dejah Thoris well? Did she wait for you?”

“I went back, aye, to Barsoom,” he said, his voice softening, “and the story is long, far too long for the few hours I have left before I must return. I have uncovered the secret now, nephew: I can stride across the empty, trackless void between worlds at will, come and go between a thousand different planets as the mood takes me. But my heart will never leave Barsoom, not while it lives safe in the keeping of my Martian princess. That dying red world is my life now.
I came back to Earth one last time only to see you, before you pass on to that other afterlife I will never know. I have died three times already, as you measure death, and I will die again tonight – but no matter how many times I cross that threshold, I can no more fathom its mysteries than you can. Even the ancient, secretive therns of Barsoom, that cult that has hidden in their impregnable mountain fastness on the near slopes of the Mountains of Otz for a million years, claiming they hold all the secrets of life and death, know no more of it than we do. I proved that, too – though I nearly lost my life doing it. You will find all of it written down in the notes I have scribbled over the last three months I spent on Earth.” He patted the bulging leather portfolio sitting on the table at his elbow.

“I know you will believe me, no matter how wild the tale sounds. The rest of the world will be curious, too, but they will not believe it for decades – for centuries, most like. Earth’s people have not yet grown to a place where they can wrap their heads around the things that happened to me on Barsoom. Publish whatever parts of the notes you think the world is ready for, whatever will do no harm. Do not feel hurt if they laugh at you for it.”

That night, the two of us walked down the tree-lined path to the old cemetery together, fireflies dancing in the thick dark air. At the heavy granite door of his mausoleum, he turned and squeezed my hand hard, calloused from a thousand battles.
“Goodbye, nephew,” he said. “I will likely never see you again. I cannot bear to leave my wife and my boy while they still live, and Barsoomians live for more than a thousand years, most of them.”

He stepped inside the vault. The massive bronze door swung slowly shut behind him. The heavy iron bolts grated home with a low, thunderous rumble. The lock clicked.

I have never laid eyes on Captain John Carter of Virginia ever since.

What follows is the full story of his return to Mars, his second great quest to find Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, pieced together from the thousands of pages of notes he left stacked on that hotel table in Richmond. I have left out parts too terrible, too unthinkable for mortal men of Earth to know yet – but the tale you are about to read is even more wild, more glorious, more pulse-pounding than the first manuscript I shared with a disbelieving world, the one that carried you across Barsoom’s dead sea bottoms beneath the glow of the twin Martian moons.
