Caroline Helstone was just eighteen, an age at which life has not yet set its pot to a true, steady boil. Before then one lives as if in an old tale told by a winter hearth—now sweet as sugared milk, now bitter as burnt crust—but always with a taste of unreality on the tongue. The world is too coloured, too strong in scent and sound: moons look like silver fruit you might pluck; suns blaze like ovens where gods bake their bread. You believe in marvels as you believe in morning.

At eighteen the edge of that country is reached. Behind lies Elf-land with its bright deceit; ahead, the shores of Reality lift themselves—blue and soft at a distance, like meadows seen through steam rising from a kettle. You imagine, if you once step there, you will never hunger, never thirst, never be chilled again. You do not know how many stony fields must be crossed before any table is spread; how often the very water you must drink will be cold as grief. Joy, when it comes at all, is earned; it is not handed over like a loaf still warm from the baker’s board. There are prizes for which one must sweat, and bleed, and still keep one’s hands steady.

Caroline did not yet know this with the knowledge that sits in the bones. Hope, when she leaned near and promised, was believed as easily as a child believes the dinner-bell. Love, when he wandered like a lost angel to the door, was taken in and cherished; his quiver went unseen. If his arrows struck, the wound felt like a new life, a quickening. No one warns you then about the barbs no hand can draw out; no one tells you how passion can be an ache that never quite ceases.

She had been convoyed home by Robert Moore, and when she reached the rectory she had no longing to spend the remainder of the evening with her uncle. The room where he sat was sacred ground—more like a vestry than a parlour in her mind—and she seldom entered it unless summoned. She kept aloof until the bell rang for prayers.

Part of the evening church service was read, as usual, in Mr. Helstone’s household. He delivered it in that clear, loud, nasal monotone which made devotion seem as mechanical as a mill-wheel. When it was done, Caroline went to him in her accustomed way.

“Good-night, uncle.”

“Hey! You’ve been gadding abroad all day—visiting, dining out, and what not!”

“Only at the cottage.”

“And have you learned your lessons?”

“Yes.”

“And made a shirt?”

“Only part of one.”

“Well, that will do. Stick to the needle, learn shirt-making and gown-making and piecrust-making, and you’ll be a clever woman some day. Go to bed now. I’m busy with a pamphlet here.”

Piecrust-making. The words had a homely sound; she could almost smell flour and butter and the clean heat of an oven. Yet even that domestic counsel came from him like a rule barked out, not like kindness. She went up to her small bedroom, bolted the door, and exchanged day-dress for a white dressing-gown. Then she loosened her hair. It fell thick and soft, a heavy wave to her waist; and as she combed, the slow drawing of the teeth through it—like the hush of a brush on wheat—seemed to quiet the room.

She paused, elbow on knee, cheek on hand, eyes on the carpet. At eighteen, such pauses are not empty. Visions rose as naturally as scent from warm bread.

Her thoughts spoke to her, and she listened with a smile. Something brighter than her own young face seemed present there: the spirit of Hope, full of flattering prophecies. According to that sweet deceiver she had done with chill and disappointment. The dawn she had entered was no false light; it was the true beginning of morning, and her sun would rise quickly.

“When people love, the next step is they marry,” she argued with herself, as if laying out ingredients on a board in the order they must be used. “Now, I love Robert, and I feel sure Robert loves me. I have thought it many a time before; to-day I felt it. When I looked up after repeating Chénier’s poem, his eyes—what handsome eyes he has!—sent the truth straight through my heart.”

She remembered the lane, the evening air, their footsteps together, and the hush between them that had not been awkward, only deep, like the pause before music resumes.

“Sometimes I am afraid to speak to him,” she went on, “lest I be too frank—lest I seem forward. I have regretted words—overflowing, superfluous words—and feared he would disapprove what he might deem my indiscretion. But to-night I could have ventured anything: he was so indulgent. How kind he was as we walked up the lane! He does not flatter, or say foolish things. His love-making—friendship, I mean; of course I don’t yet account him my lover, but I hope he will be so some day—is not like what we read of in books. It is far better—original, quiet, manly, sincere.”

She drew the comb once more through her hair, and the sound, regular and soft, seemed to keep time with her certainties.

“I do like him; I would be an excellent wife to him if he did marry me. I would tell him of his faults—for he has a few faults—but I would study his comfort, and cherish him, and do my best to make him happy. Now, I am sure he will not be cold to-morrow. I feel almost certain that to-morrow evening he will either come here, or ask me to go there.”

Turning her head, she caught herself in the glass. Such reflections sober the plain; but Caroline, seen so—head inclined, hair loose, the softness of youth still unclouded—looked lovely. The sight seemed to confirm Hope’s whisper. She lay down and slept with gladness unbroken.

In the morning she rose with the same warmth in her veins. When she entered her uncle’s breakfast-room and wished him good-morning with a gentle cheerfulness, even he—bronze little man that he was—thought, for an instant, that she was growing “a fine girl.” Usually she was quiet with him, docile and uncommunicative; but to-day words came to her. They were small words—safe words—like the modest fare one sets before a fastidious guest.

She told him she had walked early in the garden and that certain flowers were beginning to spring; she asked when the gardener would come to trim the borders; she spoke of starlings building in the church-tower, and wondered that the tolling of the bells did not frighten them away. Through the open window the village soundscape drifted in—the faint clink of a pail, the far call of a voice, the rustle of trees disturbed by morning wind; and near at hand, the steady hush of the old house breathing.

Mr. Helstone opined that “they were like other fools who had just paired—insensible to inconvenience just for the moment.”

Caroline, made a little bolder by her good spirits, hazarded a remark she had never before dared.

“Uncle,” said she, “whenever you speak of marriage you speak of it scornfully. Do you think people shouldn’t marry?”

“It is decidedly the wisest plan to remain single, especially for women.”

“Are all marriages unhappy?”

“Millions of marriages are unhappy. If everybody confessed the truth, perhaps all are more or less so.”

“You are always vexed when you are asked to come and marry a couple. Why?”

“Because one does not like to act as accessory to the commission of a piece of pure folly.”

He spoke with a readiness that suggested relish in the topic—like a man pleased to season his discourse with pepper for the mere sting of it. Caroline, surprised to find herself not struck dumb, went further.

“But why should it be pure folly? If two people like each other, why shouldn’t they consent to live together?”

“They tire of each other—they tire of each other in a month. A yokefellow is not a companion; he or she is a fellow-sufferer.”

There was no naïve innocence in what Caroline said next; it sprang from real dislike—almost anger—at his cold conclusions.

“One would think you had never been married, uncle. One would think you were an old bachelor.”

“Practically, I am so.”

“But you have been married. Why were you so inconsistent as to marry?”

“Every man is mad once or twice in his life.”

“So you tired of my aunt, and my aunt of you, and you were miserable together?”

He pushed out his cynical lip, wrinkled his brown forehead, and gave an inarticulate grunt.

“Did she not suit you? Was she not good-tempered? Did you not get used to her? Were you not sorry when she died?”

“Caroline,” said Mr. Helstone, bringing his hand slowly down and then smiting the mahogany table with a sudden blow, “understand this: it is vulgar and puerile to confound generals with particulars. In every case there is the rule and there are the exceptions. Your questions are stupid and babyish. Ring the bell, if you have done breakfast.”

The breakfast things were cleared away. Usually uncle and niece separated until dinner; but to-day Caroline did not leave the room. She went to the window-seat and sat looking out. Mr. Helstone glanced round uneasily, wishing her gone; but she stared through the panes as if she had forgotten him, and he returned to his newspaper. It was full of the Peninsula—dispatches from General Lord Wellington, columns of marching and counter-marching. He read with the absorbed interest of a man who prefers the ordered noise of war on paper to the unruly music of feeling in his own house.

He did not know what stir his niece’s mind endured. The talk of marriage had revived old thoughts—had not sown them, for they had lived there long—like bees in a hive disturbed.

She reviewed his character and his scorn. And on the far side of the gulf between them, she saw another figure—dim, sinister, half-remembered: the image of her father, James Helstone, her uncle’s brother.

Rumours had reached her from servants: hints dropped and swallowed again. She knew he had not been good, and that he had never been kind to her. She remembered—darkly, but with a chill that never quite left—some weeks spent with him in a great town. No maid attended her there. She had been shut up day and night in a high garret without a carpet; an uncurtained bed, bare boards, scant furniture; the air close with the stale smell of poverty and neglect, as if no pot ever simmered, no clean hearth ever warmed the place. He went out early each morning and would forget to return to give her dinner; hunger, for a child, is a loud thing, yet the house kept its silence as if it were deaf. At night he came back either raging—furious, terrible—or, worse, vacant and senseless, as if the man within had been blotted out.

She knew she had fallen ill there. One night, when she was very sick, he came raving into the room and said he would kill her, for she was a burden. Her screams had brought aid. From the moment she was rescued, she never saw him again—except as a dead man in his coffin.

That was her father.

She had a mother too, though Mr. Helstone never spoke of her, and Caroline could not remember having seen her; yet she knew she lived. That mother was then the drunkard’s wife. What had their marriage been? Watching the starlings—without truly seeing them—Caroline turned from the lattice and, in a low voice, with bitterness subdued into sadness, broke the silence.

“You term marriage miserable, I suppose, from what you saw of my father and mother. If my mother suffered what I suffered when I was with papa, she must have had a dreadful life.”

Mr. Helstone wheeled about in his chair and looked over his spectacles. He was taken aback.

Her father and mother—those names he had avoided for twelve years—spoken at last in the open breakfast-room. He could not imagine the thoughts were self-matured; he could not credit that she remembered, or reasoned.

“Your father and mother? Who has been talking to you about them?”

“Nobody; but I remember something of what papa was, and I pity mamma. Where is she?”

That question had been in Caroline’s throat a hundred times; she had swallowed it always, as one swallows a mouthful too hot. Now it came out.

“I hardly know,” returned Mr. Helstone. “I was little acquainted with her. I have not heard from her for years: but wherever she is, she thinks nothing of you; she never inquires about you. I have reason to believe she does not wish to see you. Come, it is school-time. You go to your cousin at ten, don’t you? The clock has struck.”

Perhaps Caroline would have said more; but Fanny came in to say the churchwardens wanted to speak with him in the vestry. He rose and went quickly, glad of the interruption; and Caroline soon set out for the cottage.

The road from the rectory to Hollow’s Mill inclined downward. She ran almost all the way, as if speed could shake off what she had heard. The air was fresh; the hedges held a green dampness; birds stitched their small sounds through the morning—sounds that soothed, because they belonged to old, living things, not to pamphlets and cynicism. And beneath these, as she drew nearer, there began to come the heavier voice of labour: the mill’s thunder, the rush of the watercourse, that constant industrial roar that filled the valley like a hard wind that never changed.

Exercise and the thought of seeing Robert—if only of being near him, within his precincts—revived her quickly. When the white house came in sight, and the sound of the wheel and stream grew loud enough to press against the ear, the first thing she saw was Moore standing at his garden-gate. He wore a belted Holland blouse and a light cap—an undress that suited him, making him seem less a master and more a man of the place. He was looking down the lane, not in the direction from which she approached.

Caroline paused and drew a little behind a willow. She studied him.

“He has not his peer,” she thought, the words tasting like admiration itself. “He is as handsome as he is intelligent. What a keen eye he has! What clearly-cut, spirited features—thin and serious, but graceful! I do like his face, I do like his aspect, I do like him so much—better than any of those shuffling curates, for instance—better than anybody; bonny Robert!”

She stepped out and went to him.

For his part, when he saw her, it seemed he would have vanished like a phantom if he could; but being solid—tall fact, not fiction—he had to stand and meet her. His greeting was brief. It was cousin-like, brother-like, friend-like—anything but lover-like. The nameless charm of last night had fled from his manner. He was no longer the same man; or, if the man remained, the heart within had shut its door.

The disappointment struck sharp. At first she would not believe it, though she saw and felt it as plainly as one tastes salt. It was hard to withdraw her hand from his until he gave something—some warmer pressure, some tenderness—to justify it. It was hard to turn her eyes away until his eyes had spoken what she longed to hear. But his look was cool welcome only; his hand did not linger.

What could she do? A man may demand explanation; a woman—if she has any instinct for self-preservation—keeps silence. To speak would be to betray herself and then to be punished afterwards by that inward thunder of self-contempt. Nature, in such hours, is an austere guardian: she seals the lips, commands a smooth face, orders the heart to endure without sob or outcry.

Caroline endured. Robert had done her no wrong; he had spoken no lie. If she had made a romance of his kindness, it was her own weaving. He had sometimes seemed fond of her—because she had tried so hard to be pleasing, and even his efforts could not always prevent his manner from showing a warmth his judgment disallowed. Now he meant to withdraw, to disentangle himself, to avoid a marriage he believed imprudent.

So she did not cling, did not question, did not reproach. She left him quietly; went on to her lessons with Hortense as usual; and at dinner-time returned home without lingering.

When she had dined, and found herself alone in the rectory drawing-room—her uncle lingering over a temperate glass of port—she met a new difficulty: How am I to get through this day?

Last night she had expected the evening to resemble yesterday’s. She had hoped for Robert, for that sweetness of companionship that seemed to feed her better than any meal. This morning had taught her her mistake; yet she could not persuade her heart to settle into certainty. False Hope kept whispering: he may come still.

He had walked up after tea more than once to spend an hour with Mr. Helstone. The bell had rung at twilight; his voice had been heard in the passage when she least expected such a happiness. Twice it had happened after he had treated her with peculiar reserve; and though he seldom spoke to her in her uncle’s presence, he had looked at her with something like relenting as he sat opposite her work-table. A few words, dropped casually, had comforted her; his good-night had been genial.

To-day—why not to-day again?

She tried to read, but the lines slipped away from meaning. She tried to sew, but each stitch was an ennui, each draw of thread a little act of endurance; needlework, which might have been soothing if her mind had been quiet, became mere torture of repetition. She opened her desk and attempted a French composition; she wrote nothing but mistakes, as if even grammar revolted.

Then the door-bell rang sharply.

Her heart leaped; she sprang to the drawing-room door and opened it softly, peeping through the narrow gap. Fanny was admitting a visitor—a gentleman—a tall man—Robert’s height. For one second Caroline believed it was Robert; for one second she exulted.

But the voice asking for Mr. Helstone undeceived her. It was an Irish voice—Malone’s, the curate’s—not Moore’s. He was shown into the dining-room, where, no doubt, he would presently help the rector to empty the decanters.
