When we were first presented to Sir Miles Warrington at the King's Drawing-Room in St. James's Palace, I confess that I, for one—casting my eye upon his jovial, round visage, his ample waistcoat, and his hearty, rustic demeanor—anticipated that I had finally discovered a most eligible and agreeable acquaintance. I was ready to become intimate with what many consider the noblest specimen of humanity: the celebrated "Good Old Country Gentleman." Indeed, to be such a figure is to occupy a station nearest the gods, atop the pinnacle of earthly bliss.

Imagine possessing a vast, unencumbered estate whose rents flow in regularly, collected by adoring farmers who bless their stars for having such a landlord. Imagine having no tenant withhold payment unless it serves merely as an occasion for the Good Old Country Gentleman to display his sublime charity and universal benevolence. Hunt three days a week, love the sport above all else, and maintain perfect health and a voracious appetite. Enjoy not only a good appetite but a magnificent dinner. Sit in church amidst a chorus of blessings from the villagers, revered as the first man in the parish and its chief benefactor, while maintaining a conscious humility—a "miserable sinner"—solely for form's sake, to provide an example for others. Such a person is healthy, wealthy, jolly, deeply respected by the vicar, honored by tenants, and beloved by his family, whose laughter echoes through generations recounting tales of grouse-shooting in the gunroom. This perfect being, declaring himself a miserable sinner! *Allons donc!* Give any man robust health, a temper, five thousand pounds a year, the adoration of his parish, and the worship of his family, and I defy anyone to make him so heartily dissatisfied with his spiritual state as to truly call himself miserable.

If you were a Royal Highness, attending church in perfect health and comfort, with the parson waiting for your arrival, would you believe yourself a "miserable sinner"? Only if racked with gout, isolated in solitude, fearing death, and forced to subsist on arrowroot instead of claret might you feel such humiliation. But in high health, sunshine, and spirits, the word "miserable" is merely a formality. You cannot genuinely pity yourself. Consider Colin Ploughman, suffering from the ague, supporting seven children, owing two pounds a year for his cottage, and scraping by on eight shillings a week. No: a healthy, rich, jolly country gentleman, if miserable, possesses a very supportable misery; if sinful, he has very few witnesses to accuse him. Perhaps he grows slightly selfish, but at least he is content with himself. Aside from the Earl at the castle, there is no one within miles so good or so great. His admirable wife ministers to him and the entire parish; his children bow before him; the local vicar reveres him; he commands respect at quarter sessions; poachers tremble at his approach; hats fly off at the market; and around his great coach, laden with his spotless daughters and sublime lady, the tradespeople cringe bareheaded, while farmers' wives drop countless curtseys. From their cushions, the ladies gaze down beneficently, smiling upon the poor. They buy yards of ribbon with affability, condescend to purchase ounces of salts or packets of seeds, and deign to haggle over geese. Their drive resembles a royal progress, with a supposedly happy populace pressing forward to bless them. Tradesmen bow, farmers' wives bob, and town boys wave ragged hats, cheering the red-faced coachman driving the fat bays, shouting, "Sir Miles for ever! Throw us a halfpenny, my lady!"

Yet, suppose the market-woman hides her fat goose when Sir Miles's coach arrives, terrified that my lady, spotting the bird, will demand it as a bargain? Suppose no copper coins ever emerge from the royal coach window? Suppose Sir Miles regales his tenants with notoriously weak beer and his poor with especially thin broth? This may indeed be the way of our fine old English gentleman. There have been many such figures—gentlemen and ladies who patronized the poor without ever truly relieving them, who shouted "Amen!" at church as loudly as the clerk, who performed every form of piety and discharged all the etiquette of old English gentlemanhood, buying virtue as a bargain and believing themselves to honor it through mere purchase.

Poor Harry, in his distress, sought help from his relations. His aunt sent him a tract and her blessing; his uncle had business out of town and could not, of course, answer the poor boy's petition. How much of this behavior occurs daily in respectable life, do you think? You can imagine Lord and Lady Macbeth conspiring a murder, meeting with some awkwardness once the deed is done. But consider Lord and Lady Skinflint consulting in their bedroom about refusing a helping hand to their luckless nephew, deciding against it, descending to family prayers, greeting their children and domestics, discoursing virtuously before them, remaining together, and talking nose-to-nose. What can they possibly think of one another? Of the poor kinsman fallen among thieves, groaning for help unheeded? How can they maintain those virtuous airs? How can they dare look each other in the face? Dare? Do you suppose they believe they have done wrong? Does Skinflint torture himself with remorse regarding the distress he ignored and the hunger he sent away empty? Not he. He is indignant with the Prodigal for being a fool, not ashamed of himself for being a curmudgeon. What? A young man with such opportunities throwing them away? A fortune squandered on gamblers and spendthrifts? Horrible, horrible! "Take warning, my child, by this unfortunate young man's behavior, and see the consequences of extravagance."

According to the great and always Established Church of the Pharisees, here lies an admirable opportunity for a moral discourse and an assertion of virtue. "And to think of his deceiving us so!" cries out Lady Warrington. "Very sad, very sad, my dear!" says Sir Miles, wagging his head. "To think of so much extravagance in one so young!" cries Lady Warrington. "Cards, bets, feasts at taverns of the most wicked profusion, carriages and riding horses, the company of the wealthy and profligate of his own sex, and, I fear, of the most iniquitous persons of ours."

"Hush, my Lady Warrington!" cries her husband, glancing toward the spotless Dora and Flora, who hold down their blushing heads at the mention of the last "naughty persons."

"No wonder my poor children hide their faces!" mamma continues. "My dears, I wish even the existence of such creatures could be kept from you!"

"They can't go to an opera, or the park, without seeing 'em, to be sure," says Sir Miles. "To think we should have introduced such a young serpent into the bosom of our family! And have left him in the company of that guileless darling!" She points to Master Miles.

"Who's a serpent, mamma?" inquires that youth.

"First you said cousin Harry was bad: then he was good: now he is bad again. Which is he, Sir Miles?"

"He has faults, like all of us, Miley, my dear. Your cousin has been wild, and you must take warning by him."

"Was not my elder brother, who died—my naughty brother—was not he wild too? He was not kind to me when I was quite a little boy. He never gave me money, nor toys, nor rode with me, nor..." Why do you cry, mamma? "Sure I remember how Hugh and you were always fighting—"

"Silence, sir!" cry out papa and the girls in a breath. "Don't you know you are never to mention that name?"

"I know I love Harry, and I didn't love Hugh," says the sturdy little rebel. "And if cousin Harry is in prison, I'll give him my half-guinea that my godpapa gave me, and anything I have—yes, anything, except... except my little horse—and my silver waistcoat—and—and Snowball and Sweetlips at home—and—and, yes, my custard after dinner." This was in reply to a hint from sister Dora.

"But I'd give him some of it," continues Miles, after a pause.

"Shut thy mouth with it, child, and then go about thy business," says papa, amused. Sir Miles Warrington possessed a considerable fund of easy humor.

"Who would have thought he should ever be so wild?" mamma goes on.

"Nay. Youth is the season for wild oats, my dear."

"That we should be so misled in him!" sighed the girls.

"That he should kiss us both!" cries papa.

"Sir Miles Warrington, I have no patience with that sort of vulgarity!" says the majestic matron.

"Which of you was the favorite yesterday, girls?" continues the father.

"Favourite, indeed! I told him over and over again of my engagement to dear Tom—I did, Dora—why do you sneer, if you please?" says the handsome sister.

"Nay, to do her justice, so did Dora too," said papa.

"Because Flora seemed to wish to forget her engagement with dear Tom sometimes," remarks the sister.
