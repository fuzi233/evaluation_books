I won’t go into every detail of our journey from Durban to Sitanda’s Kraal, near where the Lukanga and Kalukwe Rivers meet. It was over a thousand miles—three hundred of which we walked, thanks to the dreaded tsetse fly, whose bite kills most animals but leaves men and donkeys unharmed. We left Durban at the end of January and reached our destination in mid-May. Along the way, we faced countless trials—some common to any African explorer, others unique—but only one incident stands out as truly worth telling. The rest, I’ll leave aside, lest this tale grow too long.

At Inyati, a trading post on the edge of Matabeleland ruled by the ruthless Lobengula, we said goodbye to our wagon. Of the twenty oxen I’d bought in Durban, only twelve remained. One died from a cobra bite, three starved or succumbed to lack of water, one wandered off, and three more ate the poisonous "tulip" plant. Five others fell ill from it, but we saved them with a tea brewed from boiled tulip leaves—an effective antidote if given early. The wagon and surviving oxen were left in charge of Goza and Tom, our trusted driver and guide, under the watchful eye of a kind Scotch missionary who lived nearby.

With Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and half a dozen local bearers hired along the way, we set out on foot. The moment we stepped away from the wagon, there was silence among us. Each man seemed to wonder whether we’d ever see that wagon again. For me, it felt like a farewell to the world I knew.

We marched for a while in quiet, until Umbopa, walking ahead, began singing an old Zulu chant about brave men who abandoned comfort to wander into unknown lands—only to find, after great hardship, a land rich with game, cattle, and enemies to fight. When he finished, we all laughed—it felt like a sign.

Umbopa had a rare gift: he could lift spirits even in the worst conditions. He wasn’t always cheerful—he often fell into deep, brooding moods—but when he was himself, he brought light to the camp. We came to love him.

Now, for the story I’ve been waiting to tell—one that any hunter would remember.

About two weeks from Inyati, we entered a stretch of lush woodland, cradled between hills. Thick bush covered the ravines—what the natives called *idoro*—and patches of *wacht-een-beche*, or “wait-a-little thorn,” grew alongside towering *machabell* trees, heavy with golden fruit and massive seeds. Elephants loved these trees, and the signs were everywhere: broken branches, uprooted trunks, deep hoofprints. These creatures were not gentle feeders—they tore through the forest like storms.

One evening, after a long march, we reached a beautiful spot. At the base of a wooded hill lay a dry riverbed, dotted with crystal pools surrounded by animal tracks. Opposite stood a wide plain scattered with flat-topped mimosa and glossy machabells, all bordered by endless, silent bush.

As we stepped into the riverbed, we startled a herd of giraffes. They moved with their strange, gliding gait, tails curled over their backs, hooves clattering like castanets. They were three hundred yards away—too far to shoot. But Good, walking ahead with his express rifle loaded with solid ball, couldn’t resist. He raised his gun and fired at the last, a young cow.

By some miracle, the bullet struck her neck directly, shattering her spine. She collapsed instantly, rolling forward like a rabbit. I’d never seen anything so odd.

“Curse it!” Good exclaimed—though I should warn you, he had a habit of swearing when excited, likely picked up during his time at sea. “I’ve killed her!”

“_Ou_, Bougwan!” the Kafirs shouted. “_Ou! ou!_” They called him “Bougwan”—Glass Eye—for his monocle.

“Oh, ‘Bougwan!’” echoed Sir Henry and I. From that day on, Good became legendary among the Kafirs for his shot—even though he wasn’t really much of a marksman. But we forgave his misses because of that giraffe.

We sent some of the boys to cut meat from the carcass, then built a *scherm*—a circular thorn hedge—near one of the pools. Inside, we smoothed the ground, laid down dry grass, and lit fires. By the time we finished, the moon rose, and our dinner of giraffe steak and roasted marrowbones was ready.

That marrowbone was a treat—cracking it was hard, but once inside, the richness was unmatched. Only elephant heart rivals it, and we’d have that tomorrow.

We ate by moonlight, laughing and praising Good’s skill. Then we smoked and talked, squatting around the fire. The scene must have looked strange: I, short and dark, with grizzled hair standing upright; Sir Henry, tall and fair, with long yellow locks; and Captain John Good, sitting neatly on a leather bag, dressed like he’d just stepped out of a country estate.

He wore a brown tweed shooting suit, matching hat, and neat gaiters. His face was shaved clean, his false teeth and monocle perfectly in place. Even his collar—made of white gutta-percha—was fastened tight. “They weigh nothing,” he told me when I asked why he carried them. “And I always like to look like a gentleman.”

Ah, if only he’d known what awaited him.

There we sat, three men in the wild, sharing stories under the stars, while the Kafirs sipped their intoxicating *daccha* from an eland-horn pipe. One by one, they rolled into blankets and slept—except Umbopa, who sat apart, chin in hand, lost in thought. He rarely joined the others.

Then, from the bush behind us, came a deep, rumbling *woof, woof*.

“That’s a lion,” I said.

We all stood alert.

Almost at once, from the pool a hundred yards away, came a thunderous trumpet—a sound no one could mistake.

“_Unkungunklovo_! _Indlovu_!” the Kafirs whispered. “Elephant! Elephant!”

A few minutes later, shadowy forms emerged from the water, moving slowly toward the trees.

Good leapt up, eager for a kill, perhaps thinking elephants were as easy as giraffes. I grabbed his arm.

“It’s no good,” I whispered. “Let them go.”

Sir Henry, however, broke in. “This is paradise. Let’s stay a couple of days. We can try for them.”

I was surprised—he’d always pushed forward, especially since learning that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon at Inyati and vanished into the interior. But now, his hunting instincts took over.

Good jumped at the idea. So did I—though I felt guilty letting such a herd pass without a shot.

“All right,” I said. “Let’s rest. We’ll be up before dawn and catch them feeding.”

We agreed. Good undressed carefully, shook his clothes, placed his monocle and false teeth in his pocket, folded everything neatly, and put it under his mackintosh to keep it dry. Sir Henry and I made do with simpler arrangements, then curled into our blankets and fell into a deep, dreamless sleep.

Then—*going, going, go*—what was that?

From the direction of the water came loud scuffling, followed by a series of terrifying roars.

No doubt about it—lions.

We sprang up, staring toward the pool. A shape—yellow and black—staggered toward us, tumbling over itself.

We grabbed rifles and slipped on our veldtschoons—shoes of untanned hide—and ran.

The mass had fallen. Now we saw what it was: a dead sable antelope bull, magnificent in its dark coat and sweeping horns. Transfixed through its skull was a great black-maned lion—also dead.

It was clear what happened: the antelope came to drink. The lion pounced—but was caught by the horns, impaled. Unable to escape, it tore at the bull’s neck, which charged blindly until both dropped dead.

We examined the bodies, then dragged them to the scherm. After that, we returned to bed, waking only at dawn.

At first light, we prepared for the hunt. We took the three eight-bore rifles, ammunition, and large water bottles filled with weak cold tea—the best thing to drink before a shoot, I’ve found.

After a quick breakfast, we set out, with Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel in tow. The others stayed to skin the lion and cut up the antelope.

We found the elephant trail easily—broad and fresh. Ventvögel estimated twenty to thirty elephants, mostly full-grown bulls. But they’d moved on during the night. It wasn’t until nine o’clock, when the sun beat down fiercely, that we spotted signs: broken trees, crushed bark, and steaming dung.

Then, we saw them—about two hundred yards away—standing in a hollow, ears flapping, having just fed.

It was a glorious sight.

I tossed dry grass into the air to check the wind. If they smelled us, they’d vanish before we could fire. Fortunately, the breeze blew from them to us.

We crept forward, using the cover, and got within forty yards. Three huge bulls stood broadside—especially one with enormous tusks.

I whispered my plan: I’d take the middle one, Sir Henry the left, and Good the big-tusked bull.

“Now,” I said.

*Boom! Boom! Boom!* The rifles cracked.

Sir Henry’s elephant fell dead—shot through the heart. Mine dropped to its knees, but rose and charged past me. I fired again—into its ribs—and it went down for good. I rushed forward, reloaded, and ended it with a shot to the brain.

Then I turned to Good. He was agitated. The big bull had charged him after being hit, forcing him to dive aside. It passed blindly, heading back toward camp.

The herd bolted in panic.

We debated chasing the wounded bull or the herd. We chose the herd, thinking we’d seen the last of those tusks.

But I’ve often wished we hadn’t.

Following the elephants was easy—their trail was like a road, crushing the bush as if it were straw. But catching them was harder. We trudged under the blazing sun for over two hours before finding them.

All but one bull stood together. They were restless, lifting trunks to sniff the air. The lone bull stood fifty yards off, acting as lookout—sixty yards from us.

We decided not to approach. Instead, we aimed at the sentry bull and, at my signal, fired.

The three shots hit. He fell dead.

The herd stampeded again—this time straight into a nullah, a dried-up watercourse with steep banks. It reminded me of the spot where Prince Imperial was killed in Zululand.

The elephants plunged in, scrambling wildly to climb the other side. They screamed and cried, pushing each other aside in blind terror—just like panicked humans.

Now was our chance.

We fired as fast as we could reload. Five elephants fell. Had they not suddenly stopped trying to climb and instead charged down the nullah, we might have taken them all.

We were tired, and perhaps a little sick of killing. Eight elephants in one day was enough.

After resting and cutting hearts from two of the dead beasts for supper, we headed back—pleased with our work. We planned to send the bearers next day to collect the tusks.

Shortly after passing where Good had wounded the old bull, we saw a herd of eland. We didn’t shoot—we already had meat. They trotted past, then paused behind a patch of bush a hundred yards away, turning to watch us.

Good wanted a closer look. He handed his rifle to Umbopa and, followed by Khiva, strolled toward the bush.

We sat down, happy for a break. The sun was setting, painting the sky crimson. Sir Henry and I admired the view—then we heard a scream.

An elephant, huge and furious, appeared silhouetted against the dying sun, trunk and tail raised.

Then we saw Good and Khiva running back, the wounded bull charging after them.

For a moment, we dared not fire—fear of hitting them.

Then—disaster.

Good tripped. His polished boot slipped on the dry grass, and he fell face-first, just feet from the beast.

We gasped. We knew he was done.

But something else happened.

Khiva, the Zulu boy, saw his master fall. Without hesitation, he threw his assegai straight into the elephant’s face.

It stuck in the trunk.

The beast roared in pain, seized Khiva, hurled him to the ground, placed a foot on his body, and wrapped its trunk around his upper half—*tore him in two*.

We rushed forward, firing wildly, until the elephant finally fell atop the pieces.

Good stood up, wringing his hands over the boy who’d died saving him. Even I, hardened as I was, felt a lump rise in my throat.

Umbopa stared at the dead elephant and the shattered remains of Khiva.

“Ah, well,” he said quietly. “He is dead—but he died like a man.”
