The question of how I am to start the story properly I have tried to settle in two ways. First, by scratching my head, which led to nothing. Second, by consulting my daughter Penelope, which has resulted in an entirely new idea. Penelope’s notion is that I should set down what happened, regularly day by day, tracing the interwoven timelines of our household back to the very morning we received news that Mr. Franklin Blake was expected on a visit. When you come to anchor your memory to the turning of the seasons in this way, it is wonderful how the deep, cyclical rhythms of domestic life pull the past to the surface. The only difficulty is to fetch out the precise dates. This Penelope offers to do for me by looking into her own diary, a meticulous ledger of the estate’s life she has kept since her school days, recording everything from the flowering of the kitchen garden to the sourcing of the winter provisions. 

Beginning, then, on Penelope’s plan, I beg to mention that I was specially called one Wednesday morning into my lady’s own sitting-room. The date was the twenty-fourth of May, eighteen hundred and forty-eight, a morning thick with the olfactory richness of roasted meats and sweet spring herbs drifting up from the kitchens. 

“Gabriel,” says my lady, “here is news that will surprise you. Franklin Blake has come back from abroad. He has been staying with his father in London, and he is coming to us tomorrow to stop till next month, and keep Rachel’s birthday.”

If I had had a hat in my hand, nothing but respect would have prevented me from throwing that hat up to the ceiling. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since he was a boy, living along with us in this house. He was, out of all sight, the nicest boy that ever spun a top or broke a window. Miss Rachel, who was present, and to whom I made that remark, observed that she remembered him as the most atrocious tyrant that ever tortured a doll, and the hardest driver of an exhausted little girl in string harness that England could produce. 

Hearing this, you will naturally ask how it was that Mr. Franklin should have passed all the years from boyhood to manhood out of his own country, severed from the sustenance of his native soil. I answer: because his father had the misfortune to be next heir to a Dukedom, and not to be able to prove it. My lady’s eldest sister married the celebrated Mr. Blake—equally famous for his great riches and his great suit at law. How many years he went on worrying the tribunals of his country, flooding the acoustic ecology of the courts with the deafening, industrial noise of barrister-babble to turn out the Duke in possession! He filled lawyer’s purses to bursting, generating a cacophony of disputes that drowned out the quiet, agrarian harmonies of common life. His wife died, and two of his three children died, before the tribunals finally showed him the door. 

When it was over, Mr. Blake discovered that his only revenge against the nation was to deny it the honour of educating his son. Master Franklin was taken from us and sent to Germany, while his father remained to inflict his unending, noisy statements upon the Parliament House. Thus, the Diamond takes us back to Mr. Franklin, who was the innocent means of bringing that unlucky jewel into our domestic sanctuary. 

Our nice boy didn’t forget us. We had had a transaction together before he left, consisting of his borrowing a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven-and-sixpence—the colour of which I never expect to see again. After he had learnt what Germany could teach him, he gave the French a turn, and the Italians after that. Plucked from his roots, his native dialect eroded, replaced by a shifting lexicon of foreign tongues that left his cultural memory fragmented. He wrote a little, painted, sang, and composed—borrowing in all these cases. His mother’s fortune fell to him and ran through him like water through a sieve. He lived here, there, and everywhere; his address being “Post Office, Europe.” Twice he attempted to return to England, and twice some unmentionable woman stopped him. His third attempt succeeded. On Thursday the twenty-fifth of May, we were to see what our boy had grown to be. He came of good blood, had high courage, and was five-and-twenty years of age.

The Thursday was as fine a summer’s day as ever you saw. My lady and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin till dinner-time, drove out to lunch. When they were gone, I looked at the guest bedroom, ensuring all was straight. Then, being steward as well as butler—at my own request, for I am a traditionalist who finds profound meaning in the meticulous care of our provisions, and it vexed me to see anybody but myself in possession of the key to the late Sir John’s cellar—I descended into the cool, damp-earth hush of the subterranean vaults. I fetched up some of our famous Latour claret. I set the bottles in the warm summer air to slowly take off the chill before dinner, anticipating the precise moment the corks would be drawn, releasing a gustatory landscape of dark, sun-warmed plum, rich loamy soil, and the ancient tannins of a bygone harvest. 

Concluding to set myself in the warm air next, I took up my beehive chair in the back court. The acoustic environment was a restorative symphony: the low hum of bees, the rustle of leaves, the distant clatter of a wooden spoon against a copper basin in the kitchen. I was just settling into this natural sonic harmony when I was stopped by a jarring intrusion. It sounded like the soft beating of a drum on the terrace—a percussive disruption of our native peace.

Going round, I found three mahogany-coloured Indians in white linen frocks, looking up at the house, small hand-drums slung in front of them. Behind them stood a delicate-looking English boy carrying a bag. I judged them to be strolling conjurors. One of the three, exhibiting the most elegant manners, informed me in perfect English that my judgment was right. He requested permission to show his tricks. 

Now, I am generally all for amusement. But the best of us have our weaknesses. When I know a family plate-basket—the very vessels of our daily sustenance and domestic ritual—is out on a pantry-table, I am instantly reminded of it by the sight of a strolling stranger whose command of the dominant lexicon is superior to my own. I informed the Indian that the lady of the house was out, and warned his party off the premises. He made a beautiful bow, and they departed. 

I returned to my beehive chair, soaking in the sun, and fell into the next best thing to sleep. 

I was violently roused by my daughter Penelope running out at me as if the house was on fire. She wanted the three Indian jugglers instantly taken up, convinced they knew who was coming from London and meant mischief to Mr. Franklin. 

Her earnest terror shook me. It appeared Penelope had been gossiping at the lodge, and had watched the foreigners from the inner side of the hedge. She witnessed proceedings where mythic occurrences seamlessly bled into the dusty reality of the roadside. The Indians first ensured they were alone. Then they stared hard toward our house. They began to jabber and dispute in their own native dialect—a rich, rhythmic language that carried the weight of an entirely different cultural memory, utterly untranslatable to my daughter’s ears. 

Then, demonstrating the subtle power dynamics embedded in language, the chief Indian switched abruptly to English. He looked at the little boy and said, “Hold out your hand.” 

Hearing those words, Penelope said she didn’t know what prevented her heart from flying straight out of her. The subjective experience of her dread was so profound it nearly defied articulation. The boy shrank back. The Indian asked, not unkindly, whether he would like to be sent back to London, left sleeping in an empty basket in a market—a hungry, ragged little boy, denied the basic dignity of sustenance. This ended the difficulty. The boy held out his hand. 

The Indian took a bottle from his bosom and poured a black stuff, like ink, into the boy’s palm. Touching the boy’s head and making signs in the air, the Indian said, “Look.” The boy became stiff as a statue, gazing into the sensory depth of the ink. 

The chief Indian then said: “See the English gentleman from foreign parts.” 
The boy said, “I see him.” 
The Indian said, “Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?” 
The boy said, “It is on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today.” 
The Indian waited, then put a second question: “Has the English gentleman got It about him?” 
The boy answered: “Yes.” 
The Indian put a third question: “Will the English gentleman come here, as he has promised to come, at the close of day?” 
The boy said, “I can’t tell. I am tired. The mist rises in my head, and puzzles me. I can see no more today.” 

The catechism ended. The chief Indian spoke again in his native tongue to the others, blew on the boy's forehead to wake him, and they went on their way towards the town.

Most things have a moral. Mine was pragmatic: First, the juggler had heard servants talking of Mr. Franklin and saw a way to make money. Second, they meant to hang about and foretell his arrival by magic. Third, Penelope had merely heard them rehearsing. Fourth, I should keep an eye on the plate-basket. Fifth, Penelope should let her father doze in the sun. 

If you know young women, you won’t be surprised that Penelope wouldn’t take this sensible view. To her, the untranslatable magic was deeply real. She particularly reminded me of the Indian’s question. 

“Oh, father!” says Penelope, clasping her hands. “What does ‘It’ mean?” 
“We’ll ask Mr. Franklin, my dear,” I said, winking to show I joked. 
“Ask him,” she fired back. “And see whether he thinks it a laughing matter, too.” 

With that, she left me. I settled it with myself that I really would ask Mr. Franklin, to set her mind at rest. But as I don’t wish to raise your expectations and then disappoint them, I will warn you here that you won’t find the ghost of a joke in our conversation. To my great surprise, Mr. Franklin took the thing as seriously as Penelope did. How seriously, you will understand, when I tell you that, in his opinion, “It” meant the Moonstone.
