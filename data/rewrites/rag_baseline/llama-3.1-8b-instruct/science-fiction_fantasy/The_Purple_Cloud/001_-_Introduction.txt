In the twilight of the early 20th century, a peculiar epistolary exchange transpired between Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, a respected figure in medical circles, and myself, a scholar of linguistics. The catalyst for this correspondence was a packet of notebooks penned in Pitman's shorthand, which Browne had entrusted to me prior to his untimely demise. These notebooks, a testament to his remarkable relationship with a woman named Miss Mary Wilson, would prove to be a seminal discovery in the field of hypnosis and the human psyche.

As I delved into the notebooks, I found myself immersed in the intricate dance between Browne and his subject, a woman beset by the debilitating condition of tic douloureux. A sufferer of this affliction since her youth, Miss Wilson had undergone numerous treatments, including the removal of most of her teeth, in a desperate bid to alleviate her suffering. Her experiences under the care of Browne, however, were a departure from the conventional approach, as he employed the hypnotic trance to treat her condition. This unique dynamic, which spanned over a decade, yielded profound insights into the workings of the human mind, and it is this narrative that I now wish to share with you.

Browne's letter, penned in the final days of his life, serves as a poignant introduction to this tale. As he lay bedridden, his health in decline, he reflected on his past and the remarkable woman who had been his subject. With characteristic wit and candor, he revealed the intricacies of their relationship, one that blurred the lines between therapist and patient, teacher and pupil. His words, though occasionally veiled by the mystique of Pitman's shorthand, painted a vivid portrait of a complex individual, one who had walked the fine line between the world of the conscious and the realm of the subconscious.

As I struggled to decipher the notebooks, I found myself drawn into the world of Miss Wilson, a woman of remarkable fortitude and resilience. Her story, as told through Browne's notes, serves as a testament to the human spirit's capacity for adaptation and growth, even in the face of adversity. Through their relationship, we gain a glimpse into the early 20th-century understanding of hypnosis, a discipline still shrouded in mystery and misconception. It is this narrative, one that traverses the boundaries of medicine, psychology, and the human experience, that I now invite you to join me in exploring.
