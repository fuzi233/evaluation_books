Now that the dust has settled, I must confess that during the rather uproarious episode concerning Rockmetteller Todd, I briefly entertained the terrifying notion that Jeeves might finally let me down. The man possessed an air of profound bewilderment, as if the universe itself had lost its manual.

One must appreciate Jeeves's true nature. While officially employed to press my shirts and manage my wardrobe, he operates more akin to the "guide" and "philosopher" described by the poet Johnnie regarding a certain bird of his acquaintance—a creature that rallies around its owner in times of dire need. I rely on him implicitly. Consequently, when Rocky Todd informed me of his predicament involving his aunt, I engaged Jeeves immediately.

The crisis erupted one crisp spring morning. I was deep in the restorative embrace of sleep, approximately nine hours into a dreamless slumber, when the door exploded inward. Someone prodded my lower ribs with malicious intent and vigorously shook the bedclothes. After blinking against the sudden intrusion and regaining my composure, I identified the culprit as Rocky. My initial reaction was to assume I was hallucinating a particularly vivid nightmare.

Rocky, you see, resides somewhere on Long Island, a considerable distance from New York. Furthermore, he has repeatedly assured me that he rises no earlier than noon, and rarely before one o'clock. Constitutionally the laziest young scoundrel in America, he has engineered a lifestyle that allows him to maximize idleness. He is a poet—or at least, he writes verses whenever inspiration strikes, though the majority of his existence is spent in a state of suspended animation. On one occasion, he confessed he could sit on a fence, observing a worm ponder its own existential purpose for hours on end.

His schedule is meticulously calculated. Once a month, he spends three days composing a handful of poems; the remaining three hundred and sixty-six days are dedicated to repose. I never believed poetry could sustain a gentleman living as Rocky does, yet American editors seem to fight over his rhyming efforts. He once showed me a piece beginning: *"Be! Be! The past is dead..."* It was published alongside a frontispiece featuring a semi-nude figure flexing muscles toward the rising sun. For this, he received a hundred dollars and remained in bed until four in the afternoon for over a month.

Financially, however, he is secure due to a wealthy aunt residing in Illinois. Named Rockmetteller after her and being her sole heir, his future is guaranteed. He had planned to inherit her fortune and subsequently perform no labor whatsoever, save perhaps penning occasional verses encouraging young men to light pipes and kick their feet onto mantelpieces.

And thus, it was this man who invaded my bedroom at the grey dawn!

"Read this, Bertie!" he waved a letter menacingly in my face. "Wake up and read this!"

"I cannot read before breakfast and a cigarette," I groaned, reaching for the bell.

Jeeves entered, looking as fresh as a dew-covered violet—a feat that remains a mystery to me.

"Tea, Jeeves."

"Very good, sir."

With the fluid grace of a liquid substance, he glided from the room. Rocky resumed his agitation with the letter.

"What is it?" I asked, rubbing my eyes. "What on earth is the matter?"

"Read it!"

"I can't. Not without tea."

"Well, listen then."

"My aunt."

At this revelation, I promptly drifted back into unconsciousness. I awoke to hear him muttering, "So what on earth am I to do?"

Jeeves returned with the tea tray, moving like a silent stream winding over moss. Daylight flooded the room.

"Read it again, Rocky, old top," I urged. "I want Jeeves to hear it. Mr. Todd's aunt has penned a rather peculiar letter, Jeeves, and we require your counsel."

"Very good, sir."

Jeeves stood amidst the room, radiating devoted attention, while Rocky launched into his tale:

*"MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—After much deliberation, I have concluded that it was thoughtless of me to delay so long in executing my resolve."*

"What do you make of that, Jeeves?"

"It appears somewhat obscure currently, sir, though clarity may emerge later."

"It's as clear as mud!" Rocky declared.

"Proceed, old scout," I said, chewing my bread and butter.

*"You know how I have longed to visit New York... I fear it will now be impossible. I am old and worn out. I seem to have no strength left."*

"Sad, Jeeves?"

"Extremely, sir."

"Sad nothing!" Rocky snapped. "It's pure laziness. I visited her last Christmas; she was bursting with vitality. Her doctor confirmed she was perfectly healthy. Yet, she insists on being a hopeless invalid, forcing the doctor to agree. She holds a fixed belief that New York would kill her, despite it being her lifelong ambition to visit."

"Reminiscent of the chap whose heart was 'in the Highlands,' Jeeves?"

"The parallels are striking, sir."

"Therefore, since I cannot experience the city's marvels myself, I shall enjoy them vicariously through you. Yesterday, reading a poignant poem about a man who achieved his desire only when too frail to enjoy it, I was deeply moved. As you will receive my inheritance upon my passing, I have decided to provide you with an allowance now. I have instructed New York lawyers to remit a substantial monthly sum. My sole condition: reside in New York and indulge yourself as I wish. Be my representative. Plunge into the vibrant life. Attend brilliant suppers. Above all—and I insist—you write me letters at least weekly detailing your activities, so I may enjoy the city at second hand. Expect full details; no triviality is too insignificant. Your affectionate Aunt, ISABEL ROCKMETTELLER."

"What about it?" Rocky asked.

"What about it?" I echoed.

"Yes. What on earth shall I do?"

Here, I truly grasped the absurdity of his situation. An unexpected windfall had descended from a clear blue sky, an occasion worthy of jubilation. Instead, the man sat there as if Fate had struck his solar plexus.

"Aren't you excited?" I asked.

"Bucked?"

"If I were in your shoes, I'd be thrilled. This is incredibly generous."

He emitted a yelp, stared at me, and then proceeded to describe New York in terms reminiscent of Jimmy Mundy, the reformer. Jimmy had recently toured the city, expressing disdain for its inhabitants, yet Rocky made him sound like a travel agent for the metropolis.

"Precious soft!" he cried. "To live in New York! To abandon my cottage for a stifling, smoky apartment in this heaven-forsaken Gehenna! To mingle nightly with a mob convinced life is a St. Vitus's dance, drunk on noise and gin! I loathe New York, Bertie. I wouldn't approach it unless forced to see editors. It's blighted with moral delirium tremens. The mere thought of staying more than a day makes me ill. And you call this generous?"

I felt akin to Lot's friends when their host began criticizing Sodom. I was unprepared for Rocky's eloquence.

"It would kill me," he continued. "To breathe the air of six million souls! To wear stiff collars daily! To—" He paused. "Good heavens! I suppose I must dress for dinner? What a ghastly prospect!"

I was horrified.

"My dear chap!" I reproached.

"Do you dress for dinner every night, Bertie?"

"Jeeves," I said coolly. The man stood motionless by the door. "How many evening suits do I possess?"

"We have three full sets, sir; two dinner jackets—"

"Three."

"For practical use, two, sir. We also have seven white waistcoats."

"And shirts?"

"Four dozen, sir."

"And white ties?"

"The first two shelves of the drawer are entirely filled, sir."

Turning to Rocky, I gestured.

"You see?"

The young man writhed like an electric fan.

"I won't do it! I can't! I'll be hanged if I do! How can I dress like that? Most days I don't leave my pajamas until five, then throw on a sweater!"

Poor Jeevis winced; such revelations wounded his sensibilities.

"So, what is your plan?" I asked.

"That is precisely what I wish to know."

"You could explain to your aunt."

"I might—if I desired her lawyer to disinherit me instantly."

Seeing his logic, I turned to Jeeves.

"Jeeves?"

Jeeves cleared his throat respectfully.

"The crux, sir, is that Mr. Todd is obligated to submit detailed reports of his movements. Since Mr. Todd intends to remain in the countryside, the only solution is to engage a second party to gather the required experiences and compile a report, which Mr. Todd can then expand into correspondence using his imagination."

Silence followed. Rocky looked bewildered.

"Can you clarify, Bertie?" he asked. "It flickered in meaning. What is the idea?"

"My dear old man, it is simple. Get someone to tour the city, take notes, and you draft the letters. That is it, Jeeves?"
