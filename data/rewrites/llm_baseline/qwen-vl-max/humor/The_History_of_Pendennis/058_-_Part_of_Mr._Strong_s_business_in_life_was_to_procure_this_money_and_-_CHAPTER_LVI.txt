CHAPTER LVI. In Which Fanny Engages a New Medical Man (With a Side of Whisky and Regret)

Could Helen have guessed that as Pen’s strength returned, so too would his old-fashioned crush on little Fanny? Not likely. She never said a word about the girl after her talk with the Major—she didn’t even glance in her direction. Yet Mrs. Pendennis watched Arthur like a hawk under a microscope, citing “ill health” as her excuse to keep him tethered to her side like a particularly fragile pet. She forbade all correspondence—“For now,” she declared, though we know full well she meant “forever.” Poor Arthur must’ve trembled when letters arrived at the table. He’d feel her gaze—though it was fixed firmly on her teacup or the edge of a novel—and he’d expect every day to see *that* handwriting: tiny, looping, unmistakable, even if he’d never actually seen it before. His heart would thud like a drumroll whenever the mail came. Was he relieved or disappointed when no letter materialized? Did he secretly hope for a cry from Fanny? “Help, Arthur!” Or did he wish she’d just vanish into the ether?

Ah, love! When Lovelace tires of Clarissa—or vice versa—it’s best to split clean, like a bad marriage in a Dickens novel. But human nature hates abrupt bankruptcies. We don’t want to put up the “Closed” sign overnight. We prefer a slow decline: mournful board meetings, half-hearted reconciliations, pawned jewelry to keep appearances. So Pen, despite his recovery, found himself annoyed by Fanny’s silence. Could she really leave without so much as a backward glance? Could she sink beneath the waves without throwing out a single hand? Some drown. Most just get dunked and scrambles to shore. And based on our experience with A. Pendennis, Esq., of the Upper Temple, we can say with confidence: this man was more likely to be drenched than drowned.

Pen was still too weak to walk half a mile—no sirree. He couldn’t even take a carriage ride without a nurse clinging to his arm like a limpet. But poor Helen couldn’t watch over Mr. Warrington. That gentleman had business in London, and who was she to stop him? If anything, she might’ve been glad to see him go—except conscience intervened. She remembered Warrington’s loyalty, his kindness to her boy, and so welcomed him into their home with her usual melancholy grace. Still, one morning, she somehow knew why Warrington had gone to town: to find out what had become of Fanny.

Arthur had indeed confided in Warrington, telling him everything—the whole sordid, romantic mess with Fanny, which the reader already knows better than the characters themselves. He expressed gratitude for escaping disaster—Warrington agreed wholeheartedly. Pen admitted he had no great sins to confess regarding Fanny; but if they were to part, he’d be glad to say, “God bless you,” and hope she’d remember him kindly. He spoke with such solemnity, such emotion, that George Warrington—who had previously championed the breakup—began to worry. Was Arthur truly cured? Would a reunion bring back all the temptation, all the danger?

“It’s hard to resist, Arthur,” Warrington said, “and easy to fall. The bravest thing we poor wretches can do is flee danger. I wouldn’t be where I am today if I’d done what I preach.”

“And what did you do, George?” Pen asked eagerly. “I knew there was something. Tell me.”

“There was something,” Warrington replied, “that shattered my life early. I said I’d tell you someday. Not now. Take the moral without the fable. Here I am—wrecked by a rock I hit as a boy. So beware.”

Meanwhile, young Mr. Huxter, in his letter home to Clavering, mentioned a fashionable club in London where he met an Irish officer of distinction—none other than Captain Costigan. This club was the Back Kitchen, a nightly haunt for medical students and gentlemen of questionable sobriety. Huxter, a natural mimic, delighted in imitating the General’s brogue, mannerisms, and drunken ramblings. With sixpence worth of brandy-and-water, Costigan would open up like a book—telling tales of his daughter’s triumphs, his military exploits, and his profound sorrow over Emily’s ingratitude. “My grey hairs are hastening to a premature grave,” he’d lament, eyes glistening with whiskey tears.

The boys at the Back Kitchen began to wonder: Where did this mysterious General live? Was it a watchbox? A cab at a mews? The Duke of York’s Column? Huxter, ever the fantasist, spread wild rumors. But truth was simpler: Costigan lived in Shepherd’s Inn, in a garret above the porter’s lodge—though he never revealed it, not because of shame, but because privacy had long been his armor.

One bright September morning, Huxter, fresh from Vauxhall and fueled by coffee, spotted the General reeling down Henrietta Street, trailed by a pack of giggling urchins. The old man was barely conscious, eyes glazed, staggering like a drunkard on a sinking ship. Cabmen muttered, policemen scowled, but Costigan didn’t care. He followed his usual route home—until a rogue orange peel tripped him.

Huxter sprang forward. After a moment of dazed confusion, he helped the General up and insisted on escorting him. “You’re not going anywhere alone,” he said cheerfully. The General protested, claiming he could manage, but he lurched and swayed like a drunken puppet. Huxter guided him across the street, past the ancient gate of Shepherd’s Inn, where the ornate coat of arms seemed to sneer at the chaos unfolding.

Old Mr. Bolton, the porter, emerged grumbling. “Don’t bother me,” he snapped. “Go to your own bed, Captain. Don’t keep honest men awake.” Costigan staggered inside, and Huxter followed, helping him up the stairs, unlocking the door with the key Costigan kept in his pocket. Once inside, Huxter assisted the old man undress, checked for broken bones (none), and applied compresses to his scraped knee and shin—both torn through his trousers during the fall.

At Costigan’s age, wounds healed slowly. Fever set in. The General lay ill for days, moaning and sweating.

Huxter took charge of the case with gusto. He visited daily, offering lively conversation and medicine. He instructed the nurse on whisky rations—strictly limited, since the patient couldn’t move. Bows, Mrs. Bolton, and little Fanny took turns tending the sick man. Huxter became a fixture in the inn, friendly with everyone from the porter to the garret dwellers.

He thought he’d seen Fanny somewhere before—but couldn’t place her. Of course not. She hadn’t told him where they’d met. He’d seen her when he was half-drunk, dancing at Vauxhall. And she herself had changed—pale, thin, haunted by three weeks of fever, despair, and unrequited longing. Her face was wan, her eyes often fixed on the postman’s back as he passed. Many a tear fell into the General’s gruel.

Ah, poor little Fanny! Stir the gruel well, and have courage. If every soul who suffered like you died instantly, the undertakers would make a fortune.

Huxter, charmed by the General’s plight, visited two or three times a day. He brought toys for the children, apples, brandy-balls, even a mask to frighten them. He made Fanny smile once—just once. He called Mrs. Bolton “Mrs. B.” and teased her freely, unlike “that aughty, artless beast” whom she now detested—namely, Arthur Pendennis.

From Mrs. Bolton, Huxter learned the truth about Fanny’s illness—and Pen’s behavior. Naturally, Mrs. Bolton’s version was biased. She painted Arthur as a villain: seducer, liar, breaker of promises, worthy of public scorn. Huxter, still smarting from Pen’s earlier snubs, believed every word.

But why didn’t he write to Clavering? Why not expose the young man’s misconduct?

He did write—only to say that Pendennis had narrowly escaped death, and that Clavering would rejoice. He mentioned his interesting case: a distinguished officer with a compound fracture. But Fanny? Not a word. Just like Pen himself.

O mothers of England! How much do you *really* know about your sons?

Bows, however, got the full story. Huxter confided in him, calling Pen a “confounded conceited blackguard” and vowing to punch him in the nose when he recovered.

Then Bows spoke. He told his version: how Pen had stumbled upon Fanny by accident—not design—how he’d acted with honor, how Mrs. Bolton was a fool. He recounted the conversation between himself and Pen, the young man’s noble sentiments.

Huxter felt ashamed. He admitted he’d been wrong. No punch was thrown.

But Huxter’s kindness toward Fanny didn’t go unnoticed. Bows, ever jealous, saw it as a betrayal. “I only have to like someone,” he muttered, “and someone else comes along and takes my place. It’s always been so—since I was a lad. What can a man like me expect? To be laughed at? The young succeed. The old fail. I’ve played second fiddle all my life. Why should luck change now?”

Fanny, meanwhile, received Huxter’s attentions with quiet gratitude. She laughed at his jokes, played games with his little sisters. But her sadness returned quickly—so swiftly that Bows should’ve known Huxter wasn’t winning her heart. But Bows didn’t see. He misread everything.

Fanny blamed Bows for Pen’s silence. She hated him. She turned from him when he spoke. She rejected his comfort. Poor Bows—his devotion went unrewarded, his heart unheeded.

When Warrington arrived at Shepherd’s Inn, he sought Bows’s rooms—no doubt per prior arrangement. He didn’t glimpse Fanny. He was directed to the musician’s chamber, where Bows greeted him warmly. They shook hands. After small talk, Warrington said he came from Arthur Pendennis and his family—to thank Bows for his help during Pen’s illness, and for fetching the Major.

Bows replied humbly: “It was my duty. I never thought I’d see him alive again. I’m glad he’s recovering.”

“Lucky are those who have friends,” said Bows. “I might be up here, and nobody would care whether I lived or died.”

“What about the General?” Warrington asked.

“The General likes his whisky more than anything,” Bows said. “We live together out of habit. He doesn’t care for me any more than you do. Now, what’s this about? You’re not here to visit me. Nobody visits me. It’s about Fanny, isn’t it?”

“Yes,” Warrington said.

“Does Mr. Pendennis want to see her now that he’s better? Does his lordship propose to throw his handkerchief to her? She’s been very ill—ever since Mrs. Pendennis threw her out. Kind of a lady, wasn’t it? The poor girl and I found him raving in a fever—nobody to tend him but his drunken laundress. She watched night and day. I ran to fetch his uncle. Mamma arrives and kicks Fanny out. Uncle arrives and leaves me to pay the cab. Carry my compliments to the ladies and gentlemen. Say we’re both very thankful. Why, a countess couldn’t have behaved better. And for an apothecary’s wife—well, I suppose she deserves a double-gilt pestle and mortar on her coach.”

And so, the chapter ends—with a touch of irony, a dash of regret, and a new doctor who may just be the cure Fanny needs.
