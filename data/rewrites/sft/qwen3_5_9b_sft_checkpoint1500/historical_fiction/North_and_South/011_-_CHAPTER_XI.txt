"Margaret!" Mr. Hale said, returning from downstairs where he had shown his guest down. "I watched your face with genuine anxiety when Mr. Thornton confessed to being a shop-boy. I knew the facts from Mr. Bell, so I expected the shock. Yet, I half anticipated you rising and fleeing the room."

"Oh, Papa! You do not think me so foolish?" Margaret replied, her tone brightening. "I preferred that admission above anything else he said. Everything else repelled me due to its coldness. But speaking of himself so simply—stripped of the artifice that often masks the vulgarity of commerce—and with such tender reverence for his mother, I felt less inclined to depart than when he boasted of Milton as if it were the sole marvel of the earth. Or when he dismissed people for their careless improvidence without acknowledging his duty to elevate them through the training his mother imparted, a foundation for his current standing. No! His honesty regarding his humble origins was the finest quality I observed."

"I am surprised, Margaret," her mother interjected. "You who frequently accused those at Helstone of being 'shopkeepers'! I do not believe, Mr. Hale, you acted wisely in presenting such a person to us without prior disclosure. I trembled lest his words reveal parts of his history that would deeply offend me. His father dying in 'miserable circumstances.' It sounds as though he perished within a workhouse."

"It may have been worse," Mr. Hale corrected gently. "Before arriving here, Mr. Bell shared considerable details of Mr. Thornton's youth. To complete the picture he omitted: his father gambled recklessly, squandered his fortune, and committed suicide unable to bear the resulting shame. All former acquaintances retreated from the scandal of his fraudulent dealings and desperate attempts to recover wealth borrowed from others. No friend stepped forward to aid the widow and her sons. There was a younger daughter, too young to work, necessitating support. They abandoned Milton. I learned he worked in a shop, using his wages and a fragment of his father's estate to sustain his mother. Mr. Bell noted they subsisted on water-porridge for years. Long after creditors had despaired, this young man returned to Milton. He visited each creditor individually, paying the first installment owed, all conducted in profound silence. Eventually, every debt was settled, aided significantly by a stingy old creditor who, ironically, admitted Mr. Thornton as a partner."

"That is truly admirable," Margaret murmured. "It is a tragedy that such a noble nature is tarnished by his status as a Milton manufacturer."

"Tarnished?" Her father asked.

"Oh, Papa," Margaret explained, "by measuring all existence solely against the standard of wealth. When discussing machinery, he viewed them merely as tools to expand trade and accumulate money. Regarding the impoverished workers around him, he implied they were poor because they were vicious. Their exclusion from his sympathy stemmed not from malice, but from their lack of that 'iron nature' and the inherent capabilities it affords for prosperity."

"Not vicious," Mr. Hale countered softly. "His words were 'improvident' and 'self-indulgent.'"

As Margaret began gathering her mother's sewing materials, preparing for bed, she paused at the door. A thought formed—one that might please her father, yet required admitting a lingering irritation to be fully truthful.

"Papa," she said, "I consider Mr. Thornton a remarkably extraordinary man; however, on a purely personal level, I find myself entirely unable to like him."

"And I do!" Mr. Hale laughed warmly. "In terms of personal affinity, certainly. I do not elevate him to the pedestal of a hero, nor do I dismiss him. However, farewell, child. Your mother appears exceedingly fatigued tonight, Margaret."

Margaret had observed her mother's gaunt, exhausted countenance with growing alarm for some time. This comment from her father sealed her resolve to retire, carrying with it a heavy, dim foreboding. Life in Milton differed sharply from her upbringing in Helstone, where fresh air and outdoor activity were constants. Here, the atmosphere seemed devoid of revitalizing spirit. Domestic burdens pressed upon the women of the household with a closeness and sordid intensity unknown before. There was legitimate cause to fear that Mrs. Hale's health was deteriorating rapidly. Furthermore, subtle signs pointed to deeper troubles. She and Dixon engaged in secretive consultations within Mrs. Hale's chambers; Dixon emerged from these meetings tearful and irritable, a common reaction when distress demanded her sympathy. On one occasion, Margaret entered shortly after Dixon departed and found her mother kneeling, praying fervently for strength and patience to endure severe physical suffering. Margaret longed to restore the intimate bond of trust fractured by her extended absence at Aunt Shaw's, striving with gentle touches and soothing words to reclaim a place in her mother's heart. Yet, despite receiving affection in abundance, Margaret sensed a grave secret withheld, likely concerning her mother's failing health. Lying awake later that night, she devised a plan to mitigate the harmful effects of their environment: securing a dedicated servant to assist Dixon permanently, thereby ensuring her mother received the constant personal attention she required.

For several days, Margaret's time and thoughts were consumed by visiting registration offices and encountering a myriad of unlikely individuals. One afternoon, she encountered Bessy Higgins in the street and stopped to greet her.

"Well, Bessy, how fares you? I trust the change in weather has brought improvement."

"Better and not better, if you understand what that implies," Bessy replied.

"Not precisely," Margaret smiled.

"I am improved in that I am no longer torn apart by coughing throughout the night. Yet I am weary and worn out by Milton, longing to escape to the Land of Beulah. And when I realize I grow further and further distant, my heart sinks, and I become worse."

Margaret turned to walk beside the girl, whose progress was feeble. For a moment, she remained silent. Finally, in a low voice, she asked, "Bessy, do you desire to die?" She recoiled from the thought of death herself, clinging to life with youthful vigor.

Bessy was silent for a brief interval before answering. "If you had led the life I have, grown as weary of it as I have, and occasionally thought, 'perhaps it will endure for fifty or sixty years—it does with some,' feeling dizzy and bewildered as each of those six decades spun around me, mocking me with the length of its hours and minutes, and endless fragments of time... oh, wench! I assure you, you would have been content enough when the physician declared he feared you would never witness another winter."

"Why, Bessy, what sort of life has yours been?"

"Nought worse than many others', I reckon. Only I fretted against it, whereas they did not."

"But what was it? You know, I am a stranger here, so perhaps I am slower to comprehend your meaning than someone who has lived all their days in Milton."

"If you had visited our home when you promised, I might have told you. But Father says you are just like the rest of them; it is 'out of sight, out of mind' with you."

"I do not know who 'the rest' are, and I have been extremely busy. To tell the truth, I had momentarily forgotten my promise—"

"You offered it; we requested nothing of it."

"I forgot what I said for the time," Margaret continued calmly. "I would have recalled it again when I was less preoccupied. May I accompany you now?"

Bessy cast a swift, searching glance at Margaret's face to gauge the sincerity of her offer. The sharp edge in her eyes softened into a wistful longing as she met Margaret's gentle, friendly gaze.

"I have no one so numerous to care for me; if you care, you may come."

Thus, they walked on in silence. As they ascended into a narrow courtyard off a squalid street, Bessy remarked, "You need not be intimidated if Father is present and speaks somewhat gruffly at first. He has taken a liking to you, you see, and considered greatly your willingness to visit. Precisely because he likes you, he was vexed and unsettled."

"Do not fear, Bessy."

However, Nicholas was not at home upon their entry. A large, unkempt girl, younger than Bessy but taller and sturdier, was busy at the wash-tub, knocking about the furniture with a rough capability, creating enough disturbance to make Margaret shrink in sympathy with poor Bessy, who sat down on the nearest chair as if utterly drained by the journey. Margaret requested a cup of water from the sister, who hurried off to fetch it, clumsily dislodging fire-irons and tripping over a chair. In her haste, Margaret unfastened Bessy's bonnet strings to alleviate her labored breathing.

"Do you deem such a life as this worth cherishing?" Bessy gasped finally. Margaret said nothing, holding the water to her lips. Bessy drank deeply, a feverish draught, then collapsed backward, closing her eyes. Margaret heard her muttering to herself, "They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat."
