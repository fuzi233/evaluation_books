Oh, don’t look at me like that – I know babies. Loads about ’em, actually. I was one myself once, though not for nearly as long as my baby clothes stuck around. I remember those things like they were yesterday: three full yards of unnecessary swaddle fabric, always tangling around my ankles right when I was mid-kick. Why do babies get dressed in what amounts to a full bed curtain? This isn’t a riddle, I’m genuinely asking. I never cracked it. Are parents embarrassed their new human is barely bigger than a loaf of bread, and trying to trick everyone into thinking it’s six inches longer than it actually is?

I asked a nurse about it once. She blinked at me and said, “Lor’, sir, they all wear long clothes, bless their little hearts.” When I pointed out that this was very sweet but didn’t answer my actual question, she gasped like I’d suggested we feed the baby pickles for breakfast, and went, “Lor’ sir, you wouldn’t put the poor little dears in short clothes, would you?” The tone she used made me feel like I’d just proposed a thoroughly ungentlemanly crime. I’ve never dared bring it up to anyone else since, so the whole long baby coat conspiracy remains unsolved for me.

Honestly, though, strapping any clothes at all on a baby seems absurd to me. We all spend half our lives getting dressed and undressed anyway – why start before you even have to? You’d think people who spend 23 hours a day in bed could skip the whole changing routine. Why yank the poor little thing awake at dawn, peel one set of clothes off, wrap another one on, tuck it back in bed, then drag it out again at bedtime just to swap it all back? And for what? A baby’s day shirt and its nightgown look exactly the same to anyone who isn’t a qualified nursery lunatic.

I’ll stop nitpicking the clothes now, I know I look ridiculous – people tell me that a lot – except to say it would be unbelievably convenient if someone invented a fashion system that lets you tell a boy baby apart from a girl baby. Right now it’s a nightmare. No hair difference, no outfit clues, no chattering little personality to tip you off. You just have to guess. And by some universal cruel magic, you will always guess wrong. Then every relative in the room will look at you like you’re half idiot, half villain, because calling a little boy “she” is a sin on par with calling the grandma a thief, and calling a little girl “he” is basically an insult to the entire family bloodline.

And for the love of all that is holy, never call the baby “it.” You can murder an entire respectable family in cold blood and dump their bodies in the town water reservoir, and you’ll still be less hated than you will be the second a new mom hears you refer to her little darling as “it.”

Here’s my free hack, take it to the bank: call the baby a little angel. “Angel” is perfectly gender-neutral, no way to mess that up. Throw in a silly giggle when you say it, grin as big as you can, and you can call them “pet” or “little beauty” if you want some variety. But the big one, the hack that makes you the favorite guest, is to insist at the top of your lungs that the baby has its father’s exact nose. The parents will pretend to scoff and say “oh nonsense!” – you double down, get all excited, swear it’s the spitting image. You don’t even have to lie. That baby’s nose is just a little smudge of skin, it looks as much like its dad’s nose as it looks like a potato. No one can prove you wrong.

Don’t ignore these tips, trust me. There will come a day when you’re trapped between mom on one side, grandma on the other, a whole crowd of gossiping ladies behind you, and a bald little squish of a human in front, and you’ll thank me for having something to say. There is no more embarrassing situation on earth for a single unmarried man than being roped into the “come see the baby” ritual. You get that cold shiver down your spine the second they suggest it, you paste on that sickly fake smile and say “oh I’d love to!” and you’re 90% sure the whole thing is just a trick wives use to chase off their husbands’ boring bachelor friends.

They ring the bell, send a servant to get the nurse and bring baby down. All the women in the room immediately start chattering exclusively about baby business, leaving you there panicking, trying to think of a plausible excuse to leave right now – maybe I suddenly remembered I left my goldfish in the oven? Maybe my horses escaped? – and just when you’ve settled on the most unbelievable lie ever told, the door creaks open. A tall, severe-looking nurse walks in, holding what you at first glance think is a very skinny bolster pillow with all the feathers squashed to one end. Then your brain catches up: that’s the baby. You jump up, try to look excited, and immediately fail.

Once all the ladies are done cooing loud enough to rattle the windows, their little circle of frilly dresses parts, and gestures for you to come forward. You walk over like you’re heading to the magistrate’s dock to get sentenced, stand there staring at the baby in total silence, and you know every single person is waiting for you to say something. Your entire brain empties out. No thoughts. Nothing. Your evil inner idiot seizes the chance, and you snigger out, “Wow… he doesn’t have much hair, huh?”

Dead silence for three whole seconds. Then the nurse looks at you with the gravity of a judge handing down a death sentence, and goes, “Sir, it is not normal for five week old children to have long hair.”

Another silence. You scramble for a second chance, and ask if the baby can walk yet, or what they feed it – like they give it steak and ale, I guess. Now everyone’s looking at you like you’re a little touched in the head, and they feel bad for you. But the nurse is not letting you escape. She holds the squishy bundle out to you, tone of a high priestess leading a pagan ritual, and goes, “Take her in your arms, sir.”

You’re too scared to argue. You take the baby. “Put your arm further down her middle, sir,” she commands. Everyone steps back and stares at you, like you’re about to pull a magic rabbit out of its bonnet. You have no clue what to do. The only thing you can think of is to bounce the poor little thing up and down, chanting “oopsee-daisy” like a complete fool. “I wouldn’t jiggle her too much, sir,” the nurse says sharply. “A very little jiggling upsets her.” You freeze, stop bouncing immediately, and pray you didn’t break anything.

Up until this point, the baby has been staring at you with a face that says equal parts “this man is a stranger” and “this man is deeply unpleasant.” Now it decides to end the whole farce, and screams at the top of its tiny lungs like you just stuck a pin in its bottom. The nurse snatches it out of your arms immediately, cooing “there there there, what did the nasty man do to our little love?” “How weird,” you say, trying to sound casual. “What made it do that?” “Oh you must have done something to her!” the mom snaps, clearly convinced you’ve been torturing her child. They calm it down eventually, and everything would be fine, except some nosy old lady points at you and goes “who’s that, baby?” The baby sees you, remembers, and howls so loud the windows rattle.

Then all the old ladies start muttering. “Strange how babies take a dislike to some people,” one says mysteriously. “Oh they know,” another nods. “It’s a wonderful instinct.” Every single one of them looks at you out of the corner of their eye, fully convinced you’re a secret scoundrel, and the pure innocent baby has sussed out your evil true character before anyone else did.

But for all that, babies aren’t all trouble and awkward social torture. They fill empty houses with soft love, make tired parents forget their work worries in two seconds flat, their tiny little fingers smooth out all the grumpy wrinkles off grown people’s faces. They’re the accidental comedians of the whole world’s big boring play. They’re tiny, stubborn rebels against every boring adult plan, forever doing the exact wrong thing at the exact wrong time in the exact wrong place. The nurse who sent little Jenny to check what Tommy and Totty were doing and “tell them they mustn’t” knew exactly how baby brains work. If a normal, healthy baby isn’t doing something they absolutely should not be doing at any given moment, you need to call a doctor immediately.

They do the silliest things in the most serious, stoical way possible, and you can’t help but laugh. Two tiny tots will grab hands and toddle as fast as their wobbly legs can carry them due east, while their big sister screams at the top of her lungs for them to come west, and they don’t even notice. They circle a uniformed soldier on the sidewalk, poke his calf very carefully to make sure he’s not a statue. They will point at the shyest, most embarrassed stranger on the crowded bus and yell “DADDA” loud enough for the whole vehicle to hear. They decide a busy street corner is the perfect spot to yell all their family’s private business at the top of their high little voices. Halfway across a zebra crossing, they get a sudden overwhelming urge to stop and dance. And the second you step outside a shop, they plop down right on the doorstep and start trying to take their shoes off.

At home, they grab the biggest heaviest walking stick or an open umbrella to haul themselves up the stairs. The exact second the maid is covered head to toe in stove blacklead, they decide they love her more than anything in the world and insist on giving her a huge hug. Their favorite snacks are tiny bits of coal and cat food. They hold the family cat upside down to “nurse it,” and show their love for the dog by yanking its tail as hard as they can. They’re a mess, they make the whole house untidy, they cost a fortune to feed and clothe, but no one wants to live in a house without them. It wouldn’t feel like home without their tiny pattering feet, their chattering nonstop voices, their little hands getting into every single drawer. Wouldn’t the whole place feel dead quiet without them? Wouldn’t the grown-ups drift apart if there wasn’t a little prattling voice to call them back to each other?

Though I will say, sometimes that tiny little hand feels like a wedge, driving people apart. It’s rude to make fun of mother’s love, that soft pure thing that makes women so gentle and brave – us rough old men can barely even understand it. But it doesn’t have to swallow up every other bit of love in the house. The baby doesn’t have to take over your whole heart, like that greedy rich man who bricked up the desert well so no one else could drink from it. Your husband is standing right there, thirsty for a little of your attention too. When poor old Edwin asks you to go for a walk with him, don’t snap “what, and leave the baby?” Don’t spend every single evening upstairs in the nursery, don’t only ever talk about whooping cough and measles. Your baby is not going to die every time it sneezes. The house isn’t going to burn down, the nurse isn’t going to run off with a soldier, the cat isn’t going to climb into the crib and sit on the baby’s chest the second you step out the door for 20 minutes. Stop stressing over that one little chick so much, you’re driving yourself and everyone else crazy. Think a little of your other big baby – the husband. Dance him around a little, call him silly pet names, laugh at his bad jokes. You’d be surprised: five or six kids never take up as much attention as that first precious one did. But by the time you have five or six, the damage is already done. The house feels too small for him, his wife is too busy to remember he exists, and he’s already off looking for fun and conversation somewhere else.

Alright, alright! I’ll stop, before everyone decides I’m a confirmed baby hater. I’m not. How could I be? Look at all those tiny soft faces, peering out at the big world for the first time, all shy and wobbly. That huge round world must look so massive and mysterious through baby eyes! The back garden seems like an entire uncharted continent. The dark little cellar under the stairs is full of treasures no grown-up has ever seen. They stare down the long street, wide-eyed, the same way us old big babies stare up at the stars at night, wondering where it all ends. And that longest street of all, the long dim road of life stretching out in front of them – they look down it sometimes, with such grave old little faces. I saw a tiny tot sitting on a Soho slum doorstep once at night, the gas lamp lighting up his little thin face, and I’ll never forget that look on it. Dull, quiet despair, like he could see his whole whole hard squalid life stretching out in front of him, ghost-like, scaring him half to death.

Poor little feet, just starting down this stony road. We old travelers, far further down the path, can only stop for a second and wave. They come out of the mist at the top of the hill, tiny little things, arms stretched out towards us. God speed you, little ones. We wish we could stay, take your tiny soft hands in ours, walk a little way with you. But we can hear the murmur of the great sea at the bottom of the hill, and we can’t linger. Those dark shadowy ships are waiting for us, sails unfurled, and we have to go.
