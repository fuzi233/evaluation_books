Arthur Pendennis spotted Mr. Bows in the hallway of Shepherd's Inn and immediately launched into a booming, cheerfully loud greeting. "How d'ye do, Mr. Bows!" he declared, offering a salute that was as dashing and offhand as a cavalry charge. Yet, despite the bravado, a distinct blush burned across Arthur's cheeks—a secret signal instantly mirrored by Fanny, whose own complexion erupted in a fluttering crimson flare.

After the requisite handshake and an ironic nod regarding Arthur's intention to visit Mr. Costigan's chambers, a heavy, guilt-ridden silence descended upon the group. It was a silence so profound that Pendennis felt compelled to break it with a violent, rattling noise. While the sound successfully shattered the quiet, it failed to dispel the gloom; indeed, the atmosphere grew darker, much like lighting a single tallow candle in a vast, unlit vault.

Desperate to lift the mood, Pendennis attempted a jocular recounting of the previous night's events, complete with a clumsy impersonation of Captain Costigan pleading with the ticket-taker at Vauxhall. It was a disaster. No one laughed. Mrs. Bolton stared blankly, utterly confused as to whether she was witnessing a satire of the ticket-taker or the Captain himself. Fanny wore an expression of genuine alarm, attempting a timid giggle that sounded more like a squeak. Even old Mr. Bows looked as grim as he did when fiddling in the orchestra or tackling a difficult sonata on the Back Kitchen piano. Realizing his performance had flopped, Pendennis's voice trailed off, flickering out until the room was plunged into darkness once more.

"You were coming to see me, sir?" Mr. Bows interjected, breaking the spell. "Won't you have the kindness to walk up to my chambers with me? You do them a great honor, I'm sure. They are rather high up, but—"

"Oh! I live in a garret myself," Pendennis interrupted sharply. "And Shepherd's Inn is twice as cheerful as Lamb Court."

"I knew you had third-floor apartments," Bows replied, his voice dripping with practiced sadness. "And I was just about to say—please don't take my remark as discourteous—that the air up three flights of stairs is far wholesomer for a gentleman than the stagnant breeze of a porter's lodge."

"Sir!" Pen snapped, his candle metaphorically reigniting in a blaze of righteous indignation. "Will you permit me to choose my society without—"

"You were so polite as to say you were about to honor my humble domicile with a visit," Bows countered, adopting a tone so artificially melancholic it sounded rehearsed. "Shall I show you the way? Mr. Pendennis and I are old friends, Mrs. Bolton—very old acquaintances. At the earliest dawn of his life, we crossed paths."

The old man gestured toward the door with a trembling finger, clutching a hat in the other hand, striking a pose that was undeniably theatrical. Though his words were drawn from the vocabulary of orators he'd heard before the stage lamps, he wasn't merely acting. Pendennis recognized the sincerity beneath the melodrama, even as he internally scoffed at the display.

"Come along, sir," Bows urged, "since you are so very pressing. Mrs. Bolton, I wish you a good day. Good-bye, Miss Fanny; I shall always cherish our night at Vauxhall. And be sure I will remember the theatre tickets." With a press of Fanny's hand, he turned and vanished.

"What a nice young man, to be sure!" Mrs. Bolton exclaimed, watching him depart.

"Do you think so, ma?" Fanny asked softly.

"I was thinking who he reminded me of," Mrs. Bolton continued, gazing through the window curtains as Pen ascended the courtyard with Bows. "When I was at the Wells with Mrs. Serle, there was a young gentleman from the City who used to arrive in a tilbury, wearing a white coat—the very image of him, except his whiskers were black, whereas Mr. P.'s are red."

"Law, ma! They're a most beautiful hair color," Fanny observed.

"He used to come courting Emly Budd," Mrs. Bolton recounted, her voice weaving a tale of local lore. "She danced Columbine in *Arlequin Orphee*, or 'The Battle of Navarino,' when Miss De la Bosky fell ill. A pretty dancer, a fine stage figure. He was a great sugar-baker in the City, with a country house at Omerton. One day they drove in the tilbury down Goswell Street Road, got married at St. Bartholomew's Church, Smithfield, with bands playing quite privately. Now she keeps her carriage, and I saw her name in the paper as the patroness of the Mansion House Ball for the Washwomen's Asylum. And look at Lady Mirabel—Captain Costigan's daughter. She was professional, as everyone knows."

Thus spoke Mrs. Bolton, alternating between peering through the curtains and scrubbing mugs into the corner cupboard, finally folding the dinner cloth while shaking hands with Fanny.

Although Captain Costigan had previously detailed Pendennis's financial prospects with some accuracy, he may have forgotten the specifics from Chatteris years prior, or perhaps his natural enthusiasm led him to exaggerate Pen's income. That evening, he had painted Fairoaks Park in glowing hues for Mrs. Bolton, boasting of Pendennis's wealthy uncle, the Major, and displaying an intimate knowledge of Arthur's funded and landed assets. Likely, Mrs. Bolton had spent the night speculating on these riches, envisioning Fanny driving in a carriage like her old comrade, the dancer of Sadler's Wells.

As the final folds of the tablecloth were completed, the two women stood close. Fanny took the cloth, giving it the last fold, while her mother slid a finger under the young girl's chin to kiss her. Once again, the red signal flew across Fanny's cheeks, fluttering wildly. This time, however, it was not alarm. It was pure, sweet pleasure causing the poor little Fanny to blush.

*Poor little Fanny!* Is love a sin because it tastes so sweet at the start and so bitter at the end?

Mrs. Bolton then announced she was going out on business, leaving Fanny to guard the lodge. After a faint protest, Fanny agreed. Mrs. Bolton departed with her bonnet and basket, and the moment she was gone, Fanny moved to the window overlooking Bows's door, refusing to look anywhere else.

In a corner, Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann buzzed around a picture book held upside-down, pretending to read a grave and dreadful tract from Mr. Bolton's collection. Fanny ignored them entirely, her eyes fixed solely on the door. Finally, she gave a little shake, her eyes lighting up. *He had come out. He would pass the door.*

But her hope died instantly. Pendennis emerged, yes, but Bows followed closely behind. They passed under the archway together. Bows merely tipped his hat and bowed as he glanced in; he didn't stop to speak. Within a few minutes, Bows returned alone and entered the porter's room.

"Where's your Ma, dear?" he asked Fanny.

"I don't know," Fanny replied with an angry toss of her head. "I don't follow Ma's footsteps wherever she goes, I suppose, Mr. Bows."

"Am I my mother's keeper?" Bows muttered, his usual melancholy bitterness returning. "Come here, Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann. I've brought a cake for the one who can read her letters best, and another for the runner-up."

Once the girls were examined and awarded their gingerbread medals, they scampered off to eat in the courtyard. Fanny retreated to her work, pretending to sew while her mind raced with excitement and anger, her needle moving mechanically. Bows sat positioned to command the view of the entrance from the lodge to the street. But the person he seemingly awaited never appeared.

Soon, Mrs. Bolton returned from the market to find Mr. Bows sitting exactly where she had expected to see her husband. The reader might guess the identity of the missing man.

The interview between Bows and his guest in the cramped apartment shared with the descendant of the Milesian kings was hardly satisfactory for either party. Pendennis was sulky. If Bows had anything to say, he clearly preferred not to utter it in front of Captain Costigan, who had vacated his bedchamber mere moments before Pen's arrival. Having witnessed the undressing of Major Pendennis, surely no one wishes to serve as valet to our other hero, Costigan? Before stepping out, the Captain had applied a generous amount of otto-of-whisky, emitting a rich, pungent aroma as he extended a cordial handshake. The grip was shaky; it was a marvel how the poor gentleman managed to hold the razor he used daily on his chin.

Bows's room, conversely, was immaculate. His wardrobe hung neatly behind a curtain, his books and manuscript music were trimmed on shelves, and a lithographed portrait of Miss Fotheringay as Mrs. Haller hung above his bed, signed sprawlingly by the actress. Lady Mirabel, however, wrote with far greater elegance. Since her marriage, she had diligently cultivated penmanship, signing notes with genteel grace.
