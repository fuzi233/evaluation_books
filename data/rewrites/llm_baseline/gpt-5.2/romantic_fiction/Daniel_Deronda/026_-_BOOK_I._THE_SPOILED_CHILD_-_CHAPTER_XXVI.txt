Mr. Gascoigne came to Offendene with news he believed would steady a tottering household: Mrs. Mompert had fixed the following Tuesday for Gwendolen’s interview at Wanchester. He delivered it with the air of a man setting a stone into place—firmly, as if the wall would stand because one must.

He did not mention that Mr. Grandcourt was again at Diplow. He knew nothing of Leubronn as the end of that journey, and he judged it both needless and unkind to rake up a glittering possibility now that their circumstances had gone grey and tight as overboiled linen. In his private mind he regretted his niece’s caprice—unintelligible still—yet he excused it by remembering Grandcourt’s own strange withdrawal when admiration might have ripened into avowal. The rector’s sense of duty, trained to parish weather and human frailty, urged only this: she must be helped to meet what could not be avoided.

“You will find an interest in varied experience, my dear,” he said, arranging his words as if laying out cutlery in its proper order. “And I have no doubt you will be a more valuable woman for having sustained such a part as you are called to.”

“I cannot pretend to believe that I shall like it,” Gwendolen answered. For the first time there was a sharpness, like the quick crack of a crust split too soon. “But I am quite aware that I am obliged to bear it.”

“And your good sense will teach you to behave suitably under it,” he returned, with a shade more gravity. “Mrs. Mompert will be pleased with you. You will know how to conduct yourself to a woman who holds—in all senses—the relation of a superior to you. This trouble has come on you young; that makes it in some respects easier. There is a benefit in all chastisement if we adjust our minds to it.”

But her mind would not adjust. It was not a joint that could be coaxed into place by patience and pressure; it resisted like a lid warped by heat. When her uncle was gone, she sat alone and the tears came—slowly, bitterly, as if each drop had to be forced out from some depth where pride had kept it sealed. Young? Easier? Her heart denied it. If happiness did not come while she was young, when was it to come at all?

Her old visions of ease and triumph had already begun to sour: she could no longer believe that life’s fruits might be bitten without meeting some hard kernel, some bruising truth. Yet that very disenchantment, that new suspicion of herself—of her not being fashioned for effortless pre-eminence—only widened the sterile distance around her, hemming her in with a blank horizon. She could see the path at her feet and it looked dreary; it took courage merely to put one foot before another.

She was in that first fierce rebellion of youth, not so much against pain as against the absence of joy—the rage of a morning that ought to be bright and is instead a cold drizzle of duties. Later, when years have taught us to think of calamity as a common risk, we grow impatient with the amazement that this blow should be mine. And yet—many who afterward have stood between another and hurt, keeping their own wound quiet, began by raging like this when the world refused their desire. Gwendolen’s proud nature, trained from childhood to care most for what happened to her, could not swallow the thought that she must submit: Klesmer’s verdict that she could not command fortune by mere will; Mrs. Mompert’s expected rigours; the forced cheerfulness with three Miss Momperts; the perpetual demand to be agreeable and yet silent inside. The descent from a throne is nothing beside the discovery that one’s imagined divinity cannot summon a miracle.

The drawing-room held its accustomed gentility, but it felt airless, as if all the windows had been painted shut. She rose from the low ottoman where she had been sitting without purpose and walked up and down, her elbow planted on one palm, her cheek leaning on the other. A tear slipped down with the slow insistence of something that will not be denied.

“I have always—ever since I was little—felt that mamma was not a happy woman,” she thought. “And now I dare say I shall be more unhappy than she has been.”

For a moment she pictured herself losing her youth, ceasing to care, moving through days like a shadow that no longer startles at its own outline. But the image would not stay hers alone; it pulled her mother into it. “Poor mamma! It will be still worse for her now. I can get a little money for her—that is all I shall care about now.”

Then something in her imagination shifted, as if a door opened that had never opened before. She saw her mother old, hair gone white; she saw herself no longer young, faded and dulled. And she felt, with a sudden tenderness that pierced the armour of self-absorption, the moment when their faces might meet and memory would pass between them without words—her mother thinking, Poor Gwen too is sad and faded now. For the first time she sobbed not in anger, but with a grief that had love in it.

Her face was toward the door. She saw her mother enter—barely, because her eyes were flooded and she pressed her handkerchief against them in haste, ashamed of the evidence of weakness. Before she could compose herself, her mother’s arms were round her. The touch came like the completion of her own inward picture, and it undid her resolve to be reticent. The sobbing rose again in spite of her; cheeks pressed together, warmth against her trembling skin.

Mrs. Davilow had come in holding something—paper, folded, white against her fingers—and with it an agitation that made her look paler than usual, a tremor in her mouth. But she dared not speak until her darling was calmer.

Gwendolen, to whom weeping had always been a humiliating betrayal, pressed the handkerchief hard against her eyes again, drew a deep breath, and pulled back to look at her mother.

“It was nothing, mamma,” she said, thinking her mother’s alarm was only the sight of tears. “It is all over now.”

But Mrs. Davilow had already loosened her hold, and Gwendolen saw the letter.

“What is that letter?—worse news still?” she asked, with bitterness that flared like salt on a cut.

“I don’t know what you will think it, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow, keeping the letter in her hand as if it were hot. “You will hardly guess where it comes from.”

“Don’t ask me to guess anything,” said Gwendolen impatiently, as if her bruised feeling were being pressed upon.

“It is addressed to you, dear.”

A tiny toss of the head—an old motion of pride.

“It comes from Diplow,” said Mrs. Davilow, and gave it her.

Gwendolen knew the indistinct handwriting. The colour rushed into her face—deeply, quickly—then, as she read, it drained away as if some invisible hand had turned a tap. Even her lips went pale. She held out the open note without speaking.

The words were few, formal, edged like cold metal: Mr. Grandcourt presents his compliments to Miss Harleth, and begs to know whether he may be permitted to call at Offendene to-morrow after two and to see her alone. Mr. Grandcourt has just returned from Leubronn, where he had hoped to find Miss Harleth.

Mrs. Davilow read and looked up, questioning. Gwendolen let the note fall to the floor and turned away.

“It must be answered, darling,” Mrs. Davilow said timidly. “The man waits.”

Gwendolen sank on the settee, clasped her hands, and stared straight before her, as if listening for what follows a sudden sound in the night. A few minutes earlier she had been trapped in the dull corridor of necessity; now—without warning—a door stood open. A moment of choice had come.

Triumph, yes: it stirred in her like a remembered taste of power at the very instant she had begun to learn the bitterness of insignificance. Again, someone’s will bent toward hers; again, her life seemed to turn on her own decision. But with the triumph came terror—an inward recoil that had nothing to do with modesty and everything to do with what she knew.

Quickly, like pages forced open by a hurried thumb, there flashed back what she had been through with Grandcourt: the first allurements; her vacillations; her near assent; the final repulsion; and, cutting through all, the dark-eyed lady with the lovely boy—an image that had become a symbol of her new disbelief in the worth of men’s admiration and promises. Had she pledged herself—had she, in her soul, sworn never? The memory was unalterable; it made a shape in the mind that her native dread shrank from. What good was choice, if choice only meant facing that shape again?

And yet, deep beneath fear, a new wish began to germinate—blind, urgent, unreasoning: I wish I had never known it. If something, anything, could save her from letting Grandcourt come.

Mrs. Davilow waited—too long, it seemed, though it could only have been moments—before she said gently, “It will be necessary for you to write, dear. Or shall I write an answer for you—which you will dictate?”

“No, mamma,” said Gwendolen, drawing a deep breath. “But please lay me out the pen and paper.”

That was a way of gaining time, of bringing hands into play so that thought might hide behind a small ceremony. Was she to refuse? To shut the shutters on possibility? To keep herself safe in the same narrow future she had just been railing against?

Something restless in her—the young current that could not bear stagnation—ran warm under the fear and pushed toward an event, toward the chance to speak, to look, to act with her former effectiveness. The morrow was no longer a dead wall.

“There is really no reason on earth why you should be so alarmed at the man’s waiting a few minutes, mamma,” she said, with a remonstrant lift of tone as her mother set the writing materials and then looked toward her. “Servants expect nothing else than to wait. It is not to be supposed that I must write on the instant.”

“No, dear,” said Mrs. Davilow, corrected and quiet, turning to sit down and take up a piece of work lying near. “He can wait another quarter of an hour, if you like.”

The simplicity of it—needlework resumed, time allowed—had the effect of something subtly chosen. Gwendolen felt a contradictory wish to be hurried. Hurry would rescue her from deliberation; it would let her act without feeling the full weight of deciding.

“I did not mean him to wait long enough for that needlework to be finished,” she said, lifting her hands to smooth the backward curves of her hair, then standing still as if motion might shake loose a conclusion she could not yet bear.

“But if you don’t feel able to decide?” said her mother, with sympathy that made the question gentle.

“I must decide,” said Gwendolen, and walked to the writing-table and sat down.

All the while, her mind ran a busy under-current, like someone keeping up a conversation while searching for a way to slip out of a room. Why not let him come? It bound her to nothing. He had gone to Leubronn after her; of course he meant to renew his suit plainly now. And then? She could refuse him. Why deny herself the freedom of doing what she wanted—to refuse him, to feel the old mastery in her voice?

“If Mr. Grandcourt has only just returned from Leubronn,” Mrs. Davilow said, seeing Gwendolen lean back with the pen in her hand, “I wonder whether he has heard of our misfortunes?”

“That could make no difference to a man in his position,” Gwendolen answered, with a contempt that tried to sound like certainty.

“It would to some men,” said Mrs. Davilow. “They would not like to take a wife from a family in a state of beggary almost, as we are. Here we are at Offendene with a great shell over us, as usual. But just imagine his finding us at Sawyer’s Cottage. Most men are afraid of being bored or taxed by a wife’s family. If Mr. Grandcourt did know, I think it a strong proof of his attachment to you.”

She spoke with an emphasis unusual for her—hesitant courage breaking through long reserve. And the words, meant as nothing more than a possibility, struck Gwendolen with a force her mother could not measure. They raised a fresh vision: what he might do for her mother if she—if she did what she was not going to do. The thought did not settle into resolve; it only set her blood moving with a sudden rush, as if a new flavour—dangerous and compelling—had touched her tongue.

The pressure of those ideas made her feel she must finish the immediate thing quickly, before she was overwhelmed by all that might follow. After all, she did what she had wanted: she acted in haste, and haste became a reason for postponing an absolute inward answer. She wrote:

“Miss Harleth presents her compliments to Mr. Grandcourt. She will be at home after two o’clock to-morrow.”

Before addressing the note, she said, “Pray ring the bell, mamma, if there is any one to answer it.” She truly did not know who did the work of the house now—what hands moved behind the scenes, quiet as breath, keeping their gentility from collapsing.

When the letter had been taken away, Gwendolen rose and stretched out one arm, then rested it on her head, letting out a low moan that held something like relief. Only then did Mrs. Davilow dare ask, “What did you say, Gwen?”

“I said that I should be at home,” Gwendolen answered, with a loftiness that tried to settle the matter. Then, after a pause, as if warning her mother against hope: “You must not expect, because Mr. Grandcourt is coming, that anything is going to happen, mamma.”

“I don’t allow myself to expect anything, dear. I desire you to follow your own feeling. You have never told me what that was.”

“What is the use of telling?” said Gwendolen, hearing a reproach in the truth. “When I have anything pleasant to tell, you may be sure I will tell you.”

“But Mr. Grandcourt will consider that you have already accepted him, in allowing him to come,” said Mrs. Davilow. “His note tells you plainly enough that he is coming to make you an offer.”

“Very well; and I wish to have the pleasure of refusing him.”

Mrs. Davilow looked up, amazed. But Gwendolen, as if the air itself were suddenly too thick to breathe, cut off further questioning.

“Put down that detestable needlework, and let us walk in the avenue. I am stifled.”
