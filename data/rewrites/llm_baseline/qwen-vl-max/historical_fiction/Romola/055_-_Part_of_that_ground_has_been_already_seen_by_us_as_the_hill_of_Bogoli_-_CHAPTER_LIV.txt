Romola moved swiftly through the dimming streets, her purpose growing stronger with each step—like a hidden stream swelling beneath the surface of a river, rising steadily alongside the main current. It was not so much a decision as an inner compulsion, something that could no longer be held back. She paid no mind to the shadows lengthening across the cobbles or the quiet fear that might come from walking alone. She crossed the bridge where the Arno lay dark and still, reflecting only the faint glow of dying light on the horizon, and took the shortest path to the Old Palace. If she met her husband there, it mattered little. She could not calculate risks; she had to speak, to release what was burning within her.

She did not notice the columns of the courtyard or the grand staircase leading upward. Her thoughts were fixed on one thing: to reach the Gonfaloniere. When she reached the hall, she asked an usher for Bernardo del Nero, named herself, and begged to be shown into a private room. She waited only a moment before the door opened and Bernardo entered, his white head held high above the silk lucco draped over his shoulders.

“Romola, my child, what is this?” he said, closing the door behind him with a tone of startled concern.

She had removed her veil and stepped forward without speaking. He placed a hand gently on her shoulder, drawing her slightly away so he could look at her face. Her features were worn by fatigue and long turmoil—her hair loose and tangled—but in her eyes burned a fierce, unyielding energy, as if spirit had overcome body.

“What has he done?” Bernardo asked sharply. “Tell me everything, child. Cast aside pride. I am your father.”

“It is not about myself,” Romola answered quickly. “Not at all. Dearest godfather, it is about you. I have heard things—some I cannot name. But you are in danger here. In the palace. Everywhere. There are men who hate you, fanatics who would see you harmed—and others, traitors, who wear friendly faces. Trust no one. If you trust, you will be betrayed.”

Bernardo smiled faintly. “Have you become so agitated, my poor child,” he said, raising a hand to stroke her brow, “that you bring such ancient truths to an old man like me?”

“No, no!” Romola cried, pressing her hands together tightly as if to silence the words she could not fully speak. “These are not old truths. They are new, fresh, real—things I know but cannot say. You know I am not foolish. I would not come to you unless I had reason. Is it too late now to warn you? To tell you to leave this place? To go to your villa, to wait out these last three days in safety? Oh God—perhaps it is already too late! And if harm comes to you… it will feel as though I had caused it!”

The words escaped her without thought, born of a long-suppressed dread. Then she stopped, startled by their own weight.

“I mean—I know nothing certain,” she added, faltering. “Only fears. Deep, terrible fears.”

“Poor child,” Bernardo murmured, studying her with quiet intensity. After a pause, he said, “Go, Romola. Return home and rest. These fears may be nothing more than shadows cast by small things. Even traitors act from self-interest—the rats run where they smell cheese. We do not yet know which way the scent blows.”

He turned away, gazing into space for a moment, then shrugged slowly. “As for warnings—they serve no purpose for me, child. I do not join plots. But I never abandon my cause. If I march beside stubborn men who charge into guns and pikes, I must bear the consequences. Let us not speak of it further. I have few years left in my sack; let them take what they can. Go now. Rest.”

He touched her head again, softly, tenderly. And she could not help clinging to his arm, pressing her forehead against his shoulder. His touch felt like the final remnant of a life once full of warmth and belonging—a life now seen in memory as pure, even in its sorrow, because it had known no bitterness.

“Is silence best, my Romola?” he asked.

“For now,” she whispered, lifting her head with a pleading glance. “But I cannot say if it will always be.”

“Well,” he replied, lifting the black drapery and folding it carefully around her head, “you have a father’s ear while I live—and a father’s home. Remember that.”

Then he opened the door. “Now go. Hasten. You look like a shadow—dark and swift—and that will keep you safe enough.”

That night, Romola slept deeply. The strain had reached its peak; her body needed rest before it could endure more. Though her habits were strict, she slept on past dawn.

When she awoke, the sound of gunfire echoed through the city. Piero de’ Medici, backed by thirteen hundred men, had arrived at the gate facing Rome. Maso brought her the news, adding many details of uncertain truth. A countryman had warned the Signoria just before daybreak, or Florence might have fallen without warning. Her husband had been summoned to the Palazzo hours earlier and was not at home.

She sent Maso out again to gather information, while she climbed to the loggia to watch for signs of invasion—or resistance. The great Piazza was crowded with armed men. Many citizens suspected of favoring the Medici had been called to the palace and detained. Some people seemed indifferent to Piero’s arrival; others claimed the Signoria had invited him. But whatever the truth, the reception was harsh. Soldiers from Pisa were advancing against him.

In Romola’s memory of those morning hours, few external events stood clearly apart from the storm of thought and dread. She remembered walking to the Badia at the appointed time, despite alarms in the streets. She remembered waiting in vain. And she remembered vividly the scene outside the church when she stepped onto the steps as the doors closed for the afternoon.

The faces of the people—armed and unarmed—had changed. Their voices carried a new tone. The guns fired again, but instead of fear, laughter rose among the crowd. Soon she learned why.

Piero de’ Medici and his riders had turned away from Florence, galloping hard toward Siena.

She heard this from a sturdy shopkeeper from Piagnone, who still held his pike. “It is true,” he said, with a bitter edge in his voice. “Piero is gone—but those who helped him remain. We all know that. And if the new Signoria does its duty, we shall soon learn who they are.”

The words struck Romola like a sudden pain. But the threat they hinted at was not yet upon her. As she returned home, her greatest worry was whether she had lost sight of Baldassarre for too long.
