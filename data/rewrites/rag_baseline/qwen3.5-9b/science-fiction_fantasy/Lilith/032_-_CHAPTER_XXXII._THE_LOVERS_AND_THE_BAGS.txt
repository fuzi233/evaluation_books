The night descended with a bitter, absolute cold that seeped through bone and muscle alike. The wolf pack drew closer, their padded footsteps silent on the rock, their panting breaths filling the stagnant air. Through the gloom, a circle of glowing eyes tightened its grip. My time had arrived. I surged to my feet—useless, for I possessed no weapon, no staff, no shield. They rushed forward, jaws gaping in a frenzy of predatory greed, black throats ready to consume. I stood paralyzed, awaiting the end. Just as their maws opened, a cloud of emerald fire erupted from the flank of the nearest beast. Heads snapped upward, driven by a shriek that cut deeper than any howl. Cats. Led by a massive gray sentinel whose presence commanded the chaos. I saw only his eyes, yet I knew him—their color, their scale, their lethal intent.

A slaughter ensued, a brutal dance of death played out in the dark. I would have fled, but the terrain betrayed me; a single misstep meant a broken ankle, and in the dark, both wolves and cats could smell blood before they saw it. Suddenly, the howling ceased, replaced by a cacophony of feral yowls. Then, the soft padding returned, signaling retreat. The cats had won. Before I could process the victory, sharp teeth sank into my legs. Within moments, I was engulfed by a living avalanche of fur and claw. Dozens of them clung to my body, a writhing mass of malice. I fought blindly, tearing at the swarm, strangling them in vain. When I tried to throw them off, they adhered to my hands like limpets, refusing release. I trampled them, gouged their eyes, bit down with jaws stronger than theirs, yet the tide never receded. They discovered new angles of attack constantly, hauling at my skin with curved, crushing talons, hissing and spitting in my face—but sparing it entirely until, in sheer desperation, I collapsed. Instantly, they abandoned my prone form to strike at my exposed face. I scrambled up, and they retreated to focus on my legs. In agony, I broke free and ran, stumbling blindly into the solid void. They pursued in a surging torrent, rubbing against me, leaping onto my back, tormenting me relentlessly. Yet, they held me to a relatively smooth path; I avoided the gullies, slipping past the Evil Wood unseen, leaving it behind in the encroaching dark.

Dawn broke as I reached the edge of the orchard valley, far beyond the channels. Overcome with joy, I expected to be greeted by my rescuers, but not a cat was in sight. I collapsed onto the moss, exhaustion claiming me instantly. Sleep was short-lived. A kick jolted me awake. Bound hand and foot, I was once again the property of the Giants. "What fitter?" I muttered to myself, finding a twisted sense of irony in my captivity. "To whom else should I belong?" I laughed, a sound born of self-loathing and triumph. Another kick silenced the jest. Assisted by my captors, I rose. Six figures surrounded me. They severed the rope binding my legs, attaching new lines to each limb before dragging me away. I stumbled, falling repeatedly as they pulled simultaneously; each time, a boot connected with my ribs to hoist me upright. They marched me straight to my former post of labor. Tying my leg-rope to a sturdy oak, they released my arms and handed me the hated flint. They reclined, pelting me gently with fallen fruit and stones, rarely making contact. If I could have freed my legs to grab the stick I'd spotted mere yards away, I would have taken down all six of them instantly. "But the Little Ones will come at night," I reminded myself, finding solace in the memory.

All day, I labored under the sun. As twilight fell, they bound my hands again, leaving me tethered to the tree. I drifted in and out of sleep, waking frequently from dreams of lying amidst heaps of children. At noon, weary and starving, a sudden commotion erupted in the brushwood, followed by a burst of laughter—the bell-like sound that warmed my soul. I cried out in delight. Immediately, a trumpet call echoed like baby elephants, a neighing akin to foals, and a bellowing similar to calves. Through the bushes poured a host of Little Ones riding diminutive horses, small elephants, and little bears; the sounds emanated from the riders, not the beasts. Walking alongside them were older boys and girls, including a woman cradling a baby crowing in her arms. The Giants lurched to their feet, only to be met with a storm of sharp stones. Horses charged their legs; bears rose to hug them at the waist; elephants wrapped their trunks around their necks, pulling them down and delivering a trampling unlike anything the giants had ever experienced.

In seconds, my ropes were severed. I was embraced by an innumerable sea of children. For a while, the Giants vanished. I was seated as Lona approached, silently feeding me the finest red and yellow fruits. I ate while the entire colony maintained a vigilant guard. Then, two of their largest elephants were brought forward. Placed side by side, their trunks were hooked together and their tails tied. Though the docile creatures could have untied themselves with a simple shake or forgotten the knot entirely, they remained rigid, keeping their bodies perfectly parallel. I climbed into the hollow between their backs. As the wise animals countered the weight pushing them apart, leaning inward, they created a comfortable litter. My feet protruded slightly beyond their tails, but my head rested pillowed on the ear of each. Smaller children mounted as a bodyguard, lining the backs of my bearers. The procession moved. Where they carried me, I did not guess; I yielded to their pleasure, almost as happy as they. Initially, the children chattered, laughed, and performed endless joyful tricks. The moment they sensed my drifting toward sleep, they fell as still as statues.

I awoke to a sudden, musical uproar. We traveled through the forest where the babies were found—a vast expanse stretching from the valley to the hot stream. A tiny girl sat with her feet inches from my face, looking down coaxingly before speaking, the others hanging on her words. "We make a petisson to king," she announced. "What is it, my darling?" I asked. "Shut eyes one minute," she replied. "Certainly I will! Here goes!" I answered, closing my lids tight. "No, no! Not before I tell you!" she cried. I opened them again, and we conversed and laughed for another hour. "Close eyes!" she commanded suddenly. I complied, holding them shut. The elephants halted. I heard a soft scurry, a faint rustle, then a profound silence—for in that world, SOME silences ARE heard. "Open eyes!" twenty voices shouted from a distance. But when I obeyed, no creature was visible save the elephants bearing me. I knew the children were marvelously adept at vanishing—the Giants had taught them that. Yet, raising myself to survey the open, shrubless forest, I discerned neither hand nor heel. Staring in blank astonishment, I watched the sun dip below the horizon. Birds began to sing. I lay down, confident that if left undisturbed, the hidden ones would emerge soon. The chorus grew into a storm of avian voices. "Surely the children must be responsible!—Yet how could they command the birds?" I wondered aloud.

Looking up into the tree supporting my litter, I spotted a slight movement among the leaves. Peering closely, white spots emerged in the dark foliage. The music faded, replaced by a gale of childish laughter rippling through the air. White spots appeared everywhere; the trees were teeming with children. In wild abandon, they began to descend, some dropping from bough to bough so rapidly I doubted they hadn't already fallen. I left my litter and was instantly besieged—a target for their jubilant artillery. With stately composure, the elephants walked away to bed. "But," I remarked as their uproar subsided, "how is it that I never heard you sing like birds before? Even when I suspected it was you, I couldn't believe it!" "Ah," said one of the wildest, "we were not birds then! We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures! We had hide-places in the bushes then, but when we came to no-bushes, only trees, we had to build nests! When we built nests, we grew birds, and when we were birds, we had to act like birds! We asked them to teach us their noises, and they taught us, and now we are real birds! Come and see my nest. It's not big enough for king, but it's big enough for king to see me in it!" I explained I could not climb a tree without sunlight to guide me; when he returned, I would try. "Kings seldom have wings!" I added. "King! King!" cried one, "oo knows none of us hasn't no wings--foolis feddery tings! Arms and legs is better." "That is true. I can get up without wings—and carry straws in my mouth too, to build my nest with!" "Oo knows!" he answered, departing to suck his thumb. Moments later, I heard him calling from high within an enormous walnut tree, "Up adain, king! Dood night! I seepy!" And I heard no more of him until he woke me in the morning.
