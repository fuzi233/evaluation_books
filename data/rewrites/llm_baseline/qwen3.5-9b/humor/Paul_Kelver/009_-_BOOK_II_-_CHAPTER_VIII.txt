The eighteen months that followed—though the end arrived sooner than anyone dared hope—were, I believe, the most radiant days my parents ever experienced. Or perhaps "happy" isn't quite the word; let us say they were the most beautiful, the most perfectly harmonious. To them, it felt as though God, in a stroke of divine kindness, had sent Death to tap gently on the doorframe and whisper, *"Not yet. You have a little while longer. Just a little while."*

During those final days, they shed everything artificial and meaningless. Nothing mattered except loving one another, comforting one another, and finally understanding one another. We lived poorly again, but there was no longer that pathetic straining to maintain appearances, no paralyzing fear of the neighbors' opinions. The petty anxieties regarding things not worth a single thought, the base desires and fears that usually disfigure us, simply fell away. In their place came broader minds, wider charity, and a deeper pity. Their love expanded beyond their own needs, overflowing toward all creation. Sometimes, looking back, I wonder if we err by trying to keep Death—the divine intermediary—forever out of our thoughts. Perhaps we are shutting the door on a friend who wishes to help. Who knows life better than He? He whispers softly: *"In a little while. Only a little longer to be together. Is it worth worrying so much about yourself? Is it worth being unkind?"*

From them radiated a grace that permeated everyone around. Even Aunt Fan, deciding for the second time in her life to try amiability, announced this public conversion to my mother and me one afternoon shortly after returning from Devonshire.

"I'm a beast of an old woman," Aunt Fan declared suddenly.

"Don't say that, Fan," my mother pleaded.

"What's the use of saying 'don't say it' when I've already said it?" Aunt Fan snapped back.

"It's your manner," my mother explained gently. "People sometimes think you're disagreeable."

"They'd be daft if they didn't!" Aunt Fire interrupted. "Of course you don't *really* mean it," my mother countered with a smile.

"Nonsense," Aunt Fan scoffed. "Does she think I'm a fool? I like being disagreeable. I like seeing them squirm."

My mother laughed.

"But I *can* be agreeable," Aunt Fan continued. "Nobody more so."

"Then why not choose?" my mother suggested.

"I tried it once," Aunt Fan admitted. "It fell flat. Nothing could have fallen flatter."

"It may not have attracted much attention," my mother replied, "but one shouldn't be agreeable merely to attract attention."

"It wasn't only that," Aunt Fan returned. "It gave no satisfaction to anyone. It didn't suit me. A disagreeable person is at their best when they are disagreeable."

"I can hardly agree with that," my mother said.

"I could do it again," Aunt Fan murmured to herself, with a hint of vindictiveness. "It's easy enough. Look at the sort of fools that are agreeable."

"I'm sure you could be if you tried," my mother urged.

"Let 'em have it," Aunt Fan muttered, still talking to herself. "That's the way to teach 'em sense. Let 'em have it."

Strangely enough, my aunt was right, and my mother was entirely wrong. My father was the first to notice the shift.

"Nothing the matter with poor old Fan, is there?" he asked one evening, a day or two after she carried out her threat. "Nothing happened, has there?"

"No," my mother answered. "Nothing that I know of."

"Her manner is so strange," my father explained. "So... so weird."

My mother smiled. "Don't mention it to her. She's trying to be agreeable."

My father laughed, then looked wistful. "I almost wish she wouldn't," he remarked. "We were used to it, and she was rather amusing."

But Aunt Fan, being a woman of iron will, persisted. About the same time, an event confirmed her new direction: the arrival of James Wellington Gadley. Technically, he should have been Wellington James, having been christened in 1815 in that order. However, over the years—and specifically during his schooling—he realized that "Wellington" was a burdensome name for a common mortal. Wisely, he reversed the arrangement.

Mr. Gadley was a slightly pompous yet simple-minded little old gentleman, fiercely proud of his role as head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father was now attached. Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal traced its lineage back to the Georges, a firm deeply entwined with the history—sometimes shady—of aristocratic England. True, its glory was fading. Old Mr. Stillwood, the sole survivor, refused new partners, frankly stating that the business was dying and trying to revive it would be like pouring new wine into worn-out skins. Yet, despite shrinking clientele, high-class business remained, and the firm's name still signified solid respectability. My father considered himself incredibly fortunate to secure such an appointment.

James Gadley had joined as an office boy in the firm's prime and never grasped that it was no longer the most important legal practice within a half-mile of Lombard Street. Nothing pleased him more than recounting the strange cases handled by Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal, all of which he memorized verbatim. If he found a listener, he would rehash them endlessly, using professional discretion to rename characters as "Mr. Y" and "Lady X," and locations as "the capital of a foreign country" or "a certain town not a thousand miles from here." Most friends tried to discourage him, but Aunt Fan was an inexhaustible listener, especially if the case involved mystery and crime.

During their very first conversation, when Gadley exclaimed, "Now why—why, after keeping away from his wife for nearly eighteen years, never letting her know if he was alive or dead, does he suddenly decide to return to her? That needs explaining!" he paused, expecting sympathy. Instead of yawning and replying, "Oh, I suppose he felt like seeing her," Aunt Fan promptly intoned:

"To murder her—with slow poison."

"To murder her! But why?"

"In order to marry the other woman."

"What other woman?"

"The one he just met and fell in love with. Before that, his wife's fate was irrelevant. This woman told him: 'Come back to me a free man, or never see my face again.'"

"Dear me! Now that is curious."

"Not in the least. Plain common sense."

"I mean, it's curious because, as a matter of fact, his wife died shortly after, and he did remarry."

"Told you so," Aunt Fan remarked.

Thus, every case in the Stillwood archives was scrutinized, illuminated by Aunt Fan's unique insight into the hidden springs of human action. Fortunately, the actors remained anonymous Mr. Xs and Lady Ys, for into the most innocent behavior, Aunt Fan read dark criminal intent.

"I think you are a little too severe," Mr. Gadley would occasionally plead.

"We're all miserable sinners," Aunt Fan would cheerfully affirm. "Only we don't all get the same chances."

She believed an elderly spinster, Miss Z., living in a former fashionable western town, had burned down a house containing a will and forged another to ensure her hypothetical future children would inherit £700 upon reaching adulthood. The freshness of her theories always fascinated Mr. Gadley.

"I must thank you, ma'am," he would say as he rose. "Though I may not agree with your conclusions, they provide excellent food for reflection."

To which Aunt Fan would reply, "I hate talking to anyone who agrees with me. It's like walking past one's own looking glass. I'd rather talk to someone who disagrees, even if they're a fool," which she considered gracious.

He was a stout little gentleman with a stomach protruding about a foot forward, seemingly unaware of it. Worse, his desire to lean in while talking caused his belly to act as a gentle battering ram, gradually driving listeners backward around the room until, unless they were clever, they were pinned hopelessly against a wall. Surprisingly, he never managed to get closer. On his first evening, he started from the corner of his chair; as he grew interested, he moved nearer until Aunt Fan, inching back to escape his encroachment, ended up sitting on the very edge of her seat. His next move sent her to the floor. She said nothing, surprising me. But on his next visit, she was busily darning stockings—an unusual hobby for her. He approached as before, but this time she held her ground, and it was he who eventually recoiled with an exclamation unrelated to the topic.

From then on, Aunt Fan met him with stockings in hand, maintaining a respectful distance between their chairs.

Nothing further resulted, though his status as a widower added a spicy possibility to their interactions—a flavor no woman is ever too old to enjoy; yet it was clear he admired her intellect. Once, he even cried out, "Miss Davies, you should have been a solicitor's wife!" considering it the pinnacle of feminine ambition. Aunt Fan replied, "Chances are I should have been, if anyone had ever asked." Warmed by this appreciation, her amiability took root and flourished, albeit in a somewhat fantastical shape.
