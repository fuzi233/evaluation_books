A full month of hard cross-continental travel brought Durrance home to London, where a sealed, black-edged envelope from Ethne waited on the desk of his club. Her note was short: she was staying with Mrs. Adair on Hill Street, and would be grateful if he could find time to call. He showed up at her door the very next afternoon, and found her sitting alone in the quiet drawing room.
“I didn’t write to Wadi Halfa,” she explained at once, her voice soft but steady, “I was sure you’d already be on your way home before any letter I sent could catch you. My father died at the end of last month, in the early hours of a May morning.”
“I feared as much when I saw the black border on your envelope,” Durrance said gently. “I am so sorry. You will miss him terribly.”
“More than I can ever put into words,” she replied, a quiet, deep ache running under every syllable. She went on to tell him the full story of Dermod’s final days: a chill had only triggered the end, not caused it, his life fading slow and soft, no sharp, brutal illness to tear him down. Most strange of all, in those last hours, he had reclaimed the sharp, masterful fire of his younger self.
“We knew he was dying, and he knew it too,” Ethne said. “At seven that afternoon, after he’d spoken to me for a few quiet minutes, he whispered my old retriever’s name. The dog bounded straight up onto the bed, even though his voice was barely a breath, nuzzling his muzzle under my father’s hand with a low whine. Then he told me to leave the two of them completely alone, lock the door behind me. The dog would alert me when it was time to open it again. I obeyed. I stood out in that corridor for six whole hours, watching the last of the afternoon fade to full dark. He wouldn’t let us leave a lamp in his room. I could picture him just the other side of that thin panel, lying still in his old four-poster, face turned towards the Donegal hills he loved, the shadows creeping slow across the walls, the dog curled at his side, no one else with him right to the end. Then, at one in the morning, that howl split the quiet of the whole house.”
She paused for just a beat, the only flicker of raw grief she allowed herself, before she went on, plain and unflinching, no performative sorrow. “Those six hours waiting outside the door were the hardest thing I’ve ever had to do. But it was what he wanted.”
Durrance said nothing, gave her the quiet, unpressured silence that grieving people need far more than empty words. He could easily picture her pressed to the wood of that door, hand pressed to her chest to muffle the thud of her own heart, and a sharp, hot twist of anger coiled in his gut that Feversham’s cowardice had left her to go through that alone, no steady partner at her side.
It was Ethne who broke the quiet first. “Before he sent me out of the room, my father spoke to me about someone. Guess who?”
She looked straight at him, no tell of the answer in her steady eyes or even tone, and a wild, sudden spark of hope caught Durrance’s breath in his throat. “Tell me,” he leaned forward in his chair, barely able to sit still.
“Harry Feversham,” she said.
Durrance jolted back hard in his seat, the hope dying as fast as it had flared. He dropped his gaze to the carpet, not wanting her to see the flash of raw anger cross his face.
“My father was always very fond of him,” she went on softly. “I hoped you might have news of him? Know where he is, what he’s doing these days?”
Durrance stayed silent for a long minute, staring at the pattern under his feet. It struck him as bitterly strange that even the man Feversham had wronged, even the father of the woman he’d abandoned on the eve of their wedding, would carry nothing but kindness for him to his deathbed. He feared his voice would shake if he spoke, but Ethne pressed him for an answer.
“Have you run into him at all, out in Egypt?”
Durrance stood and walked over to the window before he replied, staring out at the London street below to hide his face. Even then, he could not keep the hot edge of anger out of his tone.
“No,” he said. “I never have. And I never want to. He was my friend, once. But I can’t think of that friendship now. All I can think is: if he’d been the man we all believed him to be, you would never have had to wait those six long hours alone in that dark corridor.”
He turned back to face the room, his tone sharp and abrupt. “You’re going back to Glenalla, then?”
“Yes.”
“To live there, alone?”
“Yes.”
Silence hung between them for a while, before Durrance circled around to stand behind her chair. “You once told me you might explain why your engagement to Feversham broke off.”
“But you already know,” Ethne said, confused. “You just said as much a minute ago.”
“I don’t, not the full story. Your father dropped a few hints, the last time I spoke to him, asking for news of Harry, but I don’t know the facts. I want you to tell me.”
Ethne shook her head, leaning forward, elbows resting on her knees. “Not now.”
The quiet settled again, until Durrance spoke once more. “I’ve only got one more year left on my posting at Wadi Halfa. I’ll leave Egypt after that. I don’t see any major Soudan campaigns kicking off any time soon, so there’s no reason for me to stay. Even if you decide to stay on alone at Glenalla.”
Ethne made no pretense of missing the meaning of his words. “We’re neither of us children,” she said. “You have your whole life ahead of you. We both need to be prudent.”
“Prudent, yes,” Durrance snapped, sudden frustration bubbling over. “But the right kind of prudence. The kind that knows it’s worth risking everything for something that matters.”
Ethne didn’t move. Her back was to him, so he could not see her face, and she stayed perfectly still and silent for what felt like an age, before she spoke in a soft, very quiet voice. “Do you want me that badly?”
Before he could answer, she spun around to face him. “Please, try not to,” she said, earnestly. “For this one year, try not to. You have all that work in Egypt to keep you occupied. Try to forget me entirely.” There was no rejection stinging in her tone, only the soft regret of someone who would hate to hurt a friend so loyal, and Durrance walked away with a delusion fixed firm in his head: that the only thing holding her back from saying yes was her fear of ruining his career.
Mrs. Adair stepped into the drawing room before he could argue, so he left soon after. That evening he dined at his club, then sat smoking a cigar under the same old spreading tree in the courtyard where he’d loitered a year before, waiting in vain for any scrap of news about Harry Feversham. The night was almost identical to the one he’d run into limping Lieutenant Sutch: cloudless, star-streaked sky, the soft warm languor of a June summer night, the streetlights gilding the tree leaves to look fresh as spring foliage, the rumble of carriage wheels on the road sounding like the distant roar of the sea.
And just like that night, a man he knew walked straight across the courtyard towards him, no hesitation at all, and sat down on the same bench beside him. It was Captain Mather.
“I was half-hoping I’d spot you,” Mather grinned. “Saw in the evening papers your troop was due back in London around now. You seen the latest news out of Suakin?”
“Can’t say I have,” Durrance said, setting aside the paper he’d been half-reading.
Mather fumbled through the pages, found the short paragraph he was looking for, and handed the paper over. “Remember that final reconnaissance we ran out of Suakin, last year? We stopped at Sinkat Fort at midday, and that Arab informant hiding in the trees behind the ramparts told you that wild story about the Mahdi’s letters to Gordon, hidden in a wall in Berber?”
“Hard to forget,” Durrance nodded.
“Then this will interest you.”
The paragraph was short, no details, barely a summary. It announced that the Mahdi’s letters summoning Gordon to surrender Khartoum and convert to the Mahdist faith, plus Gordon’s sharp, unyielding replies, had been dug out of that hidden wall in Berber and brought safely back to Captain Willoughby, the Deputy Governor of Suakin.
“Must have been some reckless fool who went back to Berber to get those,” Durrance said. “That’s Abou Fatma, the old informant’s name, right?”
“Name’s not mentioned,” Mather shrugged. “Only notes Willoughby took delivery. Hardly worth risking a whole life for a few old letters, if you ask me. Doesn’t change a single fact about the siege, won’t make any difference to anyone, not even historians.”
Durrance agreed, but he was dead wrong. Right there in that courtyard, where he’d once searched for news of Feversham, news of Harry had found him – but he had no idea. He had no clue that that tiny, unremarkable paragraph touched the very roots of his own life, that the reckless man who’d risked everything inside Berber’s walls was the friend he’d once thought dead. He brushed the news aside, and got lost in thoughts of his afternoon with Ethne, no trace of discouragement in him at all. He told himself she’d only asked about Harry because her father’s dying words had brought him up, that the fact she could speak his name so calmly meant he no longer held any power over her. That her request he try to forget her for a year was not a rejection, but a promise: that if he came back to her at the end of it, she would say yes.
A few days later he called back to Hill Street, only to find Mrs. Adair alone. Ethne had left London abruptly, gone back home to Donegal. Mrs. Adair had no idea why she’d rushed off so suddenly, but Durrance knew. She was clearing herself out of his sight, to make it easier for him to move on, just like she’d asked.
What Ethne had forgotten, though, was that she’d never told him to stop writing. That autumn, his letters started arriving from the Soudan again. She was always glad to get them, but they left her troubled. Every line was full of careful, polite reserve, but every now and then a small phrase would slip out – a reference to some trivial throwaway comment she’d made years before, that she’d forgotten entirely – that made it crystal clear he had not forgotten her at all. A bright, unmistakeable hopefulness ran through every page, as if his whole world had burst into new color and sound.
Ethne had never shaken off the heavy, lingering guilt that one man – Harry – had had his whole life destroyed, all because of her. She was determined that would never happen to Durrance. But the letters forced a new, unanswerable question to the front of her mind: how? How could she keep that from happening? She walked the Glenalla hills with his letters crumpled in her pocket, turning the question over and over, and never found an answer. Even her old violin, the one that had always helped her untangle her thoughts before, could not soothe the puzzle out of her this time.
