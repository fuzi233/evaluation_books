Late that evening, I was startled by a visit from Mr. Bruff at my lodgings.

There was something different about the lawyer—his usual confidence had vanished, replaced by a quiet heaviness. He shook my hand without a word, the first time in our acquaintance he had done so in silence.

“Are you returning to Hampstead?” I asked, needing to say something.

“I’ve just left there,” he replied. “I know, Mr. Franklin, that you’ve finally uncovered the truth. But if I’d known what it would cost—what pain it would bring—I’d have preferred you remained in the dark.”

“You’ve seen Rachel?”

“I brought her back to Portland Place. It wasn’t safe to let her return alone in a carriage. I can hardly hold you responsible for the shock she’s endured—after all, you saw her in my house, with my permission. But I must prevent this from happening again. She’s young, strong-willed—she’ll recover, given time and rest. I need your promise: no more attempts to see her—unless I give you leave.”

“After what she’s suffered—and what I’ve suffered—you may count on me,” I said.

“You have my word?”

“I do.”

Mr. Bruff visibly relaxed. He set down his hat and pulled his chair closer.

“That’s settled,” he said. “Now, let’s talk about the future—your future. The way I see it now: first, we’re certain Rachel told you everything, as plainly as words could. Second, though we know something is terribly wrong, we can’t fault her for believing you guilty—her own senses, backed by circumstances that seem to point directly against you, made it hard to doubt.”

I interrupted. “I don’t blame Rachel. I only wish she’d spoken more clearly to me then.”

“You might as well wish she were someone else,” Mr. Bruff countered. “And even then, I doubt any girl of feeling, whose heart was set on marrying you, could have accused you face-to-face of stealing. That wasn’t in her nature. In another matter—though not quite like this—one where she stood in a similar position—I know she acted from the same motive. And as she told me on our way to town tonight, even if she *had* spoken plainly, she wouldn’t have believed your denial then any more than she does now. What reply could you make? There isn’t one. Come on, Mr. Franklin—my earlier view was wrong, I admit. But now, perhaps my advice still holds value. We’re wasting effort if we try to untangle this mess from the beginning. Let’s stop thinking about last year at Lady Verinder’s country house. Instead, let’s focus on what we *can* discover now—rather than what we can’t recall from the past.”

“Surely,” I said, “the whole affair is rooted in the past—as far as I’m concerned.”

“Answer me this,” he challenged. “Is the Moonstone at the heart of all this—or isn’t it?”

“It is—of course.”

“Very well. Where do we believe the stone went when it was taken to London?”

“It was pledged to Mr. Luker.”

“And we know you didn’t pledge it. Do we know who did?”

“No.”

“And where do we think it is now?”

“In the hands of Mr. Luker’s bankers.”

“Exactly. Now listen: it’s already June. By the end of the month—give or take a few days—a full year will have passed since the diamond was pawned. There’s a chance—perhaps even a strong one—that whoever pledged it may be ready to redeem it. If they do, Mr. Luker must personally retrieve the stone from his banker, per their agreement. So here’s my idea: as the month draws to a close, we keep watch at the bank. We find out who receives the Moonstone from Mr. Luker. See how that works?”

I admitted—reluctantly—that it was an original thought.

“It’s just as much Mr. Murthwaite’s idea as mine,” Mr. Bruff added. “I never would’ve thought of it if not for a conversation we had recently. If he’s right, the Indians may be watching the bank too—something serious could happen. But what happens matters little to us, except as it might help us find the person who pawned the diamond. That individual—whatever their connection—is responsible, in some way, for your present ruin. Only that person can restore your standing in Rachel’s eyes.”

“I can’t deny,” I said, “that your plan is bold, clever, and entirely new. But——”

“But you have an objection?”

“Yes. Your proposal means waiting.”

“True. As I figure it, you’d wait about two weeks—give or take. Is that so long?”

“It feels like a lifetime, Mr. Bruff, under these conditions. I can’t endure this state unless I act immediately to clear my name.”

“I understand. Have you considered what you might do?”

“I’ve thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff.”

“He’s retired. Don’t expect him to help.”

“I know where to find him. I’ll try.”

“Try,” Mr. Bruff said after a pause. “The case has changed so drastically since his time that you might stir his interest. Try—and let me know the result. Meanwhile,” he added, rising, “if you find nothing between now and the end of the month, am I free to try watching the bank myself?”

“Of course,” I replied. “Unless I manage to spare you the trouble.”

Mr. Bruff smiled and picked up his hat.

“Tell Sergeant Cuff,” he said, “that *I* say the truth depends on finding the man who pawned the diamond. And let me hear what his experience suggests.”

We parted.

Early the next morning, I set off for Dorking—the small town where Sergeant Cuff had retired, as Betteredge had told me.

At the hotel, I got directions to the sergeant’s cottage. It lay off a quiet lane just outside the town, nestled in its own garden, enclosed by a brick wall at the back and sides, and a high hedge in front. The gate, topped with brightly painted trellis, was locked. After ringing the bell, I peered through the lattice and saw roses everywhere—blooming in the garden, climbing over the door, peering into the windows. Far from the crimes and mysteries of the city, the great detective lived out his final years in peaceful luxury, buried beneath roses.

A modest elderly woman opened the gate. In one moment, she dashed all hope I’d built on securing Cuff’s help. He had left only the day before for Ireland.

“On business?” I asked.

She smiled. “He has only one business now, sir,” she said. “Roses. A gardener in Ireland has discovered something new in rose cultivation—and Mr. Cuff is gone to investigate.”

“When will he return?”

“It’s uncertain, sir. He said he’d come straight back—or stay longer, depending on whether the discovery was worth it. If you have a message, I’ll see he gets it.”

I handed her my card, adding in pencil: *I have something to say about the Moonstone. Let me hear from you as soon as you return.* Then, with no choice but to accept fate, I returned to London.

In my agitated state, the failed journey only deepened my restless urge to act. On the day I returned from Dorking, I resolved that the next morning I would launch a fresh effort to break through the darkness.

What would this new attempt be?

If Betteredge had been present during my deliberations—and if he’d known my thoughts—he’d likely have declared the German side of me was dominant. To be honest, my German training may have contributed to the tangled web of useless theories I now spun. For most of the night, I sat smoking, constructing ever more absurd hypotheses. When I finally slept, my waking thoughts pursued me in dreams. I woke the next morning with Objective-Subjective and Subjective-Objective hopelessly mixed in my mind. I began the day by questioning whether I had any philosophical right to believe anything—diamonds included—actually existed.

How long I might have wandered in this fog of metaphysics, I cannot say. But fortune intervened. That morning, I wore the same coat I’d worn during my interview with Rachel. Searching a pocket for something else, I found a crumpled piece of paper—Betteredge’s forgotten letter.

It seemed unfair to leave my old friend unanswered. I went to my writing desk and read the letter again.

It contained nothing important—yet such letters are often hardest to answer. Betteredge mentioned that Ezra Jennings, Mr. Candy’s assistant, had seen me. Mr. Candy, in turn, wished to speak with me when I next visited Frizinghall. What could I possibly say in response?

I idly sketched Ezra Jennings from memory—his piebald hair, his odd appearance—until suddenly I realized: here was the persistent Ezra Jennings interfering again! I tossed a dozen sketches into the wastebasket and wrote my reply. It was ordinary enough—but it had one powerful effect: the simple act of writing plain English cleared the confusion from my mind.

With clarity restored, I turned once more to the puzzle of my own situation. Since the events of the fateful night remained unclear, I looked further back—scanning my memory of the birthday’s earlier hours for any clue.

Had anything happened while Rachel and I finished the painted door? Or later, when I rode to Frizinghall? Or when I returned with Godfrey Ablewhite and his sisters? Or when I gave the Moonstone to Rachel? Or when guests arrived and we gathered around the dinner table?

My memory answered each question easily—until the last. Recalling the birthday dinner, I found myself stalled at the very start. I couldn’t even remember how many guests sat with me.

To realize I was utterly lost—and to conclude that the dinner itself deserved close scrutiny—was part of the same mental shift. I suspect others in similar situations would reason the same. When we become subjects of our own inquiry, we instinctively distrust what we don’t know. Once I had the names of those present, I decided to test my memory by asking others to recall the evening—and compare their accounts with what followed.

This latest experiment—what Betteredge might call the French, rational side of me taking over—deserves note. Unlikely as it seems, I had finally reached the core of the mystery. All I needed was a hint. And within a day, one came—from one of the dinner guests.

To begin this new investigation, I needed the full guest list. That I could get from Gabriel Betteredge. I decided to return to Yorkshire that day and start the inquiry the next morning.

It was too late for the noon train. I had to wait nearly three hours. Was there anything useful to do in London?

My mind returned stubbornly to the dinner.

Though I forgot numbers and names, I recalled that most guests came from Frizinghall or nearby. But not all. Some were not regular residents. I was one. Mr. Murthwaite was another. Godfrey Ablewhite a third. Mr. Bruff—no, he’d been unable to attend. Were any ladies from London present? Only Miss Clack, I could recall. Still, three guests were clearly worth seeing before leaving town. I drove straight to Mr. Bruff’s office, unsure of their addresses, hoping he could help.

Mr. Bruff was too busy to spare more than a minute. In that time, he dismissed all my questions with discouragement.

First, he called my new method too fanciful to discuss seriously. Second, Mr. Murthwaite was returning to his former haunts. Third, Miss Clack had suffered financial losses and moved to France. Fourth, Godfrey Ablewhite might or might not be in London—perhaps inquire at his club? And then, with a polite nod, he excused himself.

Now, only one possibility remained: Godfrey’s address. I followed his suggestion and went to his club.

In the hall, I met a member—an old friend of my cousin’s and an acquaintance of mine. He gave me Godfrey’s address and shared two recent developments in his life.

Godfrey, far from being disheartened by Rachel’s rejection, had quickly proposed to another wealthy young woman. Their engagement was expected to proceed—until it was abruptly broken off due to a dispute over settlements between Godfrey and the lady’s father.

As compensation, Godfrey had received a legacy of five thousand pounds from a rich, respected widow—a devoted supporter of Miss Clack’s charity, though she left her nothing but a mourning ring. With this windfall, Godfrey had declared he needed a break from his charitable duties. His doctor advised a continental trip for his health. If I wanted to see him, I should act quickly.

I went to meet him immediately.

But fate struck again. Just as I had missed Sergeant Cuff by a day, I now missed Godfrey by one. He had left London the previous morning by tidal train for Dover, bound for Ostend and likely Brussels. His return was uncertain—but he would be away at least three months.

I returned to my lodgings, spirits dampened. Three intelligent guests from the dinner—key figures—were beyond reach just when I needed them most. My hopes now rested on Betteredge and the remaining friends of the late Lady Verinder near Rachel’s home.

That day, I traveled directly to Frizinghall—the center of my new inquiry. I arrived too late to see Betteredge. The next morning, I sent a messenger with a letter, asking him to join me at the hotel as soon as possible.

Having sent the messenger in a fly, I expected to see the old man within two hours. In the meantime, I planned to begin my inquiry among the guests I knew and could reach—my relatives, the Ablewhites, and Mr. Candy. The doctor had expressed a desire to see me, and he lived next door. So I went to him first.

After Betteredge’s account, I expected to see signs of illness in Mr. Candy. But I was unprepared for the change. His eyes were dim, his hair completely grey, his face shriveled, his frame shrunken. I looked at the once lively, mischievous doctor—known for endless jokes and social blunders—and saw only a shadow. Only his taste for flashy clothes and jewelry remained unchanged, mocking the ruin of his body.

“I’ve often thought of you, Mr. Blake,” he said. “I’m glad to see you again. If there’s anything I can do, command my services—pray, command them!”

He spoke hastily, eagerly, his curiosity about my visit impossible to hide.

With my goal in mind, I’d anticipated needing to explain myself before engaging others. On the train, I’d rehearsed my story—and now I tested it on Mr. Candy.

“I was in Yorkshire recently, and I’m back now on a romantic mission,” I said. “It concerns the late Lady Verinder’s friends. You remember the disappearance of the Indian Diamond, nearly a year ago? Recent events suggest it may yet be recovered—and as a family member, I’m trying to find it. One obstacle is collecting all the evidence from that time—and more if possible. This case has peculiarities that make it vital to refresh my memory of everything that happened on Miss Verinder’s birthday. I’m appealing to those who were there—to lend me the aid of their memories——”

I stopped mid-sentence. Mr. Candy’s expression told me my attempt had failed.

He sat fidgeting with his fingers, his watery eyes fixed on me with a vacant, pleading look—painful to witness. I couldn’t tell what he was thinking. But one thing was clear: he hadn’t been listening since the first few words. The only way to regain his attention was to change the subject. I did.

“So much for why I’m in Frizinghall,” I said cheerfully. “Now, Mr. Candy—your turn. You sent me a message through Gabriel Betteredge——”

He stopped fidgeting. His face lit up.
