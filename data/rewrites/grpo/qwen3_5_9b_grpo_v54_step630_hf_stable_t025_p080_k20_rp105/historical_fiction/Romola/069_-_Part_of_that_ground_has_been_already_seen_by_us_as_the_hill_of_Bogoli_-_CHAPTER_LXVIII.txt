Romola drifted from the turbulence of her dreams into a profound, heavy slumber, only to rise again into a restless, busy dreaming until the final image solidified: she stretched her arms wide within the cold, flickering court of the Bargello, where the shadows of the tapers burned brighter and brighter until the darkness was utterly consumed by light. Her eyes snapped open to the pale, insistent gray of morning. Her boat lay motionless in a secluded cove; to her right stretched the unblemished, sapphire expanse of the Mediterranean, and to her left unfolded a landscape of recurring, rhythmic beauty, a scene etched into the shores of that loveliest sea.

Deep within a curve of the mountains, a swath of green land lay sheltered, draped in the soft shadows of trees leaning against rocky heights. Here and there, gleaming above the canopy, a path wound upward to a cluster of buildings that seemed to have scrambled hastily up the slope, securing a precarious perch solely to display its tall belfry as a beacon to the scattered village below. The sun, newly risen, cast oblique rays upon the western horn of this crescent-shaped bay; the rest of the valley remained bathed in dewy shadow. No sound disturbed the stillness; the waters themselves seemed to have curved inward to rest.

The golden rays touched Romola, thrilling her limbs with a gentle caress. She lay motionless, hardly watching the scene, but simply absorbing the presence of peace and beauty. In the early hours of youth, passive existence can sometimes become a form of Lethe, where the exquisite, indefinite sensation of the present creates a bliss devoid of memory or desire. As the warmth seeped into her young limbs and her eyes rested on this secluded luxuriance, the agitating past seemed to glide away like the dark specter of the Bargello, and the afternoon dreams of her girlhood returned. For a minute or two, the oblivion was untroubled; she did not even conceive that she could rest here forever, she merely felt that she rested.

Then, consciousness sharpened. She realized she was lying in a boat that had borne her over the waters all night. Instead of delivering her to death, it had served as the gently lulling cradle of a new life. Despite her evening despair, she was glad the morning had come, glad to rest in the familiar sunlight rather than in the unknown regions of death. *Could* she not rest here? No sound from Florence could reach her. Already, however, the oblivion was troubled; from behind the golden haze pierced the silhouettes of domes, towers, and walls, parted by a river and enclosed by green hills.

She rose from her reclining posture and sat up in the boat, willing herself to resist the rush of thoughts conjured by the conjecture of how far the boat had carried her. Why should she mind? This was a sheltered nook where simple villagers would not harm her. For a little while, at least, she might rest and resolve on nothing. Soon she would procure bread and milk, then nestle in the green quiet to feel a pause in her life. She turned to watch the crescent-shaped valley, seeking to reclaim the soothing sense of peace she had felt upon waking.

She had not been in this contemplative state more than a few minutes when a piercing cry cut across the stillness. It was not a brief cry, but continuous, growing more intense with every passing second. Romola felt certain it was the cry of a child in distress, unanswered. She started up, placing one foot on the side of the boat, ready to leap onto the beach, but she paused to listen. The mother must be near; the cry should soon cease. But it continued, drawing Romola irresistibly, seeming all the more pitiful for the preceding peace, until she jumped onto the sand and walked many paces before knowing which direction to take.

The cry appeared to emanate from a rough garden growth yards to her right, where she spotted a half-ruined hovel. Climbing a low, broken stone fence, she moved across patches of weedy green crops and ripe, neglected corn. The cry grew plainer, confirming her suspicion, yet even in this hurried walk, she felt an oppressive change in the air as she left the sea behind. Was there some taint lurking among the green luxuriance that had seemed such an inviting shelter? She could now see the opening to the hovel, and the cry darted through her like a physical pain. The next moment her foot was within the doorway, but the sight that met her in the sombre light arrested her with a shock of awe and horror.

On the straw-strewn floor lay three dead bodies: a tall man, a girl of about eight years old, and a young woman whose long black hair was being clutched and pulled by a living child—the child sending forth the piercing cry. Romola's experience in the haunts of death and disease made her thought and action prompt. She lifted the little living child, and while trying to soothe it against her bosom, she glanced back at the bodies to confirm they were truly dead. The strongly marked features and peculiar garb led her to conjecture they were Spanish or Portuguese Jews, perhaps put ashore and abandoned by rapacious sailors who claimed their property. Such things happened continually to Jews compelled to flee their homes by the Inquisition; the cruelty of greed thrust them from the sea, and the cruelty of superstition thrust them back to it.

"But surely," thought Romola, "I shall find some woman in the village whose mother's heart will not let her refuse to tend this helpless child—if the real mother is indeed dead."

This doubt lingered because while the man and the girl looked emaciated and showed signs of having been dead for some time, the woman seemed harder, retaining the robustness of her form. Kneeling, Romola was about to lay her hand on the heart, but as she lifted the piece of yellow woollen drapery across the bosom, she saw the purple spots marking the familiar pestilence. It struck her then that if the villagers knew of this, she might face greater difficulty in gaining their help; they might shrink from her with the child in her arms. But she had money to offer, and they would not refuse goat's milk in exchange.

She set out toward the village, her mind filled with the effort to soothe the little dark creature and wondering how she might win a woman's compassion. She could not help hoping, with a certain awe she had observed in herself, that her appearance as an unknown stranger in religious dress would inspire trust. As she crossed a breadth of cultivated ground, she noticed with wonder that patches of corn had been left to over-ripeness, untouched by the sickle, and that golden apples and dark figs lay rotting on the weedy earth. There were grassy spaces, but no cows, sheep, or goats. The stillness began to have something fearful in it to Romola; she hurried toward the thickest cluster of houses, where the most life would appeal on behalf of the helpless life she carried. She had picked up two figs, biting little pieces from the sweet pulp to still the child.

She entered between two lines of dwellings. It was time for villagers to be stirring, yet not a soul was in sight. The air became increasingly oppressive, laden with some horrible impurity. One door stood open, revealing grim emptiness. Another opened to show a man lying dead, fully clothed, his head resting athwart a spade handle, an earthenware cruse in his hand as if he had fallen suddenly.

Horror took possession of Romola. Was she in a village of the unburied dead? She wanted to listen for any faint sound, but the child cried out afresh when she ceased to feed it, filling her ears. Finally, she saw a figure crawling slowly out of a house, soon sinking back into a sitting posture against the wall. She hastened toward it; it was a young woman in fevered anguish, holding a pitcher. As Romola approached, the woman did not start; the one need was too absorbing for any other idea.

"Water! Get me water!" she moaned.

Romola stooped to take the pitcher and said gently in her ear, "You shall have water; can you point toward the well?"

The hand lifted toward the distant end of the little street, and Romola set off with all the speed she could manage while carrying the pitcher and feeding the child. The little one grew more content as the morsels of sweet pulp were repeated, ceasing to distress her with its cry, allowing Romola to pay less distracted attention to her surroundings.

The well lay twenty yards or more beyond the end of the street. As Romola approached it, her eyes were drawn to the opposite green slope below the church. High up, on a patch of grass between the trees, she had descried a cow and a couple of goats, and she tried to trace a line of path that would lead her close to that cheering sight once her errand was done. Occupied thus, she was unaware that she was very near the well, and that someone approaching it from the other side had fixed astonished eyes upon her.

Romola certainly presented a sight that could hardly be seen without causing pause and palpitation. With her gaze fixed intently on the distant slope, the long lines of her thick grey garment giving a gliding character to her rapid walk, her hair rolling backward and illuminated on the left side by the sun-rays, and the little olive baby on her right arm looking out with jet-black eyes, she might well startle the youth of fifteen accustomed to swinging the censer before a Madonna less fair and marvellous than this.

"She carries a pitcher in her hand—to fetch water for the sick. It is the Holy Mother, come to take care of the people who have the pestilence."

It was a sight of awe; he would perhaps be angry with those who fetched water for themselves alone. The youth flung down his vessel in terror, and Romola, aware now of someone near her, saw the black-and-white figure fly as if for dear life toward the slope she had just contemplated. But remembering the parched sufferer, she half-filled her pitcher quickly and hastened back.

Entering the house to look for a small cup, she saw salt meat and meal; there were no signs of want in the dwelling. With nimble movement, she seated the baby on the ground and lifted a cup of water to the sufferer, who drank eagerly and then closed her eyes, leaning her head backward as if giving herself up to the sense of relief. Presently she opened her eyes and, looking at Romola, said languidly, "Who are you?"

"I came over the sea," said Romola. "I only came this morning. Are all the people dead in these houses?"

"I think they are all ill now—all that are not dead. My father and my sister lie dead upstairs, and there is no one to bury them: and soon I shall die."

"Not so, I hope," said Romola. "I am come to take care of you. I am used to the pestilence; I am not afraid. But there must be some left who are not ill. I saw a youth running towards the mountain when I went to the well."

"I cannot tell. When the pestilence came, a great many people went away, and drove off the cows and goats. Give me more water!"

Suspecting that if she followed the direction of the youth's flight, she would find men and women still healthy and able, Romola determined to seek them out at once, hoping to win them to take care of the child so she could return to attend to others. She trusted her powers of persuasion to conquer the aid of the timorous once they knew what was to be done.

Promising the sick woman she would return, Romola lifted the dark bantling again and set off toward the slope. She felt no burden of choice now, no longing for death. She was thinking how she would go to the other sufferers as she had gone to that fevered woman.

But with the child on her arm, it was not so easy to walk up a slope, and it seemed a long while before the winding path took her near the cow and the goats. She was beginning to feel faint from heat, hunger, and thirst. As she reached a double turning, she paused to consider whether she would wait near the cow, which someone was likely to milk soon, rather than toil up to the church before resting. Raising her eyes to measure the steep distance, she saw peeping between the boughs, not more than five yards off, a broad round face watching her attentively, and lower down the black skirt of a priest's garment and a hand grasping a bucket. She stood mutely observing, and the face remained motionless. Romola had often witnessed the overpowering force of dread in cases of pestilence, and she was cautious.

Raising her voice in a tone of gentle pleading, she said, "I came over the sea. I am hungry, and so is the child. Will you not give us some milk?"

Romola had divined part of the truth, but she had not divined the preoccupation of the priest's mind which charged her words with strange significance. Only a little while ago, the young acolyte had brought word to the Padre that he had seen the Holy Mother with the Babe, fetching water for the sick: she was as tall as the cypresses, with a light about her head, looking up at the church. The parish priest had not listened with entire belief; he had been more than fifty years in the world without having any vision of the Madonna, and he thought the boy might have misinterpreted the unexpected appearance of a villager. But he had been made uneasy, and before venturing to come down and milk his cow, he had repeated many *Aves*. The priest's conscience tormented him; he trembled at the pestilence but also trembled at the thought of the mild-faced Mother, conscious that that Invisible Mercy might demand something more of him than prayers and "Hails." In this state of mind—unable to banish the image the boy had raised of the Mother with glory tending the sick—the priest had come down to milk his cow and had suddenly caught sight of Romola pausing at the parted way.
