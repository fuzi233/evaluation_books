More unsettling than the steady, ordinary drip of a water tap—so eerily in tune with the breath of the air—was the way it awakened in Oleron a heightened awareness of every whispering movement within the house. It had long been said that silence gains its deepest resonance when pierced by a single, faint sound; and here, silence was never complete. The spring air, soft and insistent, seemed to stir the old timbers as if they were alive. Perhaps the heat from Oleron’s fires made the structure groan and shift, or perhaps the countless insects hidden in the beams and floorboards were gnawing their way through the very bones of the building. Whatever the cause, all he needed was stillness—just a moment of quiet—and the world around him would dissolve into a symphony of unseen life.

He sat in his chair, listening. At first, only the drip. Then, beneath it, a thousand other sounds emerged: the slow sigh of a beam settling into its mortise, the tick of some unseen creature in the wall, the creak of a joint, the moan of a board under pressure. A windowpane shifted in its frame with a soft knock, though no wind stirred. And yet, no one moved. No breath was drawn. Yet the house *spoke*—not in words, but in language only the sensitive could perceive.

Oleron smiled, not with humor, but with a strange awe. He thought of how humans so neatly divide the living from the inert. But here, in this ancient dwelling, life wasn’t confined to blood and breath. It lived in the wood, in the stone, in the dust that settled like memory. The place breathed. It sighed. It remembered. And slowly, without warning, Oleron began to feel a bond forming—not just between himself and the house, but *through* it. As if his soul were mirrored in its walls. He toyed with the absurd notion that one day, another tenant might walk in and say, “This place is haunted.” And what would he say? That he, Paul Oleron, a man of letters, a harmless dreamer, had unknowingly planted the seed of a ghost?

But as his connection to the house deepened, Elsie Bengough grew more distant—more uneasy. Her dislike of the place became palpable, almost physical. She spoke plainly, her voice sharp with disapproval.

“It doesn’t belong to today,” she declared. “And for you especially, it’s dangerous. You’re already slipping away—losing touch with reality. You need concrete floors, gas meters, a real job. Something that forces you to interact with people. If I could get you work—just two or three days a week—would you take it?”

Oleron bristled slightly. He thanked her, but coldly. “Thank you, but no. We each have our own path.”

“Your own life to live?” she echoed, her tone laced with irony. “How long has it been since you’ve stepped outside?”

“About two hours.”

“No—since you last walked anywhere meaningful. Since you last stretched your legs?”

“I don’t know. Some time.”

“Since I was last here?”

“I haven’t been out much.”

“And has *Romilly* improved because of it?”

“I think so. I’m laying the foundations. I’ll begin writing soon.”

She frowned, turned away, then snapped back. “Ah! So you still cling to that ridiculous idea?”

“If you mean,” Oleron replied slowly, “that I’ve abandoned the old *Romilly* and am starting anew… yes. That’s exactly what I’m doing.”

Her eyes narrowed. “Where is the old manuscript?”

“Why?”

“I want to see it. I want to show you parts of it. I want to bring you back to your senses.”

He turned his back.

When he faced her again, his voice softened. “It’s no use, Elsie. I must follow my own instincts—even if you think them wrong. I’m thinking about it. The manuscript? I nearly burned it. But I didn’t. It’s in the window-seat—if you must see it.”

She rushed to the seat, lifted the lid. Then stopped.

A small cry escaped her. She pressed her hand to her mouth.

“You ought to knock those nails in, Paul,” she said, her voice trembling.

He lunged forward. “What? What happened?”

“I did knock them in—or rather, pulled them out.”

She held up her hand. From the wrist to the tip of her little finger, a fresh wound bled steadily, red and raw.

“Good God!” Oleron gasped.

He led her to the bathroom, turned on warm water, cleaned the cut gently, then held it under cold water. His hands shook. “I can’t understand—how could this happen? I checked the boxes myself. I opened them a dozen times. There shouldn’t have been anything there…”

She murmured, eyes closed, “One moment longer—I can bear it.”

Afterward, he bound her hand with a handkerchief. But his face remained clouded. He had spent half a day preparing the window-boxes—opening them, cleaning them, making them usable. He had seen no nail. Not once. And yet, there it had been—an inch and a half of rusted iron, protruding like a tooth from the wood.

“It shall come out now,” he muttered, fetching pincers.

He extracted it carefully.

Elsie sat in a chair, pale, but in her hand—still clutched tightly—was the manuscript of *Romilly*. She hadn’t let go of it.

Then she spoke again, her voice low but urgent. “Paul, if you don’t publish this, it will be the biggest mistake of your life.”

He hung his head. The incident with the nail replayed in his mind. *Romilly* was secondary now. But she persisted.

“What can I say?” he whispered. “Only that I hope, when you read the new version, you’ll understand why I must do this. And if you still don’t like it… well…” He gestured helplessly. “I must follow my own light.”

Silence.

“Come, Elsie,” he said softly. “We’ve worked well together. Don’t let this tear us apart.”

The moment the words left his lips, he regretted them.

She had been resting her injured hand, eyes shut. Now, her lips trembled. Her eyelids fluttered.

“I can’t help saying it,” she whispered. “You’ve changed. You’re not yourself anymore. I wish you’d never seen this house. It’s stopped your work. It’s turning you into someone I don’t recognize. And it’s making me afraid for you…”

“Oh, my hand—it’s beginning to throb,” she added, wincing.

“Poor child,” he murmured. “Let me take you to a doctor.”

“No,” she said. “I’ll be fine. Just keep it raised.”

She rested her elbow on the back of the chair. The bandaged hand touched his shoulder.

That touch sent a jolt through him—one unlike any he’d felt before. Hundreds of times, she had slipped her hand into his arm during walks, always casually, affectionately, like a sister. He had accepted it as such. But now—now it was different. Her eyes were closed. Her head tilted back. A fragile, sorrowful smile played on her lips.

And suddenly, it hit him.

She was in love with him.

Not just fondness. Not friendship. Love.

And he had never known.

And worse—he didn’t feel pity. Not guilt. Not tenderness.

Instead, something dark and unfamiliar surged within him: irritation. Resentment. A sharp, selfish impulse.

Why was she here? Why wasn’t she working? Why was she meddling in his life? Who gave her the right to judge him? To claim guardianship over him?

*Changed?* He looked at her, and realized: it wasn’t he who had changed. It was *her*.

By the time she opened her eyes, he had mastered himself.

“I wish you’d let me take you to a doctor,” he said, quietly.

She rose. “No, thank you, Paul. I’ll go now. If I need care, I’ll get it. Take my other hand, please. Goodbye.”

He didn’t stop her.

He walked her to the stairs. Halfway down the narrow alley, she paused.

“It would be a long way to come,” she said, “if you weren’t home. I’ll send you a postcard next time.”

At the gate, she turned once more.

“Leave here, Paul,” she said, her voice heavy with sorrow. “Everything’s wrong with this house.”

Then she was gone.

Oleron stood alone.

He returned to the room. Went straight to the window-box. Opened the lid.

Stared.

Then closed it.

He turned away, his breath shallow.

“That’s frightening,” he whispered. “It’s impossible. I should have seen that nail. I *did* see it. I removed it. I swear I did…”

But the box was empty. The nail was gone.

And the house, silent again, seemed to watch.
