"The sun's rim dips; the stars rush out; At one stride comes the dark." — Coleridge.

"Land! Land! Land!"

There it lay, distant to the south and west, framed against the dying light—a long, azure ribbon stretching between the crimson waves and the golden heavens. After months of uncertainty, the promise of solid ground was finally within reach. Ahead lay fresh waters, cooling fruits, and open space for limbs stiffened by scurvy and weakness. Perhaps, too, lay gold, jewels, and the untold riches of India. Who could say? Why not? The harsh reality of the Old World lay thousands of miles behind them, swallowed by history. Before them, and surrounding them, stretched a realm of wonder, fable, boundless hope, and infinite possibility.

Sick men dragged themselves from their stifling hammocks; strong men fell to their knees, offering thanks to God. Every eye and hand reached eagerly toward the far-off blue cloud, which seemed to fade as the sun sank, only to rise higher and broader as the ship surged forward under the embrace of the trade wind. That wind whispered affectionately against brows and canvas alike: *"I am the faithful friend of those who dare!"*

"Blow freshly, blow freshlier yet, O good trade wind!" Frank murmured to himself, his gaze strained upon that first glimpse of the New World, which held everything he possessed. His cheeks were gaunt and wasted, the feverish flush on each glowing crimson in the sunset's light. "Blow on, and save me from death, or her from a fate worse than death. Carry me to her feet, that I may call the lost lamb home, and die in peace!"

Minutes passed. The western rainbows—emeralds, topazes, amethysts, rubies—faded into a silvery gray. Above, through the deepening sapphire of the twilight, the Moon and Venus reigned supreme over the sea.

"That must be Barbados, your worship," said Drew, the master. "Unless my reckoning is wildly off, which Heaven knows it shouldn't be after such a voyage. God be praised."

"Barbados?" Amyas replied, unfamiliar with the name.

"Very likely, sir. Yeo and I were here with Captain Drake, and I returned afterward with poor Captain Barlow. I recall excellent harborage to the south and west. No Spanish ships, no cannibals, nor any other evil beasts," added Yeo. "It is a true Garden of the Lord, hidden in the seas as an inheritance for those who love Him. I heard Drake speak of planting it, should fortune ever smile upon him."

"I remember now," Amyas mused, "some conversation between him and poor Sir Humphrey regarding an island here. Oh, that he had gone there instead of Newfoundland!"

"Nay, then," said Yeo gently. "He is in bliss now with the Lord. Would you have withheld him from that?"

"He would have waited as willingly as he sailed, if serving his Queen allowed it. But what say you, my masters? Is there no better course than to spend a few days here, recovering our sick, before pressing on to the mainland and beginning our work?"

All agreed, save Frank, who remained silent.

"Come, fellow-adventurer," urged Cary. "We need your voice as well."

"To my impatience, Will," Frank whispered back, "there is but one place on earth, and I ache daily for wings to fly there. Yet, the counsel is sound. I approve."

The decision was unanimous, met with a hearty cheer from the crew. By early morning, they had skirted the southern coast and entered the bay where Bridgetown now stands. Every eye fixed itself on the low, wooded hills sleeping in the moonlight, dotted by fireflies like a million dancing stars. Every nostril drank greedily of the fragrant breeze sweeping from the land, heavy with the perfume of a thousand blooms. Every ear welcomed the shift from the monotonous lapping of water to the symphony of the tropics: the hum of insects, the snores of tree-toads, and the plaintive cries of shorebirds filling the night with vibrant life.

At last, the ship halted. The cable rattled through the hawsehole. Ignoring the risk of lurking Spaniards or Caribs, an instinctive cheer erupted from every throat. Poor souls! Amyas struggled to stop them from landing immediately in the darkness, reminding them that daylight was but two hours away.

"Never were two hours so long," complained a young lad, pacing anxiously.

"You've never faced the Inquisition," retorted Yeo dryly. "If you had, you'd know how slowly time crawls. Stand still and give thanks for where you are."

"Gunner, is there gold on that island?" someone asked hopefully.

"Never heard of any; and the less the better," Yeo replied flatly.

"But, Gunner," piped a scurvy-ridden cripple, licking his lips, "are there oranges and lemons?"

"Not that I've seen, but plenty of fine fruit down to the beach, thank the Lord. Here comes the dawn."

Up flushed the rose of sunrise; up rushed the sun. Its level rays glittered on the smooth trunks of palm trees, cast rainbows across the foam of coral reefs, and gilded distant uplands where, centuries hence, stately estates and bustling factories would rise. Lines of pelicans clanged out to sea; the insect hum subsided, replaced by a thousand birds bursting into jubilant song. A thin blue mist crept upward toward the inner hills and vanished, leaving them to shimmer in the burning glare. The land breeze, which had carried them out all night, died into a glassy calm, marking the start of the tropical day.

The sick were hoisted overboard, landing boatload by boatload onto the beach to rest in the palm shadows. Within half an hour, the entire crew was scattered across the shore, save for a dozen volunteers keeping watch aboard until noon.

Nature's first demand was immediate: *Fruit! Fruit!* The crippled crawled from place to place, greedily plucking the violet grapes of the creeping vines, staining their mouths and blistering their lips on the prickly pears despite Yeo's warnings. The healthier men began hacking down coconut trees, achieving little but blunting their hatchets. Finally, Yeo and Drew gathered half a dozen sensible men and ventured inland. An hour later, they returned laden with the treasures of a primordial orchard: sour juniper apples, luscious guavas, and crowned ananas—the queens of all fruits—found by the hundreds on the broiling ledges of low tufa cliffs.

Then, all sat on the sandy turf, defiant of galliwasps and jackspaniards, and armed against the insect hosts, sharing an equal banquet. Old blue land-crabs sat in their burrow doors, brandishing claws in defiance, while solemn cranes stood in the shallow water, tilting their heads as they pondered how long it had been since featherless bipeds broke the island's solitude.

Frank wandered silently, moved more by wonder than sorrow, while Great Amyas followed close behind, mouth full of juniper apples, acting the part of a seasoned showman with a patronizing air, as if he had already seen all such marvels and was above astonishment.

"New, new! Everything new!" Frank observed meditatively. "An awful feeling! All things changed around us, even to the tiniest fly and flower; yet we remain the same, forever unchanged."

Amyas, finding such musings entirely cryptic, responded simply: "Look, Frank. That's a colibri. Have you heard of them?"

Frank stared at the living jewel hovering loudly over a fantastical bloom, then darting away to join its mate. They whirred and danced around the flower-studded bushes, flashing fresh rainbows with every shift of light. Frank watched solemnly for a moment longer.

*"Qualis Natura formatrix, si talis formata? Oh my God, how fair must be Thy real world, if even Thy phantoms are so fair!"*

"Phantoms?" Amyas asked, uneasy. "That's no ghost, Frank, but a jolly little honey-sucker, with a wee wife and children no bigger than peas. Still, I warrant they are solid, greedy little fellows."

"Not phantoms in your sense, good fellow, but in the sense of those who know the worthlessness of all below."

"I'll tell you what, brother Frank," Amyas continued, "you are far wiser than I, I know. But I cannot abide seeing you turn your nose up at God's good earth. See now, God made all these things. Perhaps no man has set eyes on them until fifty years ago, yet they were as pretty then as they are now, ever since creation. Why do you think God placed them here, then, but to please Himself—and with the sight of them?" Amyas removed his hat. "Now, I say, brother Frank, what pleases God is good enough to please you and me."
