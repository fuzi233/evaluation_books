Gerard found himself nearly as eager for Burgundy as Denys, for the soldier never ceased singing its praises. At every minor inconvenience along their journey, Denys would scoff and declare, *"In Burgundy, they do things better!"*—and above all, he played upon Gerard’s fastidious nature by promising clean linens at the inns.

*"If I can but sleep in a naked bed once more,"* Gerard sighed, *"I ask no greater luxury. To think I’ve not lain in one since leaving home! Each time I see their foul sheets, I wish I could pluck out my eyes and nose rather than endure them."*

Denys’s patriotism ran so deep that he had marched twenty leagues in shoes split at the seams rather than buy from a German cobbler, whose incivility was matched only by his stubbornness. As evening fell, they stood at a crossroads—a small town lay one way, a monastery the other, though the latter lay off the road. Denys favored the inn; Gerard, the convent. After some debate, the soldier relented—on one condition: once they reached Burgundy, they would lodge only at inns. Gerard agreed readily, for his chart of monastic hospices ended here.

As they turned toward the monastery, Gerard could not help but ask why Denys, who had endured convents without complaint until now, suddenly recoiled from them. The soldier hesitated, then burst forth with pent-up grievances.

*"I held my tongue for your sake,"* Denys admitted. *"I saw they had you under their spell—feeding you their honeyed words like sacramental wine. But now, since this is the last, let me speak plainly. These stone dungeons have been my purgatory!"*

Gerard, taken aback, murmured an apology.

*"Ah, no matter,"* Denys waved him off. *"You’re not the first fool led astray by a monk’s robes. But mark me—these places are built to crush a man’s spirit. Every one the same: great gates, echoing cloisters, cold refectories where a man must choke down his words along with his supper. And that cursed bell! Tinkle, tinkle—summoning you to meals you’ve no stomach for, prayers you’ve no heart in, sleep that’s broken before you’ve sunk into it!"*

Gerard sniffed. *"The only bell you love is the jingle of a wench’s laughter."*

*"At least there’s music in that,"* Denys shot back, *"even when it scolds. But these monks? They fret over every grain of salt! ‘Use a knife, not your fingers!’ ‘Wipe your blade on bread, not the cloth!’ Small souls, pinched by their own rules. And if a man speaks freely—"*

*"If he blasphemes, you mean,"* Gerard interjected.

Denys scowled. *"At the last house, I tried to cheer the company—called out ‘Courage, all! The devil—’ And before I could finish, some sour-faced brother hissed, ‘Blaspheme not!’ I told him it was but a soldier’s rallying cry. And what does the fool say? ‘A habit is worse than a slip of the tongue.’ Then he warns me that shouting the devil’s name summons him!"*

Gerard stifled a laugh. *"And was he wrong?"*

Gerard opened his mouth to argue, but Denys barreled on, denouncing monks as hypocrites. *"They skulk about, eyes downcast—not from piety, but guilt! Strip off the cowl, and you’ll find a thief; clothe a rogue in black, and lo—a monk!"*

Gerard’s face darkened, but before he could retort, a bell tolled. Denys perked up at once. *"Ah! The one sweet chime—the call to dinner. My stomach roars ‘Amen!’"* And with that, he strode toward the convent, triumphant in his blasphemies, unaware of the silent disgust festering in his companion’s heart.
