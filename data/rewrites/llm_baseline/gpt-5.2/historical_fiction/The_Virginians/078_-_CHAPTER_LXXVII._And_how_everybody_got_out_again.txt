If Captain Miles Warrington had been minded to make a bulletin of every conquest, the mess-room would have been a post-office of perfumes and protestations. But Miles—who could dress a wig-curl as carefully as another man trusses a capon—only laughed, tilted his head before the glass, and said to his father with that easy insolence youth calls prudence, “Not such a fool, Squire!”

The old gentleman, who loved an argument as he loved a well-browned crust, took the jest and kneaded it, turning it from his son to the women with the same hearty hand that, at Oakhurst, had carved joints and distributed praise. Had Miss Theo—little Theo, the healthy one—run home and poured into her mother’s ear every syllable she had murmured in the shrubbery to some Joe Blake, junior? Had mademoiselle, with her ribbons and her French accent, translated aloud the whole of that scented billet slipped to her under Sir Thomas’s frank? The very mention of the letter brought color to her cheeks, as a warm kitchen brings bloom to apples.

“No, mademoiselle,” the Squire said, giving her his broadest bow, “we all have our secrets.”

And then—because husbands, like cooks, are commonly found out by their own hearth—Lady Warrington, with a little whisper that was half triumph and half tenderness, betrayed the mystery of that long-ago ride in a landau on the Hampstead Road: she had said “Yes.” That was all. As if one small word were not sometimes a whole lifetime of meaning; as if its taste were not different on every tongue, untranslatable as the scent of crushed mint when you were a child.

So, too swiftly as George Warrington thought, the carriage had galloped out and back, and in that short compass his own “Yes” had been met by another—quiet, steadfast, and spoken without flourish. Theodosia Lambert had not promised any open defiance, not the theatrical kind. She had only told him, in the simplest terms—terms that might have been spoken over a loaf put in the oven and taken out again—that she would marry no other creature breathing. Not if she lived to Methuselah. Not if a prince offered her his hand. She had given her heart away with her father’s consent—nay, at his bidding—and she could not take it back as if it were a ribbon mislaid.

And George, who had come to measure his life by the sound of her breath, believed her as he believed the beat of his own pulse. If her father relented in a day, in twenty years, or in some other world, she would keep her promise. It was as plain, and as terrible, as salt.

When the brief interview ended, Hetty—bright Hetty—saw her sister’s face composed and sweet, and she could scarcely contain herself for joy. The doctors’ bitter draughts and bark had done less, she said, than that one stolen hour. The girls went home after their act of disobedience, and George surrendered the seat he had held beside his invalid. Hetty sprang back into place, Charley climbed to his box, and later, with the solemnity of a little chap who could judge sermons by their length and their orthodoxy, declared he had heard the dullest discourse of his life. Dissenting preachers did not suit him; their cadence, perhaps, was wrong to his ear.

At the house, a domestic music met the returning party: clatter of plates in the lower regions, a maid’s shoes on the stair, the soft chiff of silk, and—more importantly—the altered note in Theo’s voice when she answered. Even her mother, who had been ice and ceremony these many weeks, embraced both daughters with an ardor that startled them—especially the sick one, whom she held as if her arms could press health back into her.

“There is nothing like a country ride,” Aunt Lambert declared, her whole person a rich syllabub of kindness—froth, sweetness, and a little sighing air. “Hampstead Road, was it? She must go again to-morrow. Bless us, my Lord Wrotham’s horses are at your orders three or four times a week. The child shall have the advantage of them!”

Of course Aunt Lambert never once spoke of the notion that Mr. Warrington might have crossed their path. Perhaps she never entertained it. Perhaps she entertained it as women sometimes entertain such things: silently, with a hand at the curtain, eyes turned away at the proper moment. There are ladies who will hang a rope-ladder for Strephon and Chloe and then go downstairs to see whether the custard is setting, as if love were no more than another household necessity—dangerous, delicious, best managed by those who know its heat. Aunt Lambert had loved truly in her time, and the memory of that old warmth made her tender toward the young. Miss Prim, it was whispered, would rather fling a stone at cooing turtles than permit any creature to be happy in its pairing. But Aunt Lambert only said to Theo, with a softness that did not invite confession, “The air has done you good. Another drive to-morrow. And another.”

When General Lambert came in for supper, his sister was ready with her refrain. “Don’t you think, Martin, the ride has done the child wonderful good? Must she not be made to go out?”

“Yes,” Lambert answered, smiling with that large, honest readiness which would have spent coin or blood to purchase his children’s comfort. “If a coach-and-six will do her good, she shall have it. And if there are no horses, I’ll drag the landau up Hampstead Hill myself.”

At table he watched Theo with a hungry father’s attention—the way a farmer watches a late-sown field for the first green. She picked at a bit of chicken with something like appetite; the sound of knife against plate, small as it was, seemed to everyone a bell of hope. He mixed her a little negus—wine warmed and sweetened, lemon cut to let its sharp oil rise, the steam carrying spice into the room. The very smell made the evening feel like Oakhurst again: home, hearth, the old domestic orchestra of voices, ladles, and laughter. For months—since that cruel day no one named—they had not had such an hour.

But Theo, though she had lied by omission for love, was too honest to keep feeding her father a false dish long. When he returned for the third or fourth time to her improved looks—asking whether it was the air, the Jesuit’s bark, the new medicine—she laid her hand on his, and her voice trembled, though her eyes were clear.

“Can’t you think, dear, what it is?”

“What is it, my child?” General Lambert asked, and the room seemed to hush itself, as if even the crackle of the coals had learned to listen.

“It is because I have seen him again, papa,” she said.

The words fell like a plate dropped on stone. Aunt Lambert and Mrs. Lambert turned pale; Theo’s own cheek whitened even as she held his gaze.

“It was not wrong to see him,” she went on quickly, as if speed could turn truth into mercy. “It would have been wrong not to tell you.”

“Great God!” the father groaned, snatching his hand back. Grief contorted his face so dreadfully that Hetty sprang to Theo, clasping her as Theo began to sway.

“Theo knew nothing of it, sir!” Hetty cried, head high, voice tumbling. “It was my doing—all my doing!”

Theo, half fainting, clung to her sister’s neck and covered it with kisses—twenty, fifty—like a child trying to warm cold hands with breath.

“Women, women!” General Lambert burst out, fierce and wounded. “Are you playing with my honour?”

“Martin! Martin!” Aunt Lambert sobbed, her handkerchief already in that damp, hopeless ruin of hers.

“Don’t say a word to her!” Hetty cried again; and she herself staggered back toward the wall as Theo, the sick one, fainted against her shoulder.

The next morning George Warrington sat before a breakfast that had no savor—though it might have been buttered to perfection, and the coffee as fragrant as Mocha can be in London—when his door opened and his faithful black servant announced, “General Lambert.”

One look at Lambert’s face told George the night’s confession had reached him.

“Your accomplices did not confess,” General Lambert began, when they were alone, “but sided with you against their father—a proof how desirable clandestine meetings are. It was from Theo herself I heard she had seen you.”

“Accomplices, sir?” George answered, catching at the word as a man catches at a crust when the true meal is too hot to touch. “You know how fondly and dutifully your young people regard you. If they side against you in this, it must be because justice is against you. A man like you is not going to set up sic volo sic jubeo as the only law in his family.”

“Psha, George!” Lambert cried, and behind the impatience was pain. “For though we are parted—God forbid I should desire we should cease to love each other—I had your promise you would not seek to see her.”

“Nor did I go to her, sir,” George said, flushing; for though he spoke the literal truth, he felt the lie of the spirit beneath it.

“You mean she was brought to you?” Lambert demanded, agitation rising. “Is it behind Hester’s petticoat you shelter yourself? What a defence for a gentleman!”

George’s pride and shame came up together. “I won’t screen myself behind the poor child. I attempted evasion; I am ill-accustomed to dissemble. I did not infringe the letter of my agreement, but I acted against the spirit. From this moment I annul it altogether.”

“You break your word given to me!” Lambert cried.

“I recall a hasty promise made in extreme excitement,” George answered, the words rushing now, like wine poured too fast. “No man can be bound for ever by words uttered at such a time; and no man of honour or humanity would try to bind him.”

“Dishonour to me!” the General exclaimed.

“Yes, if we are to bandy the phrase like a shuttlecock!” George flung back, too young to soften what he meant. “When that word is used there can be no question of love or age. Were you my own father—and I love you better than a father, Uncle Lambert—I would not bear it. What have I done? I have seen the woman whom I consider my wife before God and man; and if she calls me I will see her again. If she comes to me, here is my home for her, and half the little I have.

“’Tis you who have no right—having made me the gift—to resume it. Because my mother taunts you unjustly, are you to visit Mrs. Esmond’s wrong upon this tender innocent creature? You profess to love your daughter, and you cannot bear a wounded pride for her sake. Better she should perish away in misery than an old woman in Virginia should say Mr. Lambert schemed to marry one of his daughters.

“Say that to satisfy what you call honour and I call selfishness, we part, we break our hearts, we rally, we try to forget, we marry elsewhere? Can any man be to her what I have been? God forbid. Can any woman be to me what she is? You may marry her to the Prince of Wales to-morrow, and it is cowardice and treason. How can we—how can you—undo promises made before Heaven? You may part us, and she will die as surely as if she were Jephthah’s daughter. Have you vowed to Heaven to compass her murder? Kill her, if you think your promise so binds you; but this I swear: I am glad you have come, so I may formally recall that hasty pledge. Call me when she will, and I will come to her!”

It was the speech of youth—hot, absolute, certain that love is a law older than any household ordinance, certain that separation meant death. Later George would watch other passions rise, flourish, and decay, and might smile, bitterly, at his own ardours; yet he would never willingly surrender the superstition that, among all creatures, Heaven had meant these two particular hearts to beat together.

General Lambert’s reply came with an effort, as if each sentence were lifted from a weight on his chest.

“We must come, then, to what I would fain have spared myself: an unfriendly separation. When I meet you, Mr. Warrington, I must know you no more. I must order— and they will not do otherwise than obey—my family and children not to recognise you when they see you, since you will not recognise, in your intercourse with me, the respect due to my age, the courtesy of gentlemen.

“I had hoped—so far from your sense of honour, and the idea I had formed of you—that in my present grief and perplexity I should find you willing to soothe and help me as far as you might; for God knows I have need of everybody’s sympathy. But instead of help you fling obstacles. Instead of a friend—Heaven pardon me—I find in you an enemy! An enemy to the peace of my home and the honour of my children. And as such I shall treat you, and know how to deal with you when you molest me!”

He waved his hand as if cutting the air between them, set his hat on his head, and was gone.

George stood confounded, as if a familiar sound in the world—the very note by which he had tuned his days—had been struck mute. The happiness of yesterday seemed not merely ended, but poisoned. Since the first separation he had not felt so exquisitely unhappy as now, with quarrel added to parting, and himself suddenly not only alone but banished.

In a year of intimacy he had come to revere and love Lambert as he had loved no man except his dear Harry. To have that face turned away in anger was like losing the sun. Yet George felt, with the stubborn certainty of a young man who would rather be right than be comforted, that in withdrawing his promise he had acted rightly: his fidelity to Theo, and hers to him, stood first. Promises had passed between them from which no parent could absolve either; and all the priests in Christendom could only witness, not undo, the contract which had been sealed without altar or ring, but with that inward assent that makes a vow.

That day he fell, half by habit and half by aimless misery, into the library of the new Museum. There, by chance, he saw Jack Lambert; and because sorrow makes any friendly face into a harbour, George seized him—almost literally—drawing him out into the gardens where the city’s noise thinned to a distant grumble, and the sparrows, at least, kept their honest, unbought music.

Jack was a prig, to speak plainly; he loved a Latin tag as a cook loves a garnish, and sometimes the garnish was all. George did not much care for him in prosperous hours. But Jack had suffered too—in his affair with the little American—and being, as he would have said, haud ignarus mali, he was ready to pity another man’s wound.

George poured out everything: the meeting with Theo, the morning’s rupture, his resolve never to part with her. Jack’s eyes brightened with the pleasure of having an argument to arrange; his tongue produced Greek and Latin as if they were spices in a pantry. By degrees it appeared—through quotations and solemn nods—that Jack leaned to George’s side. At once George thought him a man of profound sense, clung to his elbow, and bestowed a warmth of affection upon him which Jack did not often attract.

They walked up to the Lambert lodgings in Dean Street. George watched Jack go in at the dear door and remained outside, staring at the windows with a sick desire to know whether Theo lay within, what sounds moved there—whether there was weeping, or a low voice reading, or only the hush of a house holding its breath.

He took himself to a coffee-house—Jack’s Coffee-House, by name—and called for a bottle. Port came, dark as old mahogany, smelling of raisins and warmth, and he drank as if he could drown his thoughts in it. He called another bottle. The world outside had its own harsh percussion—carts, hawkers, iron on stone—and the watchman’s cry at intervals; compared to that, the clink of glass and murmur of the room seemed almost a lullaby.

Jack did not return fast enough for a desperate man. When at last he appeared, his face was a little scared. He came to George’s box, poured himself two or three bumpers from the second bottle, and began.

Theo, it seemed, had kept her room, shaken by the previous day. Jack had come home to find dinner on the table, and afterward the General, eager for speech now that Theodosia was absent, held forth on the morning’s scene at George’s lodgings. He bade Hester be silent—poor Hester, as dumb as a mouse. He told Aunt Lambert—already dissolving handkerchiefs with tears—that women were all against him, and, in a fury, called them pimps for one another. Then he wheeled round upon Jack and demanded his view.

To Lambert’s surprise, and to his wife’s and Hetty’s delight, Jack spoke on George’s side. He ruled—quoting authorities, which he could not now precisely name between sips of port—that the matter had passed out of the parents’ hands. Consent once given had put the elders out of court. Though Jack did not hold with a host of divines those sacramental views of marriage, he held the bond yet more sacredly: marriages made before the civil magistrate without priest were, before Heaven, binding and indissoluble.

“It is not merely, sir,” Jack said to his father—here he removed his hat as if repeating a sermon, “those whom I, John Lambert, priest, have joined, let no man put asunder. It is those whom God has joined let no man separate. These young people were joined, or permitted to plight themselves to each other, by your consent—you, the priest of your own family. My views are clear, and I will lay them down at length in two or three discourses which, no doubt, will satisfy you.”

Upon which, Jack reported, his father said, “I am satisfied already, my dear boy.” And Hetty, with her arch little triumph, whispered, “Jack, mother and I will make you a dozen shirts, as sure as eggs is eggs.”

While they were thus talking, Theodosia came in—very pale, much agitated—kissed her father, and sat down at his side. She took a sippet of toast—plain, domestic, the simplest food in the world, yet sometimes the most eloquent—and dipped it in her father’s negus. Jack, between his pompous phrases, could not help noticing the detail; for a household tells its truth in such small acts: whether a sick girl can swallow, whether the warmth of wine and spice can coax life into her.

“You should have been here to hear Jack’s sermon!” Hester said.

“Has he?” Theo asked faintly, too weak to care for eloquence or citations.

“He has talked three quarters of an hour by Shrewsbury clock,” the General said—though Jack protested his own watch gave him less—“and his discourse has been you, my dear,” Lambert added, playing with Theo’s hand, “you and—and Mr. Warrington—and—George, my love.”

At that, Theo moved closer, laid her head upon her father, and wept on his shoulder. Jack, determined to be classical even in tenderness, remarked that it differed from a passage he remembered in Pausanias. Lambert asked, in honest simplicity, who Pausanias might be; and Jack, smiling at his father’s ignorance, told of Ulysses taking Penelope from her father, and the father pursuing, begging his daughter to return. Ulysses offered Penelope her choice; she covered her face with her veil—sign enough.

“For want of a veil,” Jack said, “my sister has taken refuge in your waistcoat, sir.”

They laughed, even in their ache; though Mrs. Lambert declared that had such a choice been offered her, and had Penelope been a girl of spirit, she would have gone home with her father at once.

“But I am not a girl of any spirit, dear mother,” Theo murmured, still in her father’s bosom.

After more talk, they insisted Theodosia return to bed. Jack, remembering George, slipped away to bring him news. “Passion,” Jack added, with the air of diagnosing an ailment, “is full of solliciti timoris; it racks the spirits, and, if indulged—especially by women who have little philosophy—must be injurious to health. My service to you, brother.”

From grief to hope the change was so swift it made George dizzy. Happiness flooded him, warming him like spiced wine after cold rain. “Landlord—more port!” he cried; and had Jack drunk a whole binful, George would have paid for it gladly. Jack’s sympathy was generous that day; and George, in the lavish gratitude of a man saved from drowning, swore their brotherhood would be eternal, and that if he could give Jack a bishopric—by George—he should have it.

How the night ended George could not rightly say. Jack later claimed the watchman interrupted their rhapsodies by chanting verses beneath the loved one’s window. George remembered only waking the next morning with a racking headache and yet a heart singing, as if the very birds outside had changed their tune.

He did not yet know the full extent of his relief: that the conversion of his noble enemy—the angry man of the previous morning—was already quietly accomplished. It must have galled an elder pride to yield to remonstrances spoken so little dutifully as George’s. But Lambert, the true Christian gentleman, had left that quarrel wounded and mortified, and gone about his business with a heavy thoughtfulness. In the afternoon, as was sometimes his custom, he stepped into a church open for prayers.

There, on his knees—submitting his trouble to the quarter where he came, privately, for guidance and comfort—it seemed to him that his child was right in her steadfast fidelity, and that he was wrong to demand absolute submission. So Jack’s cause had been won almost before it was pleaded. The brave, gentle heart that could not hold rancour, that bled at giving pain, that shrank even from asserting authority when authority would crush those it loved, was only too glad to return to its natural pulse of affection.
