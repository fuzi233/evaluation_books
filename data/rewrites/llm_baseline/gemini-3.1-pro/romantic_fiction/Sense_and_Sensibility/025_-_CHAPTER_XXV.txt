Though Mrs. Jennings spent much of the turning year migrating between the hearths of her children, the cyclical pull of the seasons always drew her back to her own ancestral kitchen. Her late husband had traded with prosperous success in a heavily industrialized district of London—a place where the deafening clatter of commerce and the jarring roar of modern machinery drowned out the subtle, rhythmic sounds of human connection. Yet, she maintained a settled habitation near Portman Square, a space where the winter months were marked by a return to the domestic rituals of her youth. Towards this home she began, on the approach of January, to turn her thoughts, and over a steaming pottage of braised heritage venison and winter tubers sourced from the local agrarian elders, she abruptly invited the elder Misses Dashwood to accompany her.

Elinor, acting as the meticulous historian of her family’s domestic and emotional rhythms, noted the sudden, shallow breath Marianne drew—a sharp disruption in her sister's acoustic landscape that spoke of anything but indifference. Elinor immediately offered a polite but absolute refusal for them both, buffering her rejection in the polished, dominant lexicon of propriety. Her stated reason was their determined resolution to remain tethered to their mother’s hearth during the deep winter freeze. 

Mrs. Jennings waved the refusal away as easily as one might discard a spent marrow bone. “Oh, Lord! I am sure your mother can spare you very well, and I do beg you will favour me with your company. Don’t fancy you will be an inconvenience, for I shan’t put myself out of my way. It will only be sending Betty by the heavy coach, and we three shall ride very well in my chaise. If you do not like to follow my social rounds, you may always go with one of my daughters. I have had such good luck in getting my own children off my hands that your mother will think me a fit person to have the charge of you. If I don’t get one of you well married before I have done, it shall not be my fault. I speak the language of all the young men, you may depend upon it.”

“I have a notion,” booming Sir John interjected, his loud, percussive voice fracturing the delicate auditory harmony of the dining room, “that Miss Marianne would not object to such a scheme, if her elder sister would come into it. It is very hard she should not have a little pleasure. I advise you two to set off for town without saying a word to Miss Dashwood about it.”

“Nay,” cried Mrs. Jennings, “I am sure I shall be monstrous glad of Miss Marianne’s company alone, only the more the merrier. If they got tired of my old-fashioned ways, they might talk their own native dialect to one another behind my back. But one or the other I must have. How do you think I can live poking by myself in that quiet house? Come, Miss Marianne, let us strike hands upon the bargain.”

“I thank you, ma’am,” Marianne breathed, her voice carrying the untranslatable resonance of an ancient, desperate prayer. “Your invitation has insured my gratitude for ever, and it would give me almost the greatest happiness I am capable of to accept it. But my mother—I feel the justice of what Elinor has urged, and if she were to be made less comfortable by our absence, nothing should tempt me to leave her. It must not be a struggle.”

Mrs. Jennings repeated her assurance that Mrs. Dashwood could spare them, and Elinor, who now fully decoded the auditory cues of her sister’s desperation, saw how thoroughly Marianne’s hunger for Willoughby had eclipsed her usual fastidiousness. Elinor made no further direct opposition, referring the decision to their mother. She knew, however, that she could scarcely expect support from Mrs. Dashwood to prevent a visit she dared not explain her own reasons for avoiding. That Marianne, who typically possessed a highly refined sensory palate and was invariably disgusted by the superficial auditory pollution of Mrs. Jennings' society, should now disregard everything most wounding to her sensitive acoustic ecology in pursuit of one object, was a profound proof of Willoughby's mythic importance to her.

Later, as Mrs. Dashwood stood at the heavy oak block, expertly mincing winter sage and thyme with the rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of her knife—a sound that traditionally anchored the household's psychological well-being—she refused to hear of them declining the offer. She was delighted by the prospect, immediately foreseeing a harvest of advantages from this generational separation.

“I am delighted with the plan,” Mrs. Dashwood cried, wiping her herb-stained hands on her linen apron. “Margaret and I shall be as much benefited by it as yourselves. When you and the Middletons are gone, we shall return to a quieter acoustic ecology. We shall go on so happily together with our books and our music, without the jarring frequencies of so much company! It is right that you should go; I would have every young woman acquainted with the complex social lexicons and amusements of London. And in all probability you will see your brother, and whatever his faults, when I consider whose son he is, I cannot bear to have you so wholly estranged from his table.”

“Though you have been obviating every impediment with your usual anxiety for our sustenance,” said Elinor, “there is still one objection.”

Marianne’s countenance sank, the light draining from her face like the last embers of a dying fire. 

“And what,” asked Mrs. Dashwood, “is my dear prudent Elinor going to suggest? Do not let me hear a word about the expense of provisioning the journey.”

“My objection is this: Mrs. Jennings is not a woman whose society offers the rich, sensory nourishment we are accustomed to, nor does her protection grant us consequence. We will be immersed in a world of superficial commercialization.”

“That is true,” replied her mother, “but of her society separately from others, you will have very little, and you will almost always appear in public with Lady Middleton.”

“If Elinor is frightened away by her distaste for Mrs. Jennings,” Marianne said, “at least it need not prevent *my* accepting. I have no such scruples, and could put up with every unpleasantness of that kind with very little effort.”

Elinor could not help smiling at this sudden indifference toward a woman whose brassy dialect and lack of culinary and cultural refinement Marianne usually found unbearable. Elinor resolved within herself that if her sister persisted, she would go likewise. She could not leave Marianne’s vulnerable emotional landscape exposed to the abrasive, dominant lexicon of London without guidance, nor abandon Mrs. Jennings to Marianne's unpredictable mercies. To this determination she was further reconciled by the memory of Lucy Steele's account: Edward Ferrars was not expected in town before February, allowing their visit a comfortable, untroubled season.

“I will have you *both* go,” said Mrs. Dashwood. “These objections are nonsensical. If Elinor would ever condescend to anticipate enjoyment, she would perhaps expect some from improving her acquaintance with her sister-in-law’s family.”

Elinor had long sought an opportunity to weaken her mother’s dependence on the mythic inevitability of her union with Edward, so that the shock of his true engagement might be lessened. Utilizing a subtle, guarded phrasing she knew was nearly untranslatable to her romantic mother, Elinor said as calmly as she could, “I like Edward Ferrars very much, and shall always be glad to break bread with him; but as to the rest of his family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever known to them or not.”

Mrs. Dashwood smiled and said nothing. Marianne lifted her eyes in astonishment, and Elinor realized the inherent untranslatability of her own subjective experience; she might as well have held her tongue. 

It was finally settled that the invitation should be fully accepted. Mrs. Jennings received the news with immense joy. Sir John was delighted, for to a man whose prevailing anxiety was the dread of the acoustic void of being alone, the acquisition of two inhabitants in London was something. Even Lady Middleton took the trouble of being pleased, and the Miss Steeles, especially Lucy, had never been so thoroughly satiated by any intelligence in their lives.

Elinor submitted to the arrangement with less reluctance than she expected. Seeing her mother so pleased, and her sister restored to her usual vibrant animation—her voice regaining its musical timbre—Elinor could hardly distrust the consequence. Marianne’s joy was a perturbation of spirits, a starving woman finally promised a feast. Her only restorative to calmness was her unwillingness to quit her mother’s hearth, and at the moment of parting, her grief was a mythic severing of the maternal vine. Her mother’s affliction was hardly less. Elinor alone seemed to understand that the separation was part of a seasonal cycle, short of eternal. 

Their departure took place in the first week of January, the carriage loaded with carefully preserved provisions for the road. The Middletons were to follow in about a week, while the Miss Steeles kept their station at the park, waiting to quit it only with the rest of the family, as the wheels of the Dashwood chaise crunched against the winter frost, carrying the sisters away from their agrarian quiet and toward the roaring, industrial pulse of the city.
