Without quite knowing how he came to be there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose pine board he’d jammed temporarily across the step Miss Bengough had cracked when she fell. His scalp prickled with the house’s sharp, unwholesome draft, and he realized he was hatless, bare headed, descending the stairs without any clear memory of deciding to stand up from his desk. Only hours later did the hazy fragments drift back: he’d left his candle sputtering on the table, its wick melted long and drooling wax in thick, rivulet-like trails; he’d edged the front door open no wider than his own shoulders, as if terrified something cooped up inside might slip past him before he could escape; he’d sidled out, then pulled the door shut so softly the latch barely clicked.

At the foot of the stairs a shock tore a thin, high sob out of his throat. Something burst from the dark of the disused cellars in a flurry of fur and a shriek, its eyes glowing molten green for half a second before it darted out the open gate. It was only a cat, but his hands were shaking so hard he could barely curl his fingers around the cold iron of the gatepost. He stood for a minute under the crookedly nailed “To Let” sign, plucking at his lower lip until he drew blood, staring up at the smudge of candlelight behind one of his red blinds. For a heartbeat he swore he saw a long, loose shadow drift across the glass, slow as honey, as if someone standing inside was brushing their hair over their shoulder. He did not wait to confirm it. Stumbling, he moved up the square, craning to glance back over his shoulder every few steps.

A tiny, grubby public house huddled around the corner, a spot he’d walked past a hundred times and never once entered. He burst through the door now, fumbling a shilling out of his coat pocket that clattered and missed the sawdust-dusted counter by three full inches. “B-brandy,” he stammered, teeth chattering so hard the word split apart, and he ducked to grope for the coin under a sticky stool. The little bar compartment he’d stumbled into was empty save for him; the regulars—carters, labourers, the neighbourhood tradesmen who knew everyone’s business before it happened—huddled in the far end of the space, behind the white-haired landlady who moved slow between taps and dust-furred bottles.

Oleron sank onto a perforated hardwood settee that dug sharp little holes into the back of his thighs, drank half the brandy in one burning swallow, then drained the rest before he could think to spill it. He sat there, staring at the blurred shapes of the men across the bar, and wondered idly which of them would volunteer to haul his corpse out of that cursed house come morning. He waved for a second brandy. There was no way he was going back to that room where the candle still guttered. Not back to the broken step, not back to that pith-white, breathless, watchful space that felt like it had been waiting for him to leave. He’d beg his old landlady to put him up for the night, he decided, he’d move his work and his sleeping quarters back to the familiar flat first thing tomorrow, never step foot in this house again—

His glass was empty. He ordered a third. The grievances bubbled up hot and sharp in his chest: nails that drove themselves back into solid oak just to gash his palm, stair steps that rotted themselves hollow overnight to trip people, invisible presences that drifted through his rooms in the dark brushing their hair until the air crackled with static like trapped summer lightning. He’d paid good money for this place, for his work on *Romilly*, not for some nameless thing to make itself at home uninvited. That lawyer in Lincoln’s Inn would get a piece of his mind before the sun was high the next day. It was outrageous, trapping a man in a tenancy with a ghost.

A cut-glass partition separated his little nook from the landlady’s station, but it stopped eight inches above the counter top. He glanced up, and saw three shadowy faces pressed to that gap, staring at him. They vanished the second his eyes met theirs, as if they’d never been there at all. He shifted to a corner out of their line of sight, and found himself face to face with the landlady, who’d been pretending to wipe down the same tap for ten minutes. She made a small, meaningless comment about the lingering winter cold, the influenza that had laid half the neighbourhood low, that spring was bound to break soon. The mundanity of it steadied him a little, and he crushed down a stupid, creeping thought: what if the landlady brushed her hair every night in front of her own mirror, and what if it made that same sharp, fizzing crackle?

The fourth glass of brandy hit, and suddenly he was furious at himself for running. What was he, a skittish child? He was a grown writer, halfway through a major novel, not to be chased out of his own home by tricks of the dark. He laughed, loud enough that the patrons at the far end went quiet for a beat. He’d just been overworking, that was all. He’d let his head fill with moonshine about rewriting *Romilly*, even stupidly toyed with burning fifteen whole finished chapters. An empty head gives the devil leave to play, that was the whole of it. He drained the last sip of brandy and stumbled out the door to walk the fumes off.

The fresh cold air made his head swim at first, then cleared, and the false bravado seeped right out of him, leaving a cold, heavy dread in its place. He wasn’t scared for himself anymore. He was scared for Elsie. She’d said it, hadn’t she? I’m not wanted here. She’d seen the rot in the house long before he had, and now he knew it was true: whatever was living in those walls meant her harm. He could not, under any circumstance, let her step foot on that property again. That was non-negotiable. But he, he was not going to run. He would not let a shadow chase him out of his own home, not if he could help it. He turned on his heel and headed back toward the square.

By the time he reached the short street leading to the square, the pub was shuttered for the night, the loitering patrons clustered on the kerb. Their conversation cut dead the second he passed. Long-nosed old Barrett, the handyman who’d been bringing him breakfasts and cleaning his rooms, glared at him under the streetlamp, and pointedly did not return his goodnight. Oleron slipped through the broken gate, hesitated only a heartbeat in the dark alley between the houses, then climbed his stairs. The candle in his Sheffield stick was no more than an inch long, guttering fitfully. He did not light a new one. Deliberately, he forced himself to walk through all five rooms, slow, peering into every dark corner, before he headed for his bedroom.

As he crossed the hall, he spotted a letter on the floor, slid under the front door by hand, no stamp, clumsy scrawl that ran from edge to edge without a single comma or period. It was from Barrett. The man wrote that he would no longer prepare Oleron’s breakfasts, no longer step foot in his house, and Oleron would have to make other arrangements entirely. The sting was in the postscript: a single, pointed line of Scripture, its implication about Elsie and Oleron so lewd and unearned his face burned hot enough to singe. The whole nosing, foul-minded neighbourhood had been whispering filth about them, all because Elsie had tripped on a rotten stair. Oleron crumpled the letter up, held it to the tiny guttering candle flame until it burned to black ash, then ground the flecks under his heel. The rage burned off the last of his frayed unease.

He was undressing, pulling his shirt over his head, when he glanced down at his bed. The coverlet he’d smoothed perfectly before he left that night bore a clear, deep indentation, as if someone heavy had been lying on it for a full hour, right where his chest would rest. He could not remember lying down on the bed at any point that day. But the fumes of the brandy, and his white-hot anger over Elsie’s name, crowded the oddity right out of his head. He blew out the last of the candle, fell back onto the mattress, and sank into a deep, black, dreamless sleep that lasted nearly a full day—so deep he never heard the Barretts knock at his door the following morning.
