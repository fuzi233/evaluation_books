Late that evening, my lodgings were unexpectedly graced by a visit from Mr. Bruff.

The sharp, unshakable confidence that usually defined the lawyer was entirely gone. For the first time in all the years I had known him, he shook my hand in heavy, exhausted silence.

“Have you come from Hampstead?” I asked, scrambling to break the weight of the unspoken tension hanging between us.

“I left there not half an hour ago,” he answered. “I know, Mr. Franklin, that you have finally uncovered the full truth of what Rachel saw that night. But I will say plainly: if I could have foreseen the cost of that knowledge, I would have left both of you in the dark forever.”

“You have spoken to Rachel, then?”

“I escorted her all the way back to her home in Portland Place; there was no question of letting her travel alone after what you two went through. I don’t hold you to blame for the shock of that unfortunate meeting—you saw her in my house, under my permission, after all. But I will not risk a repeat of that harm. She is young, stubborn, and strong-willed; with rest and time, she will heal. All I ask is your word that you will make no attempt to see her again, not until I give you explicit permission to do so. Can I depend on that?”

“After all the pain she has borne, and all I have suffered, you have my promise,” I said.

He visibly relaxed at that, set his hat down on the table beside him, and pulled his chair closer to mine.

“Good, that is settled. Now for the future—your future. The extraordinary turn this case has taken leaves us with two unassailable facts. First: Rachel told you the full, unvarnished truth of what she saw, plain as words can make it. Second: even though we both know some unthinkable, hidden mistake must be at the root of it, you cannot blame her for believing you guilty. She saw you take the Diamond with her own two eyes, and every circumstantial detail that followed seemed to confirm it beyond any possible doubt.”

“I do not blame her,” I cut in. “I only regret that she could not bring herself to speak plainly to me of it at the time.”

“You might as well wish Rachel was a completely different person,” Bruff laughed shortly. “No girl of her delicacy, who was already set on marrying you, could ever bring herself to call you a thief to your face. That is not her nature, and I know for a fact she has acted from that exact same instinct before, in a different situation that left her feeling just as trapped. Besides, she told me on the drive back to town tonight: even if she had spoken up then, she would no more have believed your denial than she believes it now. There is no answer to that, is there? Do not waste your time, do not waste your energy, trying to unpick every tangled thread of that night last year at Lady Verinder’s country house. We will get nowhere digging in the past we cannot fully unpack. Look to the future, instead, to what we *can* find.”

“Surely you see,” I objected, “that for me, the entire mystery is rooted firmly in the past?”

“Answer me this,” Bruff pressed, “is the Moonstone the source of all this harm, or is it not?”

“Of course it is.”

“Very well. We both know the Diamond was taken to London and pledged to Mr. Luker. We know you were not the man who pawned it—but do we know who was?”

“No.”

“We know the Moonstone is sitting in Luker’s bankers’ vault at this very moment. It is June now. By the end of this month, almost exactly a full year will have passed since that pledge was made. The man who pawned it will almost certainly come forward to redeem it the second that year is up. By the terms of Luker’s own agreement, he will have to pull the Diamond out of the bank himself to hand it over. All we have to do is post a watch outside the bank as the month draws to a close, and we will see exactly who he is. Do you see it now?”

I admitted, a little reluctantly, that the plan was at least original, if nothing else.

“It is as much Mr. Murthwaite’s idea as mine,” Bruff said. “He mentioned it to me in a conversation some weeks back. If he is right, the three Indian jugglers will be staking out that same bank at the end of the month too, and there may be serious trouble come of it. But whatever happens, that unknown man who pawned the Diamond is the one who framed you. He is the only person alive who can clear your name in Rachel’s eyes.”

“I will not deny your plan is daring, ingenious, and new,” I said. “But I have one objection. It demands that I wait.”

“All of a fortnight, more or less,” Bruff said. “Is that such an unbearable stretch of time?”

“To me, in this state, it feels like a lifetime. I cannot endure sitting idle, not when my name is tainted and I have no way to prove myself innocent. I have already thought of what I can do: I am going to consult Sergeant Cuff.”

“Cuff retired from the force years ago. You cannot expect him to take on the case now.”

“I know exactly where to find him. I can at least try.”

“Try, then,” Bruff said after a moment’s thought. “This case has grown so far beyond anything he could have imagined back when he was first investigating it, you might well reawaken his old professional interest. Come back to me with what he says. In the meanwhile, if you have not found any answers by the end of the month, you will not object to me posting that watch at the bank?”

“Of course not,” I said. “Unless I find the full truth long before that, and make the whole scheme unnecessary.”

Bruff smiled, picked up his hat to leave. “Tell Cuff that I say the whole truth of this case hinges entirely on finding the man who pawned the Moonstone. I want to hear what his old detective experience makes of that.”

We parted shortly after.

The next morning at dawn, I set out for the small Surrey town of Dorking, where Betteredge had told me Sergeant Cuff had settled into retirement.

After asking directions at the local inn, I found his cottage tucked down a quiet side lane, surrounded by a large walled garden, fronted by a tall hawthorn hedge. The gate, topped with neatly painted trellis, was locked. When I peered through the slats after ringing the bell, roses were everywhere: blooming in borders, climbing the cottage doorframe, peeking through every window. Far from the city’s dark alleys and unsolved crimes, the legendary thief-taker was living out his days in peaceful, rose-scented seclusion.

An elderly housekeeper opened the gate, and immediately dashed every last hope I had of securing Cuff’s help. He had left the day before, travelling to Ireland.

“On detective business?” I asked, bewildered.

The woman smiled. “Oh, he has no other business these days but roses, sir. A head gardener for a great Irish house found a new, secret trick for growing prize roses, and Mr. Cuff went all the way over to learn it.”

“Do you know when he will be back?”

“There is no telling, sir. He said he would come straight home if the new trick was worthless, or stay as long as it took to master it if it was worth his time. If you leave a message, I will make sure he gets it.”

I scribbled a note on my card, telling him I had a matter concerning the Moonstone to discuss, and left it with her. There was nothing for it but to turn back to London, empty-handed.

My frayed temper only grew worse after that wasted journey. I could not sit still, could not rest, until I had taken some new step toward cracking the mystery. That night, I sat up for hours smoking, spinning wild, improbable theories rooted in the abstract German philosophy I had studied in my youth, until I was so tangled in metaphysics I could barely convince myself the Moonstone was even a real, physical object.

Accident rescued me from that fog the next morning. I pulled on the same coat I had worn for my disastrous meeting with Rachel, and rummaging in its pockets for a handkerchief, I found a crumpled, forgotten letter from Betteredge. I sat down at my desk to read it again, and realized I owed the old man a reply. His note was trivial, mostly: Ezra Jennings, Mr. Candy’s strange piebald-haired assistant, had told Candy he had seen me, and the doctor wanted a word with me the next time I was anywhere near Frizinghall. I found myself idly sketching portraits of Jennings on the blank page of paper I meant to use for my reply, until the plain act of writing a simple, ordinary letter to Betteredge cleared all that foggy metaphysical nonsense right out of my head.

With my mind clear at last, I decided to approach the mystery practically. I could not recall a single solid detail of the birthday dinner held the night the Moonstone vanished. I could not even remember how many guests had been there. That gap in my memory, I realized, was the exact place the clue I had been hunting for was hidden. I would get the full list of everyone who had attended that dinner, write to every single one of them, and ask them to recount every tiny, trivial thing they could remember about that evening. The small, forgotten detail no one had thought worth mentioning before would be the key that unlocked everything.

I was determined to head back to Yorkshire that very day to see Betteredge and get that guest list, but I missed the midday train by minutes, leaving me with three hours to kill before the next one departed. I realized three of the birthday guests were in London: Mr. Murthwaite, Godfrey Ablewhite, and Miss Clack. I drove straight to Bruff’s office to ask for their addresses, but he was far too busy to do more than dash my hopes. Murthwaite had already sailed back to the Middle East for his next expedition. Miss Clack had lost most of her money and moved to France to live cheaply. Godfrey Ablewhite? Bruff said I could try looking for him at his club.

At the club, a friend of Godfrey’s gave me his address, and two pieces of news I had never heard before: Rachel had turned down Godfrey’s proposal, but he had very quickly secured another engagement to a wealthy heiress, only for that arrangement to collapse suddenly over a dispute about marriage settlements. Worse, he had just inherited five thousand pounds from a rich old charity patron, and had left for the Continent the day before, bound for Ostend and Brussels, planning to be gone for a full three months.

My luck was rotten. Every promising lead was out of reach. I boarded the train north for Frizinghall at last, and arrived late that evening. The next morning I sent a carriage to fetch Betteredge from the old Verinder house, and while I waited for him, I remembered Mr. Candy had asked to see me. The doctor lived only a street away, so I went to call on him first.

I knew from what Betteredge had told me that Candy had never fully recovered from the terrible fever he’d caught on the night of the birthday dinner. But I was not prepared for the wreck of a man who greeted me. His eyes were dim, his hair had turned completely white, his face was gaunt and shriveled, his frame shrunk to half its former size. Only his gaudy, brightly coloured clothes and cheap jewellery remained the same, a cruel mockery of the lively, jokey, prank-loving little doctor I had once known.

“Mr. Blake, I have thought of you often,” he said, fidgeting restlessly. “I am so glad to see you again. Whatever you need, whatever I can do to help, you have only to ask.”

He could not hide his fidgety, childishly curious eagerness to know why I had come. I launched into the explanation I had rehearsed on the train: that we hoped to recover the lost Moonstone, and I was collecting every memory of the birthday night from everyone who had been present at the dinner. The words barely registered. He picked at his fingertips the whole time I spoke, his watery eyes blank and unfocused, obviously not hearing a single word I said after the first few. I switched subjects immediately.

“So that is why I have come to Frizinghall,” I said, cheerfully. “Now it is your turn. You sent me a message by Gabriel Betteredge, some time back——”

He dropped his fingers from his lap, and suddenly, all at once, brightened right up.
