We lingered within the heavy, timbered walls of Castlewood until the turning of the new year, anchored by the cyclical, agrarian rhythms of the estate’s kitchens. For a fortnight—which felt like an eternity to my infatuated, youthful heart—we were immersed in the mythic, everyday rituals of hearth and harvest, where the deep, resonant thud of the butter churn and the yeasty breath of proving dough marked the passage of time far more truly than the ticking of any clock. Yet, inevitably, I returned to the side of my charmer, while Madame de Bernstein seemed relieved to leave her father’s ancestral home, the complex olfactory landscape of roasting game and drying herbs fading behind our carriage.

As the iron-rimmed wheels ground against the turnpike—an abrasive, mechanical intrusion upon the quiet acoustic ecology of the winter countryside—my aunt spoke freely. Whatever battle-royal had transpired between her and her niece remained a closely guarded secret, but the old lady no longer spoke of disciplining *cette petite*. Her nephew Eugene, she observed with a sharp, nervous laugh, was destined to be henpecked, but the household desperately required the orderly, sustaining structure of a proper steward. She had heard the whispers that the young Countess’s mother had beaten hemp as a convict, and that Mr. Van den Bosch had amassed his immense fortune through the brutally commercialized, utilitarian trades of enslaved labor, smuggled molasses, and bulk spices. Yet, to Madame de Bernstein, the sensory realities of such origins mattered little against the raw power of capital. 

“She will learn English presently,” my aunt declared, casually dismissing the girl's native Dutch. To her, a dialect was merely an impediment, an older cultural memory to be overwritten by the dominant lexicon of the British aristocracy. “The Countess of Castlewood can afford to be anybody’s daughter. A grocer or a smuggler—what does it matter?” She understood the subtle power dynamics embedded in language and title; once the girl adopted the English tongue and the Castlewood name, her past would be effectively untranslatable, assimilated into the sterile, high-society parlance. 

While Madame de Bernstein was willing to extend this linguistic and social amnesty to her nephew's new bride, she remained deaf to my pleas regarding Mr. Hagan and Lady Maria. Though I spoke of Hagan’s profound transformation—how he had traded the superficial commercialization of the stage for the deeply resonant, acoustic command of the pulpit—my aunt was obdurate. Maria’s name was banished from our conversational soundscape. (I will only note, for the generations who may read this chronicle, that Hagan’s subsequent life was exemplary. His lady later claimed a peculiar spiritual illumination, which I often suspected was less divine intervention and more the earthly consequence of a too-free indulgence in distilled spirits—a false sustenance. But they fed me when my soul and stomach were both empty, and I do not forget the warmth of their table when the Pharisees of the world turned away.)

It is a curious phenomenon how quickly one's personal identity is categorized and condemned by the dominant voices of the family. Following the success of my play, I had been invited to dine among the town-wits and macaronis. I partook of their richly sauced sweetbreads and truffled capons, holding my own in their layered, rapid-fire dialects of fashion. But I found their feasts to be spiritually hollow, viewing meals merely as utilitarian fuel for their endless, abstract philosophical musings. I preferred the quiet, grounding presence of a pair of kind eyes over the painted beauties of the Ranelagh ring. Because I loved your mother—because I chose the profound, deeply rooted sustenance of little Miss Lambert’s traditional agrarian family—my godly Aunt Warrington and my worldly Aunt Bernstein lamented my tastes.

“Why, with his name, he might marry anybody,” sighed meek Religion, keeping one eye on heaven and the other on the accounting ledger. 

My uncle’s voice possessed the grating, industrial dissonance of a man who evaluated the world solely by its monetary weight. “I admire genius,” he told me, his tone entirely devoid of the sensory appreciation it claimed. “But it is a pity you consort with those authors and poets. You might have had a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, yet you let her slip to make up to a country girl without a penny-piece.”

“But if I had promised her, uncle?” I asked.

“Promise, promise! These are matters of arrangement,” he barked. “Your duty to the Fifth Commandment warranted fulfilling your mother's intentions regarding Miss—what was the Countess’s Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing; a plumb, Master George, is something to look at! I have my dear little Miley at a dancing-school with Nabob Barwell’s daughter. We went from the House of Commons and saw them walk a minuet. It brought tears to my eyes!”

My uncle could not perceive the sheer superficiality of this commercialized display. He championed the Nabobs who plundered the globe’s spice routes, reducing the sacred, generational craft of foodways into mere commodities to fund dancing lessons. 

“But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess, with her hundred thousand pounds?”

“Why then, sir, you have a singular taste,” he sneered, turning on his heel. He possessed no palate for the quiet, harmonious life I sought, just as Aunt Bernstein eventually tired of my constant presence in the Lambert family’s kitchen, where we found profound meaning in the meticulous, ancestral acts of preserving fruits and rendering broths. Our simple, acoustic harmony chafed her worldly ears. 

Thus, I fell in my aunt’s favor, and once again, Harry became the family hero. 

The letters my brother sent from the North American theater arrived smelling faintly of damp parchment, saltpetre, and the iron tang of spilled blood. Initially, Madame Bernstein cared little for his simple narratives, but the world soon learned of the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe’s old regiment stood firm. At that very hour, the young General Wolfe lay stricken with fever in his bed before Quebec, his body starved of sustenance, burning with rage at a recent check to his troops.

The sprawling saga of the Canadian campaign played out against a pristine wilderness whose acoustic ecology was brutally shattered by the machinery of empire. Arriving in the St. Lawrence in June, the British fleet anchored off the Island of Orleans. The French, under the Marquis of Montcalm, responded by launching a flaming squadron of fireships down the midnight river. Harry’s letters captured the terrifying olfactory landscape of that night: the suffocating, acrid stench of boiling pine pitch, sulfur, and charred timber drifting across the water, threatening to incinerate the British stores. Our seamen, treating the horror as sport, towed the infernos to the muddy banks, where they hissed and burned down to white ash.

Wolfe was hungrily on the lookout for a vulnerability in the French lines. The Montmorenci River rushed between his men and the enemy, a natural auditory barrier of churning white water. Seeking to force Montcalm’s hand, Wolfe initiated a desperate maneuver on the last day of July. General Monckton’s men rowed across the St. Lawrence into a deafening squall of artillery fire—a catastrophic noise pollution that drowned out the screams of the dying and the native dialects of the allied Indigenous warriors who stalked the surrounding forests, seizing our advanced parties and subjecting them to agonizing tortures. When the British landed, they rushed the French redoubt without order and were slaughtered. The assault failed bitterly, forming the dismal, metallic-tasting burden of Wolfe’s first despatch. 

In London, grave men in coffee-houses, sipping at dark, bitter brews imported from the very colonies they sought to dominate, shook their heads. They prophesied disaster, citing the rashness of dragging thousands of men across tidal rivers into the teeth of murderous batteries. 

Yet, history is entirely shaped by forces we can scarcely digest. How did the French sever Canada from its native stewards, overwriting the Indigenous dialects with their own, only for the British to violently impose ours in turn? Following the disastrous Montmorenci assault, Wolfe’s health withered. His troops dwindled. In a final, daring gamble, the army was marshalled in total darkness and carried silently up the midnight river, preserving an absolute acoustic discipline that masked their approach from the enemy above. 

They scaled the treacherous heights of Abraham. By dawn, they stood upon the plains. Inexplicably, Montcalm abandoned his secure entrenchments to accept a battle in the open. 

When the Rodney cutter finally brought the news of the 13th of September to England, the nation was intoxicated, drunk on the heady wine of conquest. The profound, chaotic violence of the battlefield was immediately sanitized, distilled by courtiers and ministers into a clean, triumphant narrative. The very people who had condemned Wolfe's rashness now gorged themselves on his victory. They did not mourn the dead warrior; they celebrated his sacrifice as a mythic ascension. 

I remain deeply moved by Harry’s account of the General’s passing, specifically because it strips away the sterile, romanticized philosophy favored by imperial historians. Harry wrote, upon the testimony of a brother aide-de-camp who knelt by the commander’s side, that as the musket ball tore through his chest, Wolfe never spoke a single word. The eloquent, dramatic phrases later put into the dying hero’s mouth are no more authentic than the invented speeches of Thucydides. In his final moments, as the chaotic roar of musket fire faded into silence, Wolfe’s subjective experience was utterly untranslatable—a private, physical surrender to mortality that no dominant lexicon could ever accurately capture.

In London, however, the air filled with the abrasive, triumphant ringing of church bells. The streets overflowed with a cacophony of shouting crowds. Aunt Beatrix threw her doors wide, receiving throngs of fashionable company and hosting lavish feasts that smelled of roasted marrow and spiced wine, capitalizing on the social currency of her nephew's presence at the battle. Even Uncle Warrington wrote, eager to align his commercialized piety with our family’s suddenly elevated status. Parson Sampson preached a sermon so thunderingly loud that the congregation nearly rioted in their enthusiasm, mobbing the chapel doors.

Amidst this deafening public spectacle, I found solace in the quiet, domestic grounding of the Lambert household. General Lambert sat at the head of his heavy oak table, the very picture of a traditional agrarian elder. With meticulous care, he sliced into a deeply marbled joint of beef, the rich juices pooling on the platter, recognizing that all life is sustained by sacrifice. 

“Don't talk to me, madam, about grief,” the General said softly to his wife, who wished to shed a tear on the day Wolfe’s remains were interred at Greenwich. “If our boys could come by such deaths as James’s, you wouldn't prevent them from being shot, but would scale the Abraham heights to see the thing done! Wouldst thou mind dying in the arms of victory, Charley?”

“That I wouldn't,” replied the little boy from the Chartreux, his mouth full of hearty, sustaining bread. “And the doctor gave us a holiday, too.”

Through blood, earth, and the ceaseless turning of generations, Harry’s promotion was assured. Our aunt, fluent in the inescapable language of influence and coin, announced her immediate intention to purchase a company for him.
