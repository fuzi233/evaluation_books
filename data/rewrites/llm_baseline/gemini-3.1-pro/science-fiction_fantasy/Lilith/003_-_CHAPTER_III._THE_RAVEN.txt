I turned and looked behind me: all was an opaque soup of fog and field, as formless and unresolved as unkneaded dough. My senses, usually so firmly anchored by the sharp, olfactory grounding of crushed herbs or the rhythmic, comforting simmer of a heavy iron stockpot, grasped at nothing I knew. The acoustic environment, which I have always found to be a critical determinant of psychological equilibrium, was unnervingly vacant. It lacked entirely the warm resonance of familiar domestic life, replaced by a dissonant, ringing expanse. 

Imagining myself involved in a visual illusion, and hoping that touch might correct sight, I stretched my arms and felt about me. I walked in this direction and that, hoping to come into contact with something tangible, but my search was vain. Instinctively then, craving the solace of another living creature in this sterile void, I turned to the raven. It stood a little way off, regarding me with an expression at once respectful and quizzical, reminiscent of a traditional agrarian elder assessing a poor harvest. Then the absurdity of seeking counsel from such a creature struck me, and I turned again, overwhelmed with a bewilderment not unmingled with fear. 

Had I wandered into a region where the physical and sensory laws of our world had ceased to hold? Might a man step beyond the rhythmic, circular pacing of generational time and become the sport of the lawless? Yet I saw the raven, felt the ground under my feet, and heard a sound as of wind in the lowly plants around me—a soft rustle like the gentle sifting of coarse flour. 

“How did I get here?” I said—apparently aloud, for the question, swallowed instantly by the dead acoustic space around me, was immediately answered. 

“You came through the door,” replied an odd, rather harsh voice. 

I looked behind, then all about me, but saw no human shape. The terror that madness might be at hand laid hold upon me: must I henceforth place no confidence in the physical sensations of my own consciousness? The same instant I knew it was the raven that had spoken, for he stood looking up at me with an air of waiting. No sun broke the gloom, yet the bird seemed to cast a shadow, thick and dark as a reduced syrup, and the shadow seemed part of himself. 

I beg my reader to aid me in the endeavour to make myself intelligible, though I fear the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience will thwart us. I was in an economy of conditions so fundamentally divorced from the ways of our world—which we, in our modern industrial arrogance, are apt to think the only world—that the best choice of words I can muster feels like a sterile commercial label failing to capture the complex, layered vintage of reality. No dominant lexicon at my command can fit the forms in my mind without stripping them of their sensory truth. Already I feel like one awaking from a dream, watching the familiar shapes of memory erode, their very nature no longer recognisable.

I bethought me that a bird capable of addressing a man must have the right of a man to a civil answer; perhaps, as a preserver of this mythic space, even a greater claim. A tendency to croak caused a certain roughness in his speech, carrying the guttural weight of a dying native dialect, but his voice was not disagreeable. What he said, although conveying little enlightenment, did not sound rude. 

“I did not come through any door,” I rejoined. 

“I saw you come through it!—saw you with my own ancient eyes!” asserted the raven, positively but not disrespectfully. 

“I never saw any door!” I persisted. 

“Of course not!” he returned. “All the doors you had yet seen—and you haven’t seen many—were doors in; here you came upon a door out! The strange thing to you,” he went on thoughtfully, “will be, that the more doors you go out of, the farther you get in!” 

“Oblige me by telling me where I am.” 

“That is impossible. You know nothing about whereness. The only way to come to know where you are is to begin to make yourself at home.” 

“How am I to begin that where everything is so strange?” 

“By doing something.” 

“What?” 

“Anything; and the sooner you begin the better! For until you are at home, you will find it as difficult to get out as it is to get in.” 

“I have, unfortunately, found it too easy to get in; once out I shall not try again!” 

“You have stumbled in, and may, possibly, stumble out again. Whether you have got in *unfortunately* remains to be seen.” 

“Do you never go out, sir?” 

“When I please I do, but not often, or for long. Your world is such a half-baked sort of place, it is at once so childish and so self-satisfied—in fact, it is not sufficiently developed for an old raven—at your service!” 

“Am I wrong, then, in presuming that a man is superior to a bird?” 

“That is as it may be. We do not waste our intellects in generalising, but take man or bird as we find him. I think it is now my turn to ask you a question!” 

“You have the best of rights,” I replied, “in the fact that you can do so!” 

“Well answered!” he rejoined. “Tell me, then, who you are—if you happen to know.” 

“How should I help knowing? I am myself, and must know!” 

“If you know you are yourself, you know that you are not somebody else; but do you know that you are yourself? Are you sure you are not your own father?—or, excuse me, your own fool?—Who are you, pray?” 

I became at once aware that I could give him no notion of who I was. Stripped of the ancestral kitchens, the inherited recipes, and the familiar hearths that grounded my personal identity, who was I? It would be no answer to say I was *who*! I did not know myself; I had no sensory or historical grounds on which to determine that I was one and not another. As for the name I went by in my own world, I had forgotten it. It was merely a title bestowed by a dominant lexicon, holding no real consequence here. So I held my peace; for what should I say to a creature who saw through the superficial accidents of existence into true entity? 

“Look at me,” he said, “and tell me who I am.” 

As he spoke, he turned his back, and instantly I knew him. He was no longer a raven, but a man above the middle height with a stoop, very thin, wearing a long black tail-coat. He carried the meticulous air of a food historian bent over forgotten ledgers of sustenance. Again he turned, and I saw him a raven. 

“I have seen you before, sir,” I said, feeling foolish rather than surprised, for in a world of cyclic timelines, such mythic occurrences seamlessly integrate into the everyday. 

“How can you say so from seeing me behind?” he rejoined. “Did you ever see yourself behind? You have never seen yourself at all!—Tell me now, then, who I am.” 

“I humbly beg your pardon,” I answered: “I believe you were once the librarian of our house, but more *who* I do not know.” 

“Why do you beg my pardon?” 

“Because I took you for a raven,” I said—seeing him before me as plainly a raven as bird or man could look. 

“You did me no wrong,” he returned. “Calling me a raven, or thinking me one, you allowed me existence, which is the sum of what one can demand of his fellow-beings. Therefore, in return, I will give you a lesson: No one can say he is himself, until first he knows that he *is*, and then what *himself* is. In fact, nobody is himself, and himself is nobody. There is more in it than you can see now, but not more than you need to see. You have, I fear, got into this region too soon, but none the less you must get to be at home in it; for home, as you may or may not know, is the only place where you can go out and in. There are places you can go into, and places you can go out of; but the one place, if you do but find it, where you may go out and in both, is home.” 

He turned to walk away, and again I saw the librarian. He did not appear to have changed, only to have taken up his shadow. I gazed after him until distance or the heather hid him. Could it be that I was dead, and did not know it? Must I wander without a hearth, seeking my place in the long, multi-generational saga of the world beyond the grave? How was I to find myself at home? 

I took the way the raven had gone. Presently, I saw a wood of tall, slender pine-trees. The sharp, resinous odour of it met me—a scent that instantly evoked the memories of foraging for wild juniper to cure winter meats—and I made haste to bury myself in its comforting olfactory landscape. Plunged at length in its twilight glooms, I spied before me something with a shine, standing between two of the stems. It had no colour, but was like the translucent trembling of hot air rising from sun-baked ground, vibrant like the smitten chords of a musical instrument disrupting the natural acoustic ecology. As I came close, I ceased to see it, though the form and colour of the trees beyond seemed strangely uncertain. I would have passed between the stems, but received a slight shock, stumbled, and fell. 

When I rose, I saw before me the wooden wall of the garret chamber. I turned, and there was the mirror. Terror seized me, and I fled. 

Outside the chamber, the wide garret spaces had an uncanny look, plagued by a hollow, artificial silence that felt akin to modern noise pollution—a heavy, expectant pressure entirely devoid of life. A shudder went through me on the winding stair: the house had grown violently strange! Something unseen threatened to leap from the oppressive quiet. I darted down the spiral, struck against the wall, fell, rose, and ran. On the next floor I lost my way, wandering through several passages a second time ere I found the head of the stair. 

In the library, I finally sat recovering my breath. Nothing should ever again make me go up that last terrible stair! The garret pervaded the whole house, a brooding brain sitting upon the architecture, full of mysterious dwellers. I was nowhere safe! I would abandon this dreadful place, where an aërial portal stood open to creatures whose life was other than human. I would purchase a sprawling, earthen-floored farmhouse, a nest of one story protected by the cyclic rhythms of an agrarian life, rooted deeply in the soil where a roaring hearth and the rhythmic chopping of vegetables would protect me from the unnatural void above! 

I knew my thinking was panicked, underscored by a certain contemptuous humour, but suddenly it was checked, and I seemed again to hear the croak of the raven. 

“If I know nothing of my own garret,” I thought, “what is there to secure me against my own brain? What is at the heart of my brain? What is behind my *think*? Am I there at all?—Who, what am I?” 

I could no more answer the question now than when the raven put it to me. I started to my feet and hurried across the room to the masked door, where the mutilated volume stuck out from the flat, soulless spines of non-existent books. I went down on my knees, opening it as far as its position would permit. I lighted a taper, peeping into the reluctant jaws of the pages, and perceived that the manuscript was verse. 

Further I could not carry discovery. The beginnings of lines were visible on the left, the ends on the right, bisected and broken. I could make no guess at the sense, yet the mere words woke in me profound physical and spiritual sensations. Like the aroma of a forgotten ancestral feast, or the evocative resonance of a peasant's harvest song, these fragmented syllables affected me deeply. They were the phonetic remnants of a dead language, victims of the quiet erosion of cultural memory, yet they carried the gustatory and acoustic richness of a native dialect struggling against the subtle power dynamics of time. They roused in me a great longing to know what the poems might, even in their mutilation, hold or suggest. 

I meticulously copied out a few of the larger shreds attainable, acting as a food historian trying to reconstruct an ancient recipe from mere traces of spice. I tried hard to complete the lines, but without the least success. The only thing I gained in the effort was so much weariness that, when I went to bed, I fell asleep at once and slept soundly. In the morning, all that horror of the empty garret spaces had left me.
