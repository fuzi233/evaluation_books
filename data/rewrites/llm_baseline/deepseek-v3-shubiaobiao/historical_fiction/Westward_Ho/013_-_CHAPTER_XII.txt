*"Three lords sat drinking late yestreen,
And ere they paid the lawing,
They set a combat them between,
To fight it in the dawing."*
—*Scotch Ballad*

To know Bideford is to know its bridge—its heart, its pride, its very soul. Just as Edinburgh is defined by its castle, Rome by its capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids, so is Bideford inseparable from its ancient bridge. But few understand the legends woven into its stones, the unseen hands that shaped its fate.

They say that when the bridge was first to be built, the stones laid by day were mysteriously carried downstream by night to their present resting place. Sir Richard Gurney, the parish priest, vexed by these strange doings, dreamt of an angel who bade him build where the unseen mason had already laid the foundation—for only there, amid the shifting sands, would the bridge stand firm. And so it was done, blessed by Bishop Grandison of Exeter, who promised indulgences and spiritual blessings to all who aided its construction, ensuring men might profit in both this world and the next.

Yet the bridge is more than stone and mortar. It is a living thing—a giver of alms, a patron of schools, a landlord of estates, and, most famously, a host of feasts. Its cellars, stocked with the finest wines in Devon, have for centuries filled the cups of Bideford’s worthiest.

And so it was that in the year 1583, the bridge trustees dined in grand fashion, their company graced by Mr. St. Leger of Annery, a man of open hand and generous heart, who gave so freely of his wealth that his children would never be burdened by its temptations.

Over salmon and wine, a debate arose: which was finer—a fish caught above the bridge or below? Mr. St. Leger, ever one for practical proof, wagered five pounds that the salmon from the pool below Annery surpassed even the famed Appledore catch they had just enjoyed. And in the spirit of hospitality, he invited them all—wives, daughters, and even the Spanish guest, Don Guzman—to dine at Annery three days hence and judge for themselves.

***

The great hall of Annery had not seen such a feast since Judge Hankford entertained King Edward IV. Laughter and music filled the air, goblets gleamed, and plates were piled high. Yet amid the revelry, two figures played a silent game—Rose Salterne and Don Guzman, their eyes carefully averted, yet drawn together all the same.

For six months, they had exchanged no words, though their paths had crossed often. The Spaniard had learned his lesson well—no longer did he press his suit openly. Instead, he wore indifference like a cloak, speaking little to her, yet ensuring that whenever she was near, he shone brightest—witty, gallant, full of tales that set the room alight.

Rose, for her part, had grown weary of her own feigned disdain. Each stolen glance from him sent her pulse racing, each flash of his smile left her flustered. She had thought herself above such folly, yet here she was—trapped in the very snare she had once scorned.

That afternoon, as the company strolled along Annery’s sunlit terrace, Rose stood apart, torn between laughter and tears. When Lady Grenville bade her sing, she took up the lute with trembling fingers.

The ballad was too bold, too careless—a relic of happier days, when Frank’s praise still rang in her ears. But she dared not refuse. And so she sang, her voice steady despite the storm within.

*"It was Earl Haldan’s daughter,
She look’d across the sea..."*

When the last note faded, Don Guzman’s voice cut through the silence.

*"In the East, they say the nightingale sings to the rose. But Devon is blessed—for here, the rose herself is the songbird."*

Rose flushed, her heart pounding. Lady Grenville, sensing the tension, steered the talk elsewhere—but the damage was done.

Then came the blow.

*"I must soon return to Spain,"* Don Guzman announced, holding up a letter. *"From there, to the Indies—I am to be Governor of La Guayra."*

A murmur of surprise rippled through the company. Rose’s breath caught. Half a world away.

*"Where is La Guayra?"* she asked, too quickly.

*"On the Spanish Main,"* he replied, his tone light. *"A paradise of palms and mountains. All it lacks is a wife to make it perfect."*

Lady Grenville laughed. *"Doubtless some lady of Seville will leap at the chance."*

Rose’s cheeks burned. The talk turned to jealous husbands and foreign customs, but she scarcely heard.

Then Don Guzman, ever the storyteller, was pressed for a tale—and he obliged, spinning a yarn of love and treachery in the New World, of a Spanish lady’s courage and a savage king’s obsession.

*"Imagine,"* he said, *"a woman so virtuous, so brave, that even a heathen ruler dared not force her—but sought to win her instead."*

His eyes flickered to Rose as he spoke.

And though the story ended in tragedy, the message was clear.

Patience. Persistence.

And victory.
