A profound hesitation, bordering on sheer awe, seized Mr. Gryce before he could regain his composure. The dim illumination, the oppressive silence, and the setting—evocative of a romanticized past—combined to create an atmosphere that unsettled even the hardened veteran of the law. His mind, seasoned by decades of solving complex cases, struggled to reconcile the motionless figure lying prone before him with the reality of the situation. The man, recently the master of the house, lay stretched out as if prepared for burial, yet the sacred symbol pinned to his chest violently contradicted the earthly rage that had driven the dagger home.

For merely a heartbeat, Mr. Gryce faltered. However, before his subordinate could voice his own confusion, the detective stepped forward, positioning himself beside the fallen form. There could be no doubt: the man was dead. Yet, his immediate instinct was to verify the cessation of life, placing a hand on the pulse and inspecting the eyes. Their gaze held an expression of such bitter reproach that Gryce had to summon every ounce of professional coolness to withstand the weight of them. The body was still warm, confirming death beyond any shadow of a doubt. Convinced, he refrained from removing the dagger, instead studying the wound with meticulous care before shifting his focus to the rest of the room.

The weapon was extraordinary—a curiosity sourced from an Oriental shop. While such an item might suggest suicide, the angle of entry and the specific placement of the wound ruled out self-infliction. Clues were scarce. Despite the evident violence of the assault, the impeccably ordered apartment bore no marks of a struggle. Aside from a scattering of rose petals on the floor, the room exuded a serene, luxurious tranquility. Even the massive central table, positioned where the victim had stood moments before, offered no initial sign of chaos. Its surface, cluttered with useful and ornamental items, appeared undisturbed, as if the shock of the tragedy had somehow bypassed the objects themselves.

Gryce examined the table's contents: pipes, pens, portraits, weights, measures, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a musical score, potted flowers, and, most strangely, a row of electric buttons. As soon as Gryce pressed one, the red glow emanating from the cage-like iron fixture above instantly shifted to a sickly green glare, bathing the room in ghastly hues. Seizing another button, he restored a soft, white radiance, banishing the unnatural tint.

"Childish tricks for a man of his station and age," Gryce muttered internally. Yet, glancing once more at the upturned face beneath him, a doubt crept in. Could the owner of those sharply defined, purposeful features possess anything resembling childish whimsy? The face itself was arresting, exerting a magnetic pull that disrupted the flow of the detective's reasoning whenever it captured his attention. To attribute foolishness to such a visage was to admit a failure of judgment regarding human nature. Consequently, the overhead lamp, with its scientific mechanism, took on a deeper significance, hinting at the advanced studies evidenced by the books and apparatus surrounding the corpse.

Setting the light to remain steady, Gryce turned his attention to the walls, draped in tapestries and curios. Nothing immediately aided his investigation, except for one detail: a bent nail, ripped from its socket. It was the nail that had held the cross now resting against the victim's heart. The cord suspending the cross still clung to it, tangling with the crimson thread of the fatal wound. Who had torn down that symbol of peace? Not the victim; with such a grievous injury, such movement would have been impossible. Furthermore, the empty hook on the wall was located at the farthest possible distance from the body within the confined circular room.

Another hand had undoubtedly removed the cross, placing it directly in the path of the dying man's final breaths—a bizarre display of either desperate religious hope or frantic remorse. Gryce spared this only a fleeting thought, too precious were the minutes he spent alone amidst the carnage.

Behind the table, halfway up the wall, hung a single large painting: the portrait of a young woman of striking, poignant beauty. Her attire and hairstyle dated the piece to the era of the Civil War. She shared a haunting intellectual charm with the man slumped on the floor. Though she was fair-skinned and he dark-haired, the resemblance argued a familial bond. Beneath the portrait rested a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor during the conflict.

"Mementoes that may prove vital," Gryce mused.

Moving onward, he encountered a narrow curtain, dark in hue and matching the adjacent drapery, which had previously escaped notice. It was not a window, for the apartment's windows sat high near the ceiling. Instead, it concealed an entrance to another chamber. Pushing it aside, Gryce entered a narrow closet furnished with a bed, a dresser, and a small table. The bed was a narrow cot suitable for a bachelor, while the dresser reflected the luxurious habits of its occupant. Everything was in perfect order, save for a silver-backed comb missing from the dresser, now found lying on the floor at the far end of the room. Nearby, on a small stand, stood a pearl-handled parasol. These items confirmed the recent presence of a woman.

Her identity became clear almost immediately. On the threshold separating this room from the study lay a tiny black sequin. As Gryce, drawn by its sparkle, stooped to examine it, his eye caught a second sequin further along the floor, and a third closer to the large center table. This dainty trail affected him strangely. As a keen observer, he knew that sequins were often strung on a thread; if broken, they fell one by one, allowing a detective to track a woman's path by the glittering debris left behind. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate using vanity as a trap that pleased him, a knowing smile played on Gryce's lips as he regarded the table.

Before inspecting the furniture, however, he sought the origin of the loose thread. Had it snagged on a projection? He checked the doorways and chairs—all cushioned and smooth. Then, his gaze landed on the cross. Its base featured intricate gold filigree. Could this delicate wire have caught in the woman's dress as she tore the cross from the wall, initiating the chain of falling sequins? Rushing to the spot where the cross had hung, he searched the floor but found nothing until he reached the rug beneath the prostrate body. Amidst the long hairs of the bearskin lay another sequin. The woman in shimmering garments had definitely stooped there before him.

Satisfied, Gryce returned to the table for a thorough inspection. The results were promising. He smiled as he rubbed his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand, then carefully lifted the stand and replaced it exactly where he had found it. Had he suspected something hidden beneath? A detective rarely reveals his hypotheses.

Lost in contemplation before the table, Gryce was startled by a quick step behind him. Styles, the officer, had completed his sweep of the house and stood ready to report.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, turning sharply.

Styles gestured toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, unwilling to leave the room that currently captivated him. Styles grew agitated, breaking his silence. "Come! I've found the culprit. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce expressed surprise at the gender assumption.

"Yes, no doubt about it. When you see him—but what is that? Is he coming down? I'm certain no one else is in the house. Can't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded; someone was indeed descending.

"Let us withdraw," Styles suggested. "Not because he is dangerous, but because it is crucial you observe him before he observes you. He acts strangely, sir. If he enters here, he will surely incriminate himself. Where shall we hide?"

Recalling the small room he had just vacated, Gryce guided the officer inside and drew the curtain shut, leaving only a small aperture. The approaching footsteps grew louder, accompanied by the rapid, labored breathing of the intruder.

"Does he know we are here? Did he see you upstairs?" Gryce whispered.

Styles shook his head, pointing urgently toward the opposite door. Just then, the intruder lifted the portière and stood revealed in the doorway.

Both men stared. Was this the murderer? A pale, gaunt servant holding a tray with a single glass of water? So stunned was Gryce that he looked to Styles for clarification. But the officer, concealing his own surprise at this peaceful apparition, urged patience. Together, they watched as the man advanced, stopped, glanced briefly at the body on the floor, and then dropped the glass with a low cry that quickly escalated into a howl.

"Look at him! Watch what he does next!" Styles urged in a hushed tone. "You'll see a murderer at work."

With passionate intensity, he gazed at empty air, then thrust one hand back in a cramped position while plunging the other toward the invisible table.
