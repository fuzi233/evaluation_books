“Where are the sounds that swam along
The buoyant air when I was young;
The last vibration now is o’er,
And they who listened are no more;
Ah! let me close my eyes and dream.”

The name of Helstone had stirred in Mr. Bell’s mind during his talk with Mr. Lennox, and from that moment it haunted his dreams all night long. He found himself once more the young tutor at the college where he now stood as a Fellow—a man of rank and years, yet in spirit still the eager scholar of his youth. It was again the long vacation, and he was staying with his friend, newly married, proud in his role as Vicar of Helstone, and blissfully devoted to his wife. Together, they leapt across babbling brooks with impossible grace, suspended in midair for what felt like endless days. Time and space dissolved; only emotion remained real. The world existed not in its physical form but in the warmth of memory—the rustling leaves of autumn trees, the sweet scent of herbs and flowers carried on the breeze, the gentle movements of the young wife through the house, her manner a blend of quiet pride in her husband and a subtle irritation at their modest means—just as Mr. Bell remembered her from twenty-five years past.

The dream was so vivid, so deeply alive, that when he awoke, the reality around him seemed thin, unreal. Where was he? In a narrow, well-appointed room of a London hotel, surrounded by silence and cold order. Who were those voices he had heard, those hands that had touched him, those faces that had smiled? They were gone—dead, buried, lost beyond recall. And here he was, an old man, once full of vigor and purpose, now standing alone in a life stripped of companionship. The weight of solitude pressed upon him, unbearable in its clarity.

He rose quickly, determined to drown the ache in routine. Dressing hurriedly, he prepared for breakfast in Harley Street, seeking refuge in the mundane. But even there, the world refused to offer comfort. As Mr. Lennox read aloud the legal documents, each sentence stripping away another thread of hope for Frederick’s innocence, Margaret’s face grew pale, her eyes wide with growing dread. She had known the outcome already, yet the relentless unfolding of evidence—each detail a blow—left her trembling. At last, she could hold back no longer, and tears fell freely.

Mr. Lennox paused, his voice softening. “I had better not go on,” he said quietly. “It was a foolish idea of mine.”

“Lieutenant Hale,” he added, using the title that had been stripped from him, “is happy now. More secure in fortune and future than he ever could have been in the navy. He has, no doubt, made his wife’s country his own.”

“That is it,” Margaret whispered, forcing a smile. “It seems selfish to regret it—and yet… he is gone from me. And I am so lonely.”

Mr. Lennox turned over his papers, silent, wishing he possessed the wealth and peace he believed might one day be his. Mr. Bell wiped his nose with a handkerchief, saying nothing. But within moments, Margaret had regained her composure. She thanked Mr. Lennx with quiet dignity, her gratitude tempered by awareness that her sorrow might have seemed excessive to him. Yet she would not have spared herself the pain—it was part of what made her whole.

Then Mr. Bell stepped forward, adjusting his gloves. “Margaret,” he said, his voice thick with emotion, “I’m going down to Helstone tomorrow—to see the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it be too painful? Speak plainly. Don’t hold back.”

“Oh, Mr. Bell,” she murmured, unable to speak further. But she reached out and gently kissed his gnarled, gouty hand.

“Come, come,” he said, blushing with discomfort. “That’s enough.”

“I suppose your aunt Shaw will trust you with me,” he continued. “We’ll leave early tomorrow morning—we should arrive about two o’clock. We’ll take a light meal, order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, as it used to be called—and walk through the woods to work up an appetite. Can you manage it, Margaret? I know it will be hard for both of us—but it will be a joy for me, at least. We’ll dine on venison if we can get it, then I’ll rest while you visit old friends. I’ll bring you back safe and sound—unless the railway fails, which I won’t risk. I’ll insure your life for a thousand pounds before we start, just to ease your family’s mind. Otherwise, I promise to return you to Mrs. Shaw by lunchtime on Friday. So—if you say yes—I’ll go upstairs and tell her.”

“It’s no use trying to express how much I’d love it,” Margaret said, her voice breaking.

“Well then,” Mr. Bell replied, “prove your gratitude by keeping those tearful springs dry for the next two days. If you don’t, I’ll feel strange myself about the lachrymal ducts—and I don’t care for that.”

“I won’t cry a single drop,” she promised, blinking rapidly to clear her lashes, and forcing a smile.

“There’s my good girl,” he said, pleased. “Then let’s go upstairs and settle it.”

Margaret stood beside him, her heart fluttering with excitement and sorrow, as he discussed the plan with Aunt Shaw. The older woman was at first startled, then uncertain—weighing propriety against affection, duty against desire. She hesitated, unable to decide whether it was right or wrong, proper or improper. Only after Margaret’s safe return and the joyful success of the journey would she feel confident enough to declare: “I’m certain it was a kind thought of Mr. Bell’s—and exactly what I’d hoped for Margaret, a change she truly needed after all this anxiety.”
