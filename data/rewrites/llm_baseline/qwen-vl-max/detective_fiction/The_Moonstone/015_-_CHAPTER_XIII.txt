I found my lady in her private sitting room, seated by the window with a book in her hand. She started visibly when I mentioned that Sergeant Cuff wished to speak with her. “_Must_ I see him?” she asked, her voice tight with unease. “Can’t you represent me, Gabriel?”

I stared at her, baffled. Her tone and expression told me she was not merely tired—she was genuinely disturbed. I could only offer a helpless shrug.

She sighed, then spoke more quietly. “I am afraid my nerves are unsteady today,” she confessed. “There’s something about that London officer—something I can’t explain—that makes me recoil. It’s foolish, I know, and quite unlike me. But I have a presentiment he brings trouble with him. Misery, even.”

I had no words for such an admission. The more I observed Sergeant Cuff, the more I admired his calm precision. He seemed to me the very model of a man who knew his duty—and how to carry it out without alarm or exaggeration.

My lady rallied somewhat after speaking so frankly. Naturally courageous, as I’ve said before, she drew herself up with quiet dignity. “If I must see him,” she declared, “then I must. But I cannot bring myself to face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel—and stay here while he is here.”

This was the first time I’d seen such a fit of nervousness from her since she was a girl. I left her side and returned to the boudoir.

Mr. Franklin had stepped into the garden, where he joined Mr. Godfrey, whose departure was now imminent. Sergeant Cuff and I made our way directly to my mistress’s chamber.

When we entered, I saw her flush pale at the sight of the sergeant. Yet she held herself steady, her composure returning swiftly. She addressed him with polite formality: “Do you object to my servant being present during this conversation?”

She added, with a gentle grace that spoke volumes of trust, “Gabriel has been with me for years—not only as a servant, but as a trusted adviser. In matters concerning the household, I consider his judgment invaluable.”

Sergeant Cuff inclined his head slightly. “I accept your presence as a favor, madam,” he replied. “I intend to discuss the servants’ roles in this matter, and I’ve already found your experience useful. Your insight may prove essential.”

She gestured toward two chairs. We sat down immediately.

“I have formed an opinion on this case,” Sergeant Cuff began, “but I ask your permission to keep it to myself—for now. My purpose today is to report what I discovered in Miss Verinder’s sitting room, and what steps I propose to take next.”

He described the smear on the paintwork, just as he had done to Superintendent Seegrave—only with greater care and respect in his phrasing. When he concluded, he stated plainly:

“One thing is certain: the Diamond is missing from the drawer in the cabinet. Another is almost certain: the marks from the smear must be on some article of clothing belonging to someone in this house. We must find that garment before we proceed further.”

“And that discovery,” my lady remarked, “implies, I presume, the discovery of the thief?”

“With all due respect, madam,” the sergeant replied, “I do not say the Diamond has been stolen. Only that it is missing. The stained garment may lead us to its recovery—but not necessarily to a thief.”

She turned to me. “Do you understand this?” she asked.

“Sergeant Cuff understands it, my lady,” I answered.

“How do you propose to find the stained dress?” she asked, turning back to him.

“The other officer has already searched the servants’ rooms and trunks,” she continued. “I will not allow them to be subjected to such treatment again. It is degrading—and unjust.”

A noble woman, truly—there was no one like her.

“That,” said Sergeant Cuff, “is precisely the point I meant to raise. The previous investigation damaged the inquiry by making the servants feel suspected. If they believe they’re under suspicion once more, they may resist us—especially the women. But their belongings _must_ be examined again. The first search looked only for the Diamond. Ours must look for the stain.”

He paused. “I agree with you, madam, that their feelings must be respected. But I also believe their wardrobes must be searched.”

It seemed we were at an impasse. My lady put it bluntly: “Then we are stuck.”

But Sergeant Cuff smiled faintly. “I have a plan—if you’ll consent. I propose explaining the situation to the entire household. Not as suspects, but as participants in a fair inquiry.”

I interrupted. “The women will think themselves accused.”

“No,” said Cuff firmly. “Not if I tell them I’m searching the wardrobes of _everyone_—from you, madam, down to the youngest maid—who slept in the house on Wednesday night. It’s a mere formality,” he added, glancing toward my lady, “but it will appear fair. They’ll see it as equal treatment. Instead of resisting, they’ll want to help.”

I saw the wisdom in it. My lady, once surprised, nodded slowly. “You are certain this is necessary?”

“It’s the shortest path to our goal,” he said.

She rose and rang for her maid. “You may address the servants,” she said, “with the keys to my wardrobe in your hand.”

But Sergeant Cuff stopped her with a question. “Shall we first confirm that the others will consent?”

“The only other lady is Miss Verinder,” she replied. “And the gentlemen—my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There’s no reason to expect refusal.”

I reminded her that Mr. Godfrey was leaving soon.

As I spoke, Mr. Godfrey knocked at the door to bid farewell. Mr. Franklin followed, bound for the station with him.

My lady explained the situation. Mr. Godfrey responded instantly. He called through the window for Samuel to retrieve his portmanteau and then handed the key himself to Sergeant Cuff.

“My luggage can follow me to London,” he said, “once the inquiry is over.”

The sergeant accepted it with a respectful apology. “I regret any inconvenience, sir. But the example of those above will greatly ease the servants’ acceptance of this process.”

After bidding my lady a kind farewell, Mr. Godfrey left a message for Miss Rachel—words that hinted clearly he would not accept rejection, and intended to renew his proposal at the earliest opportunity.

Mr. Franklin, following his cousin, informed the sergeant that all his clothes were open to inspection—nothing locked away. Sergeant Cuff thanked him sincerely.

So far, all had gone smoothly. Only Miss Rachel remained.

Now, my lady’s aversion to the sergeant seemed to deepen. As soon as we were alone again, she said sharply: “If I send you Miss Verinder’s keys, have I done all you require?”

“Not yet, madam,” said Cuff. “Before we begin, I should like, if possible, to see the washing-book. The stained item might be linen. If the search yields nothing, I must account for every piece of linen in the house—and every item sent to the laundry. If anything is missing, there will be a strong presumption it bore the stain and was deliberately removed yesterday or today.”

He turned to me. “Superintendent Seegrave drew the maids’ attention to the smear when they gathered in the room on Thursday morning. That,” he added, “may have been another of his errors.”

My lady instructed me to summon the washing-book. She stayed until it arrived, in case the sergeant needed more.

Rosanna Spearman brought it in. The girl had come down for breakfast that morning looking ill—pale and worn—but capable of her duties. Sergeant Cuff watched her closely: her face as she entered, her crooked shoulder as she left.

“Have you nothing more to ask?” my lady asked, eager to end the meeting.

Cuff opened the book, studied it briefly, then closed it. “One final question, madam,” he said. “Has the young woman who delivered this book served you as long as the others?”

She frowned. “Why do you ask?”

“Because,” he replied calmly, “the last time I saw her, she was in prison for theft.”

A silence fell.

Then my lady spoke. “I must tell you the truth. Rosanna has been in my service for over a year. Her conduct has been irreproachable. The matron at the Reformatory speaks highly of her. She has shown great reform.”

“You don’t suspect her?” she asked, earnestly.

“I have already said, madam,” Cuff replied, “that I do not suspect anyone in this house of theft—at this moment.”

She rose to leave, intending to fetch Miss Rachel’s keys.

But the sergeant beat me to the door. He bowed deeply as she passed.

She shuddered.

We waited.

And waited.

No keys came.

Sergeant Cuff said nothing. He turned to the window, slipped his hands into his pockets, and began to whistle softly—“The Last Rose of Summer.”

At last, Samuel appeared—not with the keys, but with a small scrap of paper for me.

I fumbled for my spectacles, feeling the sergeant’s gaze fixed upon me.

The note was in my lady’s handwriting. It read:

*Miss Rachel refuses to have her wardrobe examined. When asked why, she burst into tears. Asked again, she said: “I won’t, because I won’t. I must yield to force if you use it, but I will yield to nothing else.”*

I understood her reluctance. To confront the sergeant with such defiance from her daughter would have been unbearable.

And I confess—I would have blushed myself if I’d been younger.

“Any news of Miss Verinder’s keys?” Cuff asked.

“My young lady refuses,” I said.

“Ah,” he replied.

His voice—usually so controlled—held a subtle shift. It was the sound of a man who had expected exactly this.

He half-angered, half-frightened me. I couldn’t say why.

“Must we abandon the search?” I asked.

“Yes,” said Cuff. “The search must be abandoned. Because your young lady refuses to submit—like the rest. We must examine all wardrobes or none. Send Mr. Ablewhite’s portmanteau to London by the next train. Return the washing-book to the girl who brought it—with my thanks.”

He placed the book on the table and took out his penknife. He began trimming his nails.

“You don’t seem disappointed,” I said.

“No,” he replied. “I am not much disappointed.”

I pressed him. “Why would Miss Rachel oppose you? Isn’t it in her interest to help?”

“Wait a little, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “Wait a little.”

Even cleverer minds than mine might have grasped his meaning. Or perhaps someone less devoted to Miss Rachel might have seen it.

And my lady’s fear of him—perhaps, as I’ve since thought, she saw his intent “in a glass darkly.”

I did not.

“What’s next?” I asked.

He finished his nail, studied it for a moment with melancholy interest, then closed his knife.

“Come out into the garden,” he said. “Let’s have a look at the roses.”
