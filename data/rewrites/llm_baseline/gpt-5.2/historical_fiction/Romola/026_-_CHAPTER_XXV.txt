Outside the Duomo, while Baldassarre still trembled under Savonarola’s voice—as if that sound had entered him like strong wine—he had not marked the quiet opening of a door behind, nor the tread that made no haste. Another man had slipped in and stood a little aside, not to pray, not to be converted, but to look, as a painter looks, for a face that cannot be invented.

It was Piero di Cosimo.

The sermon had sunk into one of those pauses when a multitude ceases to be many bodies and becomes one breathing creature—here a stifled sob, there a throat clearing itself as if it could cough out terror; a soft rustle of sleeves, like dry leaves worrying each other. In that break of utterance, Piero moved forward, and with the sure touch of one who handled knives for pigments and canvases as easily as for bread, laid his hand on the escaped prisoner’s arm.

Baldassarre turned. Tears were still travelling down the furrows of his cheeks, slowly, as if the flesh were too worn to carry them faster; but his chest rose with a long, vigorous sigh, the sort that ends a storm and leaves a bitter stillness. Piero spoke low, so the sound of his words would not be stolen by listening piety.

“Shall I cut your cords for you? I have heard how you were made prisoner.”

Baldassarre did not answer at once. Suspicion sharpened his wet eyes. His gaze went over Piero’s face as if reading it in a dialect he had once known and half forgotten, looking for the trick of a familiar enemy in a stranger’s mouth.

At last he said, “If you will.”

“Better come outside,” Piero murmured.

Again the old man looked at him sidelong. Piero, guessing that this was a man to whom help always came with a hook in it, smiled a little, drew out a knife, and with a swift, clean motion severed the cords. The sound was small—just a faint creak of strained fibre giving way—but to Baldassarre it was like the first crack of ice under spring sun, the promise of movement.

Piero watched him fold his arms and flex his hands, taking a slow pleasure in the return of muscular sensation, as a starved man tastes bread crumb by crumb to be sure the mouth is still his. There was something in the old face—its fierce inward listening, its abrupt changes, as if some other voice were always speaking behind the visible one—that made Piero think, not without a shrug of pity, that talk of madness might not be mere excuse.

“Well,” he thought, “if he does mischief, they’ll tie him again. But the poor devil shall have a chance—at least.”

“You are afraid of me,” Baldassarre said, in the same undertone, not accusing so much as naming what he felt. “You don’t want to tell me anything about yourself.”

“No,” Baldassarre answered, and his tone had a quiet decision in it, like a door shutting without a slam. “I have nothing to tell.”

“As you please,” said Piero. “But perhaps you want shelter, and may not know how hospitable we Florentines are to visitors with torn doublets and empty stomachs. There’s an hospital for poor travellers outside all our gates; and if you liked, I could put you in the way to one. There’s no danger from your French soldier. He has been sent off.”

Baldassarre nodded—no thanks in the movement, but assent—and turned as if the offer were not kindness but information. Together they left the church.

Outside, Florence received them with her Sunday noises: the clustered murmurs under archways, the uneven beat of sandals on stone, a distant hammer where someone, impatient of salvation, had gone back to work; from a stall near the way, the sharp, friendly call of a vendor; and always, above and around, the great piazza’s reverberation—sound that did not die where it was born, but wandered, crossed itself, came back altered, as voices do in memory. Piero had the habit of hearing these things as part of a picture: a city’s sound was its underpainting, the ground on which colour must sit.

They went along the Via dell’ Oriuolo, toward the gate of Santa Croce. Piero walked at Baldassarre’s side, studying him with the same appetite with which he studied fish in a market—knowing that every surface held some truth about its hidden life.

“You wouldn’t like to sit to me for your portrait, should you?” he said suddenly, as if proposing a supper. “I am a painter. I would give you money to get your portrait.”

At that, Baldassarre’s suspicion returned with a sudden contraction of the brow, like a muscle remembering pain. He looked at Piero sharply.

“No,” he said, and the word was a blade.

“Ah!” Piero answered curtly, the sound clipping short. The refusal did not offend him as rudeness; it struck him as the refusal of a rare thing to be possessed. “Well, go straight on, and you’ll find the Porta Santa Croce, and outside it there’s an hospital for travellers. So—you’ll not accept any service from me?”

“I give you thanks for what you have done already,” Baldassarre said, and there was a stiff propriety in the phrase, as if he spoke it in a language learned for occasions of debt. “I need no more.”

“It is well,” said Piero, with a shrug that dismissed the business without dismissing the image. They parted—Baldassarre moving forward with the hard directness of a man led by something inward; Piero turning back, already tasting the thought of him.

“A mysterious old tiger!” the painter said to himself, as he went toward the Piazza del Duomo again. “Well worth painting. Ugly—with deep lines—looking as if the plough and the harrow had gone over his heart. A fine contrast to my bland and smiling Messer Greco—my Bacco trionfante—who has married the fair Antigone in contradiction to all history and fitness. Aha! his scholar’s blood curdled uncomfortably at the old fellow’s clutch!”

When Piero re-entered the piazza, the multitude who had listened to Fra Girolamo were pouring out from all the doors. Their haste to go each upon his own way showed how important they held the preaching that had detained them from the day’s other duties: the market to be visited, the cook to be scolded for stale oil, the oxen to be watered, the ledger to be balanced, the dough to be kneaded before it cooled and turned stubborn under the hands.

Piero leaned against an angle of the Baptistery and watched. The crowd unreeled like a long woven cloth, pattern after pattern passing the eye: the cut of a sleeve, the shine of a fur edging, the sober black lucco that made authority visible even when the voice was silent. Faces came by—keen, various, stamped with Florentine daylight—faces such as Masaccio had painted more than fifty years before, such as Domenico Ghirlandajo had not yet quite ceased to paint: the same city breeding the same bone and gaze, handing down its expressions like recipes, altered a little by each generation’s salt.

This morning was a peculiar occasion, and the Frate’s audience, always mixed, had represented even more completely than usual the classes and parties of Florence.

There were men of high birth, used to public charges at home and abroad, newly conspicuous as enemies of the Medici and friends of popular government, but also thorough Piagnoni, embracing to the utmost the Frate’s doctrines and practical teaching, frequenting San Marco as if another Samuel lived there. Some had an authoritative, handsome presence—Francesco Valori among them—hot in temper, made hotter by the comfort of thinking Heaven had signed their policies. Others—like Soderini—carried less of the ardent Piagnone and more of the prudent statesman, whose wisdom is not a flame but a slow fire that cooks without scorching.

There were men, too, of family like Piero Capponi, brave lovers of sober republican liberty, who preferred fighting to arguing, and had no special need to call any idea false if it kept out the Medici and made room for public spirit. At their elbows went doctors of law, their heads full of Accursius and the brethren, yet not so consumed by commentary that ardour had been boiled away: Messer Luca Corsini himself, who on a memorable day to come would lift his learned arms in street stone-throwing for religion, freedom, and the Frate.

And among the dignities carrying their black lucco or furred mantle with habitual authority, there was abundant sprinkling of faces more contemplative and sensitive—scholars inheriting names like Strozzi and Acciajoli, already minded to take the cowl and join San Marco; artists, too, wrought to a new and higher ambition by Savonarola’s teaching. One young painter, lately surpassing himself in a fresco of the divine child on the wall of the Frate’s bare cell, passed by with eyes still stained, as it were, with his own colours—unaware that one day he would wear the tonsure and cowl and be called Fra Bartolommeo.

There was the mystic poet Girolamo Benevieni, hurrying perhaps to carry tidings of the beloved Frate’s speedy coming to Pico della Mirandola, who was never to see the light of another morning. There were well-born women dressed with scrupulous plainness, whose finer grace remained the chief distinction between them and less aristocratic sisters. There were many of the genuine popolani—men of the Major and Minor Arts—conscious of purses threatened by war-taxes, their faces set as if they could already hear the coins clink away. And more striking and various than any other portion of the Frate’s disciples, there was the long stream of poorer tradesmen and artisans, whose faith and hope in his divine message ranged from the rough, undiscriminating trust in him as friend of the poor and enemy of luxurious oppressors, to that eager tasting of biblical subtlety which takes hold of the sedentary artisan: a pale flame lighting the long dim spaces beyond the board where he stitched, as if those far-off texts were broth for the mind, warming him through the cold of daily labour.

Yet among all these disciples were scattered many who were not disciples at all. Some were Mediceans who, out of fear and policy, had begun to show the presiding spirit of the popular party a feigned deference, like men who bow to a new master while counting the silver still hidden under the floor. Others were sincere advocates of free government, but regarded Savonarola simply as an ambitious monk—half sagacious, half fanatical—who had made himself a powerful instrument with the people, and must be taken as one takes weather: disliked, discussed, but endured.

There were even bitter enemies: members of the old aristocratic anti-Medicean party, determined to get the reins once more tight in the hands of certain chief families; and licentious young men who hated him as the killjoy of Florence, the man who would snuff out song and feast as if joy were a theft. The sermons in the Duomo had already become political incidents, drawing ears of curiosity and malice as well as faith. Men of ideas—young Niccolò Macchiavelli among them—came to observe and report to friends away in country villas; men of appetites, like Dolfo Spini, came to feed hatred and lie in wait for accusations, angry as hunters whose game is driven away by bells.

Perhaps no preacher ever had a more massive influence than Savonarola; perhaps no preacher ever had more heterogeneous material to work upon. One secret of that massive influence lay in the mixed character of his preaching itself. Baldassarre, wrought into an ecstasy of self-martyring revenge, was only an extreme case among the partial, narrow sympathies of the audience. In Savonarola’s preaching there were strains that appealed to the finest susceptibilities, and elements that gratified low egoism, tickled gossiping curiosity, and fascinated timorous superstition. His need of personal predominance, his labyrinth of allegorical interpretations, his enigmatic visions, his false certainty about divine intentions—these never ceased, within his large soul, to be ennobled by fervid piety, passionate sense of the infinite, active sympathy, and clear-sighted demand that selfish interests submit to the general good.

But for the mass, all the pregnancy of his words lay in the strong assertion of supernatural claims, in denunciatory visions, in the false certainty that made sermons like political bulletins; and once that audience had been mastered, it was necessary to his nature—and, as he believed, to their welfare—that he should keep the mastery. The effect was inevitable. No man struggles to retain power over a mixed multitude without suffering vitiation; his standard must be their lower needs rather than his own best insight.

As Piero stood listening now to the piazza’s ebbing roar—the cries thinning, footsteps scattering into side streets, the Duomo’s great doors swallowing the last reverberations—he watched not only faces but the way voices changed as people left the sacred vaulting and entered open air. In the church, sound had gathered, been held, made solemn by stone; outside, it broke apart, each man reclaiming his own tone, his own dialect, his own habitual inflection—some clipped and dominant, some soft with country vowels, some thick with the shop’s jargon. It was in these differences, untranslatable one into another, that Florence kept her memory; and it was also here that power worked, for the loudest lexicon always tried to rename what it did not own.

The mysteries of human character have seldom been presented in a way more fit to check the easy judgments of those who think they know, than in Girolamo Savonarola. Yet one may reverence him without closing eyes to fact, if one regards his life as a drama with great inward modifications answering to outward change. Up to this period, when his more direct action on political affairs had only just begun, it is likely that his imperious need of ascendancy had burned undiscernibly within the strong flame of his zeal for God and man.

It was the fashion of old, when an ox was led out for sacrifice to Jupiter, to chalk the dark spots and give the offering a false show of unblemished whiteness. Let the chalk be flung away. Boldly say: the victim is spotted; yet it is not therefore in vain that his mighty heart is laid on the altar of men’s highest hopes.
