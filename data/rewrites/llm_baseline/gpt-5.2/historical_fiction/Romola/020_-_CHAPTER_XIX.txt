Messer Bernardo del Nero held to his counsel as one holds to a well-seasoned rule at the hearth: the marriage must wait till Easter. He said it with the same quiet firmness with which an old housekeeper will bid a young cook let the broth stand, and not stir it into haste. Bardo, who could be obstinate on points that lived in books and syllables, yielded at once on a matter that smelt to him of accounts and contracts, for here Bernardo’s practical judgment had long been the hand that guided his.

Yet even Bernardo—who had never warmed to Tito with the easy liking others felt, and who watched the young Greek’s pliancy as a man watches oil on water—found himself answering, in spite of his distrust, to that worldly argument which is like a strong spice: success makes the most doubtful dish seem savoury. Tito was rising swiftly among those whose favour in Florence was as necessary as salt. He had grown especially welcome to the young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, who had spoken of taking him into his learned retinue on an approaching journey to Rome. And at the gay suppers in the Via Larga, amid the clink of cups and the soft chorus of laughter that ran like a ripple over the talk, the bright Greek—always ready of tongue, never quarrelsome—was more and more desired. He could admire the incomparable skill of Piero de’ Medici at those Florentine games where he himself made no pretence to excel; his praise fell with the exact lightness of grated cheese, neither too much nor too little, and somehow made every host feel his own entertainment the more delectable for having called it forth.

So it came about, by an unfailing sequence, that Tito’s reputation as an agreeable companion in “magnificent” society cast a richer glaze on his learning and talent. He was accomplished enough that the estimate which grew around him did not crack when tested. And Bernardo’s old attachments—those strong Florentine fibres that bind a man to names and houses as vines bind to a trellis—began to argue down his newer, feebler prejudice against this supple stranger. To an old Florentine it was impossible to despise the recommendation of standing well with the best families; and as Tito became thoroughly received into that circle whose views were the unquestioned standard of social value, it seemed irrational to keep a check upon satisfaction in him as Bardo’s son-in-law and Romola’s husband.

It was undeniable that Tito’s coming had been like the lighting of a lamp in a room long shut from the sun. For Bardo and his daughter, the first promise of it had even been surpassed. The blind old scholar—whose proud truthfulness would never enter into that trade of feigned, preposterous admiration, and then the answering measurelessness of vituperation, which made the warp and woof of learned intercourse—had fallen into neglect among his fellow-citizens. When his name was spoken at all, men said, with the easy cruelty of those who do not hear their own harshness, that though his blindness and the loss of his son were pitiable, he was tiresome in contending for the value of his labours; and that his discontent sat oddly on one who had been openly regardless of religious rites, and who in past days had refused offers made on the slight condition that he would take orders—without which patrons could not readily provide for every scholar.

But since Tito’s coming, Bardo’s name no longer suggested only that stale monotony of complaint. It was understood that the old man had left off his plaints, and that the fair daughter was not to be shut up in dowerless pride, waiting for a _parentado_. The winning manners and growing favour of the handsome Greek, who was expected to enter into the double relation of son and husband, made the new interest thoroughly friendly; and it was no longer rare for a visitor to enliven the quiet library.

Then the house began to have its own sound again: not the empty hush that presses on the ears and makes the heart listen too much to itself, but the wholesome acoustic life of footsteps on stone, of a cloak brushed back, of voices lowered instinctively under a roof of books. Elderly men came from that half-conscious prompting to renew former intercourse which arises when an old acquaintance begins to be newly talked about. Young men, whom Tito had once asked leave to bring, found it easy to go again when they overtook him on his way to the Via de’ Bardi; and resting their hands on his shoulder, fell into easy chat, their speech mingling with the faint crackle of parchment turned and the far-off call from the street that rose and fell like wind in a narrow lane.

For it was pleasant to look at Romola’s beauty; to see her, like old Firenzuola’s type of womanly majesty, “sitting with a certain grandeur, speaking with gravity, smiling with modesty, and casting around, as it were, an odour of queenliness.” She seemed to unfold like a strong white lily under this genial breath of admiration and homage. In those days the house itself had a gentler atmosphere, as if some careful hand had set fresh herbs to sweeten it; and the change was all one to her with the new bright life in Tito’s love. She took in the sounds and looks of friendliness as one takes in the scent of warm bread—without analysing, only feeling that it nourished.

Tito had even strengthened, in Bardo’s mind, the old hope he hugged like a last jar of precious honey: that before he died he might see his library secured—never merged in another collection, never transferred to a body of monks and called by the name of a monastery, but remaining for ever the Bardi Library, for the use of Florentines. The old habit of trusting in the Medici could not die out while their influence was still the strongest lever in the State; and Tito, once he had the ear of Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, might do more even than Messer Bernardo toward winning the desired result, for he could speak to a learned audience of the peculiar value of Bardo’s collection with that quick, persuasive exactness which made knowledge seem like a dish brought steaming to the table rather than a thing stored cold on shelves.

Tito himself talked sanguinely of it, willing to cheer the old man. And he was conscious—though he did not say so—that Romola repaid those gentle words to her father with a kind of adoration which no direct tribute to herself could have won. Here was her deepest appetite: not for praise, but for the comfort that fed her father’s spirit. When Tito touched that, he touched her.

As Christmas went by and the prospect of the marriage drew nearer, the question of the library came up more than once between Bernardo del Nero and Romola—always out of Bardo’s hearing. For Bardo nursed a vague belief, which they dared not disturb, that his property, apart from the library, was adequate to meet all demands. He would not even—except under some momentary pressure of angry despondency—admit to himself that the will by which he had disinherited Dino would leave Romola the heir of nothing but debts; or that he needed anything from patronage beyond the security of a separate locality assigned to his books, in return for a deed of gift by which he made them over to the Florentine Republic.

“My opinion is,” said Bernardo to Romola, in a consultation they had under the loggia, where the air carried every word with a clear, measured resonance, “that since you are to be married, and Messer Tito will have a competent income, we should begin to wind up the affairs, and ascertain exactly the sum that would be necessary to save the library from being touched, instead of letting the debts accumulate any longer. Your father needs nothing but his shred of mutton and his macaroni every day, and I think Messer Tito may engage to supply that for the years that remain; he can let it be in place of the _morgen-cap_.”

“Tito has always known that my life is bound up with my father’s,” Romola answered. “And he is better to my father than I am: he delights in making him happy.”

“Ah, he’s not made of the same clay as other men, is he?” said Bernardo, smiling.

Romola’s eyes had that happy brightness which made even grave speech seem like music. “Thy father has thought of shutting woman’s folly out of thee by cramming thee with Greek and Latin; but thou hast been as ready to believe in the first pair of bright eyes and the first soft words that have come within reach of thee, as if thou couldst say nothing by heart but Paternosters, like other Christian men’s daughters.”

“Now, godfather,” said Romola, shaking her head with playful protest, “as if it were only bright eyes and soft words that made me love Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito too because he is so good. I see it, I feel it, in everything he says and does. And if he is handsome, too, why should I not love him the better for that? It seems to me beauty is part of the finished language by which goodness speaks.” She looked up with one of her loving smiles at the stately old man. “You know you must have been a very handsome youth, godfather—you were about as tall as Tito, and you had very fine eyes; only you looked a little sterner and prouder, and—”

“And Romola likes to have all the pride to herself?” said Bernardo, not inaccessible to such coaxing.

Then, with the same return to what was solid and measurable that always marked him, he added, “However, it is well that in one way Tito’s demands are more modest than those of any Florentine husband of fitting rank that we should have been likely to find for you; he wants no dowry.”

So the matter was settled in that way between Messer Bernardo del Nero, Romola, and Tito. Bardo assented with a wave of the hand when Bernardo told him it would be well now to begin to sell property and clear off debts. He had long been accustomed to think of debts and property as a sort of thick wood his imagination never penetrated, still less got beyond; he could hear the talk of it as one hears distant chopping, without seeing the tree that falls.

And Tito set himself to win Messer Bernardo’s respect by inquiring, with his ready faculty, into Florentine money-matters: the secrets of the _Monti_ or public funds, the values of real property, the profits of banking. He made figures sound friendly, turning them over with the same deftness with which a practised hand turns thin slices on a hot pan—quick, sure, never letting anything burn.

“You will soon forget that Tito is not a Florentine, godfather,” said Romola. “See how he is learning everything about Florence.”

“It seems to me he is one of the _demoni_, who are of no particular country, child,” said Bernardo, smiling. “His mind is a little too nimble to be weighted with all the stuff we men carry about in our hearts.”

Romola smiled too, in happy confidence, hearing in Bernardo’s tone no warning that could pierce her. The house, with its renewed voices and softened silences, seemed to her a place where every sound promised continuance—where even the oldest hopes might yet be kept warm, like a pot set at the side of the fire until Easter.
