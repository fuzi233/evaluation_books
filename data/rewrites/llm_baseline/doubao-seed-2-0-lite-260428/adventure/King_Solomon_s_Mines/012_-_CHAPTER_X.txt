The moment we stepped across the threshold of our hut, I waved Infadoos inside to join us. The distant wail of a Kukuana village woman drifted through the thatch gaps, sharp with quiet grief.
“Infadoos,” I said, “we have words to say to you.”
“My lords may speak.”
“It seems clear to us that Twala the king is a monster of cruelty.”
“Ay, that is the truth,” the old warrior groaned, his shoulders slumping. “The whole land cries out under his yoke. Tonight you will see the worst of it: the annual great witch-hunt. Scores of men will be ‘smelt out’ as sorcerers before the moon dips low, and slaughtered. No one’s life is safe. If Twala covets your cattle, your wife, or fears you might stir a rebellion, Gagool — the haggard old witch you met at the palace — or one of her trained witch-doctors will mark you for death, and no plea will save you. I could be next. So far my skill in battle and the loyalty of the troops have spared me, but I never know if I’ll wake at dawn to find a spear at my throat. The whole nation groans under his red, blood-soaked rule.”
“Why don’t the people overthrow him?” Sir Henry cut in.
“It is not so simple, my lords. Twala is the anointed king, and if he dies, Scragga his brutal, blood-mad son will take the throne. Scragga’s heart is blacker than his father’s. His yoke would be heavier still. Things might have been different if King Imotu had never been murdered, or if his son Ignosi had lived. But both are long dead.”
“How do you know Ignosi is dead?”
The voice came from behind us, and we all spun around in shock. It was Umbopa, our quiet, sharp-eyed attendant, leaning against the hut wall, his face set like flint.
“Boy, what do you mean by speaking out of turn?” Infadoos snapped.
“Listen, uncle, and I will tell you a story,” Umbopa said, stepping forward. “Years ago King Imotu was killed, and his pregnant wife fled the realm with their infant son Ignosi. Is that not the tale you all tell?”
“It is.”
“Everyone said they died in the ice and snow of the mountains, yes?”
“Ay, that is what was believed.”
“They did not die. They crossed the peaks, and wandering desert nomads led them across the burning sands beyond, until they reached grass and water once more. They traveled for months, until they found their way to the land of the Amazulu — our own distant kin, who live by their spears. They settled there, and the boy grew up, until his mother died of old age. Then Ignosi wandered further, all the way to the lands of white men, where he learned their ways, their wisdom, and their trade of war. He worked as a servant and a soldier for years, all the while holding tight to the stories his mother told him of his birthright, waiting for his chance to come home. That chance came when he met three white men who were heading into the unknown lands of the Kukuanas, searching for a lost relative. He joined them. They crossed the desert, they climbed the snow peaks, they found their way here — and they found you, Infadoos.”
Infadoos stared at him, thunderstruck. “You are mad to talk such nonsense.”
“Am I?” Umbopa smiled, and in one smooth motion he slipped off his moocha, the leather girdle around his waist, and stood bare-chested before us. “Look. What do you see here?”
He pointed to a vivid blue tattoo coiled around his midriff: a great serpent, its tail disappearing into its own open mouth, the exact royal mark of the Kukuana line, no one but the rightful heir could bear it.
Infadoos’s eyes bulged so far I thought they would pop right out of his head. He crashed to his knees on the dirt floor.
“Koom! Koom!” he gasped. “It is my brother’s son! It is the true king!”
“Rise, uncle,” Ignosi said, for that was his name at last, not Umbopa. “I am not king yet, not while Twala sits on my father’s throne. But with your help, and the help of these white men who have stood by me, I will take what is mine. Now choose. Will you swear fealty to me, share the dangers ahead, help me overthrow this tyrant who killed my father and drove my mother out? Or will you side with Twala? Choose.”
The old soldier paused only a moment. He stood, stepped forward, knelt again, and took Ignosi’s hand in his. “I dandled you on my knees when you were a babe in arms. I am your man until death. My old arm will strike for you, and for the freedom of this land.”
“Good,” Ignosi said. “If we win, you will be second only to me on the throne. If we fail, we all die, and death is already close on your heels anyway.” He turned to the rest of us. “And you, my white friends? What will you have from me? I promise you all the white diamonds you can carry out of these mines, if you will help me. Is that enough?”
I translated for the others. Sir Henry shook his head. “Tell him he does not understand English gentlemen. We do not sell our loyalty for gold, but I have liked this man Umbopa since the day we met. I will stand with him, to bring that brute Twala down. Good? Quatermain?”
“Well,” Good grinned, adjusting his monocle, “speaking as a man who enjoys a good scrap more than most, you can tell him I’m all in. My one only condition: he lets me keep my trousers on during the battles. None of this moocha nonsense.”
I passed that on, and Ignosi laughed, then turned to me. “And you, Macumazahn, the old hunter cleverer than a wounded buffalo? Are you with me too?”
I scratched my head. “I don’t like revolutions, lad. I’m a man of peace, and a bit of a coward if I’m honest.” He smiled at that. “But I don’t abandon my friends. You stuck by us through the desert and the mountains, so I’ll stick by you. And I’ll happily take you up on that diamond offer, seeing as I’m a trader who has to make his living. One other thing, though: we came here to find Incubu’s lost brother. You have to help us look for him.”
“I swear it,” Ignosi said, then turned to Infadoos. “Tell me true, by the sign of the snake on my waist: has any white man ever set foot in this land before us?”
“Never, my king,” Infadoos said.
Sir Henry sighed heavily when the news was translated. “Then he never made it this far. Poor fellow. God’s will be done, I suppose.”
“Enough of sad what-ifs,” I cut in, eager to get back to business. “Right now you’re the rightful heir on paper, Ignosi. How do you plan to actually get that crown off Twala’s head?”
“Infadoos, you know the land best. What’s the play?”
“Tonight is the witch-hunt,” Infadoos said. “Dozens will be murdered before dawn, and every chief in the ranks will be seething with rage at Twala’s cruelty. Once the ceremony ends, I’ll speak to the loyal chiefs, show them your royal snake mark, and rally their regiments. By sunrise tomorrow, you will have twenty thousand spears at your back. Now I must go and make ready. If we are all alive after the hunt ends, I will meet you back here, and we will make our final plans. There will be war, no way around it.”
A loud shout from outside the hut cut him off: messengers from the king had arrived. We went to the doorway, and three Kukuana warriors stepped in, each carrying a shining, perfectly woven shirt of chainmail, and a sharp, heavy battle-axe.
“Gifts from King Twala to the white lords from the Stars!” their herald called.
We thanked the king and sent the messengers away, then crowded around to examine the armour. It was the finest chainwork any of us had ever seen — the whole coat folded up small enough to fit in two cupped hands, not a single gap between the links.
“Did your people make these, Infadoos?” I asked.
“Nay, my lord. They are heirlooms from our ancient ancestors, no one knows who forged them. There are barely a handful left. They are magic: no spear, no blade can pierce them. Twala only gives these to people he fears very much, or wants very much to please. You must wear them tonight, under your clothes, for the witch-hunt.”
The rest of the day we spent resting, going over our plans until the sun dipped below the horizon. Thousand of watchfires flared across the valley, and we heard the thunder of thousands of marching feet and clashing spears as the regiments filed to their positions for the great ceremony. The full moon rose over the peaks, silvering the tops of ten thousand raised spears, and Infadoos arrived in full war dress, with an escort of twenty guards to lead us to the kraal. We’d already pulled on the chainmail under our ordinary clothes, and found it light, far more comfortable than we expected — it hung a little loose on me and Good, but it fit Sir Henry’s broad, tall frame like a custom-made glove. We strapped our revolvers around our waists, hefted the battleaxes the king had gifted us, and set off.
The great central kraal was packed tight with twenty thousand armed warriors, arranged in perfect regiments, rows of glinting spears catching the moonlight, plumes waving over their dark faces, shields painted in every shade of ochre and black. The silence was so heavy it pressed on my eardrums.
“Is this your whole army?” I asked Infadoos.
“This is only a third,” he said grimly. “Another third is camped outside the gates in case the crowd riots during the killings, ten thousand more man the outposts around Loo, and the rest are patrolling the outer villages. This is a great nation.”
“They’re so quiet,” Good muttered, fidgeting.
“They know Death is hovering over the crowd,” Infadoos said. “Many will not leave this kraal alive tonight.”
We reached the line of stools set in the center of the open circle, just as Twala arrived with his retinue. The one-eyed tyrant limped at the head of his party, Gagool the haggard old witch hobbling at his heels, and a dozen giant executioners behind him, each armed with a broad-bladed spear and a heavy wooden club stained black with old dried blood.
“Greetings, white lords!” Twala boomed as he took his central stool. “Be seated. The night is far too short for the work we have to do. Look around you — have the Stars ever shown you a sight as grand as this? All these wretches shake in their skins, knowing their evil thoughts against the king will be found out.”
“Begin! Begin!” Gagool piped in that thin, piercing voice of hers. “The hyenas are hungry, they howl for food. Begin!”
For a heartbeat the whole kraal was dead silent. Then Twala lifted his spear, and twenty thousand feet stamped down on the dirt at the exact same second, three times in a row, so hard the ground shook under our feet. A lone wailing voice started up the song from the far edge of the circle:
“What is the lot of man born of woman?”
Twenty thousand voices roared back in unison, so loud it echoed off the mountain peaks:
The song rolled on, swelling from a soft lament to a roaring war chant, then fading to a blood-curdling dirge that hung in the moonlit air. Then the witch-doctors came bounding out of the ranks, ten haggard old women, their hair strung with dried fish bladders, their faces painted in streaks of white and yellow, snakeskins draped over their shoulders, human bone circlets rattling around their waists, each clutching a forked wooden wand.
“Mother! Old mother Gagool, we are here!” the lead one shrieked, pointing her wand at the old witch hunched at Twala’s feet.
“Good, good, good!” Gagool cackled. “Are your eyes sharp enough to see evil hidden in men’s hearts?”
“Sharp, mother!”
“Good, good, good! Can you smell the blood of traitors, can you purge this land of all who would harm the king?”
“We can!”
“Then go!” Gagool yelled, waving her gnarled hand at the executioners. “The slayers have sharpened their spears! The white men from far away are waiting to watch! Go!”
The ten witch-doctors scattered into the crowd like mad things, bones rattling at their waists. We fixed our eyes on the closest one, who spun in wild, dizzying circles, foam flying from her lips, screaming that she could smell the evil wizard, she could hear the traitor’s hidden thoughts. Suddenly she froze, stiff as a hunting dog that has scented game, and crept forward. The warriors in front of her shrank back, pale. Then she sprang, and tapped a tall, broad-shouldered warrior on the chest with her wand.
The two men on either side of him seized his arms instantly. He didn’t even struggle — his spear fell out of his limp hand, his legs dragged as if they were made of lead. The executioners stepped forward.
“Kill!” Twala rumbled.
“Kill!” Gagool squeaked.
“Kill!” Scragga cackled, hollow and cruel.
The deed was done before the words finished leaving their mouths: one man drove his spear through the victim’s heart, the other dashed his brains out with his club.
“One,” Twala counted, like he was tallying heads of cattle. The body was dragged a few paces back, dumped in a growing row of corpses.
It went on like that, hour after hour. Rank after rank of men were dragged up, marked for death over no crime at all, until a hundred bodies stretched in neat lines behind our stools. I’ve read about the gladiator fights in ancient Rome, and Spanish bullfights, but neither could ever be half so cruel. At least those were entertainment. Here, every man in that crowd knew he could be the next one dragged to the executioners. We tried to protest once, but Twala snarled that the law of the land must run its course, and we shut up, realizing we would get ourselves killed if we pushed him too far.
Then there was a lull. The witch-doctors gathered, breathless, and we thought the horror was over. But Gagool herself staggered to her feet, propped on her stick, and hobbled out into the open circle. The bent, vulture-like old crone wove through the ranks, chanting under her breath, until she tapped a tall, noble-looking man wearing a leopard skin cloak, a cousin of Twala’s we learned later. The whole regiment the man commanded groaned out loud, but two officers seized him anyway, dragged him up, and he was slain.
“One hundred and three,” Twala counted.
Gagool kept moving, dancing closer and closer to our little group, her eyes glowing like embers.
“Hang me if that old hag isn’t coming for us,” Good whispered, horrified. My own heart sank into my boots, staring at the rows of dead bodies behind us.
Nearer she came, and nearer, every soul in that twenty thousand strong crowd holding their breath to see who she would pick next. Then she stopped dead, pointed straight at Ignosi, and sprang. She tapped him hard on the shoulder.
“I smell him out!” she shrieked. “Kill him! This stranger is full of black evil! Slay him before he casts his curse on the king!”
I jumped to my feet before the executioners could move. “O king!” I roared. “This man is the servant of your white guests. Our dog’s blood is our blood. By the sacred law of hospitality, I claim his protection!”
“Gagool has marked him for death,” Twala snarled. “He must die.”
“Nay,” I said, “he will not die. Any man who lays a hand on him will die first.”
“Seize him!” Twala roared at the dozen executioners, their hands still red with the blood of their victims.
They started forward, then hesitated. I had my revolver out, the barrel pressed dead straight to Twala’s broad chest. Sir Henry had his pistol aimed square at the lead executioner. Good took slow, careful aim straight at Gagool’s shriveled old head.
Twala flinched, I saw him.
“Put down those magic tubes of yours,” he spat. “I spare him. Not because I fear you — out of respect for the law of hospitality. Go in peace. The hunt is over.”
The regiments filed out of the kraal in perfect silence, the fatigue teams staying behind to drag the rows of corpses out for the hyenas and the vultures. We turned and walked back to our hut, our chainmail hidden under our clothes, our revolvers still warm in our hands. The night’s work was done, but we all knew: the fight for the throne, and for King Solomon’s mines, was only just beginning.
