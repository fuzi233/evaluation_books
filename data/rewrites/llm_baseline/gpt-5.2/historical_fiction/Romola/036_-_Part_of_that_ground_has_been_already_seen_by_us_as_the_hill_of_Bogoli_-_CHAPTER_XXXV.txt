For several days Tito saw little of Romola, though he made it appear—by the very economy of his visits—that he was sparing her.

The morning after, he came to her with a softened voice, as one speaks in a house where someone lies feverish. He told her it would be better if she removed any small things that were peculiarly her own from the library, since agents would soon be coming to pack the antiquities. He did not name the sale again; he let it stand like a dish already carried from the table, leaving only its scent to accuse the appetite that had been mocked.

Romola sat in her own room, near the little painted tabernacle that had always seemed to watch with fixed, tender eyes over human wavering. Tito, leaning to kiss her brow, suggested she remain there—away from the noise of strange footsteps. She assented quietly, without resistance and without consent that felt like consent. The night had been one long waking to her; and though her frame was sound and accustomed to endurance, sensation had become a dull, continuous pain—as if something within her had been struck, not once, but again and again, until the place could no longer cry out and could only ache.

Tito saw she was ill—saw it in the way her lips kept their line as if speech would tear them, in the way her gaze had withdrawn, leaving the eyes open but not receiving him. Yet he dared say no more. He only dared, perceiving her hand and brow were stone-cold, to fetch a furred mantle and lay it lightly about her shoulders, as one wraps a child who has forgotten to shiver.

And every brief interval that he returned to her the scene was nearly the same. He offered a small service, a mild word, the measured touch of tenderness that asks no questions. She seemed to have lost the power of speaking to him, or of looking at him. If she answered at all, it was with that obedience that is not submission but absence.

“Patience!” he said to himself, in the corridor where the house-sounds—the creak of boards, the faint clink of a distant vessel, the stir of servants—came and went like indifferent breaths. “She will recover it, and forgive at last. The tie to me must still remain the strongest.”

When a stricken person is slow to lift her eyes and behave as if nothing had happened, the striker glides easily into the posture of the aggrieved; he feels no bruise himself, and the sweetness of his own recent conduct is ever present to him, like a draught he has just tasted and can still savour. Tito, indeed, was not made for resentment. The constant bent of his mind was toward propitiation. He would have borne much for the sake of feeling Romola’s hand rest on his head again as it had rested on that morning when he first shrank from looking at her—when he had felt, like a man who has dropped a precious cup, the instant before the crash.

Yet waiting was not hard, because his life out of doors was growing more and more engaging to him. Often what is in strictness the slow outgrowth of the whole character may still be traced back to one impression, a single taste that lingers and begins to govern the appetite. Since that moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when he, mounted on the bales, had felt a keen pleasure in the consciousness that he could tickle the ears of men with any phrase that pleased them, his imagination had kept glancing toward political activity—toward that kind of public life which troubled Florence, like a pot forever set to boil, was always ready to demand.

Only the fresh dread of Baldassarre—waked in the same moment—had lain across that path like a huge rock in a narrow street, forcing him into the sale of the library as a preparation for possible flight, at the very instant when Florence had begun to taste new to him. That dread was nearly removed now. He must still wear his armour; he must keep ready the coolness and ingenuity by which a man turns danger into a game. But he no longer felt compelled to leave Florence and seek fortune elsewhere. His father had refused the offered atonement—had forced him into defiance; and an old man in a strange place, with his memory gone, was weak enough to be defied.

Tito’s implicit desires were shaping themselves into explicit thought. As the freshness of young passion faded, life took on for him more and more the look of play—an agreeable mingling of skill and chance, where the winnings are sweeter because one pretends they are not one’s very life. And the game in Florence promised to be rapid and exciting: revolutionary shifts, party struggle, the restless sound of crowds and councils. It was a game sure to include much of that unavowed action in which brilliant ingenuity—able to rid itself of inconvenient beliefs while keeping the one certainty that “ginger is hot in the mouth”—sees the path of superior wisdom.

No sooner were the French guests gone than Florence was agitated like a colony of ants when a sudden shadow has passed and the whole shaken order must be repaired. In the streets, in shops, at doorways where women bargained for bread and greens, in the little wine-shops where men leaned close over their cups, the same questions rose and fell, threaded through daily need: How were they to raise the money for the French king? How were they to manage war with those obstinate Pisan rebels? Above all, how were they to mend their plan of government, so as to find the best way of choosing magistrates and voting laws?

Until those questions were answered, trade would falter—flour would not grind into bread without fear, silk would not pass from loom to market without hesitation. And that large body of working men, not counted as citizens, with not so much as a vote to serve as an anodyne to their stomachs, were likely to grow impatient. Hunger hears and remembers; it is more acute than any clerk of records. Something must be done.

First, the great bell was sounded to call the citizens to a parliament in the Piazza de’ Signori. Its voice rolled across the stone like a command the city could not refuse. The sound gathered men as scent gathers bees—drawing them out of workshops and counting-houses, out of arcades, out of doorways where the day’s simple food had been set down unfinished. Voices multiplied beneath it; boots struck the pavement; the air filled with that particular Florentine mixture of argument and mirth that can, in an instant, harden into menace.

When the crowd was wedged close, and hemmed in by armed men at all the outlets, the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight Priors for the time being—came out and stood by the stone lion on the platform in front of the Old Palace. They proposed that twenty chief men of the city should be given dictatorial authority, by which for one year they might choose all magistrates and set the frame of government in order. And the people shouted assent, feeling themselves, for that moment, the electors of the Twenty.

It was a very old Florentine fashion, this “parliament,” by which the will of the few was made to seem the choice of the many. The shouting in the Piazza soon ended; but not the debating inside the palace. There the talk went on day after day and far into the night, when lamps made wavering islands of light and the air grew thick with human breath and irritation.

Was Florence to have a Great Council after the Venetian mode—where officers might be elected and laws voted by a wide number of citizens of a certain age and ascertained qualifications, without question of rank or party? Or was it to be governed on a narrower, less popular scheme, where the hereditary influence of good families would be less diluted by the votes of shopkeepers?

Doctors of law disputed, each argument served with care, like dishes set forth in splendid order: each had its own seasoning of precedent, its garnish of Latin, its aroma of public virtue. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini alleged excellent reasons for the popular scheme; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci alleged reasons equally excellent for a more aristocratic form. It was a question of boiled or roast—prejudged by the palates of the disputants—and the excellent arguing might have been prolonged a long while with no other result than deferring the cooking.

The majority inside the palace, power already in their hands, agreed with Vespucci and thought change should be moderate. The majority outside, conscious of little power and many grievances, feared change less. And outside the palace another force was steadily giving the vague desires of that majority the shape of a determinate will.

That force was the preaching of Savonarola.

Impulsed partly by the spiritual necessity laid upon him to guide the people, and partly by the prompting of public men who could get no measures carried without his aid, he was passing rapidly in his daily sermons from the general to the special—from telling his hearers they must postpone private passions and interests to the public good, to telling them precisely what sort of government they must have in order to promote that good—from “Choose whatever is best for all” to “Choose the Great Council,” and “the Great Council is the will of God.”

To Savonarola these were as good as identical propositions. The Great Council was, for him, the only practicable plan for giving expression to a public will broad enough to counteract the vitiating influence of party interest. It was a plan that would make honest, impartial public action at least possible. And the purer the government of Florence could become—the more secure from the designs of men who saw their own advantage in the moral debasement of their fellows—the nearer would the Florentine people approach the character of a pure community, worthy to lead the way in the renovation of the Church and the world.

Fra Girolamo’s mind never stopped short of that sublime end. The objects toward which he felt himself working always kept the same moral magnificence; he had no petty malice, no private grudges to gratify. Even in the last terrible days, when ignominy and torture—and the fear of torture—laid bare every hidden weakness, he could say to his importunate judges: “Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great.”
