I’ve been a shameless sucker for a good meal and a decent drink my whole life, even back when I was small — and in those early days, it was 90% eating, let’s be real. I had an appetite that could outrun a farmhand, and a digestion so ironclad I could chomp through a bag of unripe green gooseberries before breakfast and not even burp once. I still remember one pasty, dead-eyed gent coming over to dine at our house once. He sat watching me shovel food into my mouth for a full five minutes, looking like he’d just stumbled on a unicorn in the dining room, then turned to my dad and went, “Does your boy ever get dyspepsia?”
“Never heard him complain of anything of the sort,” Dad said, then nudged me. “Colly Wobbles, do you ever get dyspepsia?” (That stupid nickname stuck to me for years, for no reason I can fathom. It’s not even close to my real name.)
“Nope, Pa,” I mumbled around a mouthful of mashed potato, then added, “What even is dyspepsia, anyway?”
That pale dyspeptic looked at me like I was some kind of miracle he’d never thought possible, mixed with the bitter envy of a man who hasn’t tasted a good pork chop without paying for it with three hours of stomach ache in ten years. He sighed slow as molasses and said, quiet as a funeral parlor attendant, “You’ll find out someday, kiddo.”
My sweet old mum used to say seeing me eat made her happy, and that’s one of my favorite old memories now — I must have given her a whole lot of joy on that front, because I ate enough to feed three scrawny toddlers back then. A healthy growing lad who runs around all day and makes very, very sure he never overdoes the studying? He’ll easily out-eat every expectation you could possibly have for his feeding capacity.
It’s uproariously funny watching teen boys chow down, as long as you’re not the one footing the bill. Their idea of a normal “light” meal is a pound and a half of roast beef, six mealy big potatoes (the soggy ones, obviously, they fill you up more), heaps of greens, four thick slabs of Yorkshire pudding, two currant dumplings, a handful of sour green apples, a penny’s worth of walnuts, six jam biscuits, and a whole bottle of ginger beer. And after that? They run off to play at horses like they didn’t just pack away half a larder. They must look at us grown men like we’re absolute weaklings, who need to slump on the couch for two hours after a meal that’s just a spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a single chicken.
But boys don’t have all the wins, not really. A boy never gets the pure luxury of being properly, blissfully full. He can never flop back, stretch his legs, tuck his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and sink into that hazy, ethereal bliss that wraps around a man who’s just finished a perfect dinner. A meal doesn’t change a boy’s whole world. For a grown man? A good dinner is magic potion. Suddenly the whole world looks brighter, kinder, softer. A man who’s eaten to his heart’s content feels warm, fuzzy love for every single person he passes. He pets the cat so gently he calls it “poor little puss” in this wobbly, tender voice. He pauses when he hears the German buskers outside, and worries if their fingers are cold in the wind. For ten whole minutes, he doesn’t even hate his wife’s side of the family.
A good meal pulls out every soft part of you. Even the grumpy, sour-faced old curmudgeon who spends all day looking like he survives on vinegar and laxatives will crack a smile after dinner, start patting small kids on the head, and mumble vaguely about giving them a sixpence sometime. Serious stiff types thaw out and get all mild and jolly, even those snobby young guys with the big fuzzy mustaches who usually spend all day being insufferable, they forget to be rude for an hour.
I get all sentimental myself after dinner, it’s the only time I can stand sappy love stories. When the hero clasps the heroine to his chest for that last dramatic embrace and stifles a sob, I get so sad I feel like I got dealt nothing but low cards at whist all night. When the heroine dies at the end? I full-on weep. If I read that exact same story first thing in the morning, I’d snort and throw it across the room. Digestion — or more accurately, bad digestion — does wild things to your heart. If I ever want to write something super tragic, I eat a giant plate of hot buttered muffins an hour before I sit down at the desk. By the time I pick up my pen, I’m drowning in unutterable melancholy. I can picture heartbroken lovers parting forever at a country stile, twilight sinking all around them, the only sound a distant sheep bell tinkling through the sad silence. Old men sit staring at dried, wilted flowers till their eyes blur over with tears. Dainty young maidens wait at their open windows for their sweethearts, but “he never comes,” their sunny blonde hair fades thin and white, the years roll past. The babies they once bounced on their knees are now grown up with their own headaches, the friends they laughed with are buried under the waving grass. But they still wait, till the dark night creeps up and the whole sad old world fades away from their sore eyes. I see pale corpses bobbing on white foam, deathbeds streaked with tears, graves out in the middle of empty deserts. I hear women wailing, little kids whimpering, strong men sobbing dryly. It’s all the muffins. You could never get that much tragic fancy going on a mutton chop and a glass of champagne.
A full stomach is poetry fuel, plain and simple. No sentiment in the world can survive on an empty belly. We don’t have the time or energy to mope over made-up woes till we’ve fixed our real problems first. You’re not going to sigh over your dead pet canary if the bailiff is standing in your front parlor. When you have no clue where your next shilling is coming from, you’re not going to lie awake worrying if your girlfriend’s smiles are cold, hot, or lukewarm, or anything else about her.
Folks will tell you mental hurt is way worse than physical pain — the kind of folks I call fools, who happen to have opinions different from mine. If there’s one type of person I can’t stand, it’s the guy who doesn’t agree with me on every single topic. That romantic tripe about heartbreak being worse than a sore body? It’s so comforting to the lovesick young idiot who looks down at some poor starving guy and thinks “wow, you’re so lucky compared to me,” and so soothing to fat rich old gents who cackle about how poverty is so much better than being wealthy. But it’s all nonsense, all hot air. A splitting headache will make you forget a broken heart in ten seconds. A cut finger will wipe every memory of the person who left you out of your brain. When you’re actually, properly hungry? You don’t feel a single other thing.
Us well-fed soft city folks have no idea what real hunger is. We know what it’s like to have no appetite, turn down fancy food laid out in front of us, but we don’t know what it’s like to crave food so bad it hurts — to be dying for a crust of bread while other people throw loaves away, to stare through a grimy window at a plate of steaming cheap pea pudding, your mouth watering, but you don’t even have a penny to buy it. Hunger is just a fancy fun sauce for us, the thing that makes a meal taste better. It’s worth getting ravenously hungry just to see how much pure joy you can get out of a simple meal. If you want to enjoy your dinner more than you ever have, go on a thirty mile walk after breakfast and don’t eat a single thing till you get home. Your eyes will light up the second you see that white tablecloth and the steaming dishes! You’ll put down your empty mug of beer with a happy sigh, grab your knife and fork, and then lean back after, light a cigar, and beam at everyone in the room like you’re the luckiest man alive.
Just make very sure that amazing dinner is actually there waiting for you, though, or the disappointment will break your heart a little. I remember once my old pal Joe and I — dear old Joe, man. It’s been eight years since I saw Joseph Taboys last, how weird we all drift apart in life. I’d love to see his big jovial face again, shake his strong hand, hear that loud cheery laugh of his. Also, he still owes me 14 shillings. Anyway, we were on holiday, got up super early for breakfast, and headed out for a massive long walk. We’d ordered a duck for dinner the night before, told the landlady “get a big one, we’re gonna be so hungry when we get back.” Right as we left, she ran up to us grinning, holding up a bird the size of a doormat, and said “If you two lads finish that, you’re legends.” We snickered, puffed out our chests like we were world champion eaters, and promised we’d give it our best shot.
Then we immediately got lost. That’s what I do in the country, every single time. It’s infuriating, because asking a local for directions is useless, you might as well ask a chambermaid how to run a bank. You have to yell your question three times before it even penetrates their thick skull. The third time, they slowly lift their head, stare at you blankly. You yell it a fourth time, they parrot it back to you, then stand there pondering for two whole minutes, speaking three words a minute, going “Well, you couldn’t do better than —” then they spot another dumb yokel walking down the road, yell the question at him, and the two of them argue for 15 minutes before they tell you to go down the lane, turn right, cross the third stile, keep left past Jimmy Milcher’s cowshed, cut across the seven-acre field, go through the gate by Squire Grubbin’s haystack, follow the bridle path till you’re opposite the old windmill — which isn’t there anymore, obviously — then turn right, leave Stiggin’s plantation behind you. You thank them, walk away with a throbbing headache, and have no clue where you’re going, the only thing you’re sure of is that somewhere there’s a stile you need to climb. Then you turn the next corner, and find four stiles, all leading in completely different directions.
We went through that whole mess three separate times. We tramped across fields, waded through brooks, scrambled over hedges and stone walls. We had a full blown fight about who was the idiot who’d gotten us lost in the first place. We were grumpy, footsore, exhausted. But the thought of that duck kept us going. It was like a fairy tale vision floating in front of our eyes, pulling us forward. We talked about it the whole time, cheered each other up going “come on, the duck’s gonna get cold.” We passed a village inn halfway, got so tempted to go in and get a loaf of bread and a block of cheese, but we were heroes. We decided to hold out, because the duck would taste even better if we were starving. We thought we smelled it the second we walked back into town, and did the last quarter mile in three full minutes flat.
We ran upstairs, washed up, changed our clothes, zoomed back down, pulled our chairs up to the table, rubbed our hands together while the landlady took the lids off the dishes. I grabbed the knife and fork and went to carve the duck.
It did not want to be carved. I hacked at it for five whole minutes, didn’t make a single dent. Joe, who was already shoveling potatoes into his mouth, went “Uh, maybe someone who actually knows how to carve should do that?” I ignored his stupid comment, attacked the duck again so hard the whole bird slid off the plate and landed in the fender. We fished it out, of course, and I was about to try again, but Joe was getting mad. He said if he’d known we were gonna play blind hockey with our dinner he would’ve stopped for bread and cheese back in the village. I was too tired to argue. I put the knife and fork down all dignified, sat to the side, and Joe stepped up to fight the wretched thing. He worked in silence for a while, then muttered “Damn the duck” and took his coat off.
We did finally break it apart, with a chisel we found in the kitchen, but it was completely inedible. It was like trying to chew a piece of rubber. We had to eat our dinner off the vegetables and an apple tart. That duck was so tough, no one should’ve been allowed to kill it, it was older than the Queen. But there you go, no one respects old age in this country anymore.
I started this whole essay meaning to talk about eating and drinking, and I’ve only gone on and on about eating so far. Well, drinking is one of those subjects you don’t want to look like you know too much about, right? The days where going to bed blackout drunk every night was considered manly are long gone. Now if you smell like stale beer, have a blotchy red face, can barely walk straight, and sound like you gargled gravel? Everyone assumes you’re a total cad, not a gentleman.
Even so, humans have a thirst that’s straight up supernatural. We drink for every excuse under the sun. No man feels comfortable unless there’s a glass sitting in front of him. We drink before meals, with meals, after meals. We drink when we meet a friend, drink again when we say goodbye. We drink when we’re talking, when we’re reading, when we’re thinking. We drink to everyone else’s health and ruin our own. We toast the Queen, the army, the ladies, every person we can think of that we can raise a glass to. I swear if we ran out of other people to toast, we’d raise a glass to our mothers-in-law. And hey, we never eat someone’s health, always drink it. Why don’t we ever stand up and eat a whole tart to someone’s success?
I still don’t get why everyone has to drink all the time, honestly. I get drinking to drown your sorrows, that makes sense. I get why the poor folks living in damp cellars drink to get drunk — of course we all act all shocked about it, us cozy people sitting in our warm nice houses with all our little luxuries, going “how terrible that the poor people go to the pub and drink gin to forget their lives for a little while.” But before you clutch your pearls, stop and think what their lives are actually like. They drag out every year in a tiny, stinking room that’s no better than a sewer, huddled with a dozen other people. Dirty kids scream and fight, loud drunk women yell and cuff each other, the street outside is full of filth and noise, the whole building is bedlam. Their life has no joy, no softness, no light. A horse in a stall gets to eat sweet hay and munch corn in peace. A dog in a kennel gets to nap in the sun and dream about chasing rabbits. These people never get a single moment of that. No fun, no friends, no nice things. They never feel a little bit of kindness, never have a single happy hope. In the name of all that’s merciful, let them drink that gin, if it makes them feel like they’re alive for ten whole minutes.
We can spout all the sappy sentiment we want, but the stomach is the real seat of all happiness on this planet. The kitchen is our main temple, the roaring fire is our sacred flame, and the cook is our high priest. He’s a kind, powerful magician. He soothes away all your sorrows, chases all the fights out of you, makes every happy feeling twice as bright. Our God is great, and the cook is his prophet. Let’s all eat, drink, and be merry.
