In Which the Major Makes His Appearance

Major Arthur Pendennis arrived at Fairoaks in due time, though the journey there had been a trial of endurance that would make a modern commuter weep. He had spent a dreary night in a mail-coach where the atmosphere was thick with the sins of others: a burly fellow-passenger, swollen by layers of great-coats, had crowded him into a corner and kept him awake with snoring that bordered on indecency; opposite sat a widow who had sealed the windows shut, turning the vehicle into a sauna of Jamaica rum and water, which she sucked perpetually from a reticule bottle. Every moment of slumber was shattered by the twang of turnpike horns, the scuffling of his neighbor wedging him tighter, or the widow's feet brushing his tender toes. It was a life of horrors, a jolly old time of travel that has since vanished into fond memory, leaving behind only the question: how did anyone bear eight miles an hour for twenty-five hours on a hard seat with a gouty tendency and grumbling coachmen? Yet they did, and they were merry.

Among the chaos were two schoolboys heading home for midsummer holidays, sitting next to the Major's servant on the roof. At the inn at Bagshot, they had unloaded a cargo of ham, eggs, pie, pickles, tea, coffee, and boiled beef, a spectacle that left the Major, sipping feeble tea with a yellow face and bristly beard, in a state of tender dejection. He imagined Lord Steyne's dinner happening simultaneously, while the little lord beside him slept soundly through the noise, emerging at Fairoaks looking fresh as paint. The Major, meanwhile, descended with rheumatic griefs shooting through his limbs, a wig out of curl, and a yellow complexion, to be greeted reverentially by the porteress, the gardener's wife, and Mr. Morgan, his man.

Helen was waiting in the window, watching for her brother-in-law. She knew the Major did not like surprises; he required preparation. Pen, as a boy, had once incurred sad disgrace by stealing the Major's morocco box containing his back teeth, which he habitually removed during jolting coach rides. Without them, the Major refused to appear. Morgan made a mystery of the wigs, curling them in private places before introducing them to the master. The Major groaned, moaned, wheezed, and cursed Morgan through his toilet, behaving like an old buck who had been up all night with rheumatism and a long duty ahead. Finally belted, curled, and set straight, he descended into the drawing-room with a grave, majestic air, fitting for a man of business and fashion.

Pen was not there. Only Helen and little Laura were present, sewing at her knees. The Major offered her only a forefinger after saluting his sister-in-law. Laura took the finger trembling, dropped it, and fled the room. The Major did not wish to keep her; he had private reasons for disapproving of her existence in the house, reasons we shall reserve for a future occasion. Laura wandered out seeking Pen, finding him in the orchard pacing a walk in earnest conversation with Mr. Smirke. So absorbed was he that he did not hear Laura's clear voice until Smirke pulled him by the coat and pointed her out.

She ran up, seizing his hand. "Come in, Pen," she said. "There's somebody come; Uncle Arthur's come."

"He is, is he?" Pen replied, feeling her small hand. He looked at Smirke with uncommon fierceness, signaling *I am ready for him or any man*. Smirke cast up his eyes and sighed gently.

"Lead on, Laura," Pen said, with a half-fierce, half-comic air. "Lead on, and say I wait upon my uncle." He was laughing to hide a great anxiety, screwing his courage inwardly to face the ordeal he knew awaited him.

Pen had taken Smirke into his confidence over the last two days, especially after the outbreak surrounding the discovery of Doctor Portman. For forty-eight hours, he had talked nothing but Miss Fotheringay—Miss Emily Fotheringay—to his tutor. Smirke listened with ease, for he was in love himself, eager to propitiate Pen and enraptured by the goddess Emily, whose like he had never seen until now. Pen's fire, volubility, hot eloquence, and rich poetic tropes had half-convinced Smirke that the arrangement was feasible: Emily settled at Fairoaks, Captain Costigan in the yellow room for life, and Pen married at eighteen.

Indeed, the boy had almost talked his mother down too, parrying her objections with indignant good sense. He had convinced her that if the marriage was doomed in heaven, it was doomed; if the young woman was good, that was all she could ask. He dreaded the arrival of the guardian uncle, who would view the matter differently from the widow's simple, romantic, and utterly absurd perspective.

For as in the allegory of the gold and silver shield, each knight is right from his own point of view: so with marriage. Whether it is foolish or good depends on the vantage point. To the town folk, it means a snug house in Belgravia, dinner parties, a brougham for the Park, and a decent provision for the little Belgravians to come. To talk of love in lodgings is a babyish folly that cannot pay coach-hire. But to the country-bred, the book of life tells a different story. Helen, like many soft and sentimental women, had long speculated about her son's love life. It pleased her with a dismal pleasure to imagine the day she would give up everything—keys, the best bedroom, her place at the table—to see him happy. An empress was too good for him, but even humble Esther would do. However, an actress—a mature woman who had long ceased blushing except with rouge, standing under the eager glances of thousands, likely illiterate and ill-bred—was hard to accept. It was hard that such a one should be chosen, deposing the matron to give place to a Sultana.

These doubts Helen laid before Pen during the two days before the uncle's arrival. He met them with happy frankness, routing her objections with infinite satisfaction. Miss Costigan was a paragon of virtue, as sensitive as a timid maiden, as pure as unsullied snow, possessing the finest manners, graceful wit, and charming refinement. Her father was a good old gentleman of high family and fallen fortunes, accustomed to the best society in Europe. He was in no hurry and could wait till Pen was twenty-one. But Pen felt, with awful solemnity, that he was engaged in the one only passion of his life, and that DEATH alone could close it.

Helen told him, with a sad smile, that people survived these passions, citing the fatal example of Laura's poor father. Pen, however, was resolved that death must be his doom in case of disappointment. Rather than baulk him, the widow would have submitted to any sacrifice, gone on her knees, and kissed the feet of a Hottentot daughter-in-law.

Arthur knew his power over the widow. In those two days, he brought her almost into submission, patronizing her kindly. He spent one evening with the lovely pie-maker at Chatteris, bragging of his influence over his mother, and the other night composing a flaming, conceited copy of verses to his divinity, vowing like Montrose to make her famous with his sword and glorious by his pen, loving her as no mortal woman had been adored since the creation of womankind.

It was late that night, long after midnight, that wakeful Helen passed her son's door. She saw a light streaming through the chink into the dark passage and heard Pen tossing, tumbling, and mumbling verses. She waited anxiously, then turned the lock softly and entered. Pen did not see her at first; his face was turned away. Papers were scattered on his desk and bed. He was biting a pencil, thinking of rhymes and follies and passions. He was Hamlet jumping into Ophelia's grave; he was the Stranger taking Mrs. Haller to his arms, beautiful Mrs. Haller with raven ringlets. Despair, Byron, Thomas Moore, the Loves of the Angels, Waller, Herrick, Beranger—all the love songs he had ever read—were seething in his mind. He was at the height of imaginative frenzy when his mother found him.

"Arthur," said the mother's soft silver voice. He started up, turning round, clutching papers and pushing them under the pillow.

"Why don't you go to sleep, my dear?" she asked with a sweet tender smile, sitting on the bed and taking one of his hot hands.

Pen looked at her wildly. "I couldn't sleep," he said. "I—I was—I was writing." Upon this, he flung his arms around her neck and cried, "O mother! I love her, I love her!" How could such a kind soul help soothing and pitying him? The gentle creature did her best, wondering with tenderness that it was only yesterday he was a child in that bed, and how she used to say her prayers over it before he woke on holiday mornings.

The verses were grand, though Miss Fotheringay did not understand them. Old Cos, with a wink and a knowing finger on his nose, said, "Put them up with th' other letthers, Milly darling. Poldoody's pomes was nothing to this." So Milly locked up the manuscripts.

When the Major, dressed and presentable, presented himself to Mrs. Pendennis, he found within ten minutes that the poor widow was not merely distressed by the contemplated marriage, but actually more distressed by the thought that the boy himself was unhappy, fearing a violent altercation between uncle and nephew. She besought Major Pendennis to be very gentle with Arthur, hinting that he had a high spirit and would not brook unkind words. She recalled Dr. Portman's rough speech the previous night and how Pen's indignant answer had frightened her. "Recollect he is a man now," she said, laying a fair long hand on the Major's sleeve. "Be very—very cautious."

He took the hand, kissed it gallantly, and looked in her alarmed face with wonder and a scorn he was too polite to show. *Bon Dieu!* thought the old negotiator. *The boy has actually talked the woman round, and she'd get him a wife as she would a toy if Master cried for it. Why are there no such things as lettres-de-cachet—and a Bastille for young fellows of family?* The Major lived in such good company that he might be excused for feeling like an Earl. He kissed the widow's timid hand, pressed it in both his, and laid it down on the table with one of his own over it as he smiled.

"Confess," said he, "now, that you are thinking how you possibly can make it up to your conscience to let the boy have his own way."

She blushed and was moved in the usual manner of females. "I am thinking that he is very unhappy—and I am too——"

"To contradict him or to let him have his own wish?" asked the other, adding with great comfort to his inward self, "I'm d——d if he shall."

"To think that he should have formed so foolish and cruel and fatal an attachment," the widow said, "which can but end in pain whatever be the issue."

"The issue shan't be marriage, my dear sister," the Major said resolutely. "We're not going to have a Pendennis, the head of the house, marry a strolling mountebank from a booth. No, no, we won't marry into Greenwich Fair, ma'am."

"If the match is broken suddenly off," the widow interposed, "I don't know what may be the consequence. I know Arthur's ardent temper, the intensity of his affections, the agony of his pleasures and disappointments, and I tremble at this one if it must be. Indeed, indeed, it must not come on him too suddenly."

"My dear madam," the Major said with an air of deepest commiseration, "I've no doubt Arthur will have to suffer confoundedly before he gets over the little disappointment. But is he, think you, the only person who has been so rendered miserable?"

"No, indeed," said Helen, holding down her eyes. She was thinking of her own case, feeling seventeen again and most miserable.

"I, myself," whispered her brother-in-law, "have undergone a disappointment in early life. A young woman with fifteen thousand pounds, niece to an Earl—most accomplished creature—a third of her money would have run up my promotion in no time, and I should have been a lieutenant-colonel at thirty: but it might not be. I was but a penniless lieutenant: her parents interfered: and I embarked for India, where I had the honour of being secretary to Lord Buckley, when commander-in-chief—without her. What happened? We returned our letters, sent back our locks of hair (the Major here passed his fingers through his wig), we suffered—but we recovered. She is now a baronet's wife with thirteen grown-up children; altered, it is true, in person; but her daughters remind me of what she was, and the third is to be presented early next week."

Helen did not answer. She was still thinking of old times. One supposes that if one lives to be a hundred, there are certain passages of early life whereof the recollection will always carry one back to youth again, and that Helen was thinking of one of these.

"Look at my own brother, my dear creature," the Major continued gallantly. "He himself, you know, had a little disappointment when he started in the—the medical profession—an eligible opportunity presented itself. Miss Balls, I remember the name, was daughter of an apoth—a practitioner in very large practice; my brother had very nearly succeeded in his suit.—But difficulties arose: disappointments supervened, and—and I am sure he had no reason to regret the disappointment, which gave him this hand," said the Major, once more politely pressing Helen's fingers.
