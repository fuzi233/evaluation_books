"When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new,—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?"

The shock had been terrible. Margaret sank into a state of prostration that offered no outlet in sobs or tears, finding no solace in words. She lay upon the sofa, her eyes closed, silent save for a whisper when compelled to answer. Mr. Bell sat beside her, perplexed and paralyzed. He dared not leave her alone, yet he dared not urge her to return to Oxford; her physical exhaustion was absolute, rendering any journey impossible, regardless of the horrors she would inevitably face upon her return. Sitting by the fire, he considered his options while Margaret lay motionless and breathless beside him. He refused the invitation to dine downstairs with Dixon, whose hospitality, though tinged with sobbing, he declined. A plate of food was brought up to him; usually, Mr. Bell was fastidious, discerning every nuance of flavor, but now the devilled chicken tasted like sawdust. He minced some chicken for Margaret, seasoning it carefully, but when Dixon attempted to feed her, a languid shake of her head signaled that food would only choke her, offering no sustenance.

With a heavy sigh, Mr. Bell rose, his stiffened legs protesting the movement, and followed Dixon out of the room.

"I cannot leave her," he muttered, pacing. "I must write to Oxford immediately to ensure preparations are underway; they can proceed until I arrive. Can Mrs. Lennox not come? I will instruct her to do so. This girl needs a female friend nearby, if only to coax her into a proper fit of weeping."

Dixon, weeping as if for two, wiped her eyes and steadied her voice to inform Mr. Bell that Mrs. Lennox was confined and unable to travel.

"Well!" Mr. Bell sighed, rubbing his chin. "We shall have to summon Mrs. Shaw. Has she returned to England?"

"Yes, sir, she has returned, but..." Dixon hesitated, disapproving of a stranger intruding upon her mistress's domain to oversee Margaret. "...she fears leaving Mrs. Lennox at such a critical juncture."

"Critical time, be—" Mr. Bell cut himself off with a cough. "She could easily reside in Venice or Naples, or some of those Catholic locales, during the 'critical time' that occurred in Corfu. And what does that little prosperous woman's predicament amount to, compared to this poor, helpless, friendless creature lying still as a stone upon an altar-tomb? I tell you, Mrs. Shaw shall come. Ensure a room is prepared for her by tomorrow night. I will see to it personally."

True to his word, Mr. Bell penned a letter. Mrs. Shaw, reading it, shed many tears, remarking that it sounded exactly like the General when he threatened a gout attack, promising to cherish it forever. Had he merely suggested or gently urged her visit, implying refusal were an option, she likely would have remained true to her natural reserve. Instead, the sharp, ungentle command shattered her *vis inertia*, compelling her to pack her trunks with the assistance of her maid. Edith, dressed in caps and shawls, emerged from the carriage to greet her mother as Captain Lennox assisted her down.

"Do not forget, Mamma," Edith cried, clutching her mother's arm. "Margaret must come and live with us. Sholto departs for Oxford on Wednesday; you must send word via Mr. Bell when we expect you. If you require Sholto, he can travel from Oxford directly to Milton. Remember, Mamma—you must bring Margaret back."

Upon returning to the drawing-room, Mr. Henry Lennox was seated, cutting open a fresh issue of a review. Without raising his gaze, he remarked, "If you do not wish Sholto to remain absent from you for so long, Edith, pray let me descend to Milton to render whatever aid I can."

"Oh, thank you," Edith replied, her spirits lightening. "Old Mr. Bell will undoubtedly manage everything, and further assistance may prove unnecessary. However, it seems fortunate that Uncle should pass away precisely now, when we have returned home and are fully prepared to receive Margaret. Poor soul! How drastically her life will change from Milton!" She looked around dreamily. "I shall procure new chintz for her bedroom, brighten the space, and lift her spirits."

Meanwhile, Mrs. Shaw traveled to Milton, oscillating between dread of the initial encounter and fervent plans to whisk Margaret away from "that dreadful place" to the comforting warmth of Harley Street.

"Oh, dear!" she murmured to her maid, staring at the smokestacks. "Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I could not have slept in Naples had I foreseen this misery. I ought to have fetched her sooner." Privately, she conceded that she had always viewed her brother-in-law as somewhat frail, yet never so weak as in witnessing the trade-off he had made for the lovely Helstone home.

Margaret remained unchanged: pale, motionless, speechless, tearless. They informed her that Aunt Shaw was arriving, but she displayed neither surprise, delight, nor distaste. Mr. Bell, his appetite restored and appreciative of Dixon's efforts, vainly urged her to sample sweetbreads stewed with oysters; she shook her head with the same quiet obstinacy as before, forcing him to consume the entire dish himself. Yet, Margaret was the first to notice the cab halting outside, bringing her aunt from the station. Her eyelids quivered, her lips flushed and trembled. As Mr. Bell descended to greet Mrs. Shaw, Margaret rose, steadying her dizzy form. Upon seeing her aunt, she moved forward, seeking the embrace extended to receive her, and instantly found the passionate relief of tears soaking her aunt's shoulder. All notions of habitual affection, tender memories spanning years, the bond of kinship to the dead, and that inexplicable familial resemblance in look, tone, and gesture—which had so vividly recalled her mother to Margaret's mind—melted together, softening her numbed heart into an overflowing torrent of warm tears.

Mr. Bell slipped quietly out of the room and descended to the study, where he lit a fire and distracted his agitated thoughts by examining various volumes. Each book evoked a memory or suggestion of his departed friend. While this marked a shift from his two days of vigil, it represented no change in his thoughts. Hearing Mr. Thornton's voice inquire at the door, he welcomed him. Dixon, however, dismissed Mr. Thornton with cavalier indifference; the arrival of Mrs. Shaw's entourage had awakened visions of former grandeur, the Beresford lineage, and the "station" (as she termed it) from which her young mistress had fallen, only to be restored by divine providence. These fantasies, which she had indulged in her conversations with Mrs. Shaw's maid (skillfully extracting details regarding the Harley Street establishment for Martha's edification), rendered her inclined toward snobbism. Thus, though she usually stood in awe of Mr. Thornton, she treated him as curtly as her courage permitted, denying him access to the house that evening. Her statement was abruptly contradicted when Mr. Bell opened the study door and called out, "Thornton! Is that you? Enter for a moment; I wish to speak with you." Mr. Thornton entered, forcing Dixon to retreat to the kitchen and bolster her pride with a prodigious tale of Sir John Beresford's coach and six during his tenure as High Sheriff.

"Honestly, I cannot recall why I wished to speak to you," Mr. Bell admitted, looking around the dim room. "It is sufficiently dull sitting in a chamber where everything speaks of a lost friend. Nevertheless, Margaret and her aunt must possess the drawing-room exclusively."

"Has Mrs.—has her aunt arrived?" asked Mr. Thornton.

"Arrived? Indeed! Maid and all. One would have expected her to travel alone under such circumstances! Now I am reduced to finding my way to the Clarendon."

"You must not go to the Clarendon. We have five or six vacant bedrooms at home."

"Freshly aired?"

"I trust my mother's judgment on that score."

"Then I shall merely ascend to bid that wan girl goodnight, pay my respects to her aunt, and depart with you immediately."

Mr. Bell lingered upstairs for some time. Mr. Thornton, preoccupied with business and having barely spared time to visit Crampton and inquire after Miss Hale, began to wonder at the delay.

Once they were walking, Mr. Bell observed, "Those women detained me in the drawing-room. Mrs. Shaw is eager to return home—due to concerns for her daughter, she states—and wishes for Margaret to accompany her forthwith. Yet, she is scarcely fit for travel, no more so than I am for flight. Furthermore, she argues, and with justice, that she possesses acquaintances she must visit, friends to whom she owes farewells. Then her aunt harried her regarding old claims, accusing her of forgetting old friends. Finally, she erupted in tears, expressing how gladly she would depart from a place where she has endured so much suffering."
