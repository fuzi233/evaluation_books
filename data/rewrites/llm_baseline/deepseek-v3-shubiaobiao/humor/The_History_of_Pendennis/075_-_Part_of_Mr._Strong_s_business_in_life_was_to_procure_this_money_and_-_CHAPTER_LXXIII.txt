Arthur Pendennis sat in his chambers, turning over a perfumed letter in his hand—one of those delicate, gilt-edged missives that seem to carry the scent of a woman’s vanity along with them. The words were elegantly penned, the sentiments artfully arranged, but the meaning? Ah, that was another matter entirely.

*"Dear Blanche,"* the letter began, *"you are always dreaming of pretty dramas and romantic heroines. But tell me, are you prepared to play the part fate has assigned you—not the triumphant lady who inherits a palace, but the forsaken one who discovers she has married a beggar?"*

He read it again, chuckling darkly to himself. Blanche’s reply had been equally theatrical—full of wounded pride, poetic sighs, and a refusal (or was it an acceptance?) wrapped in so many layers of ambiguity that even Pen, no stranger to artifice, found himself baffled.

*"I will write more when I am more collected,"* Blanche had concluded dramatically. *"My racking brain renders thought almost impossible. I long to see Laura!"*

Pen tossed the letter aside with a grim smile. How neatly life had contrived to mock him! His grand ambitions—Parliament, wealth, social triumph—had crumbled like Alnaschar’s porcelain, and now even love seemed to be slipping through his fingers in the most absurd fashion.

A knock at the door interrupted his musings. His servant announced a visitor—*Mr. Samuel Huxter.*

Huxter entered, twirling his walking stick with nervous energy, his face a picture of mingled defiance and sheepishness.

*"Mr. Huxter on particular business!"* Pen greeted him with exaggerated politeness. *"Pray, what can I do for you?"*

Huxter shifted uncomfortably. *"I’d rather not speak before the servant."*

Once alone, the young surgeon blurted out his confession. *"I’ve gone and done it, sir. I’ve married Fanny."*

Pen’s eyebrows shot up. *"Married her? Without a word to anyone?"*

*"It was a quiet affair,"* Huxter admitted. *"St. Clement’s, no fuss. But now the governor’s coming to town, and there’ll be the devil to pay."*

Pen couldn’t help but laugh. *"So you’ve eloped with the girl, and now you want me to smooth things over with your father?"*

*"If you wouldn’t mind, sir,"* Huxter said, with a hopeful grimace. *"He respects you. And Fanny—well, she’s a good girl, sir. Just a bit lively."*

*"She’s got half the medical students from Bartholomew’s dangling after her,"* Huxter admitted glumly. *"But she’s my wife now, and I mean to keep her."*

Before Pen could respond, another knock sounded—this time, it was Bows, the old musician, his face weary, his voice gruff.

*"Mrs. Huxter sent me,"* he announced. *"She thought you might need reminding of your promise."*

Huxter flushed. *"I was just going—"*

*"Yes, yes, off you go,"* Bows said dryly. *"Your young friends from the hospital are waiting. No hurry, of course."*

With a hasty bow, Huxter fled.

Bows sighed, sinking into a chair. *"She’s a clever one, that Fanny. Played us all, she did. Even me."*

Pen studied the old man’s face. *"You cared for her."*

*"More fool I,"* Bows muttered. *"But she’s made her choice. Let’s see how long it lasts."*

Left alone once more, Pen turned Blanche’s letter over in his hands again. Was it rejection? Or merely a test? And did it matter, when his own heart seemed so uncertain?

With a shake of his head, he reached for his hat. There was only one person whose counsel he truly valued now.
