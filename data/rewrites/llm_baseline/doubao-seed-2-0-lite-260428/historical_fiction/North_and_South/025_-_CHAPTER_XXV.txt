*Revenge may have her own; Roused discipline aloud proclaims their cause, And injured navies urge their broken laws.* — BYRON

Margaret Hale found herself wondering if every marriage proposal landed with the same unforeseen, gutting weight as the two she had received. Unbidden, a comparison rose unspooled in her mind between Henry Lennox and John Thornton. With Lennox, she had felt only gentle regret: circumstances had lured a confession of love out of him that strayed over the line of their easy friendship, and he had seemed almost as embarrassed by the slip as she was, their awkwardness shared.

But this second proposal left her reeling, the deep, rough timbre of Thornton’s voice still ringing in the corners of the room long after he had left. There had been no soft, gradual stretch of friendship between her and the master of Marlborough Mills. Their every interaction had been a series of headstrong clashes, their views on labor, on justice, on the very rhythm of life in Milton forever at odds. She had never once suspected he cared for her opinions, let alone for her as a person; he had brushed off her protests against his harshness as if they were no more than gnat stings, until she grew tired of expending breath on arguments that never landed. And now he had burst in on her, wild and passionate, to lay his love bare.

At first she had assumed his declaration was no more than pity, spurred by the riot the day before, when he had seen her throw herself between him and the rioters, and mistaken that act of kindness for something softer. But before he had even crossed the threshold to leave, certainly no more than five minutes after he had gone, the truth settled over her clear and bright: he loved her. He had loved her for months, he would go on loving her no matter how she pushed him away.

She shuddered as if some great, unyielding force had wrapped itself around her will, alien to the quiet, gentle life she had known back in Helstone. She fled to the far end of the room to hide from the thought of him, but it would not leave her. To twist a line from Fairfax’s translation of Tasso: his strong, stubborn idea wandered through every corner of her thought. She resented him all the more for having forced his way past the guards of her inner life. How dare he claim he would love her still even if she spurned him with all the contempt she could muster? Too late, sharp, cutting replies crowded into her head, things she should have said to drive him off for good. The memory of their interview clung to her like the horror from a half-waking nightmare: no matter how hard she rubbed her eyes, no matter how she forced a stiff, bright smile to her lips, the ghastly thing lurked in the corner of the room, waiting for her to dare speak of it to anyone else.

She could not bear the weight of the air around her a moment longer, and flew to throw open the sash of the window, then wrenched the door open too, desperate to outrun the memory in company or work. But the house lay wrapped in the heavy, midday stillness that settled over Milton homes when invalids caught the shallow, unrefreshing nap that eluded them through the night hours. She would not be alone. Of course, she would go see Bessy Higgins — the message the poor girl had sent the night before came rushing back to her. She set off at once.

When she arrived at the Higgins cottage, Bessy lay stretched flat on the settle, dragged as close to the fire as she could get even though the day was sweltering and close, as if she could not shake a deep, inner chill after a fit of coughing pain. Margaret said nothing, just lifted her gently and adjusted the pillows so she could sit half-upright, easier on her straining lungs.

“I thought I’d never see you again,” Bessy whispered at last, gazing up at her face with heavy, wistful eyes.

“I’m so sorry you’re worse. I couldn’t come yesterday — my mother was very ill, for so many reasons,” Margaret said, her cheeks flushing as she thought of the riot.

“Might’ve thought I was out of my place sending Mary round to fetch you. But the shouting and the wrangling last night tore me all to bits, and when father left, I thought if I could just hear your voice, reading me some words of peace and promise, I could drift off into God’s quiet rest just like a baby lulled to sleep by its mother’s lullaby.”

“Shall I read you a chapter now?”

“Ay, do. I might not take in the sense at first — it’ll seem far away. But when you get to the texts I know and love, the comforting ones, it’ll feel close, like it’s sinking right through me.”

Margaret began to read, but Bessy tossed and turned restlessly on the settle, every effort to pay attention for a second leaving her more convulsed with agitation the next. At last she burst out: “Stop. It’s no use. I’m blaspheming in my own head the whole time, thinking angry thoughts about all the things that can’t be mended. You heard about the riot yesterday down at Marlborough Mills, Thornton’s factory, didn’t you?”

“Your father wasn’t there, was he?” Margaret asked, her face going scarlet.

“Not he. He’d’ve given his right hand if it never happened. That’s what’s eating at him, right now. He’s broken-hearted over it. You never saw a man so down.”

“But why?” Margaret frowned, confused.

“Because he’s on the strike committee. The Union elected him, and between you and me, they were right — he’s sharp, and loyal to the bone. Him and the rest of the committee drew up all the plans. They agreed every man would stand together, no splitting off, no matter how hungry they got. And above all things, no breaking the law. People would stand with them if they saw them starving quiet and patient, they knew — but the second there was any noise, any fighting, even sticks thrown, all their public support would vanish, same as it had a dozen times before. They planned to go out and talk to the troublemakers, coax them, reason with them, warn them off. The committee ordered every Union man to lie down and die before he struck a blow. They thought they had the whole public on their side, sure they were in the right with their wage demands, no risk of all their good cause tangled up with wrong, no one could mix the two up no more than I could pick the bitter physic powder out of the sweet jelly you gave me to mix it in. But I’ve rambled on too long, and I’m worn out. You think for yourself what it’s like for father, all his work undone, by a fool like Boucher who went right against committee orders and ruined the whole strike, just like he was a Judas on purpose. Father gave it to him good last night. He even said he’d go tell the police where to find the riot ringleader, hand Boucher over to the mill owners to do with what they would. He wanted to show the world the real strike leaders weren’t thugs like Boucher, but steady, thoughtful men — good workers, good citizens, who respected law and order, who only wanted their fair wage, wouldn’t go back to work even if they starved until they got it, but who’d never hurt a soul or break a single window.” She dropped her voice to a hoarse whisper. “They say Boucher threw a stone at Thornton’s sister that nearly killed her.”

“That’s not true,” Margaret said, first flushing red, then draining white. “It wasn’t Boucher who threw that stone.”

“Were you there, then?” Bessy asked, languid, speech coming slow and hard between gasps.

“Yes. Never mind that. Go on — what did Boucher say to your father?”

“Hardly a word. He was shaking all over, spent from passion and hunger, I could scarce bear to look at him. His breath was coming quick, and at one point I thought he was sobbing. When father said he’d hand him over to the police, he let out a great cry, punched father right in the face with his fist, and ran off like lightning. Father was stunned at first, even though Boucher was weak with rage and starvation. He sat down a minute, covered his eyes, then made for the door. I don’t know where I got the strength, but I threw myself across the settle and clung to his legs. ‘Father, father!’ I said. ‘You’ll never peach on that poor starving man. I’ll never let go of you till you promise you won’t.’ ‘Don’t be a fool,’ he said. ‘Words come easier than deeds to most men. I never really meant to tell the police on him — though God knows he deserves it, and I wouldn’t have minded if someone else had done the dirty work and clapped him in jail. But now he’s struck me, I could never do it, not now — it’d be getting other men to fight my quarrel for me. But once he’s past this hunger, and well again, him and I will have it out face to face, and I’ll make him see sense.’ Then he shook me off, and I was so faint I nearly fell. His face was as white as clay, where it wasn’t bloody, and it turned my stomach to look at it. I don’t know if I slept or fainted dead away until Mary came in, and I told her to go fetch you. Now don’t talk to me. Just read me a chapter. I feel easier now I’ve spat it all out, but I want thoughts of that far-off world to take the bitter taste out of my mouth. Don’t read a sermon. Read the story of the New Heavens and the New Earth, the ones with pictures I can see behind my eyelids when I shut my eyes.”

Margaret read in her soft, low voice. Bessy’s eyes stayed shut, but she listened long enough for heavy tears to gather on her lashes, until at last she drifted off to a fitful sleep, full of small starts and muttered, pleading half-words. Margaret covered her gently, and left, her chest tight with a guilty feeling that she might be needed at home, though it felt cruel to leave the dying girl alone.

Her mother was in the drawing room when she got back, having one of her rare good days, full of nothing but praise for their new water bed. It was more like the feather beds they had had at Sir John Beresford’s estate than anything she had slept on in years. She could not understand why no one made beds like that anymore — the feathers were the same, surely, but she had not had a full, restful night’s sleep in decades until the night before. Mr Hale suggested maybe the better rest back in their youth was down to the energy of youth, but his wife brushed the idea off sharply.

“No, Mr. Hale, it was all the beds at Sir John’s. Margaret, you’re young, you move about all day — are your beds comfortable? Do you wake feeling perfectly rested, or do you toss and turn trying to find a soft spot, and wake up just as tired as you were when you laid down?”

Margaret laughed. “To tell the truth, mamma, I’ve never even thought about my bed. I’m so tired by nightfall I drop off the second my head hits the pillow. I’m no good as a witness. And besides, I never got to try the beds at Sir John’s — I never went to Oxenham, remember?”

“Didn’t you? Oh, no! That’s right. I took poor dear little Fred there once, after we married, for your Aunt Shaw’s wedding. Fred was just a baby then. I remember Dixon didn’t want to step down from being my lady’s maid to being a nurse, and I was afraid if I took her near her old home she’d want to leave me. But baby Fred got sick teething while we were there, and I was busy so much with Anna in the days before her wedding, and I was still weak myself, that Dixon ended up caring for him almost full time. She got so fond of him, so proud when he’d cling to her and turn away from everyone else, that I don’t think she ever thought of leaving me again. Everyone loved Fred. He was born with the gift of winning hearts. It makes me think very ill of Captain Reid, to hear he disliked my sweet boy. It proves for certain he had a bad heart.” She paused, and sighed. “Oh, your poor father, Margaret — he left the room. He can’t bear to hear Fred spoken of.”

“I love hearing stories about him, mamma. Tell me all you want, you can never tell me too much. What was he like as a baby?”

“Margaret, you won’t be hurt if I say he was far prettier than you were. I remember the first time I saw you in Dixon’s arms, I said ‘Dear, what an ugly little thing!’ and she said ‘Not every child’s like Master Fred, bless him!’ I could have held Fred every minute of the day, his cot was right next to my bed. And now, Margaret — I don’t know where my boy is. Sometimes I think I’ll never see him again.”

Margaret sank down on a small stool by her mother’s sofa, took her hand gently and kissed it, to soothe her. Mrs Hale cried freely, no restraint. At last she sat up straight, her face wet with tears, and looked at her daughter with solemn, earnest eyes.

“Margaret, if I get better — if God lets me recover — it will only be from seeing Frederick once more. It will wake up all the little bits of health left in me.” Her voice shook, quavering as if she was staring at some half-formed, urgent vision right in front of her. “And Margaret — if I am meant to die, if God calls me home in the next few weeks — I must see my child first. I cannot die without blessing him. Only five minutes, Margaret. There can’t be any danger in five minutes. Oh, Margaret, bring him to me before I die.”

Margaret did not stop to question the reasonableness of the request. No one looks for logic in the desperate pleas of a person sick unto death. The thought of all the small, trivial wishes she had denied her mother over the years pricked at her, and she would have laid down her own future happiness at her mother’s feet without a second thought to grant this one. It was so natural, so right — for her mother’s sake, for Frederick’s too, she would overlook every risk. The great, pleading eyes of Mrs Hale were fixed on her, steady, her pale lips trembling like a small child’s. Margaret stood up, and faced her, her own face calm and steady, so her mother could read her promise there.

“Mamma, I will write tonight, tell Frederick exactly what you said. I know as sure as I’m alive that he will come home to us at once. Be easy. You shall see him, as far as any earthly thing can be promised.”

“Write tonight? The post goes out at five! You’ll write by that post, won’t you? I have so little time left — sometimes your father talks me into hoping I’ll get better, but I know I won’t. Don’t miss a single post — I could miss him entirely, just by one day’s delay.”

“But mamma, papa is out —”

“Papa is out? What of that? You don’t mean he would deny me my last wish, Margaret? I wouldn’t be sick, dying, if he hadn’t dragged us away from Helstone to this smoky, sunless, unhealthy Milton!”

“Oh mamma!” Margaret protested, hurt.

“It’s true! He knows it himself, he’s said so a dozen times. He’d do anything for me. You don’t mean he’d refuse me this last prayer? And Margaret, this longing to see Frederick stands between me and God. I can’t pray, not until I have this. Don’t waste time, dear. Write right now, by the five o’clock post. Then he can be here in twenty-two days! No chains, no law will stop him. In twenty-two days I’ll hold my boy again.”

She fell back against the cushions, and was quiet for a minute, until she noticed Margaret sitting motionless, one hand shading her eyes. “You’re not writing! Bring me pen and paper, I’ll write it myself.” She sat up, trembling all over with feverish eagerness.

Margaret laid a gentle hand on hers. “Only wait till papa comes in. Let us ask him the best way to do it?”

“You promised me, Margaret, not fifteen minutes ago, you said he would come.”

“And he will, mamma. Don’t cry, my dearest mother. I’ll write it right now, here, you can watch me. It will go by the five o’clock post. And if papa wants to, he can write a follow-up letter when he gets home — that’s only a day’s delay, nothing to Frederick. Don’t cry so pitifully, it breaks my heart.”

Mrs Hale could not stop her hysterical tears, she even called up images of her own corpse laid out in the drawing room while Frederick arrived too late to see her alive, until she dissolved into exhausted sobs that ached Margaret’s heart. At last she calmed, and watched greedily as Margaret wrote the letter, swift and full of urgent pleading, sealed it up, and then ran herself to the post office to make sure it went out on time, per her mother’s orders.

She was on her way home when her father overtook her on the lane. “And where have you run off to, my pretty maid?” he asked.

“To the post office, with a letter — to Frederick. Oh papa, I think I might have done wrong! But mamma was seized with this terrible, desperate longing to see him. She said it would make her well again, and then she said she had to see him before she died. I can’t tell you how urgent she was. Did I do wrong?”

Mr Hale was quiet for a long moment. “You should have waited for me to come back, Margaret.”

“I tried to make her —” Margaret’s voice trailed off.

“I don’t know,” Mr Hale said, after another long pause. “If she wants to see him that badly, she should see him. I truly believe it would do her more good than all the doctor’s medicine, it might even set her up entirely. But the danger to Frederick is very great.”

“All these years since the mutiny, papa?”
