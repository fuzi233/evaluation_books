For Margaret, the profound stillness of the Harley Street residence during Edith's convalescence proved a necessary balm. It afforded her the rare luxury of time to process the seismic shift in her fortunes over the preceding two months. Suddenly, she was an inhabitant of a sumptuous mansion where the mere awareness of worry or hardship seemed alien to the atmosphere. The intricate gears of domestic life here ran with a frictionless, oily smoothness. Mrs. Shaw and Edith treated Margaret with an abundance of affection, insisting on calling it "home," yet a faint pang of ingratitude occasionally stirred within her. Could she truly prefer this opulent sanctuary to the Helstone rectory—or even the modest cottage at Milton, surrounded by her anxious father, her frail mother, and the humble, tangible cares of a poorer existence? That was the definition of home for her soul.

Edith, eager to reclaim her health, rushed to furnish Margaret's former bedroom with the soft linens and decorative trinkets that adorned her own rooms. Mrs. Shaw and her maid delighted in restoring Margaret's wardrobe to a state of fashionable elegance. Captain Lennox was a model of gentle amiability; he spent hours in Edith's dressing room, played with his young son, and otherwise idled at his club or attended dinners.

Just as Margaret began to crave the quietude she had previously endured, Edith descended the stairs, resuming her role as the household's center of gravity. Margaret reverted to her old instinct of service, eagerly assuming the burdens of correspondence, scheduling, and nursing whenever Edith's mood turned somber. Consequently, she often feigned illness simply to justify her caretaking role. Yet, the rest of the family was immersed in the frenetic energy of the London Season, leaving Margaret frequently solitary. In these moments, her thoughts drifted back to Milton, struck by a poignant contrast between the vibrant struggle of her past and the stagnant ease of her present. She feared she was becoming surfeited by a life requiring no effort, worried that she might slowly drift into a numb oblivion, unaware of anything beyond the velvet cushion of luxury surrounding her. Though toils existed in London, she rarely glimpsed them; the servants inhabited a shadowy underworld of their own, appearing only to serve the whims of their masters, invisible in their hopes and fears. A hollow void began to open in Margaret's heart.

One evening, she tentatively voiced this unease to Edith. Exhausted from a night of dancing, Edith languidly stroked Margaret's cheek as the younger woman sat on a footstool beside her on the sofa.

"Poor child!" Edith sighed. "It is somewhat melancholy to be left alone night after night when the whole world is so festive. But soon we shall host dinner parties—once Henry returns from circuit—and there will be delightful variety. No wonder you are moping, my darling!"

Margaret sensed that dinner parties were no cure-all. Edith, however, prided herself on them, boasting they were vastly superior to the stiff, dowager-led gatherings of her mother's era. Even Mrs. Shaw seemed to derive genuine pleasure from these modern social circles, tailored to the tastes of Captain and Mrs. Lennox, mirroring her own enjoyment of the formal events she once hosted.

Captain Lennox remained consistently kind and brotherly toward Margaret. She genuinely loved him, save for those moments when he became overly preoccupied with Edith's attire, striving to ensure his sister-in-law's beauty made a lasting impression on society. At such times, the dormant pride of a Vashti rose within Margaret, threatening to spill forth.

Her days followed a predictable rhythm: a couple of quiet hours before a belated breakfast; a leisurely, half-asleep meal followed by discussions of plans that concerned her only insofar as she was expected to offer sympathetic agreement; an endless stream of notes to compose, which Edith delegated to her with flattering remarks on her "eloquence"; playful interactions with Sholto upon his return from walks; childcare duties during the servants' meals; drives or receiving callers; and finally, engagements for her aunt and cousins. These activities, while freeing her technically, left her exhausted by the day's inertia, her spirits depressed, and her delicate health taxed.

With longing, Margaret awaited Dixon's return from Milton, where he had been diligently settling the Hale family's affairs. Her heart felt a sudden famine at the cessation of news regarding the people she had known so intimately. True, Dixon's businesslike letters occasionally cited Mr. Thornton's opinions on furniture or landlords, but references to Milton itself were sparse. One evening, sitting alone in the Lennox drawing-room, holding Dixon's letters unread, Margaret reflected on the bustling life from which she had been plucked. She wondered if anyone in that whirlwind missed her—not Higgins, whom she wasn't considering—but questioned silently whether the crowd cared if she and her father were gone.

Suddenly, Mr. Bell was announced. Margaret hastily shoved the letters into her workbasket, rising with a blush that suggested guilt.

"Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!"

"But I trust you extend a welcome as warm as that startled surprise," he replied cheerfully.

"Have you dined? How did you arrive? Shall I order dinner?"

"If you're planning to eat. Otherwise, nothing pleases me less than dining. But where are the others? Out? Leaving you alone?"

"Oh, yes! It is quite refreshing. I was just contemplating—"

"Shall I risk dinner? I'm unsure if there's anything suitable in the house."

"To tell the truth, I dined at my club. Their cooking hasn't improved, so I thought if you were dining, I might attempt to manage. But forget it! There aren't ten trustworthy cooks in England for impromptu meals. If their skills hold, their tempers surely won't. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. Now, what were you thinking of? You were about to tell me. Whose letters were those you concealed so quickly, god-daughter?"

"Only Dixon's," Margaret admitted, turning crimson.

"Phew! Is that all? Who do you suppose arrived on the train with me?"

"I don't know," Margaret lied, refusing to guess.

"Your... cousin-in-law's brother? What is the precise term?"

"Mr. Henry Lennox?" Margaret ventured.

"Yes," Mr. Bell confirmed. "You knew him formerly, did you not? Describe him, Margaret."

"I liked him long ago," she said, glancing downward briefly before lifting her eyes to her natural composure. "We have corresponded regarding Frederick, but I haven't seen him in nearly three years; he may have changed. What is your impression of him?"

"I am uncertain. He was so intent on discovering who I was initially, and what I was subsequently, that he revealed nothing of himself. Unless, of course, that veiled curiosity regarding the nature of his conversational partner was not a virtue, hinting at his character. Do you consider him handsome, Margaret?"

"No, certainly not. Do you?"

"Not I. Yet, I presumed you might. Does he frequent this area much?"

"He seems to be in town when he is. He has been on circuit since my arrival. However, Mr. Bell, have you come from Oxford or Milton?"

"Milton. Don't you see I am smoke-stained?"

"Certainly. Though I assumed it might be the result of Oxford's antiquities."

"Come now, be sensible! In Oxford, I could have managed every landlord and dictated terms with half the trouble your Milton landlord has inflicted upon me, only to be thwarted in the end. He refuses to release the house until next June. Fortunately, Mr. Thornton secured a tenant. Why do you not inquire after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proven a diligent friend. He has alleviated more than half my burden."

"And how fares he? How is Mrs. Thornton?" Margaret asked urgently, lowering her voice despite her efforts to sound casual.

"They are presumably well. I stayed at their home until I was expelled by the incessant chatter concerning that Thornton girl's marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself, though she was his sister. He retreated to his room constantly. He is past the age for such concerns, whether as protagonist or accessory. I was astonished to find the older lady swept up in the current, carried away by her daughter's fervor for orange blossoms and lace. I had imagined Mrs. Thornton possessed sterner material."

"She would assume any emotion to conceal her daughter's fragility," Margaret whispered.

"Perhaps so. Have you studied her closely? She does not appear particularly fond of you, Margaret."

"I know it," Margaret replied flatly.

"Oh, here is tea, at last!" she exclaimed, as if relieved.

The arrival of tea coincided with Mr. Henry Lennox, who had walked up from a late dinner, expecting to find his brother and sister-in-law at home. Margaret suspected he was as grateful as she was for the presence of a third party, easing the tension of their first encounter since his proposal at Helstone and her subsequent refusal. She struggled to find words, thankful for the distractions of the tea table that allowed her silence and gave him space to regain his composure.
