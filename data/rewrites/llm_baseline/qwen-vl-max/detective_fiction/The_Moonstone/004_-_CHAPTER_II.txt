I spoke of my lady a moment ago. And indeed, if we are to trace the history of the Diamond—lost as it was in our house—we must begin with her. For the stone could never have come into our home unless it had first been given to her daughter; and that daughter would never have existed without my lady, who bore her through pain and hardship. So if we start with her, we are safe in saying we’ve begun far enough back. And when you’re dealing with a mystery as tangled as this one, starting at the right point is more than half the battle.

Now, if you’re familiar with high society, you’ll know of the three Miss Herncastles—Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia, the youngest and, in my judgment, the finest of the trio. I had ample reason to form that opinion, as you’ll soon see. At fifteen, I entered the service of their father, the old lord—though thank God, he has no part in this tale of the Diamond. He was a man of long tongue and short temper, whether noble or not. But I was appointed page-boy, attending the three young ladies. That was where I stayed until Miss Julia married Sir John Verinder.

An excellent man, though he needed managing—and by heaven, he found someone who could do it. From the day my lady took him to church, to the day she closed his eyes forever, he prospered under her care. He lived content, died easy, and thrived on her influence.

I should mention—I went with the bride to her husband’s estate down here. “Sir John,” she said, “I can’t do without Gabriel Betteredge.” “My lady,” he replied, “neither can I.” That was his way with her—and so I entered his service. As for me, I didn’t care where I was, so long as I was with my mistress.

Since my lady took interest in the farms and country work, I did too—especially since I myself was the seventh son of a small farmer. She arranged for me to be placed under the bailiff, and I worked hard, pleased my superiors, and rose accordingly. A few years later, my lady remarked: “Sir John, your bailiff is an old fool. Pension him generously and give Gabriel Betteredge his place.” The next day, Sir John said: “My lady, the bailiff is pensioned. Mr. Betteredge has taken over.”

And so it came to pass. Not many hear of couples living together in harmony—but here was one such case. Let it be a warning to some, and a hope to others.

So there I was—comfortably settled. In charge of the estate, with a cottage of my own, morning rounds, afternoon accounts, and evenings spent with my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. What more could a man want? Remember Adam in Eden—alone. If you don’t blame him for wanting company, don’t blame me.

The woman I chose was Selina Goby, the woman who ran my cottage household. I agree with William Cobbett: choose a wife who chews her food well and walks with firm feet. Selina met both marks—one reason I married her. But I had another reason entirely of my own: she charged me weekly for board and services. As my wife, she couldn’t charge me—and would serve me freely. That was economy, with a touch of affection.

I brought it up to my lady, as duty demanded. “I’ve been thinking about Selina Goby,” I said. “I believe, my lady, it will cost less to marry her than to keep her.” My lady burst out laughing—shocked, she said, by either my words or my logic. Some joke, perhaps, only intelligible to people of quality. I didn’t understand it. But I knew I was free to act—and so I did.

What did Selina say? Lord! You’d have to know women better than I do to ask that. Of course she said yes.

As the wedding approached, talk turned to a new coat. Then doubt crept in. I’ve spoken with other men about how they felt before marriage. All admitted they wished themselves out of it about a week before. I went further—I tried to back out. Not lightly. I wasn’t the sort to expect a woman to let me off without compensation. English law says: when the man withdraws, the woman must be paid.

So I offered Selina a featherbed and fifty shillings to call it off. Hard to believe, but true—she refused. After that, I gave up. I bought the cheapest coat I could find and endured the rest just as cheaply.

We weren’t happy. We weren’t miserable. We were six of one, half-a-dozen of the other. It was as if we were always getting in each other’s way. When I wanted upstairs, she was coming down. When she wanted down, I was going up. That, according to my experience, is marriage.

After five years of such misunderstandings, Providence stepped in. My wife passed away. I was left with my daughter, Penelope—no other child. Soon after, Sir John died, and my lady was left with her daughter, Miss Rachel, and no other.

If I’ve written poorly about my lady, then I’ve failed. But surely you know that Penelope was raised under her watchful eye—sent to school, taught well, made sharp, and eventually placed as Miss Rachel’s personal maid.

As for me, I continued as bailiff year after year—until Christmas 1847, when things changed.

That day, my lady invited herself to tea in my cottage. She noted that, counting from my first days as page-boy under the old lord, I had served her for over fifty years. With that, she handed me a beautiful wool waistcoat she’d knit herself—to keep me warm in winter.

I was stunned, unable to express my gratitude. But to my surprise, it wasn’t a gift—it was a bribe. She saw I was growing old before I did, and came to coax me into retiring from farmwork and taking the easier role of steward in the house.

I resisted. I fought against the idea of ease. But my mistress knew me well. She framed it as a favor to her. Our argument ended with me wiping my eyes like a foolish old man, clutching my new waistcoat, and promising to think about it.

But the turmoil in my mind was unbearable. So I did what always calmed me in moments of doubt: I lit a pipe and read *Robinson Crusoe*.

Within minutes, I found a passage that set everything straight (page 158): “Today we love, what tomorrow we hate.”

It struck me instantly. Today I loved being bailiff. Tomorrow—by the book’s wisdom—I would hate it. So why not take myself tomorrow, in tomorrow’s mood? The decision was made.

That night, I slept as Lady Verinder’s farm-bailiff. The next morning, I woke as her house-steward. All done, all calm—all thanks to *Robinson Crusoe*.

Just now, my daughter Penelope leaned over my shoulder to read what I’ve written. She praised it—“beautifully written, every word true.” But she pointed out a flaw: “You’re supposed to tell the story of the Diamond, not your own life.”

Curious. I cannot explain it. I wonder if authors who make their living writing ever find themselves distracted by their own lives, as I am?

If they do, I feel for them.

But here we are again—a false start, and another sheet wasted. What now? Nothing I can think of, except for you to remain patient, and for me to begin once more—for the third time.
