Mr. Draper went off in his heat with the echo of his own peroration snapping at his heels. In the close passage outside the room, he thought he heard the young prisoner call after him—indeed Harry had started up from the bed, half a word on his lips, his pride already bending as need would have it. But the attorney’s dignity was in arms, and Harry’s was wounded too; the one would not pause, the other would not beg. The door shut. A draught from the corridor chilled the sour air of the chamber; the bolts and bars seemed to answer it with a little iron music that set the teeth on edge.

“An hour hence will do as well,” Harry told himself, and dropped back upon the coverlet with that stubborn sullenness which is only despair wearing a soldier’s coat. He would not—he did not—care for Maria Esmond. No. The very thought of her was an insult to his own liberty. He had been trapped into the engagement; a woman older, wily, instructed in the tricks by which hearts are hooked and held, had taken advantage of a boy’s heat as her brother had taken advantage of a boy’s rashness at the cards. They were his own blood; they should have spared him. Instead they had made him their convenience, their instrument—hospitality turned to snare, a kinsman welcomed within their gates only to be used.

He lay and stared at the stained ceiling, yet saw, as if laid upon a trencher before him, the table at Castlewood—the soft nap of the cloth, the shining of glasses, the steam of a dish uncovered, the honest Virginia abundance he had fancied in his mind when he first came to England with his simple confidence and his hungry youth. Had they come to Virginia, he thought, they should have had a different welcome: smoke from hickory, a ham cured sweet and salt both, a great dish of hominy set to swell and soften till each grain gave up its heart, chicken browned in a Dutch oven and basted till the skin shone, apple-tarts with the edges crimped by a careful hand. A house where the voices were kind and the sounds at night were only frogs and wind in the trees—not this perpetual London grind of wheels, iron upon stone, men bawling beneath windows, and keys clanking like threats.

His throat thickened, his pride melted into bitterness, and bitter tears, hot as broth, ran down and wet the pillow. He turned his face into it, as if even his sobbing must be muffled, not to give the place the satisfaction of hearing him break.

A shuffle at the door, a familiar cough smothered in a grin, roused him. Gumbo’s black face, half lighted, peeped in like a friendly moon.

“A lady come see Master Harry,” the man said; and before Harry could do more than wipe his eyes with the back of his hand, the lady herself entered, wrapped in a capuchin.

He sat up, pale and hollow-cheeked, the loose dressing-gown slipping from his shoulder. The lady gave a sob that seemed to carry with it all the warmth of a hearth into that cold room. Almost before the servant had withdrawn, she ran to him and put her arms round his neck with a real emotion and a motherly tenderness. She kissed his cheek again and again, not caring that it was fevered, and her tears fell plentifully there.

“Oh, my Harry! Did I ever, ever think to see thee here?”

He started back as if the sight of her were too strange to be borne—an apparition indeed in that place; and yet she sank down at the bedside and caught his hand, pressing it against her lips, and then, as if she could not help herself, embraced his knees, clinging to him as one clings to a living thing in a flood.

“I—I thought none of you would come!” Harry groaned; for all his anger of the morning, that one sentence unlocked something in him that had been hard shut.

Her answer was kisses and more kisses, her fingers closing over his hand as if she would warm it by mere will. There was no coquetry now, no small tyrannies of a drawing-room; she wept outright, and the poor room was the witness of a tenderness that needed no audience.

“Oh, my dear! my dear! I cannot bear to think of thee in misery,” she sobbed.

Hardened though her heart might be by practice, it was not all marble; and that dreary life of hers, with its painted smiles and desperate shifts, was not all desert. Harry’s own mother could not have spoken more softly than this kinswoman kneeling at his feet.

“Some of the debts, I fear, were owing to my extravagance,” she said—speaking truth, for she knew how he had flung away prudence to buy her pleasures. “You bought trinkets and jewels in order to give me delight. Oh, how I hate them now! I little thought I ever could! I have brought them all with me, and more trinkets—here! and here! and all the money I have in the world!”

From a little bag and a pocket in her cloak she poured out upon his lap brooches, rings, a watch, and guineas—yellow, heavy, and bright as a hoard of sun. They made a sound upon the quilt—a small clink and tumble—that seemed in that silence loud as a tavern’s till. The very sight of them, the proof of her sacrifice, shook him. It was as though she had brought not metal but her own flesh to ransom him.

“Dearest—kindest cousin!” he sobbed, his voice breaking like a boy’s. His lips could find no more words, but they found her hand, and kissed it with a gratitude too deep for speech.

Presently, as her tears lessened, his spirits rose in that strange way they will when kindness comes after cruelty. He became almost gay; he smiled as he gathered some of the trinkets—those he had bought for her—back into her keeping, and told her with rueful candour into what danger he had fallen by selling other goods purchased on credit: how he had tried to patch one hole with another, as a cook spoils a dish by over-salting to cover a burnt. He told her too of the lawyer’s insolence that morning.

He would not—he could not—take her money. He had enough for the moment, he said. And yet he prized her twenty guineas as if they were twenty thousand; their weight was not in gold but in love. He swore he would never forget her goodness, not by any thing sacred. His mother should hear of it. It had cheered him when he was upon the point of breaking under disgrace.

So they talked on, cousins again and not adversaries, until the early darkness of the London season began to creep in, turning the small window to a dull mirror. Maria rose with a sigh, her eyes still red, and bade him farewell. The air where she had been seemed warmer a moment after she went, as if the very room remembered her perfume and the rustle of her cloak.

The door had scarcely closed upon her when it opened again and admitted Mr. Draper.

“Your humble servant, sir,” said the attorney.

His voice grated upon Harry’s ear like a knife on a plate. After Maria’s weeping tenderness it sounded the more hard, the more commercial.

“I had expected you some hours ago, sir,” Harry returned curtly.

“A lawyer’s time is not always his own, sir,” Draper answered, who had been consulting not parchments but a bottle of port at the Grecian. He wore that slightly flushed assurance which comes of wine and self-approval. “Never mind; I’m at your orders now. Presume it’s all right, Mr. Warrington. Packed your trunk? Why, now there you are in your bedgown still. Let me go down and settle whilst you call in your black man and titivate a bit. I’ve a coach at the door, and we’ll be off and dine with the old lady.”

“Are you going to dine with the Baroness de Bernstein, pray?” Harry asked, with a coldness that tried to be polite and could not.

“Not me—no such honour. Had my dinner already. It’s you are a-going to dine with your aunt, I suppose?”

“Mr. Draper,” said Harry, drawing his brocade dressing-gown close about him, standing up so tall that the lawyer instinctively looked upward, “you suppose a great deal more than you know.”

“Great goodness, sir, what do you mean?” Draper asked; and there was in his tone a touch of fear, as if he heard some note in the room besides the human voices—the faint ring of the bar, the dull thud of a boot, the prison’s patience wearing thin.

“I mean, sir, that I have considered; and that having given my word to a faithful and honourable lady, it does not become me to withdraw it.”

“Confound it, sir!” shrieked Draper. “I tell you she has lost the paper. There’s nothing to bind you—nothing. Why she’s old enough to be—”

“Enough, sir,” Harry said, stamping his foot so that the boards spoke. “You seem to think you are talking to some other pettifogger. I take it, Mr. Draper, you are not accustomed to have dealings with men of honour.”

“Pettifogger, indeed!” Draper cried, all his wine turning to vinegar. “Men of honour, indeed! I’d have you to know, Mr. Warrington, that I’m as good a man of honour as you. I don’t know so many gamblers and horse-jockeys, perhaps. I haven’t gambled away my patrimony, and lived as if I was a nobleman on two hundred a year. I haven’t bought watches on credit, and pawned—touch me if you dare, sir!” He sprang to the door.

“That is the way out, sir. You can’t go through the window, because it is barred,” Harry said, pointing with a contempt that cut more than a blow.

“And the answer I take to my client is No, then!” screamed Draper.

Harry stepped forward, both hands clenched, the blood pounding in his ears so loud he could scarcely hear his own voice—a harsh little private thunder that seemed to drown even the street-noise outside.

“If you utter another word,” he said, “I’ll—”

The door banged rapidly; the sentence was never finished. Draper went off in a fury to Madame de Bernstein. He told his tale in the best dress he could give it, but even so he got fiercer language than Harry had used.

“What? Shall she trust me, and I desert her?” Harry paced up and down his narrow room, his brocade gown rustling with every turn like angry leaves. “Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I lie in prison for years, I’ll be true to her.” And he repeated it, not to persuade himself, but as men repeat a vow to keep the world from shaking it.

Meanwhile, the desolate old Baroness, her lawyer dismissed after that storm, sat down at the meal she had hoped to share with her nephew. The chair was set before her which he was to have filled. The glasses shone beside the silver; the candles burned steady, though the noise of the great town beat round the house like an endless sea.

One dish after another was laid before her by the silent major-domo: a soup that should have been taken hot, and now cooled a little as she hesitated; a fowl trussed neatly, its skin browned, the savour rising in a delicate steam; a dish of fish, the lemon sliced thin as paper; sweets and fruits brought in season by money and care. She tasted and pushed away each plate, as if the very abundance mocked her loneliness. There are meals that nourish, and meals that turn to ashes in the mouth.

At last the man pressed his mistress, his voice low, as though he too feared the sound of his own words in that room.

“It is eight o’clock,” he said. “You have had nothing all day. It is good for you to eat.”

She could not. She would have her coffee. Let Case go get her coffee.

Coffee—something dark and bitter, with a perfume strong enough to cut through sorrow—was all she could imagine taking. The lacqueys bore the dishes away, leaving their mistress sitting at the table before the vacant chair. The silence had a sound of its own then: the faint ticking of a clock, the soft stir of a curtain, the distant rumble of a cart in the Strand—London’s coarse bass note under every private grief.

Presently old Case re-entered without the coffee. His face was strangely scared, as though he had seen a ghost in the corridor. His mouth opened once and closed; then he found breath enough to say, in a voice that shook:

“Mr. WARRINGTON!”

The old woman uttered an exclamation, rose from her arm-chair, and sank back in it trembling. Her rouge, laid on by habit as a mask against age and the world, made her face look the ghastlier now, as if colour itself were a lie told too boldly.

“So you are come, sir, are you?” she said, her voice fond and shaking. “Bring back the—Ah!”

She screamed, staring wildly, clutching the arms of her chair as the visitor approached her.

A gentleman whose face and figure exactly resembled Harry Warrington—so exactly that the household might have sworn to him by candlelight—came forward. When he spoke, the tones had the same family ring, as if one voice had been divided between two brothers.

“You expected my brother, madam?” he said. “I am but now arrived in London. I went to his house. I met his servant at your door, who was bearing this letter for you. I thought I would bring it to your ladyship before going to him.” And he laid down a letter before Madam Bernstein.

“Are you—” the Baroness gasped, as if language itself failed and she must drag each word from the depths—“are you my nephew, that we supposed was—”

“Was killed—and is alive!” he answered. “I am George Warrington, madam; and I ask his kinsfolk, what have you done with my brother?”

The old lady’s bewilderment, her joy, her fright, all fought together in her. She looked from the letter to his face, as if her eyes could not be taught this new truth at once.

“Look, George!” she cried at last, as one snatches at anything firm. “I expected him here to-night—that chair was set for him—I have been waiting for him, sir, till now—till I am quite faint—I don’t like—I don’t like being alone. Do stay and sup with me!”

“Pardon me, madam,” George said; and though his bow was stately, there was iron under his politeness. “Please God, my supper will be with Harry to-night.”

“Bring him back. Bring him back here on any conditions! It is but five hundred pounds! Here is the money, sir, if you need it!” She spoke as if ransom were a household purchase—like buying spices dearer than usual because the dish must be made.

“I have no want, madam. I have money with me that can’t be better employed than in my brother’s service.”

“And you will bring him to me, sir! Say you will bring him to me!”

George Warrington made her a grave bow for answer and quitted the room, passing the amazed domestics as if he were master there already. In the hall his voice rang with authority, and the sound of it steadied the servants as a bell steadies a church.

“Gumbo!” he called. “Follow me.”

Had Mr. Harry received no letters from home? Master Harry, Gumbo stammered as he hurried after, had not opened all his letters the last day or two. Had he received no letter announcing his brother’s escape from the French settlements and return to Virginia? No—no such letter had come, else Master Harry would certainly tell Gumbo.

Quick, then: horses. Quick by Strand to Temple Bar. The street roared and rattled; the wheels struck sparks; men cried and cursed; the city’s noise, that perpetual iron commerce of sound, seemed for once to be turned to purpose, driving the Deliverer through the din toward the house of Captivity, where his brother waited unwitting, and the door stood between them like a chapter not yet read.
