Momentous events unfolded for Daniel Deronda on the very evening of his visit to the small Chelsea house, concerning the matter of Mirah's public identity. Yet, for the Meyrick family, the true ripple of this occurrence washed ashore two days later.

Around four o'clock, the familiar rhythm of the street was broken by the pause of wheels before the door. A knock accompanied by a ringing bell shattered the quiet domesticity of the neighborhood, where such signals usually belonged to the muffin-man. Inside, the atmosphere was one of cozy industry. The two rooms had been merged to accommodate Kate's latest drawing and a sprawling piece of embroidery that served as the centerpiece of the afternoon's needlework. Mrs. Meyrick bent over one corner, Mab occupied the center, and Amy stretched out at the far end.

Mirah, whose seamanship resembled nothing so much as a tailor-bird's makeshift nest due to her neglected training, acted as the group's reader. Seated on a camp-stool, she also served as the living model for Kate's title-page vignette—a fair public engrossed in the family's tea-table chronicles. She read aloud George Eliot's essay, "The Praise of Chimney-Sweeps," her voice clear and charming as the sisters laughed over the "innocent blackness" of the subject.

Then, the imposing knock returned, pulling their thoughts upward.

"My goodness!" exclaimed Mrs. Meyrick. "Could it be Lady Mallinger? Is there a grand carriage, Amy?"

"No, merely a hansom cab. It must be a gentleman," Kate replied dryly. "Hans claims the Prime Minister himself might ride in a hansom."

"Oh, oh, oh!" cried Mab. "Suppose it is Lord Russell!"

Five bright faces turned in amusement as the old maid-servant brought in a card, leaving the parlor door ajar. Bowing toward Mrs. Meyrick stood a figure entirely unlike the staid Premier: tall and physically commanding, dressed in kid gloves and kerseymere, with a massive face, flamboyant hair, and gold-rimmed spectacles. As Mrs. Meyrick glanced at the card, she recognized **Julius Klesmer**.

Even embarrassment could not have rendered the "little mother" more awkward, yet her sharp instincts instantly grasped the significance. Here was a giant of the world coming to *her* house, seeking *her* daughter, rather than demanding her attendance elsewhere. It was a sign of profound interest.

However, the moment Klesmer stepped inside, the cottage seemed to shrink. To him, the piano appeared a ridiculous toy, and the entire household felt as insignificant as a colony of mice in the Tuileries. His personality radiated vast horizons and a multitude of unseen audiences; indeed, everyone carries their inner theater within them, and those who gaze wider than their neighbors often appear vain. Klesmer was vain, though perhaps no more so than many men of heavy countenance whose self-importance springs forth startlingly, like a spear from a walking stick. His mannerisms were as natural to him as the length of his fingers; the only affectation he could possibly display would be feigned diffidence.

While his grandeur made Mab feel like a doll beside the toy piano, Klesmer surveyed the room with a keen, genuinely kind sensitivity. Having known homes no larger than this on the Bohemian outskirts, and having navigated the romantic struggles of small incomes in his own figurative homeland, he approached Mrs. Meyrick with utmost deference.

"I hope I have not taken too great a liberty," he began. "Being in the neighborhood, I ventured to save time by calling. My friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned an understanding that I should have the honor of meeting a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth."

Klesmer had spotted Mirah instantly, yet with subtle politeness, he bowed broadly at the three sisters, feigning uncertainty regarding which one was the guest of honor.

"These are my daughters," Mrs. Meyrick said, gesturing toward Mirah. "This is Miss Lapidoth."

"Ah," Klesmer responded, his tone brimming with gratified expectation. He offered a radiant smile and a deep bow to Mirah. Unlike the others, she was not startled; a calm pleasure softened her features. She liked the look in his eyes, sensing that he would treat her with the stern kindness of a great musician and a benevolent man.

"You will not object to beginning our acquaintance with a song?" he asked, eager to bypass formalities.

"I shall be very glad. It is kind of you to listen," Mirah replied, moving toward the piano.

"Accompany yourself?"

"By all means," Klesmer agreed, settling himself where he could watch her clearly. Mrs. Meyrick, hiding her own insecurity, secretly hoped that seeing her would please the critic.

The other three girls' hearts hammered with anxiety, viewing Klesmer's listening frown as terrifying. They dared only glance at him furtively, fearing harsh criticism. They comforted themselves with the memory of Prince Camaralzaman, who had supposedly preferred Mirah's singing above all others. Mirah, however, seemed more at ease than usual, pouring her soul into a fine setting of Leopardi's *Ode to Italy*. She sang of ancient walls and columns, then moved to a mournful melody, building to a climax of devout triumph—an exultant allegro celebrating love and sacrifice.

When she finished, Klesmer paused briefly. "That is old Leo's music."

"Yes, he was my last master in Vienna," Mirah said with a melancholy smile. "So fierce and so good. He prophesied my voice was unfit for the stage. And he was right."

"*Continue*, if you please," Klesmer urged, making unintelligible sounds with his lips and shaking his long fingers.

The three sisters detested his silence, but Mrs. Meyrick grew alarmed. Determined to please, Mirah proceeded to sing several of Prince Radzivill's arrangements of Gretchen's songs from *Faust*. Only when she concluded did Klesmer rise, walk to the edge of the room, and return to the piano. Mirah stood waiting, hands clasped, meekly awaiting judgment.

Suddenly, his brow unknitted, his eyes beamed, and he extended his hand. "Let us shake hands: you are a musician."

Mab nearly wept with joy, and the sisters deemed him adorable. Mrs. Meyrick breathed a sigh of relief. But instantly, the frown returned. His long hand, palm up, reached out only to touch the back of Mirah's hand with a fingertip, his lip protruding slightly.

"Not for great tasks. No high roofs. We are no skylarks. We must be modest."

He paused, allowing the weight of his words to settle. Mab lost her admiration momentarily, thinking, *As if she showed the slightest hint of conceit!* Mirah remained silent, waiting for the verdict. Finally, Klesmer continued, "I would not advise—and I urge you not to pursue singing in any space larger than a private drawing-room. But you will excel there. In London, that is one of the finest careers available. Lessons will follow. Will you join me for a private concert at my house on Wednesday?"

"Oh, I shall be grateful," Mirah said, pressing her hands together. "I would rather earn my bread that way than through anything more public. I will strive to improve. What should I focus on?"

Klesmer emitted a series of guttural noises that sounded like words bitten in half before swallowing them, shaking his fingers rhythmically. Then, clearly, he spoke: "I shall introduce you to Astorga. He is the father of good singing and will guide you." Turning to Mrs. Meyrick, he added, "Mrs. Klesmer will call before Wednesday, with your permission."

"We shall consider that a great kindness," Mrs. Meyrick replied.

"You will sing to her," Klesmer instructed, returning his attention to Mirah. "She is a thorough musician, possessing a soul with more ears than you often find in a professional. Your singing will satisfy her. 'Vor den Wissenden sich stellen,' you know the rest?"

"'Sicher ist's in allen Fälle,'" Mirah recited promptly.

Klesmer nodded approvingly, "Schön!" and extended his hand for a final farewell. He had chosen the most delicate method of praise possible, earning the total esteem of the Meyrick girls.

Yet, as he prepared to leave, he suddenly fixed his gaze on Mab. "That young lady is musical, I see!"

Mab flushed crimson, feeling scorched.

"Yes," Mirah answered on her behalf.

"And she has a touch."

"Oh, please, Mirah—it's a scramble, not a touch!" Mab cried in anguish, terrified that this divine judge might now demand she play the piano. Her heart melted like wax.

But this was a trivial price for the amazement of her joy when Klesmer turned benignly to Mrs. Meyrick. "Will she like to accompany Miss Lapidoth and hear the music on Wednesday?"

"There could hardly be a greater pleasure for her," Mrs. Meyrick assured him. "She will be most grateful."
