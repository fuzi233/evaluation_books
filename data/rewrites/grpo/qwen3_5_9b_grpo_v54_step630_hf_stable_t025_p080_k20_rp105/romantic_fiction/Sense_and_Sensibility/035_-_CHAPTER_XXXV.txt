Elinor's curiosity regarding Mrs. Ferrars was finally sated. She had discovered in the older woman everything that rendered a deeper alliance between their families not merely difficult, but impossible. She had witnessed enough of Mrs. Ferrars's pride, her meanness, and her rigid, unyielding prejudice against herself to fully comprehend the labyrinth of obstacles that had delayed Edward and her own union. Yet, she had seen almost enough to be grateful, for her own sake, that a greater barrier remained intact—one that spared her from the torment of depending on Mrs. Ferrars's caprice or seeking her approval. Perhaps she did not quite rejoice that Edward was bound to Lucy, but she resolved that, had Lucy been more amiable, she *ought* to have rejoiced.

She marveled at how Lucy's spirits could be so buoyed by Mrs. Ferrars's mere civility. How could her vanity be so blinded that she mistook attention paid solely because she was *not* Elinor for a personal compliment? How could she draw encouragement from a preference granted only because her true situation was unknown? These truths were evident in Lucy's eyes at the time, and confirmed the following morning when, at Lucy's specific request, Lady Middleton deposited her in Berkeley Street to convey her happiness.

The timing proved fortunate, for a message from Mrs. Palmer shortly after Lucy's arrival whisked Mrs. Jennings away.

"My dear friend," Lucy exclaimed the moment they were alone, "I come to speak of my happiness. Was there anything so flattering as Mrs. Ferrars's treatment of me yesterday? She was so excessively affable! You know how I dreaded the thought of seeing her; yet the instant I was introduced, her behavior radiated such affection that it seemed she had instantly taken a fancy to me. Was it not so? You saw it all; were you not struck?"

"She was certainly very civil to you."

"Civil! Did you see nothing but civility? I saw far more. Such kindness reserved exclusively for me! No pride, no hauteur, and your sister was just the same—all sweetness!"

Elinor wished to change the subject, but Lucy pressed her to admit she had cause for happiness. Reluctantly, Elinor continued.

"Undoubtedly, if they had known your engagement, nothing could be more flattering than their treatment of you; but since that was not the case—"

"I guessed you would say so," Lucy interrupted quickly. "But there was no reason in the world why Mrs. Ferrars should seem to like me if she did not, and her liking me is everything. You won't talk me out of my satisfaction. I am sure it will all end well, with no difficulties whatsoever. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is your sister. They are both delightful! I wonder I never heard you say how agreeable Mrs. Dashwood was!"

Elinor had no answer and offered none.

"Are you ill, Miss Dashwood? You seem low; you don't speak. Are you well?"

"I have never been in better health."

"I am glad of it with all my heart, but you certainly did not look it. I should be sorry to have *you* ill; you have been my greatest comfort in the world. Heaven knows what I should have done without your friendship."

Elinor attempted a civil reply, though doubting its success. It seemed to satisfy Lucy, who immediately responded,

"I am perfectly convinced of your regard for me, and next to Edward's love, it is my greatest comfort. Poor Edward! But now, there is one good thing: we shall be able to meet, and meet often. Lady Middleton delights in Mrs. Dashwood, so we shall be frequent visitors in Harley Street. Edward spends half his time with his sister, besides, Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars will be visiting soon. Both Mrs. Ferrars and your sister have said more than once they would always be glad to see me. They are such charming women! If you ever tell your sister what I think of her, you cannot praise her too highly."

But Elinor refused to give her any encouragement to hope she *would* tell her sister. Lucy persisted.

"I am sure I would have seen it in a moment if Mrs. Ferrars had disliked me. If she had merely offered a formal courtesy, said nothing, and never looked at me pleasantly afterward—you know what I mean—if I had been treated in that forbidding manner, I would have given up in despair. I could not have stood it. Where she *does* dislike, I know it is most violent."

Elinor was prevented from replying to this civil triumph when the door opened and the servant announced Mr. Ferrars. Edward walked in immediately.

It was an awkward moment; the countenance of each betrayed it. They all looked foolish, and Edward seemed equally inclined to retreat from the room as to advance further. The very circumstance they had most anxiously sought to avoid had fallen upon them. They were not only all three together, but together without the relief of any other person. The ladies recovered themselves first. It was not Lucy's role to put herself forward, and the appearance of secrecy must be maintained. She could only offer a look of tenderness and, after a slight address, said no more.

Elinor, however, had more to do. Anxious for his sake and her own to manage the situation well, she forced herself, after a moment's recollection, to welcome him with a look and manner that were almost easy, almost open. Another struggle, another effort, improved them further. She would not allow Lucy's presence, nor the consciousness of some injustice toward herself, to deter her from expressing her happiness at seeing him and her regret at having been away when he called previously in Berkeley Street. She would not be frightened from paying him those attentions due to a friend and almost a relative by Lucy's observant eyes, though she soon perceived them watching her narrowly.

Her manners gave Edward some reassurance, and he had the courage to sit down; but his embarrassment still exceeded that of the ladies in a proportion the case rendered reasonable, though rare for his sex. His heart did not possess the indifference of Lucy's, nor could his conscience find the ease Elinor's did.

Lucy, with a demure and settled air, seemed determined to contribute nothing to the comfort of the others and remained silent. Almost everything said proceeded from Elinor, who was obliged to volunteer all information regarding her mother's health, their arrival in town, and the like—details Edward ought to have inquired about but never did.

Her exertions did not stop there. Soon, she felt so heroically disposed that she determined, under the pretext of fetching Marianne, to leave the others to themselves. She did so, and in the handsomest manner possible, loitering on the landing-place for several minutes with high-minded fortitude before descending to her sister. Once that was done, however, Edward's raptures had to cease, for Marianne's joy hurried her into the drawing-room immediately. Her pleasure in seeing him was, like every other feeling, strong in itself and strongly spoken. She met him with a hand ready to be taken and a voice expressing the affection of a sister.

"Dear Edward!" she cried. "This is a moment of great happiness! This would almost make amends for everything!"

Edward tried to return her kindness as it deserved, but before such witnesses, he dared not say half what he truly felt. Again they all sat down, and for a moment or two, silence reigned. Marianne looked with speaking tenderness, alternating between Edward and Elinor, regretting only that their delight was checked by Lucy's unwelcome presence. Edward was the first to speak, noting Marianne's altered looks and expressing his fear that London was not agreeing with her.

"Oh, don't think of me!" she replied with spirited earnestness, though tears filled her eyes. "Don't think of *my* health. Elinor is well; you see. That must be enough for us both."

This remark did little to ease Edward or Elinor, nor did it conciliate Lucy, who looked up at Marianne with no very benignant expression.

"Do you like London?" Edward asked, willing to introduce another subject.

"Not at all. I expected much pleasure, but found none. The sight of you, Edward, is the only comfort it has afforded; and thank Heaven! you are what you always were!"

She paused; no one spoke.

"I think, Elinor," she added presently, "we must employ Edward to take care of us in our return to Barton. In a week or two, I suppose, we shall be going; and I trust Edward will not be very unwilling to accept the charge."

Poor Edward muttered something, but no one knew what it was, not even himself. Marianne, seeing his agitation and easily tracing it to whatever cause best pleased her, was perfectly satisfied and soon changed the topic.

"We spent such a day in Harley Street yesterday! So dull, so wretchedly dull! But I have much to say to you on that head which cannot be said now."

With this admirable discretion, she deferred the assurance of finding their mutual relatives more disagreeable than ever and being particularly disgusted with his mother until they were more private.

"But why were you not there, Edward? Why did you not come?"

"I was engaged elsewhere."

"Engaged! But what was that when such friends were to be met?"

"Perhaps, Miss Marianne," cried Lucy, eager to take revenge, "you think young men never stand upon engagements if they have no mind to keep them, little as well as great."

Elinor was very angry, but Marianne seemed entirely insensible to the sting. Calmly, she replied,

"Not so, indeed; for seriously speaking, I am very sure conscience kept Edward from Harley Street. I really believe he *has* the most delicate conscience in the world; the most scrupulous in performing every engagement, however minute, and however it may make against his interest or pleasure. He is the most fearful of giving pain, of wounding expectation, and the most incapable of being selfish of anybody I ever saw. Edward, it is so, and I will say it. What! Are you never to hear yourself praised? Then you must be no friend of mine; for those who accept my love and esteem must submit to my open commendation."

The nature of her commendation, in this instance, happened to be particularly ill-suited to the feelings of two-thirds of her audience, and so unexhilarating to Edward that he very soon rose to go away.

"Going so soon!" said Marianne. "My dear Edward, this must not be."

Drawing him aside, she whispered her persuasion that Lucy could not stay much longer. Even this encouragement failed; he insisted on leaving, and Lucy, who would have outstayed him had his visit lasted two hours, soon departed.

"What can bring her here so often?" Marianne asked upon her leaving. "Could she not see that we wanted her gone? How teasing to Edward!"

"Why so? We were all his friends, and Lucy has been the longest known to him of any. It is but natural that he should like to see her as well as ourselves."

Marianne looked at her steadily and said, "You know, Elinor, that this is a kind of talking which I cannot bear. If you only hope to have your assertion contradicted, as I must suppose to be the case, you ought to recollect that I am the last person in the world to do it. I cannot descend to be tricked out of assurances that are not really wanted."

She then left the room, and Elinor dared not follow her to say more. Bound by her promise of secrecy to Lucy, she could give no information that would convince Marianne. Painful as the consequences of Marianne continuing in an error might be, she was obliged to submit to it. All she could hope was that Edward would not often expose himself or her to the distress of hearing Marianne's mistaken warmth, nor to the repetition of any other part of the pain that had attended their recent meeting—and this she had every reason to expect.
