The air in the bedroom was thick with the scent of lavender and the metallic tang of dried blood. Harry Warrington lay in the bed, a pale, beautiful object suspended between life and death, his shoulder recently reset by Colonel Lambert's steady hands. The Colonel had bled him, a crude medical necessity that left the boy's arm stark white against the sheets, a visual proof of the violence inflicted upon the flesh. To Mrs. Lambert, watching him breathe in a sweet, inarticulate slumber, he was simply a pretty lad, a stranger who had fallen through the cracks of the world into her sanctuary. She compared him silently to her own sons, finding none half so handsome, unaware that the comparison was a trap she had willingly stepped into.

Outside the door, the Colonel held his wife's arm, guiding her down the broad oak staircase lined with the painted ghosts of past Lamberts. They moved with a rhythm that suggested a shared conspiracy, stepping carefully around the sleeping boy's fate. In the dressing closet, smelling of fresh lavender and old wood, the Colonel spoke with a waggish humor that masked the gravity of the situation. He teased his wife about her burning desire to sell her daughters, framing marriage as a transactional exchange of goods, yet his tone betrayed a deep-seated reluctance to part with them. He questioned whether good husbands were as common as sacks of coal found by the roadside, a cynical observation that clashed with the providential coincidence of the Virginian's arrival at their very gate.

Mrs. Lambert sighed, admitting defeat against her husband's penetrating gaze. She confessed that she had seen him in the candlelight three nights prior, a vision that had haunted her dreams alongside memories of his wounds in Minorca and Scotland. The Colonel dismissed her visions as the natural superstition of a woman who projected her fears onto empty space, yet he allowed her to hold his arm, walking together into the adjacent room where the French lessons awaited.

There, the atmosphere shifted from the somber reality of injury to the artificial brightness of theater. The Colonel treated his daughter Theo with mock reverence, addressing her as Madam, elevating her status through the pretense of ceremony. They read scenes from Molière's *Tartuffe*, the text unfolding in a language of polite deception that mirrored the real-world charades they navigated daily. Orgon spoke of recognizing Mariane's gentle spirit, praising her worthiness as a spouse, while Mariane responded with the submissive laughter of a girl trapped in a script written by a father. The Colonel laughed aloud at the end of the passage, turning to his wife with a grin that illuminated the room, oblivious to the fact that the play they were performing was a rehearsal for the very tragedies awaiting them all. The performance ended not with applause, but with the quiet settling of the house, leaving the strangers—the Virginian in the bed, the parents in the closet—to exist in the same space, separated by silence and the unspoken weight of the future.
