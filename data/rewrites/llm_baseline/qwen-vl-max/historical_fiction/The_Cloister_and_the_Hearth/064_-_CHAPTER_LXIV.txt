In a sumptuously appointed chamber, where the floor was strewn with the hides of beasts—striped and mottled in wild patterns—a woman sat rigidly, her arms outstretched before her, fingers curled into half-fists. Her face, pale one moment and flushed the next, betrayed a storm within; her foot tapped restlessly against the polished stone. Then, softly, a curtain parted as a servant stepped forward. The lady’s brow darkened.

“Altezza,” the maid whispered, trembling, “the man has arrived.”

She nodded curtly, snatching up a small black mask from the table beside her and slipping it over her features. Instantly, her color drained away, leaving her cheeks like cold marble. The contrast between the deep shadow of the mask and her pallid skin was unsettling, almost spectral.

A man entered, bowing low, his movements stiff with deference. He was unremarkable: short hair, round head, plainly dressed but neatly so. His face offered no charm, nor any sign of malice—save for his eyes, which darted about like those of a bird caught in a trap.

After a long series of bows, he stood before her, waiting.

“They have told you why you are here?” she asked.

“Yes, Signora.”

“And did they agree on the price?”

“Yes, Signora. The full sum is promised—enough to purchase the greater vendetta. Unless, by your grace, you would settle for the lesser.”

“I do not understand,” she said.

“The first time,” he replied gently, “many do not. The lesser vendetta means only the death of the body. We wait—perhaps outside a church, or when he is unsuspecting—and strike. But the greater vendetta… that takes both body and soul. We catch him while he is still hot from sin, unrepentant, and then we end him in such a way that his soul too perishes. It was more common once, but now fewer seek it.”

“Then silence his tongue,” she said, voice tight with fury. “Let his traitorous heart cease beating. But I care nothing for his soul.”

“As you wish, Signora. The man who sent me knew nothing of him—his name, his home, or his face. From whom shall I learn these things?”

“From me.”

At this, the assassin faltered slightly, his expression shifting for the first time to one of caution. “Be careful, my lady,” he murmured. “Such details are dangerous.”

“I am not afraid,” she answered, her voice rising. “Listen well. He is young, tall, with auburn hair and deep blue eyes. His face is honest—so honest it could fool a saint. He lives in the Via Claudia, in the corner house—the glover’s shop. There are only three men there: he; a painter, short and dark; and a slim boy. The one who has wronged me is a stranger—fair-haired, taller than you.”

The bravo listened intently, every sense alert.

“That is enough,” he said at last. “Does he frequent any secret place where I might meet him?”

“My spies tell me he has lately gone to the banks of the Tiber after dusk—doubtless to meet his lover, who calls me her rival. There, kill him. Let her come and find him—dead, broken, the smooth, heartless, insolent thief.”

“Do not distress yourself, Signora,” the man said quietly. “He will betray no woman again.”

“I know not that,” she snapped. “He carries a sword and knows how to use it. He is young, bold, and strong.”

“None of that will save him.”

“Are you so certain? What weapons do you carry?”

He raised his hand, revealing a steel gauntlet. “We strike hard. We must protect ourselves. This is our hammer.”

Then, with a slow motion, he opened his doublet, showing the glint of chainmail beneath. Finally, he drew forth a slender stiletto and laid it upon the table with a flourish.

The lady shivered—not from fear, but from something deeper. She reached out, her white fingers brushing the blade. She tested its edge against her own fingertip.

“Beware, madam,” the bravo warned.

“Is it poisoned?” she asked.

“God forbid! We do not steal life with poison. We take it with steel. But this blade is freshly honed. I feared for your delicate skin.”

“She has skin as fair as mine,” she said, a fleeting tenderness crossing her face—gone in an instant. “But his heart is black as soot. Tell me—do I not act rightly in destroying a traitor who slanders me?”

“You will decide that with your confessor,” he replied. “I am merely an instrument—like this knife.”

She seemed not to hear. Her thoughts wandered elsewhere.

“Oh, how I could have loved him—truly, to the death. And now I hate him. Fool! He thinks he can toy with princes, scorn them one moment, fawn on them the next, prefer the lowest scum to nobility, and make us all laughstocks!”

She looked up sharply. “Why do you delay? Go! Revenge me!”

“It is custom,” he said, “to receive half the payment before the deed.”

“Ah—I forgot. Your revenge is bought.” She pushed a bag across the table. “Here is more than half. When the blow is struck, return for the rest.”

“You will see me soon, Signora,” he said, bowing deeply, then retreating from the room.

Left alone, the princess remained seated—masked, arms stretched forward, nails digging into the table’s surface. She sat as though carved from stone, like the mythic sphinx, or a tigress guarding her young. Yet now, with the assassin gone, a coldness crept through her veins. Doubts stirred—quiet, creeping misgivings, the first whispers of regret.

Gerard and Margaret were ahead of their time. This was the true Middle Ages: proud, passionate, cruel, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, without principle—and utterly ignorant. Power was the curse of such a creature. Without it, her nerves would have curbed her rage, her threats outweighed by hesitation. But power gave her hands—male hands—to wield. And so, a weak, furious woman became terrifying. In this case, she had sharpened a dagger for a man already drowning in despair.
