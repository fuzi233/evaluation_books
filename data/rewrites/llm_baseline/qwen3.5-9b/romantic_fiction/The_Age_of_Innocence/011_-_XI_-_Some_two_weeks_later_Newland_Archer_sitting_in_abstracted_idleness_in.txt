Two weeks had passed since Newland Archer last saw the Countess Olenska. Sitting in the quiet, dust-moted stillness of his private office within the chambers of Letterblair, Lamson, and Low, he drifted in a state of abstracted idleness until the heavy oak door swung open and the senior partner entered.

Old Mr. Letterblair, the legal guardian of three generations of New York aristocracy, sat throned behind his mahogany desk, his face etched with a perplexity that seemed to deepen with every breath. He stroked his closely clipped white whiskers and ran a trembling hand through the rumpled grey hair atop his jutting forehead. To Newland, watching him from across the room, the elder lawyer resembled nothing so much as the Family Physician, baffled by a patient whose symptoms refused to fit any known diagnosis.

"My dear sir—" Mr. Letterblair always insisted on addressing Archer with the formal distance of "sir"—"I have summoned you regarding a delicate matter. One I prefer, for the present, not to discuss with Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood."

These were the names of the other senior partners. In the ancient legal firms of New York, where tradition held sway over personnel changes, the partners listed on the letterhead were often ghosts; Mr. Letterblair, for instance, was professionally his own grandfather. Leaning back, his brow furrowed deeper, he added, "It concerns family reasons."

Archer lifted his gaze. "The Mingott family," Mr. Letterblair supplied with a tight, explanatory smile and a stiff bow. "Mrs. Manson Mingott called upon me yesterday. Her granddaughter, the Countess Olenska, intends to sue her husband for divorce. Certain documents have come into my possession."

He paused, tapping a rhythmic beat on the desk surface. "Given your impending alliance with the family, I wished to consult you—to deliberate on the case—with you before proceeding further."

A sudden heat flushed the temples of Newland Archer. He recalled seeing the Countess only once since their brief encounter at the Opera, where she had appeared less vibrant and demanding, retreating from his mental forefront as May Welland reclaimed her central position in his life. He hadn't heard whispers of divorce since Janey's casual, offhand remark, dismissing the notion as mere gossip. Divorce was theoretically repugnant to him, much as it was to his mother, and he was irritated that Mr. Letterblair—perhaps spurred by the late Catherine Mingott—should so blatantly attempt to drag him into the fray. After all, there were numerous Mingott lawyers available for such tasks, and he was not yet a member of the clan by marriage.

Waiting for the senior partner to elaborate, Archer watched as Mr. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and produced a thick packet of paper. "If you would glance through these..."

"I beg your pardon, sir," Archer interrupted, frowning. "Precisely because of the prospective relationship, I would prefer you consult Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood."

Mr. Letterblair blinked, taken aback and slightly wounded. It was rare for a junior partner to decline such an opportunity. He bowed politely. "I respect your scruples, sir; however, in this instance, true delicacy demands you follow my instruction. Indeed, the suggestion does not originate with me, but with Mrs. Manson Mingott and her son. I have met with Lovell Mingott, and also Mr. Welland. They all pointed to you."

A spark of irritation ignited in Archer's chest. For the past fortnight, he had allowed events to drift lazily, letting May's radiant nature eclipse the persistent pressure of the Mingott expectations. But this summons from the formidable matriarch stirred him to a realization of what the family believed they were entitled to demand from a future son-in-law, and he resented the role being forced upon him.

"Her uncles should handle this," he said sharply.

"They have," Mr. Letterblair replied smoothly. "The family has deliberated. They oppose the Countess's course; yet she remains firm and insists on a legal opinion."

The young man fell silent, refusing to open the packet in his hand. "Does she intend to remarry?"

"It appears so, though she denies it."

"Then—"

"Will you oblige me, Mr. Archer, by reviewing these papers first? Once we have discussed the case, I shall provide my assessment."

Reluctantly, Archer accepted the unwelcome documents. Since their final meeting, he had subconsciously aided in shedding the burden of Madame Olenska. Their solitary hour by the fire had created a fleeting intimacy, shattered providentially by the Duke of St. Austrey's arrival with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers and the Countess's boisterous welcome. Two days later, he had witnessed the comedy of her restoration to favor among the van der Luydens, telling himself with a touch of bitterness that a lady capable of thanking powerful elders so effectively for a bouquet of flowers required neither private solace nor public advocacy from a man of his limited stature. Viewing matters thus simplified his own predicament and polished the dim virtues of domesticity. He could not imagine May Welland, in any conceivable crisis, airing her private troubles or confiding in strangers. She had never seemed fairer than in the week following, leading him to yield to her desire for a prolonged engagement—a response that had disarmed his pleas for haste.

"You know," he had argued gently, "when it comes to the point, your parents have always allowed you your way since you were a child."

She had answered with her most piercing clarity: "Yes; and that is precisely what makes it so difficult to refuse the very last thing they will ever ask of me as a child."

That was the quintessential New York tone; the kind of assurance he hoped to hear from his wife forevermore. If one breathed the New York air constantly, anything less crisp felt suffocating.

Yet, the papers he retreated to examine offered little factual clarity, instead plunging him into an atmosphere where he gasped for breath. They comprised primarily an exchange of correspondence between Count Olenski's solicitors and a French legal firm engaged to settle the Countess's finances. There was also a brief letter from the Count to his wife. Upon finishing it, Newland Archer stood abruptly, shoved the documents back into their envelope, and returned to Mr. Letterblair's office.

"Here are the letters, sir," he said, his voice tight. "If you wish, I will see Madame Olenska."

"Thank you—thank you, Mr. Archer. Please join us for dinner tonight if you are free, and we shall discuss the matter afterward, should you wish to call on our client tomorrow."

Newland walked straight home that afternoon. It was a winter evening of crystal-clear transparency, with an innocent young moon hovering above the rooftops. He needed to fill his soul's lungs with this pure radiance, avoiding conversation with anyone until he and Mr. Letterblair were locked away together post-dinner. There was no other option: he must see the Countess himself rather than allow her secrets to be laid bare to others.

A profound wave of compassion washed away his previous indifference and impatience. She stood before him as an exposed, pitiful figure, needing salvation from further self-inflicted wounds in her reckless leaps against fate. He remembered what she had told him about Mrs. Welland's wish to avoid anything "unpleasant" in her history, wincing at the thought that perhaps this mindset was what kept the New York air so sterile.

*"Are we merely Pharisees after all?"* he wondered, struggling to reconcile his instinctive revulsion at human vice with his equally instinctive pity for human frailty.

For the first time, he recognized how elemental his own principles truly were. He was known as a young man unafraid of risks, aware that his secret liaison with the unfortunate Mrs. Thorley Rushworth had not been entirely hidden, lending him an air of romantic adventure. But Mrs. Rushworth was "that kind of woman"—foolish, vain, and inherently clandestine, far more drawn to the danger of the affair than to the charms he possessed. When the truth of this had dawned on him, it had nearly broken his heart; now, it seemed the saving grace of the situation.

In short, the affair belonged to the category most young men of his generation experienced, emerging from it with clear consciences and an unshaken belief in the abyssal divide between the women one loved and respected versus those one merely enjoyed—and pitied. This perspective was carefully reinforced by their mothers, aunts, and other elderly female relatives, all sharing Mrs. Archer's conviction that when "such things occurred," it was undoubtedly foolish of the man, yet invariably criminal of the woman. Every elderly lady Archer knew viewed any woman who loved imprudently as necessarily unscrupulous and calculating, trapping the simple-minded male in her grasp. The solution, they agreed, was to persuade him, as early as possible, to marry a nice girl and trust her to manage him.

However, in the complex old European communities, Archer began to suspect, love-problems were far less straightforward and easier to categorize. Wealthy, idle, ornamental societies inevitably generated many such scenarios; there might even be one where a woman, naturally sensitive and aloof, driven by circumstance, sheer defenselessness, and loneliness, was drawn into a bond deemed inexcusable by conventional standards.
