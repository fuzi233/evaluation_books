On the first of May, we—twenty boarders and four teachers—were summoned to rise at five in the morning, dressed and ready by six, under the command of M. le Professeur Emanuel, who would lead us on an excursion into the countryside. It was the day he had promised to take us for breakfast beyond the city walls. Though I had not originally been included in the invitation—indeed, I had been left out deliberately—I now raised the matter gently, only to be met with a sharp tug on my ear. I did not dare press further. “Je vous conseille de vous faire prier,” M. Emanuel said, his tone imperious, as if warning me against any more questions. One such reprimand was enough. I decided to go.

The morning dawned clear and still, like summer’s breath. Birds sang in the garden, and a soft dew-mist hung over the grass, promising warmth. We all agreed it would be hot, and with joy we shed our heavy coats, donning lighter clothes suited to the season. The fashion of the day was simple yet refined: clean cotton prints, light straw hats, carefully trimmed by French hands—modest, unassuming, yet perfectly suited to each wearer. No one wore faded silks or second-hand finery. At six, the bell rang cheerfully, and we descended the stairs in a steady stream, through the carré, down the corridor, into the vestibule.

There stood our professor, transformed. Gone was his stern paletôt and severe bonnet-grec; in their place, a young-looking belted blouse and a cheerful straw hat. He greeted us all with warm good-morrow, and most of us returned his smile with gratitude. We were lined up and set off.

The streets were quiet, the boulevards peaceful as open fields. I believe we were all happy as we walked along. M. Emanuel had a gift for stirring joy when he chose, just as he could awaken fear when he wished. He did not lead or follow, but moved among us, speaking to each in turn—more freely to his favorites, but never entirely ignoring those he disliked.

I had my own reason for wishing to remain unnoticed. Paired with Ginevra Fanshawe, I carried her slight but ever-present arm across mine—a burden both sweet and taxing. She remained in excellent health, and I confess it was no small task to bear the weight of her beauty. More than once that day, I wished there had been less of it. Still, I used her presence as a shield, shifting my position whenever I heard M. Paul approaching, placing her between us like a barrier.

My motive? The pink print dress I wore. Under ordinary circumstances, it might have seemed harmless. But under M. Paul’s gaze, it felt like walking through a field with a red border while a bull grazed nearby.

For a time, my strategy worked—along with careful adjustments to my black silk scarf. But soon he noticed that no matter which side he approached from, Miss Fanshawe remained beside me. Their acquaintance had never been smooth. His temper flared at her English accent; she found him rough, intrusive, repulsive. They clashed in every way.

After about the sixth attempt to shift positions, he suddenly leaned forward, fixed his eyes on me, and demanded sharply, “Qu’est-ce que c’est? Vous me jouez des tours?”

Before I could answer, he understood. I tried to hide behind the fringe of my scarf, but it was too late.

“A-h-h! c’est la robe rose!” he exclaimed—his voice carrying the sudden fury of a startled beast.

“It’s only cotton,” I said quickly. “Cheaper, and washes better than any other colour.”

“Et Mademoiselle Lucy est coquette comme dix Parisiennes,” he replied. “A-t-on jamais vu une Anglaise pareille? Regardez plutôt son chapeau, et ses gants, et ses brodequins!”

These things were no different from what the others wore—perhaps even plainer. But once he had seized upon this point, I braced myself for a lecture. Yet it never came. Instead, he offered only a single flash of amusement—a fleeting, teasing smile—and then said, “Courage! À vrai dire, je ne suis pas fâché. Peut-être même suis-je content qu’on s’est fait si belle pour ma petite fête.”

“But my dress is not beautiful, Monsieur—only clean.”

“J’aime la propreté,” he said.

And so, the storm passed. The sun of good humour prevailed. Clouds scudded away before its glow.

We reached the country, where they called it “les bois et les petits sentiers.” A month later, these woods and paths would be dusty and bare. Now, in May’s greenness and morning hush, they looked delightful. We arrived at a well, encircled by lime trees arranged in neat order, like a scene from Labassecour’s gardens. Here we paused. We sat on the grassy slope around the well, M. Emanuel taking his place among us, allowing us to gather close. Some approached eagerly—mostly children. Others kept their distance. And some, whose affection had softened even their fear, stayed farthest of all.

He began to tell a story.

He had a rare gift for narration—simple yet powerful, direct yet rich, spoken in a voice that charmed children and captivated scholars alike. There were moments of beauty in that tale—glimpses of feeling, strokes of description that settled deep into my mind and never faded. He painted a twilight scene—so vivid, so real, I can still see it clearly, as though drawn by a master hand.

I had no talent for improvisation. That very lack made me marvel all the more at someone who possessed it fully. M. Emanuel was not the kind to write books—but I had heard him speak with such effortless abundance, such mental richness, that his words felt like gold dust scattered on the wind. His mind was my library. Whenever he opened it to me, I entered a realm of bliss.

Though I was intellectually limited, unable to endure long reading, his thoughts were medicine to my spirit. His ideas cleared my vision, strengthened my inner sight. I often thought how wonderful it would be for someone who loved him deeply to collect and preserve these treasures—these fleeting gems he tossed so carelessly into the air.

When the story ended, he walked toward the knoll where Ginevra and I sat apart. In his usual manner—he never waited for volunteers—he asked, “Were you interested?”

“Yes,” I answered, as always, quietly.

“Was it good?”

“Very good.”

“Yet I could not write that down,” he said.

“Why not, Monsieur?”

“I hate mechanical labour. I hate to sit still. But I could dictate it to an amanuensis—if I found the right one. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me, if I asked?”

“Monsieur would be too quick. You’d urge me, and grow angry if my pen couldn’t keep pace.”

“Try one day. Let us see the monster I become under such conditions. But now, no dictation. I have another use for you. See that farmhouse yonder?”

“Surrounded by trees? Yes.”

“There we’ll breakfast. While the farmer’s wife prepares café au lait in a caldron, you and five others will butter half a hundred rolls.”

We formed back into line and marched to the farm. On seeing us, the farmer surrendered without resistance. Knives, plates, and fresh butter were provided. Six of us—chosen by M. Emanuel—set to work under his direction, spreading butter on a large basket of rolls that the baker had delivered in anticipation.

Coffee and chocolate were already hot. Cream and fresh eggs were added. M. Emanuel, generous as ever, wanted to order ham and jam, but some of us—perhaps presuming on our influence—objected, calling it reckless waste.

He railed at us, calling us “des ménagères avares.” But we let him speak, managing the meal our own way.

He stood by the hearth, watching with a pleased expression. He was happiest when others were joyful. He thrived on movement, laughter, abundance. When we asked where he would sit, he smiled and said, “You know well I am your slave, and you my tyrants. I dare not choose a chair without your permission.” So we placed him in the farmer’s great chair at the head of the table.

It was easy to love him, despite his storms. At his worst, it was only his nerves that flared—not his heart. Soothe him, understand him, comfort him, and he became gentle as a lamb. Harmless to all but the foolish, the stubborn, or the indifferent.

Always mindful of his faith, he asked the youngest girl to say a prayer before we ate. He crossed himself with the same devotion as a woman. I had never seen him pray before. He did it simply, with childlike sincerity. Watching him, I smiled. He caught my gaze and reached out his hand. “Donnez-moi la main! I see we worship the same God, in the same spirit, though by different rites.”

Most of his fellow professors were freethinkers, skeptics, even atheists—men whose lives bore scrutiny poorly. M. Emanuel was different. He was more like a knight of old—religious in his way, upright, pure. Innocent children and lovely youth were safe near him. He had strong passions, deep feelings, but his honor and piety held them in check. He was a man of strength tempered by grace.

Breakfast was joyful—not empty chatter, but genuine merriment. M. Paul led it, shaped it, elevated it. His lively nature flourished, unburdened by men or constraints. Surrounded only by women and children, he was free to be himself—and it was a pleasant self.

After the meal, we were free to play in the meadows. A few stayed to help the farmer’s wife put away her dishes. M. Paul called me aside, asking me to come sit with him beneath a tree, where he could watch the others frolic across the pasture. He would read aloud while he smoked his cigar.

He sat on a rustic bench. I sat at the base of the tree. I read from a pocket edition of Corneille—something I didn’t particularly enjoy, but he liked. He listened with a calm sweetness that contrasted sharply with his usual impetuous nature. Deep peace filled his blue eyes, smoothed his broad brow.

I, too, was happy—happy in the bright day, happier in his presence, happiest in his kindness.

After a while, he asked if I wouldn’t rather join my companions.

“No,” I said. “I’m content where I am.”

Then he asked, “If I were your brother, would you always stay with me?”

“I believe I would,” I said—and I meant it.

Again, he asked, “If I were to leave Villette, travel far away, would you be sorry?”

I dropped Corneille. I could not answer.

“Petite sœur,” he said softly, “how long could you remember me if we were parted?”

“That, Monsieur, I cannot say. Because I do not know how long I shall remember anything earthly.”

“If I went beyond the seas for two, three, five years—would you welcome me back?”

“Monsieur, how could I live in the meantime?”

“Pourtant j’ai été pour vous bien dur, bien exigeant.”

I hid my face in the book. Tears covered the pages.

Why did he talk like this? I asked.

He said he would not talk so again, and tried to cheer me with gentle words. But the tenderness he showed me for the rest of the day touched something deep within me. It was too soft. Too sorrowful. I would have preferred his usual abruptness, his whimsy, his anger.

When noon arrived—the day glowing like June—our shepherd gathered his flock and began the journey home. We had walked nearly a league. The children were tired. Most spirits flagged at the thought of walking back over hard, dusty roads.

But this had been anticipated. Just beyond the farm, we met two large vehicles—特意 hired for school parties. With careful arrangement, we all fit inside. Within an hour, M. Paul brought us safely back to Rue Fossette.

It had been a pleasant day. Almost perfect. Only briefly marred by a shadow of melancholy.

That evening, as sunset painted the sky, I saw M. Emanuel emerge from the front door with Madame Beck. They walked slowly down the central path, talking earnestly. He looked grave, restless. She appeared amazed, pleading, dissuading. I wondered what they discussed. When Madame Beck returned to the house as darkness fell, leaving M. Paul lingering in the garden, I thought to myself: *He called me ‘petite sœur’ today. If he were truly my brother, how I would like to go to him now, ask what troubles him. Look at him—leaning against that tree, arms crossed, brow bent. He needs comfort. Madame does not console—she only warns. What now?*

Suddenly, he broke from stillness. He strode swiftly, purposefully, down the garden. The carré doors were still open. I assumed he was going to water the orange trees, as he sometimes did. But instead, he turned sharply and headed straight for the berceau and the first-classe glass door.

I was in the first classe—watching him from there. But I could not wait. He moved so fast, so strangely. Fear took hold. Without thinking, I fled—my pulse racing, my breath short—until I reached the oratory, now empty.

I stood there, listening, heart pounding, gripped by a nameless dread. I heard him pass through the classrooms, slamming doors impatiently. I heard him enter the refectory, where the “lecture pieuse” was in session. I heard him demand, “Où est Mademoiselle Lucie?”

Just as I gathered courage to go down—just as I prepared to do what I most desired—to meet him—the voice of St. Pierre answered smoothly, falsely: “Elle est au lit.”

And he passed on, stamping with frustration, into the corridor.

There, Madame Beck met him, stopped him, scolded him, escorted him to the street door—and dismissed him.

As the door closed behind him, a sudden shock struck me like a blow. From the beginning, I knew he sought me. I had wanted him too. So why had I run?

What had driven me away? What had pulled me beyond his reach?

He had something to tell me. He was coming to tell me. My ears strained to hear it—and I had made it impossible.

Longing to listen, to comfort—while believing it hopeless—yet when the moment came, I fled as if from death itself.

Well, my madness had its price. Instead of comfort, certainty, the satisfaction I might have won—if only I had faced my fear for two minutes—I received silence, doubt, and bitter suspense.

I carried my punishment to bed, and spent the night counting it.
