CHAPTER XLII. One final visit to Harley Street, where Elinor received her brother’s warm wishes for their journey to Barton without expense, and news that Colonel Brandon would soon follow them to Cleveland, marked the end of the sisters’ time in town with their brother. A polite but distant invitation from Fanny to call at Norland—though it was unlikely they ever would—alongside John’s more heartfelt promise to visit Elinor at Delaford, hinted at future connections. It amused Elinor to notice how everyone seemed eager to send her to Delaford—a place she now found less appealing than ever. Not only did her brother and Mrs. Jennings see it as her future home, but even Lucy had pressed her to come when they parted.

Early in April, shortly after sunrise, the two parties set out from Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, meeting by arrangement along the road. To accommodate Charlotte and her child, their journey would take over two days, while Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon traveled faster and were expected to join them at Cleveland shortly after their arrival.

Marianne, though long eager to leave London, felt a deep sorrow as she said farewell to the house where she had once known such hope and trust in Willoughby—feelings now gone forever. She could not bear the thought of him still there, moving forward with new plans and engagements, unaware or indifferent to her pain. Tears came easily as she left behind the place that held so much of her past.

Elinor, on the other hand, welcomed the departure with quiet relief. She had no lingering ties to leave behind, no one whose absence would trouble her heart. She was glad to escape the constant presence of Lucy, grateful that Marianne had been able to leave without encountering Willoughby again since his marriage, and hopeful that a few months of peace at Barton might help restore her sister’s spirits and bring her own mind greater calm.

Their journey passed smoothly. On the second day, they entered Somerset—once a cherished place in Marianne’s thoughts, now tinged with melancholy. By midday on the third, they arrived at Cleveland.

Cleveland was a large, modern residence nestled on a gentle slope. Though it lacked a park, its gardens were well-kept and spacious, featuring open shrubberies, shaded walks, and winding gravel paths leading to the front. The lawn was dotted with trees, and the house stood sheltered beneath firs, mountain ashes, and acacias, with tall Lombardy poplars forming a natural screen around the service buildings.

Marianne stepped into the house with a heart full of emotion—knowing she was just eighty miles from Barton and not far from Combe Magna. Within minutes, she slipped away, wandering through the blossoming shrubberies to reach a distant hilltop. From the Grecian temple atop it, she gazed across the countryside, letting her eyes rest on the farthest ridges, imagining she could see Combe Magna beyond the horizon. In these moments of quiet sorrow, she found comfort in tears, yet also joy in being at Cleveland. Returning by a different path, she savored the freedom of country life—the ability to wander alone, undisturbed, through beautiful landscapes—and resolved to spend every day exploring the grounds in solitude.

She returned just in time to join the others as they began a tour of the estate. The morning passed pleasantly: strolling through the kitchen garden, admiring the flowering walls, listening to the gardener’s complaints about frost damage; browsing the greenhouse, where Charlotte laughed at the loss of her favorite plants; and visiting the poultry yard, where the antics of hens abandoning nests or falling prey to foxes provided light amusement.

The weather remained fair until after dinner, when steady rain began to fall. Marianne, who had planned evening walks, was disappointed—especially as she had hoped for another twilight stroll to the temple. But even she recognized that heavy rain was no fit weather for walking.

With only a small group, the hours passed quietly. Mrs. Palmer occupied herself with her child, Mrs. Jennings with her needlework, and conversation turned to friends left behind, Lady Middleton’s schedule, and speculation about whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would reach Reading that night. Elinor joined in politely, while Marianne quickly found her way to the library—her usual refuge—and selected a book.

Mrs. Palmer, though lacking in refinement, made everyone feel welcome with her cheerful, open manner. Her warmth more than compensated for her occasional lapses in grace. Her kindness, softened by her gentle appearance, was winning; her quirks, though noticeable, were never pretentious. Elinor could forgive nearly all of her faults—except her laugh, which often seemed too loud and sudden.

The next day, the two gentlemen arrived late for dinner, bringing fresh energy and lively conversation to the gathering. After a long morning of rain, their arrival lifted the mood greatly.

Elinor had seen little of Mr. Palmer before and noticed differences in how he treated her and her sister. Now, in his own home, she found him courteous and proper toward guests, though occasionally brusque with his wife and mother-in-law. He was agreeable enough when not absorbed in his sense of superiority—particularly over Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte. His habits were typical of his age and class: particular about food, irregular in routine, fond of his child despite pretending otherwise, and spending mornings at billiards instead of business. Yet, despite these flaws, Elinor found herself liking him more than expected—though she was relieved not to like him too much. His self-indulgence and pride reminded her of Edward’s opposite qualities: generosity, simplicity, and modesty. She found herself drawing comfort from the memory of his quiet strength.

Colonel Brandon brought news of Edward Ferrars, having recently visited Dorsetshire. Speaking frankly and kindly, he shared details about the parsonage at Delaford—its shortcomings and his plans to improve it. He treated Elinor as both a trusted friend and a valued confidante. His evident pleasure at seeing her again after only ten days, his eagerness to talk, and his respect for her opinions, made Mrs. Jennings’s belief in his affection seem reasonable. Yet Elinor, who had always believed Marianne to be his true favorite, saw no sign of such feelings. She observed him closely—not just his words, but his expressions. When Marianne showed early signs of illness—headache, sore throat, and a cold beginning to take hold—his silent concern, visible in his eyes, revealed a depth of care that escaped Mrs. Jennings entirely. Elinor understood what those looks meant: the tender worry of someone deeply attached.

Two lovely evening walks, on the third and fourth nights, led Marianne deeper into the grounds—beyond the formal paths, into wilder areas where trees were older and grass longer. Despite the dampness, she lingered too long, sitting in wet shoes and stockings. These indulgences, combined with her lack of caution, led to a severe cold. At first she dismissed it, but soon the symptoms worsened—fever, fatigue, cough, and sore throat—forcing her to acknowledge the illness. Remedies were offered from every side, but she refused most, insisting that rest alone would heal her. Only with great effort did Elinor persuade her to try a few simple remedies before retiring for the night.
