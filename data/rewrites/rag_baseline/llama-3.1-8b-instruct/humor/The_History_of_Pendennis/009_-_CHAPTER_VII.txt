As Major Arthur Pendennis alighted at the Fairoaks lodge-gate, his arrival marked the culmination of a protracted journey beset by the vicissitudes of the mail-coach experience. The arduous conditions had tested the limits of his constitution, exacerbated by a stout companion's nocturnal snoring, a widow's excessive indulgence in Jamaica rum, and the incessant din of the vehicle's horn. The Major's physical discomfort was further compounded by the grueling pace of eight miles per hour, a speed that seemed woefully inadequate in the face of his aching joints and the perpetual changes in coachmen, each clamoring for a higher gratuity.

Upon entering the drawing-room, Major Pendennis was greeted by the tranquil atmosphere and the soothing presence of his sister Helen, who sat quietly sewing alongside their niece, little Laura. The Major's demeanor, however, betrayed a certain reserve, a trait honed by years of navigating the complexities of high society, where appearances often held sway over genuine connections. As he acknowledged his sister's presence with a courteous nod, his gaze lingered on the vacant chair, anticipating the arrival of his nephew, Pen, whose impulsive nature had often led to unfortunate incidents, including the infamous incident involving the Major's treasured back teeth, which had been pilfered during a previous jolting mail-coach ride.

With his personal needs attended to by Morgan, his loyal manservant, the Major donned a dignified air, one that commanded respect and deference from those around him. Yet, beneath this façade, the Major's thoughts turned to the impending dinner at Lord Steyne's estate, an event that promised to be a highlight of the social season, despite his own reservations about the aristocrat's dubious reputation. As he settled into the drawing-room, the Major's attention was drawn to the two schoolboys who had shared his journey, their youthful exuberance and camaraderie a poignant reminder of the innocence and vitality that often eluded him in his own adult life.
