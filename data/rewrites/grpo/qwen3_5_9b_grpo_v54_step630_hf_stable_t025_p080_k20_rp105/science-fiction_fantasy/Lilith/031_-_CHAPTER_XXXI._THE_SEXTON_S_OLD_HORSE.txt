I stood and watched the last shimmer of the white leopardess dissolve into the void, then turned to follow my guide—though with a heavy, reluctant heart. Why should I succumb to sleep? Reason, surely, was a constant across all realities, and what logic dictated resting while the living called? Besides, who would wake me? How could I trust in awakening at all, when those within that house allowed morning to bleed into noon, and noon to drown in night, never stirring? I murmured my protest, yet followed, for I knew no other path.

The librarian walked on in silence, and I matched his step with a matching quietude. Time and space flowed past us like water. The sun dipped below the horizon; darkness thickened, carrying with it the chilling breath of the chamber of death. My spirit sank deeper with every passing second. The lean, long-coated figure of my guide began to fade from my sight, his swishing stride through the heather eventually lost to the gloom.

Then, the sound shifted. The slow, rhythmic flapping of a raven's wings replaced the footsteps. At intervals, a firefly or a glowing butterfly rose into the rayless air, tiny lanterns in the deepening night.

By and by, the moon crept slowly across the distant horizon.

"You are weary, are you not, Mr. Vane?" the raven asked, landing heavily on a stone. "You must make acquaintance with the horse that will carry you in the morning!"

He emitted a strange, piercing whistle through his long black beak. Instantly, a dark spot bloomed on the face of the half-risen moon. To my ears came the drumming of swift, soft-galloping hooves, and within moments, thundering from the very disc of the moon itself, emerged the terrible steed. His mane streamed behind him like the crest of a wave fighting the wind, torn seaward in a spray of hoary mist, while the lash of his tail blinded the lunar light. He stood nineteen hands high, massive in bone, taut in skin, hard in muscle—a creature the holy Death himself might choose to ride abroad and slay! Even the moon seemed to regard him with awe; in her eerie light, he appeared a magnificent skeleton, loosely roped together by sheer force of will. Yet, terrifyingly large, he moved with the lightness of a winged insect. As he drew near, his speed slackened, his mane and tail drifting about him, settling into stillness.

Now, I was not merely a lover of horses; I adored every beast I beheld. I had spent my wealth only upon them and never sold one. The sight of this mighty, terrible creature ignited a fierce longing within me to possess him. It was pure greed, rank covetousness—an evil thing in all worlds. I did not mean I would steal him, but that regardless of his sacred purpose, I would have bought him if I could. I laid my hands upon him, stroking the protuberant bones that humped a hide smooth, thin, and shiny as satin—so polished that the very shape of the moon was reflected in it. I fondled his sharp-pointed ears, whispered words into them, and breathed into his red nostrils the warm breath of a man's life. In return, he breathed into mine the raw, primal breath of a horse's life, and we loved one another. What eyes he possessed! Blue-filmy, like the gaze of the dead, yet behind each eye burned a glowing coal! The raven, wings half-extended, watched my courtship of his magnificent steed with evident pleasure.

"That is well! Be friends with him," he said. "He will carry you all the better tomorrow! Now, we must hurry home!"

My desire to ride the horse had grown into a passion.

"May I not mount him at once, Mr. Raven?" I cried.

"By all means!" he answered. "Mount, and ride him home."

The horse bent his head over my shoulder, affectionate. I twisted my hands into his mane and scrambled onto his back, aided by certain sturdy protrusions of his spine.

"He would outspeed any leopard in creation!" I exclaimed.

"Not that way at night," the raven replied. "The road is difficult. But come; loss now will be gain then! To wait is harder than to run, and its reward is fuller. Go on, my son—straight to the cottage. I shall be there as soon as you. It will rejoice my wife's heart to see her son on that horse!"

I sat silent. The horse stood like a block of marble.

"Why do you linger?" the raven asked.

"I long so much to ride after the leopardess," I answered, "that I can scarce restrain myself!"

"You have promised!"

"My debt to the Little Ones appears, I confess, a greater thing than my bond to you."

"Yield to the temptation, and you will bring mischief upon them—and upon yourself also."

"What matters it for me? I love them; and love works no evil. I will go."

But the truth was, I had forgotten the children, infatuated by the horse.

Eyes flashed through the darkness, and I knew Adam stood in his true form beside me. I knew also by his voice that he repressed an indignation almost too strong for him.

"Mr. Vane," he said, "do you not know why you have not yet done anything worth doing?"

"Because I have been a fool," I answered.

"Wherein?"

"In everything."

"Which do you count your most indiscreet action?"

"Bringing the princess to life: I ought to have left her to her just fate."

"Nay, now you talk foolishly! You could not have done otherwise than you did, not knowing she was evil! But you never brought anyone to life! How could you, yourself dead?"

"I dead?" I cried.

"Yes," he answered. "And you will be dead, so long as you refuse to die."

"Back to the old riddling!" I returned scornfully.

"Be persuaded, and go home with me," he continued gently. "The most—nearly the only foolish thing you ever did, was to run from our dead."

I pressed the horse's ribs, and he was off like a sudden wind. I gave him a pat on the side of his neck, and he went about in a sharp-driven curve, "close to the ground, like a cat when scratchingly she wheels about after a mouse," leaning sideways until his mane swept the tops of the heather.

Through the dark, I heard the wings of the raven. Five quick flaps, and he perched on the horse's head. The horse checked himself instantly, ploughing up the earth with his hooves.

"Mr. Vane," croaked the raven, "think what you are doing! Twice already has evil befallen you—once from fear, and once from heedlessness: breach of word is far worse; it is a crime."

"The Little Ones are in frightful peril, and I brought it upon them!" I cried. "But indeed I will not break my word to you. I will return, and spend in your house what nights—what days—what years you please."

"I tell you once more you will do them other than good if you go tonight," he insisted.

But a false sense of power, a sensation with no root and merely vibrated into me from the strength of the horse, had rendered me too stupid to listen!

"Would you take from me my last chance of reparation?" I cried. "This time there shall be no shirking! It is my duty, and I will go—even if I perish for it!"

"Go, then, foolish boy!" he returned, with anger in his croak. "Take the horse, and ride to failure! May it be to humility!"

He spread his wings and flew away. Again I pressed the lean ribs beneath me.

"After the spotted leopardess!" I whispered in his ear.

He turned his head this way and that, snuffing the air; then started, and went a few paces in a slow, undecided walk. Suddenly he quickened his pace; broke into a trot; began to gallop, and in a few moments his speed became tremendous. He seemed to see in the dark; he never stumbled, not once faltered, not once hesitated. I sat as on the ridge of a wave. I felt under me the play of each individual muscle: his joints were so elastic, and his every movement glided so seamlessly into the next, that not once did he jar me. His growing swiftness bore him along until he flew rather than ran. The wind met and passed us like a tornado.

Across the evil hollow we sped like a bolt from an arbalest. No monster lifted its neck; all knew the hooves that thundered over their heads! We rushed up the hills, shot down their farther slopes; from the rocky chasms of the river-bed he did not swerve; he held on over them his fierce, terrible gallop. The moon, halfway up the heavens, gazed with a solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and in the pride of my life, I sat like a king and rode.

We were near the middle of the many channels, my horse clearing one, sometimes two in his stride, gathering himself for a great bounding leap, when the moon reached the keystone of her arch. Then came a wonder and a terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune's wheel bowled by the gods, speeding faster and faster. Like our own moon, this one had a human face, and now the broad forehead, now the chin was uppermost as she rolled. I gazed aghast.

Across the ravines came the howling of wolves. An ugly fear began to invade the hollow places of my heart; my confidence was on the wane! The horse maintained his headlong swiftness, with ears pricked forward and thirsty nostrils exulting in the wind his career created. But there was the moon jolting like an old chariot-wheel down the hill of heaven, with awful boding! She rolled at last over the horizon-edge and disappeared, carrying all her light with her.

The mighty steed was in the act of clearing a wide, shallow channel when we were caught in the net of the darkness. His head dropped; his momentum carried his helpless bulk across, but he fell in a heap on the margin, and where he fell, he lay. I got up, knelt beside him, and felt him all over. Not a bone could I find broken, but he was a horse no more. I sat down on the body and buried my face in my hands.
