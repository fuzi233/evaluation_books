The opening pages of *Robinson Crusoe* offer a warning that has haunted me since I first stumbled upon it yesterday: *"Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it."*

It was only this morning, May twenty-first, 1850, that Mr. Franklin Blake, my lady's nephew, visited me. He spoke briefly but with a gravity that settled heavily in my chest.

"Betteredge," he began, "I have consulted the lawyer regarding certain family affairs. Among them, the loss of the Indian Diamond from my aunt's home in Yorkshire two years past. Mr. Bruff and I are of one mind: the full truth of this affair must be committed to writing, and soon."

I nodded, agreeing readily. It is always prudent to align with the lawyer's counsel, and peace is a commodity I value highly. Yet, Mr. Blake pressed on, his eyes fixed on me.

"In this matter," he continued, "innocent lives have already been tarnished by suspicion. Their reputations may suffer further without a permanent record for future generations to consult. This strange saga of our family demands to be told. Mr. Bruff and I believe we have found the proper method."

He explained their plan with the confidence of men who have already solved the puzzle. They intended to reconstruct the history of the Moonstone chronologically. First, they would detail how the gem fell into the hands of my late master, Captain Herncastle, during his service in India half a century prior—a narrative they claimed to possess in an old family document. Next, they would recount its arrival at the manor in Yorkshire and its subsequent disappearance within twelve hours.

"And then?" I asked, feeling the weight of the question.

"Then," Mr. Blake smiled, "we turn to you, Betteredge. You know the household better than anyone. You witnessed the days leading up to the theft. You must take the pen and begin where we leave off."

At that moment, I understood my role. I was to be the chronicler of the final act. My heart, however, did not leap with pride; it sank with dread. I feigned humility, declaring myself unequal to such a monumental task. Privately, I knew I possessed the wit to undertake it, provided I were given a fair chance. But Mr. Blake saw through my modesty instantly. He refused to accept my refusal and insisted I proceed.

Two hours have now elapsed since Mr. Blake departed. As soon as his footsteps faded down the hall, I retreated to my writing desk. I sat there, paralyzed, staring at the blank page. Despite my capabilities, I found myself unable to move. The words of *Robinson Crusoe* echoed in my mind, a prophecy I had foolishly ignored until it was too late.

Do not mistake my hesitation for ignorance. I am a scholar, a man of seventy years with an active memory and steady legs. I have spent decades debating the very existence of *Robinson Crusoe*, arguing that no such book could ever be written. Yet, I have relied on it for comfort in every crisis: when my spirits were low, when I sought counsel, when my wife vexed me, and even when I had taken a drink too many. I have worn out six sturdy copies of the book in my service; my lady gifted me a seventh on her last birthday. That night, I drank too freely, and it was *Robinson Crusoe* that restored my balance. A fine volume, bound in blue for four shillings and sixpence, complete with an illustration.

But does this rambling reflection truly constitute the start of the Diamond's story? Am I merely wandering aimlessly? Let us try again, on a fresh sheet, with my deepest respect for your patience.
