Philip Trent, a painter of some standing in the English artistic circles, had reached the zenith of his twenties before fame truly took root. His works sold, buoyed by an original, driving talent and a work ethic that blended leisure with relentless industry, punctuated by bursts of fierce creative fervor. While his father's name and a comfortable inheritance spared him the stigma of the starving artist, Trent's true engine for success was a magnetic charm. He possessed a genial spirit and a humorous wit that endeared him instantly, coupled with a profound, albeit internal, insight into human nature. Others enjoyed him because he seemed perpetually amused, his countenance rarely losing its spark of vivacity, whether he was indulging in whimsical chatter or laboring intensely. Broadly cultured, with a specific devotion to poetry, Trent remained at thirty-two the embodiment of youth, unburdened by cynicism.

His ascent to a celebrity disproportionate to his actual output stemmed from a fleeting whim. Stumbling upon a newspaper report of a peculiar railway murder—one of those rare crimes where the circumstances defied immediate logic and two suspects were held—Trent, newly intrigued by such puzzles, immersed himself in the details. The narrative ignited a thrill in him akin to the rush of artistic inspiration. By day's end, he had penned a lengthy letter to *The Record*, modeled on Edgar Allan Poe's solution to the Mary Rogers case. He highlighted seemingly trivial facts, arranging the evidence to cast a pall of suspicion over a key witness. Sir James Molloy, the astute editor, published Trent's letter in bold type. Later that same evening, *The Sun* announced the arrest and confession of the culprit.

Sir James, a man who navigated all strata of London society, wasted no time in befriending Trent. Their rapport flourished; Trent's innate tact bridged the gap of age, making him appear eternally youthful. The roar of the printing presses in *The Record*'s basement sparked a new passion in Trent, leading Sir James to purchase a "machinery-scape" Trent produced there, a piece in the manner of Heinrich Kley. Months later, the Ilkley Mystery arrived. Sir James treated Trent to a convivial dinner and offered him a substantial sum to act as his special correspondent. "You can write," Sir James urged, "you can talk, and I can teach you the technicalities in half an hour. You have a head for mysteries. Think how it would feel to pull it off!"

Trent, initially reluctant due to a fear of the unfamiliar, conquered this hesitation through a lifelong habit of reacting against dread. He accepted the challenge. He succeeded magnificently, delivering a blow to the authorities that earned him renown. Though he retreated to painting and resisted the lure of a permanent journalistic salary, Sir James, respecting Trent's artistic integrity, did not tempt him with money. Yet, over the ensuing years, Trent was summoned repeatedly to unravel domestic and foreign enigmas. Occasionally, he declined due to his existing workload, or his rivals beat him to the truth, but his irregular association with *The Record* cemented his status as one of England's most famous names. Characteristically, the public knew almost nothing of Trent himself; he had imposed a strict silence upon himself via the Molloy papers, and other editors wisely avoided advertising his employment.

Walking briskly up the slope toward White Gables, Trent mused that the Manderson case might prove deceptively simple. While the hotel manager, Cupples, was a shrewd observer, his impartiality regarding his niece was questionable. Still, the manager's description of Mrs. Manderson lingered in Trent's mind. Describing her not merely as beautiful but possessing "backbone," "pluck," and a capacity to "brighten up" everyone around her, the manager hinted at a formidable spirit beneath a kind exterior. Trent felt a genuine desire to meet her.

Beyond a vast lawn and shrubbery stood the house itself: a two-story edifice of dull red brick, named for its twin gables. A modern construction of perhaps a decade prior, it radiated an opulent peace typical of the wealthy English countryside. To the north, rich meadows dipped toward the cliffs; to the south, woods spread across a broad valley toward the moors. The tranquility of the setting made the recent discovery of a body near the gardener's toolshed seem absurd. Trent approached the gate, passing through to a path lined with rhododendrons, eventually reaching the wooden shed where the body lay. Inside, he found nothing but the trampled grass where the victim had rested.

His search was cut short by the sound of a front door closing. A man emerged, walking swiftly toward the gate. At Trent's footsteps, the man whirled around, his face pale and gaunt, yet bearing the physical vigor of a young man. Despite evident exhaustion, he projected an air of "clean living" and robust health, marked by broad shoulders and a lithe frame. "Oxford was your playground, I think, my young friend," Trent observed internally.

"If you are Mr. Trent," the stranger said with a pleasant smile, "you are expected. Mr. Cupples telephoned from the hotel. My name is Marlowe."

"You were secretary to Mr. Manderson?" Trent asked, sensing a kinship despite the man's weary state. "It is a terrible business. I fear it has shattered you completely."

"A little limp, that's all," Marlowe replied wearily. "I drove the car all Sunday night and yesterday, and slept not a wink after the news broke. But I have an appointment with the doctor to arrange the inquest, presumably for tomorrow. If you seek Mr. Bunner, the American secretary, he expects you; he knows the lay of the land and will look after you. By the way, there is a detective here, Inspector Murch from Scotland Yard. He arrived yesterday."

"Murch!" Trent exclaimed. "An old friend. How did he get here so swiftly?"

"I haven't the foggiest," Marlowe said. "He was here last evening before I returned from Southampton, interviewing everyone, and has been about since eight this morning. He is currently in the library—you'll spot the open French window at the far end. Would you care to join him?"

"Yes, I will," Trent said. Marlowe nodded and departed. Trent moved silently across the lawn, peering through the open leaves of the southern window. Within, a man of immense bulk, bent over a spread of papers, caught Trent's eye.

"'Twas ever thus," Trent murmured in a wistful tone. The man spun around with startling speed. "From childhood's hour, I've watched my fondest hopes decay. I believed I had outpaced Scotland Yard this time, only to find the greatest officer in the Metropolitan force already entrenched."

The detective, Mr. Murch, grinned grimly. "I was expecting you, Mr. Trent. This is precisely the sort of case you enjoy."

"Since my talents were being considered," Trent retorted, stepping into the room, "I wish they had excluded my hated rival. You have a considerable head start, I'm aware. I know all the facts. How did you manage it? Is Scotland Yard operating an aviation corps? Are they in league with the devil? In either scenario, the Home Secretary demands a statement."

"It is simpler than that," Murch replied with professional calm. "I was on leave with my wife at Havley, merely twelve miles away. Upon hearing of the murder, I wired to the Chief and assumed command immediately. I cycled over last evening and have been at it ever since."

"And how fares Mrs. Inspector Murch?" Trent asked, feigning casual interest.

"Never better," Murch answered. "She speaks of you and the games we played with the children constantly. However, you need not waste your time with nonsense while you inspect the premises. I am familiar with your methods. You have fallen on your feet, as usual, and have the lady's permission to conduct inquiries."

"So it stands," Trent said. "I intend to cut you out again, Inspector. I owe you one for the Abinger affair, you old fox. But if you prefer business to pleasantries, let us proceed." He moved to the table, scanning the documents, then opened the roll-top desk. "I see this has been cleared. Very well, Inspector, let us play the game as before."

Trent recalled their frequent collaborations. Murch was a quiet, shrewd, and courageous officer, a giant of a man whose humanity matched his stature. Through some mysterious sympathy, they had become friends, bound by a unique code of "detective sportsmanship." They shared insights and theories, yet both respected the boundary of withholding vital discoveries from one another to preserve the honor of their respective institutions.

Murch agreed cordially. Leaning against the French windows, framed by the hazy splendor of the summer landscape, they reviewed the case. Trent consulted a thin notebook, sketching a rough plan of the room with practiced ease. It was a large, light apartment, spacious and airy. A broad table dominated the center, flanked by a roll-top desk against the left wall and an inner door at the far end facing a casement window. Old, carved corner cupboards stood guard near the fireplace, alongside Harunobu prints that offered little inspiration. Rows of bound novels stood like soldiers in formation, while a few chairs, including a modern swivel chair before the desk, completed the sparse, costly decor.
