Rebecca the mare, galloping with all the dignity of a lady in a hurry, carried Pen straight into Baymouth, where he deposited her at the inn stables and dashed off to Mr. Foker’s lodgings—directions kindly provided by said gentleman the day before. The apartment turned out to be above a chemist’s shop that seemed to specialize in cigars and soda water, both of which were disappearing at an alarming rate thanks to the youthful clientele living overhead.

Pen arrived breathless and rosy-cheeked, looking like a man who’d just won a race and eaten a whole pie. Inside, he found only Mr. Spavin, Foker’s chum and half-owner of the tandem that had once dragged poor Foker into Chatteris like a runaway circus wagon. Spavin was smoking and teaching his dog tricks with a biscuit—apparently the canine was more talented than the human.

“Who’s that fellow?” Spavin thought, squinting. “Looks like he’s been dipped in fresh air and boiled in health. His hand doesn’t shake. I’d bet five to one it wouldn’t.”

Foker? Not there. Not even a trace. Mr. Spavin shrugged. “He might come back tomorrow. Or next week. Or never. Depends on whether he’s got money or not.” He asked what college Pen attended (Pendennis, naturally), offered him a pint of ale (“Very fair tap, sir”), and exchanged pleasantries until Pen politely excused himself and went to wander aimlessly along the shore.

There, beneath a sky as blue as a baby’s bonnet, he stood on the sand, biting his nails like a nervous squirrel, staring at the sea. The waves crashed in, thunderous and endless, but Pen barely noticed. Inside his head, a tidal wave of emotion was rising—faster than any ocean swell. He wanted to see Foker. Desperately. He must see Foker. Otherwise, life would be incomplete, like a play missing its final act.

“I’ll ride back,” Pen decided. “Maybe I’ll catch him on the road.”

And so, within half an hour, Rebecca was saddled again, and they were galloping down the grassy Chatteris road. About four miles from Baymouth, the Clavering turn branches off—everyone knows this, because it’s written in every local guidebook and whispered by drunken shepherds. Rebecca, being a mare with strong opinions, veered toward the fork. But Pen, with a swift cut across her shoulder, kept her going straight. They passed the turning without seeing a single red wheel or black tandem.

At the turnpike, Pen considered: “Well, if I’m this far, why not go further?” And so he rode on, eventually arriving at the George Inn.

The hostler greeted him cheerfully. “Mr. Foker’s here, sir. Been making quite a racket last night—drinking, singing, trying to fight Tom the postboy. Wouldn’t have done well, though. Tom’s a sturdy lad.”

“And did you bring up his hot water for shaving?” the hostler added, grinning at the servant emerging from the yard, carrying Foker’s clothes—brushed, pressed, and arranged with the care of a royal wardrobe master.

“Show Mr. Pendennis up,” said the hostler.

Pen followed the man to a room where, nestled in a feather bed like a sultan in a nest of pillows, lay Mr. Harry Foker.

His face was barely visible beneath the mountain of bedding. Only a little sallow chin and a red silk nightcap peeked through.

“Hullo!” said Pen.

“Who goes there?” sang out the voice from the depths. “Brother, quickly tell! What! Pendennis again? Is your mamma aware of your absence? Did you sup with us last night? No stop—who supped with us last night, Stoopid?”

“There was three officers, sir,” replied Stoopid, the domestic, with perfect seriousness. “Mr. Bingley, sir. Mr. Costigan, sir.”

“Ah yes!” Foker yawned, stretching his arms over his head like a cat. “The cup and merry jest went round. We chanted. I remember wanting to fight a postboy. Did I thrash him, Stoopid?”

“No, sir. Fight didn’t come off, sir,” said Stoopid, still solemn as a judge.

He was arranging Foker’s dressing-case—a trunk, gifted by a mother who clearly believed in extravagance. Inside: silver dishes, silver mugs, silver bottles for essences, and a full set of razors, ready for the day Foker finally grew facial hair.

“Do it some other day,” Foker muttered, yawning again. “No fight. But chanting. Bingley chanted, I chanted, the General chanted—Costigan, I mean. Did you ever hear him sing ‘The Little Pig under the Bed,’ Pen?”

“The man we met yesterday,” Pen blurted, trembling. “The father of—”

“Of the Fotheringay—the very man. Ain’t she a Venus, Pen?”

“Please, sir,” interrupted Stoopid, “Mr. Costigan’s in the sitting-room, sir. Says you asked him to breakfast. Called five times, sir. Wouldn’t wake you on no account. Has been here since eleven o’clock, sir.”

“How much is it now?”

“One, sir.”

“What would the best of mothers say,” cried Foker, rolling over dramatically, “if she saw me in bed at this hour? She sent me down here with a grinder. Wants me to cultivate my neglected genius—he, be! I say, Pen, this isn’t quite like seven o’clock school, is it, old boy?” And he laughed like a mischievous child.

“Go in and talk to the General while I dress,” he added. “And ask him to sing you ‘The Little Pig under the Bed.’ It’s capital.”

Pen, heart pounding, went to meet the General—Captain Costigan.

Now, Captain Costigan—though technically a captain, he preferred to be called “General,” perhaps because the title sounded more impressive when you were trying to collect debts—was seated in the window, reading the newspaper at arm’s length. His eyes were dim, his lips moving silently as he spelled each word. He wore a hat cocked jauntily on one ear, and one foot propped up on the windowsill, revealing boots so shabby they looked like they’d been dug out of a ditch.

Poverty, it seems, begins at the extremities. First the feet, then the hands, then the head. Captain Costigan’s extremities were suffering accordingly.

When he saw Pen, he descended from his perch with military precision—first saluting with two fingers (covered in a torn black glove), then removing his hat entirely.

He was balding, but he pulled a mass of lank iron-grey hair over his pate like a wig made of rope. Whiskers hung down on either side of his face like wet seaweed. His complexion had been ruined by whisky—once handsome, now copper-coloured, like a penny left in the rain.

He wore a high stock, stained and scarred, and a dress coat buttoned tightly, as if it were holding back something worse.

“The young gentleman to whom I had the honour to be introjuiced yesterday in the Cathedral Yard,” he announced, bowing deeply. “I hope I see you well, sir. I marked ye in the theatre last night during me daughter’s perfawrumance; and missed ye on my return. I did but conduct her home, sir, for Jack Costigan, though poor, is a gentleman; and when I reintered the house to pay me respects to me joyous young friend, Mr. Foker—ye were gone. We had a jolly night of ut, sir—Mr. Foker, the three gallant young dragoons, and your ’umble servant. Gad, sir, it put me in mind of one of our old nights when I bore His Majesty’s commission in the Foighting Hundtherd and Third.”

With great ceremony, he pulled out an ancient snuff box and presented it to Pen.

Arthur, flustered beyond belief, could barely speak. This shabby, smelly, boastful man—this was her father.

But the Captain was already regaling him with stories of past glories, pulling at his chin tuft like a dandy adjusting his cravat.

“I hope Miss F—, Miss Costigan is well, sir,” Pen stammered, blushing. “She—she gave me greater pleasure than—I—I ever enjoyed at a play. I think, sir—I think she’s the finest actress in the world.”

“Your hand, young man!” cried the Captain, seizing his hand. “For ye speak from your heart. Thank ye, sir—an old soldier and a fond father thanks ye. She is the finest actress in the world. I’ve seen the Siddons, sir, and the O’Nale—they were great, but what were they compared to Miss Fotheringay? I do not wish she should ashume her own name while on the stage. Me family, sir, are proud people; and the Costigans of Costiganstown think that an honest man, who has borne Her Majesty’s colours in the Hundred and Third, would demean himself by permitting his daughter to earn her old father’s bread.”

“There cannot be a more honourable duty, surely,” Pen said.

“Honourable! Bedad, sir, I’d like to see the man who said Jack Costigan would consent to anything dishonourable. I have a heart, sir, though I am poor; I like a man who has a heart. You have: I read it in your honest face and steady eye. And would you believe it?” he whispered, pathetically, “that that Bingley who has made his fortune by me child gives her but two guineas a week—out of which she finds herself in dresses, and which, added to me own small means, makes our all?”

Which, of course, amounted to nothing. But nobody understands how Irish lambs survive on wind and scraps.

Captain Costigan had once been dazzling: fine legs, a beautiful voice, and a pair of colours. He sang Irish ballads with such feeling that he’d cry at them himself. Brave, sentimental, generous, and utterly unreliable—he drank with anyone, backed anyone’s bill, and ended his days in a spunging-house, beloved by the sheriff’s officer who arrested him.

Before long, he extracted two sovereigns from Pen for tickets to his daughter’s benefit performance. Pen handed over his entire purse—two pounds—and feared offering more might offend the Captain’s delicate pride.

Costigan scribbled an order for a box, slipped the coins into his waistcoat, and slapped his chest like a man warming himself by a fire.

“Faith, sir,” he said, “the bullion’s scarcer with me than it used to be, as is the case with many a good fellow. I won six hundred of ’em in a single night, sir, when me kind friend, His Royal Highness the Duke of Kent, was in Gibralther.”

And so began a stream of tales about claret, bets, and races, keeping Pen amused until Foker appeared, smelling of tobacco and regret.

Breakfast arrived—devilled turkey, mutton chops—and the Captain came alive. Stories poured forth like wine. He described the death of his wife, Bessie; the challenge he sent to Captain Shanty Clancy for rudely staring at Miss Fotheringay on her carriage; the dinner at Kildare Street where six men drank twenty-one bottles of claret.

He declared it an honour to sit with such noble young gentlemen, and after a second glass of Curacoa, burst into tears.

Not a man of strong mind, certainly. But not a bad companion—just a bit of a fraud. Though he’d committed more rogueries than most honest men, he’d never lied about being poor.

After breakfast, the trio walked out. The Captain held an arm of each young man, winking at shops where he likely owed bills, as if to say, “Look who I’m with—sure I’ll pay you soon.”

They parted at a billiard room, where Foker had a date with some officers from Colonel Swallowtail’s regiment.

Pen and the Captain continued walking. The Captain, ever curious, asked about Foker’s background.

Pen told him: brewer father, aristocratic mother—Lady Agnes Milton, daughter of Lord Rosherville.

The Captain exploded into praise. “Mr. Foker’s native aristocracie!” he boomed, “could be seen with the twinkling of an oi—and only served to adawrun other qualities which he possessed, a foin intellect and a generous heart.”

Not a word of it true. But the Captain believed it all.

Pen listened, amazed, amused, confused. He didn’t doubt a thing. He took everything at face value, and Costigan, delighted by his honesty, felt compelled to invite him to his humble abode.

“Would you do me the fever,” he asked, “to enter my humble abode, where I may have the honour of inthrojuicing my dear young friend to my daughther, Miss Fotheringay?”

Pen nearly fainted. He was going to meet her! The woman whose voice had stirred his soul, whose presence had made the world spin!

He followed the Captain through Prior’s Lane, a quiet street near the cathedral, to a low gabled house with a brass plate reading “Creed, Tailor and Robe-maker.” Creed was dead. His widow opened pews in the cathedral. His son was a choir-boy with a voice like an angel—and a penchant for tossing pennies.

Two children jumped up to greet the Captain, grabbing wildly at his swallow-tails. Pen stumbled down the step, knees weak, heart hammering.

Inside, he entered a room—and froze.

A figure in black waved slightly, as if curtseying. The Captain spoke, grandly introducing Pen to “me child.”

“It is very fine weather,” Miss Fotheringay said, in a deep, melancholy Irish voice.

“Very,” Pen replied, barely able to breathe.

She sat down. Her movements were regal. Against the mantelpiece, her robe draped itself like a Greek statue. On a cane chair, her arm curved over the back, her hand poised as if awaiting a sceptre. She looked like an empress.

Her hair was blue-black, her skin dazzling white with a faint blush. Her eyes—grey, with long lashes. Her mouth—so red, so bright, so perfectly crimson, that even a geranium would envy it.

“And very warm,” she added.

“Very,” Pen agreed.

She asked about the evening. The Captain recounted the punch and the fights. Then she asked what she’d done that morning.

“Bows came,” she said, “at ten, and we studied Ophalia. It’s for the twenty-fourth, when I hope, sir, we shall have the honour of seeing ye.”

“Indeed, indeed, you will,” Pen said, wondering why she said “Ophalia” and spoke with an Irish accent, when on stage she had none.

“I’ve secured ’um for your benefit, dear,” said the Captain, tapping his waistcoat where Pen’s sovereigns lay. He winked. Pen blushed.

“Mr—the gentleman’s very obleging,” said Mrs. Haller.

“My name is Pendennis,” Pen said, blushing harder. “I—I—hope you’ll—you’ll remember it.”

His heart thumped so hard he thought he’d choke.

“Pendennis,” she said slowly, looking him straight in the eyes. Her voice was sweet, low, round. The glance—clear, bright, deadly—struck him like lightning.

“I never knew the name was so pretty before,” Pen said.

“’Tis a very pretty name,” she replied. “Pentweazle’s not a pretty name. Remember, papa, when we were on the Norwich Circuit, Young Pentweazle, who used to play second old men, and married Miss Rancy, the Columbine; they’re both engaged in London now, at the Queen’s, and get five pounds a week. Pentweazle wasn’t his real name. ’Twas Judkin gave it him, I don’t know why. His name was Harrington; that is, his real name was Potts; fawther a clergyman, very respectable. Harrington was in London, and got in debt. Ye remember; he came out in Falkland, to Mrs. Bunce’s Julia.”

“And a pretty Julia she was,” the Captain interposed. “A woman of fifty, and a mother of ten children. ’Tis you ought to have been Julia, or my name’s not Jack Costigan.”

“I didn[’t take the leading business then,” Miss Fotheringay said modestly. “I wasn’t fit for’t till Bows taught me.”
