On the first of May, the school woke with a singular alacrity. Twenty boarders and four teachers were summoned to rise at five, to be dressed and ready by six for a grand expedition. Our commander was M. le Professeur Emanuel, who had finally fulfilled his vow to take us for breakfast in the country. As for me, the reader may recall that when this outing was first mooted, I had not shared in the invitation; quite the reverse. Yet, when I ventured to inquire why I was excluded, a sharp pull on my ear silenced me, followed by M. Emanuel's imperial admonition: "Je vous conseille de vous faire prier"—I advise you to ask politely. That single, Napoleonic compliment was sufficient to seal my fate, and I resolved to join the party.

The morning broke calm, a gentle summer dawn filled with bird song and a light, promising mist. Heavy garments were folded away, replaced by the attire of the season. A uniform of clean, fresh prints and light straw bonnets prevailed, crafted by the hands of French workwomen who possess the unique art of uniting the unpretentious with the perfectly becoming. No one flaunted faded silks or second-hand finery; we were a company of modest, neat youths.

At six, the bell rang cheerily. We flowed down the stairs, through the *carré*, along the corridors, and into the vestibule. There stood our Professor, transformed from his usual savage paletôt and severe Greek cap into a youthful belted blouse and a cheerful straw hat. His greeting was the kindest imaginable, and in return, most of us offered a smile of gratitude. We were marshalled in line and soon moved out.

The streets were silent, the boulevards fresh and peaceful as fields. I believe we were truly happy, walking together under this guidance. This man possessed a secret power to impart a certain impetus to joy; conversely, in a darker mood, he could instill a thrilling fear.

He did not lead nor follow but walked alongside the line, addressing everyone, speaking warmly to his favorites, yet failing to neglect entirely those he disliked. However, I harbored a private wish to remain slightly aloof from notice. Paired with Ginevra Fanshawe, I bore on my arm the considerable weight of her arm—she was in excellent case, and I must confess, carrying the burden of her loveliness was no trifling task. Many times that warm day, I fervently wished for less of this charming commodity. With her there, I attempted to render her useful by positioning her always between myself and M. Paul, shifting places as I heard him approaching from either flank. My motive for this maneuver stemmed from my new print dress, which was pink. Under our present convoy, this color made me feel as acutely as I had felt once, clad in a shawl with a red border, forced to traverse a meadow where a bull grazed.

For a while, this shifting strategy, aided by some adjustments to a black silk scarf, served my purpose. But eventually, he discerned the truth. Whether he came to this side or that, Miss Fanshawe remained his neighbor. The acquaintance between Ginevra and him had never run smoothly enough that his temper did not undergo a certain crisping process whenever he heard her English accent. Their dispositions jarred; he found her empty and affected, she deemed him bearish, meddling, and repellent.

Finally, after changing his position for the sixth time, finding the same untoward result, he thrust his head forward, fixed his eyes on mine, and demanded with impatience, "Qu'est-ce que c'est? Vous me jouez des tours?" ("What is it? Are you playing tricks on me?")

The words were hardly out before, with his customary swiftness, he grasped the root of the matter. In vain I shook out the long fringe and spread the broad end of my scarf. "A-h-h! c'est la robe rose!" ("Ah! it's the pink dress!") burst from his lips, sounding much like the sudden, irate bellow of a lord of the meadow.

"It is only cotton," I alleged hurriedly; "cheaper, and washes better than any other color."

"And Mademoiselle Lucy is as coquettish as ten Parisians," he answered. "Have you ever seen an Englishwoman like this? Look at her hat, her gloves, her boots!" These articles were identical to those worn by my companions; certainly not smarter, perhaps plainer than most. Yet Monsieur had seized his text, and I began to bristle under the expected sermon. It proceeded, however, as mildly as a summer storm's threat often dissipates. I received but one flash of sheet lightning—a single, bantering smile from his eyes—and then he said, "Courage! To tell the truth, I am not angry; perhaps I am even glad you have dressed so beautifully for my little feast."

"But my dress is not beautiful, sir—it is merely proper."

"I like propriety," he said. In short, he was not to be dissatisfied. The sun of good humor was destined to triumph on this auspicious morning, consuming the scudding clouds ere they could sullied its disk.

Now we were in the country, amidst what they called "les bois et les petits sentiers" (woods and little paths). A month later, these woods and lanes would offer but a dusty, doubtful seclusion; now, in their May greenness and morning repose, they looked exceedingly pleasant.

We reached a well planted round, in the taste of Labassecour, with an orderly circle of lime-trees. Here a halt was called. On the green swell of ground surrounding it, we were ordered to sit, Monsieur taking his place in our midst, allowing us to gather in knots around him. Those who liked him more than they feared drew close—chiefly the little ones; those who feared more than they liked kept somewhat aloof; those in whom affection had given even a pleasurable zest to what remained of fear observed the greatest distance.

He began to tell us a story. He could narrate with rare perfection, using a diction that children love and learned men emulate: simple in strength, strong in simplicity. There were beautiful touches in that little tale, sweet glimpses of feeling and hues of description that, while I listened, sank deep into my mind and have never faded. He tinted a twilight scene—I hold it in memory still—that is a picture I have never beheld from an artist's pencil.

I have remarked that for myself, I possess no impromptu faculty; perhaps that very deficiency made me marvel the more at one who possessed it in perfection. M. Emanuel was not a man to write books; yet I have heard him lavish, with careless, unconscious prodigality, such mental wealth as books seldom boast. His mind was indeed my library, and whenever it was opened to me, I entered bliss. Intellectually imperfect as I was, reading little else, few bound volumes failed to weary me—to fag and blind me—but his tomes of thought were collyrium to the spirit's eyes; inward sight grew clear and strong over their contents. I used to think what a delight it would be for one who loved him better than he loved himself to gather and store up those handfuls of gold-dust, so recklessly flung to heaven's reckless winds.

His story finished, he approached the little knoll where I and Ginevra sat apart. In his usual mode of demanding an opinion (lacking reticence to wait until it was voluntarily offered), he asked, "Were you interested?"

According to my wonted undemonstrative fashion, I simply answered—"Yes."

"Was it good?"

"Very good."

"Yet I could not write that down," said he.

"Why not, Monsieur?"

"I hate the mechanical labour; I hate to stoop and sit still. I could dictate it, though, with pleasure, to an amanuensis who suited me. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me if I asked her?"

"Monsieur would be too quick; he would urge me, and be angry if my pen did not keep pace with his lips."

"Try some day; let us see the monster I can make of myself under the circumstances. But just now, there is no question of dictation; I mean to make you useful in another office. Do you see yonder farmhouse?"

"Surrounded with trees? Yes."

"There we are to breakfast; and while the good fermière makes the café au lait in a caldron, you and five others, whom I shall select, will spread with butter half a hundred rolls."

Having formed his troop into line once more, he marched us straight on the farm, which, on seeing our force, surrendered without capitulation. Clean knives and plates, and fresh butter being provided, half-a-dozen of us, chosen by our Professor, set to work under his directions to prepare for breakfast a huge basket of rolls, with which the baker had been ordered to provision the farm in anticipation of our coming. Coffee and chocolate were already made hot; cream and new-laid eggs were added to the treat, and M. Emanuel, always generous, would have given a large order for "jambon" and "confitures" in addition, but that some of us, who presumed perhaps upon our influence, insisted that it would be a most reckless waste of victuals.
