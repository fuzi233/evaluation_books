The wind cut sharp and biting from the north, a physical manifestation of the cold reality that had replaced the heat of Omdurman. Beneath its breath, the camels, revitalized by the chill, broke into a trot, their hooves striking the earth with urgent rhythm.

"Quicker," Trench gritted out, the words forced through clenched teeth. "Idris may already be ahead of us."

"Even if he has," Feversham replied, his voice steady despite the tremor in his soul, "it takes time to gather men for a pursuit. Time to fetch camels. And already, the sun has surrendered to the dark."

Though his words carried a veneer of hope, Feversham's head snapped repeatedly toward the fading glow of Omdurman. The drumming had ceased—a small mercy—but he was now in a kingdom of silence, a vast, suffocating quiet where speed meant isolation. There would be no thunder of hooves to herald the approach of pursuers. Even now, Ansar riders could be closing the gap within thirty paces, invisible ghosts in the twilight. Feversham strained his eyes backward, hunting for the glimmer of a white turban, a signal of doom, but found only the swallowing blackness. Trench, conversely, did not turn. He rode with jaw locked, staring blindly forward, yet the terror coiled tighter in his gut than in Feversham's. Trench did not look back because he lacked the courage to witness the truth; his eyes were fixed on the horizon, yet his mind was trapped in the long, narrow arteries of the town behind them—the dotted fires flickering at street corners, the frantic scurry of men searching the shadows for the two prisoners who had fled the House of Stone.

When Feversham spoke, breaking the trance, Trench answered without shifting his gaze.

"What is it?"

"I no longer see the fires of Omdurman."

"The golden blot, eh? Very low down?" Trench murmured, his voice detached, as if speaking to a stranger. Feversham did not press for explanation, nor could Trench have given one; the phrase had surfaced with sudden, crushing clarity. It felt fated that the final image meeting Feversham's eyes as he embarked on his mission should reappear now that the mission was complete, a ghostly echo marking the transition from hunter to hunted. Silence stretched between them until two figures materialized from the obsidian night, rising just beneath the bellies of their camels.

"Instanna!" Abou Fatma whispered, the cry vibrating with urgency.

The camels halted, dropping to their knees.

"The new mounts?" Abou Fatma asked. Two men vanished briefly, returning with four fresh beasts. Simultaneously, the saddles were unbuckled and stripped from the weary animals Trench and Feversham had ridden from the city.

"Are they good?" Feversham asked, helping to secure the gear onto the fresh hides.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma confirmed, his eyes darting anxiously eastward, listening to the wind. "Quick! Quick!"

"The arms?" Trench demanded, his body leaning forward, scanning the ground. "Do you have them? Where are they?"

"In a moment," Abou Fatma assured him, though the delay seemed agonizing to Trench. His anxiety manifested not merely in the fear of capture, but in a frantic need to possess the means of survival.

"Ammunition?" Trench asked, his voice rising to a fever pitch.

"Yes, yes," Abou Fatma replied, confirming the presence of rifles and revolvers. He led them twenty yards away, where long desert grasses rustled around their ankles. With a grunt of effort, he dug into the soft sand with his bare hands.

"Here."

Trench threw himself to the ground beside him, scooping furiously with both hands, emitting an inhuman, whining sound akin to a hound scenting game. To Feversham, the scene was grotesque; Trench was sunk into animality, his movements rapid and claw-like, his voice a keening wail. Then, Trench's fingers brushed the cold metal of a rifle lock. Instantly, the beast receded, and the man returned. He stood upright, gripping the weapon, as the other arms were unearthed and the ammunition distributed.

"Now," Trench breathed, a laugh erupting from him—a sound thick with relief and triumph. "Now I do not care. Let them chase us from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I shall never return there. Not even if they catch us." He stroked the rifle as if caressing a living child, treating the steel with a reverence born of desperation.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and drifted slowly back toward the city. Abou Fatma and his companion stayed with the fugitives. Mounting their fresh mounts, they trotted northeast. Less than fifteen minutes had passed since the halt.

Through the night, they traversed fields of halfa grass and mimosa trees, moving with deliberate slowness. By sunrise, they emerged onto flat, barren land scarred by small hillocks.

"Is the Effendi exhausted?" Abou Fatma asked gently. "Shall we stop and eat? Food awaits on each saddle."

"No," Feversham replied. "We eat as we ride."

Dates, bread, and water from a zamsheyeh constituted their meal, consumed while seated atop their moving mounts. Freed from the tall grass, the camels surged forward at their swiftest pace. That evening, at sunset, they paused for an hour of rest. Through the following night and the next day, they pushed relentlessly, straining both human and beast. They climbed rocky heights, descended into valleys, and galloped across plains of honey-colored sand. Yet, to every man, the pace felt as sluggish as a funeral procession. Mountains loomed above the horizon at dawn, standing sentinel for the entire day, neither growing taller nor drawing nearer. Occasionally, a farmer tending a sparse plot of sorghum would cause their hearts to hammer against their ribs, forcing a wide detour; or a distant caravan spotted by Abou Fatma's keen eyes would compel them to kneel their camels and crouch behind a rock, rifles drawn, holding their breath.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, a relay of fresh camels awaited. Upon mounting these, they veered westward, keeping a day's march clear of the Nile. They crossed the desert lands of the Ababdeh until a broad grey tract barred their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma noted. "North of it, we turn east to the river. We cross tonight; if God wills, tomorrow evening we shall have crossed the Nile."

"If God wills," Trench echoed, the words hollow. "If only He wills." Fear gnawed at him, intensifying with every step toward safety. This was a land of caravans; daylight travel was suicide. They dismounted and hid behind a belt of shrubs on high ground, lying motionless all day, watching the road and the travelers like specks moving on a distant screen. They descended and crossed the road under the cover of darkness, pushing hard toward the river through the remainder of the night.

As dawn broke, Abou Fatma ordered another halt. They stood in a desolate expanse where the slightest movement was magnified by the surrounding flatness. Feversham and Trench gazed eagerly to their right. Somewhere in that direction, within the limits of their sight, the Nile flowed, yet it remained invisible.

"We must build a circle of stones," Abou Fatma instructed. "Lie close to the ground within it. I will go forward to the river to ensure the boat is ready and our friends await. I shall return after dark."

Gathering stones quickly, they erected a low wall about a foot high. Inside this protective ring, Feversham and Trench lay prone upon the earth, water-skins and rifles at their sides.

"You have dates, too," Abou Fatma reminded them.

"Yes."

"Do not stir from this hiding place until I return. I will take your camels and bring fresh ones in the evening." Accompanied by his fellow Arab, he rode off toward the river.

Trench and Feversham dug out the sand within the stone circle and settled down, peering through the gaps at the horizon. For both men, this was perhaps the longest day of their lives. So near to salvation, yet so far from safety. To Trench, it felt longer than a single night in the House of Stone; to Feversham, longer than any of those agonizing days six years prior when he had sat in his rooms above St. James's Park, waiting for the night to fall before daring to step outside. They were nearing Berber, and the pursuit must be hot on their heels. Feversham wondered how he had ever summoned the courage to enter Berber. There was no shade; the sun burned pitilessly upon their exposed backs, and within the narrow stone circle, there was no room to shift. They spoke scarcely at all. Only when the sunset arrived did the friendly darkness gather around them, and a cool wind rustled through the desert night.

"Listen!" Trench whispered. Both men strained their ears, hearing the soft, rhythmic padding of camels approaching rapidly. A low whistle signaled their emergence from the shelter.

"We are here," Feversham said quietly.
