Barton Park lay scarcely half a mile from the cottage; the ladies had passed near it on their first walks along the valley, yet the house kept itself coyly out of their daily sight, shouldered away by a green projection of hill, as if the land itself chose when to reveal its grandeurs. In the morning, when the dew still held fast to the grass and the brook spoke in thin, bright syllables over stones, the Park seemed less a neighbour than an older chapter of the same place—heard in the distant clatter of hooves and the faint, organized barking of dogs, smelled in the wind when it turned and carried to Barton Cottage a ghost of smoke from well-seasoned logs and something richer, buttery and browned, as though the kitchens there began their work early and never quite ceased.

The house, when at last approached, proved large and handsome, its windows catching the last of the day like polished pewter. The Middletons lived in a style of equal hospitality and elegance: the first for Sir John’s gratification, the second for that of his lady. They were scarcely ever without friends staying in the house, and they kept more company of every kind than any other family in the neighbourhood. It was necessary to the happiness of both; for however dissimilar in temper and outward behaviour, they strongly resembled each other in that total want of talent and taste which confined their employments—when unconnected with the bustle of society—within a very narrow compass.

Sir John was a sportsman; Lady Middleton a mother. He hunted and shot, and she humoured her children; and these were their only resources. There are households where the calendar is measured by sowing and reaping, by the apple’s first blush and the hog’s killing; but at Barton Park the seasons were counted by engagements—by who dined, who stayed, who danced, who came to be amused and who was obliged to amuse. Lady Middleton had the advantage of being able to spoil her children all the year round, while Sir John’s independent employments were in existence only half the time. Continual engagements at home and abroad, however, supplied all deficiencies of nature and education; supported the good spirits of Sir John, and gave exercise to the good breeding of his wife.

Lady Middleton piqued herself upon the elegance of her table and of all her domestic arrangements; and from this kind of vanity drew her greatest enjoyment in any party. It was not, Marianne soon perceived, the pleasure of hunger honestly satisfied, nor the old comfort of food that carries a family’s story—no talk of what the dairy yielded, whether the cream had been too long in the pan, whether the herbs in the garden were sweet with rain. It was the smoothness of napery, the exact placing of plate, the polished obedience of servants moving as silently as shadows. Sir John’s satisfaction, on the other hand, was far more real. He delighted in collecting about him more young people than his house would hold, and the noisier they were the better he was pleased; as if the worth of an evening could be weighed in laughter and the clack of voices, regardless of whether those sounds harmonized with any peace the place might otherwise have possessed. To Elinor, whose mind attended as readily to temper as to tone, there was something almost violent in that preference; the Park had its own quiet languages—wind in leaf, fire in grate, a distant owl—and Sir John would gladly drown them beneath stamping feet and shouted witticisms.

Yet, for the juvenile part of the neighbourhood, he was a blessing. In summer he was for ever forming parties to eat cold ham and chicken out of doors—great joints carved in haste, the fat turned firm and translucent by cool air, slices laid upon bread that had been cut too thick in the excitement; and in winter his private balls were numerous enough for any young lady who was not suffering under the insatiable appetite of fifteen. The arrival of a new family in the country was always a matter of joy to him, and in every point of view he was charmed with the inhabitants he had now procured for his cottage at Barton. The Miss Dashwoods were young, pretty, and unaffected. It was enough to secure his good opinion; for, in Sir John’s creed, to be unaffected was all that a pretty girl could want to make her mind as captivating as her person.

The friendliness of his disposition made him happy in accommodating those whose situation might be considered, in comparison with the past, unfortunate. In showing kindness to his cousins, therefore, he had the real satisfaction of a good heart; and in settling a family of females only in his cottage, he had all the satisfaction of a sportsman—for a sportsman, though he esteems only those of his sex who are sportsmen likewise, is not often desirous of encouraging their taste by admitting them to a residence within his own manor.

Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters were met at the door by Sir John himself, who welcomed them to Barton Park with unaffected sincerity; and, as he attended them through passages that smelled faintly of beeswax and fire, repeated to the young ladies the concern which the same subject had drawn from him the day before—his mortification at being unable to get any smart young men to meet them.

“You will see,” he said, as if he had personally failed in the provisioning of the table, “only one gentleman here besides myself; a particular friend staying at the park, but neither very young nor very gay. You must excuse the smallness of the party; I assure you it shall never happen so again. I have been to several families this morning in hopes of procuring some addition; but it is moonlight and everybody is full of engagements. Luckily, however, my Lady Middleton’s mother arrived within the last hour; and she is a very cheerful, agreeable woman—so I hope you will not find it as dull as you may imagine.”

The young ladies, as well as their mother, were perfectly satisfied with having two entire strangers of the party, and wished for no more. Indeed, Mrs. Dashwood, who could take warmth from kindness as readily as from sunshine, was disposed to be pleased with everything that bespoke welcome.

Mrs. Jennings, Lady Middleton’s mother, answered Sir John’s description in all particulars, and exceeded it in volume. She was good-humoured, merry, fat, elderly; she talked a great deal, seemed very happy, and was rather vulgar. Her laughter rolled before her like a cart over loose stones, and her voice, rich and ungoverned, filled rooms that had been made for softer sounds. If the Park had possessed at certain hours a hush that might have let a person hear the settling of embers or the turning of a page, Mrs. Jennings shattered it without malice, as a cheerful hand smacks flour from a board. Before dinner was over she had said many witty things on the subject of lovers and husbands; hoped the Miss Dashwoods had not left their hearts behind them in Sussex; and pretended to see them blush whether they did or not.

Marianne, whose sensibility quickened at once to any perceived slight, was vexed for her sister’s sake. She turned her eyes towards Elinor to see how she bore these attacks, with an earnestness which gave Elinor far more pain than could arise from such common-place raillery as Mrs. Jennings’s. For Elinor could endure a jest; it was Marianne’s anxious watchfulness—so public, so tender, so incapable of disguise—that made every foolish word feel heavier. The untranslatable part of experience, Elinor thought, is not always grief; sometimes it is composure. She could not make Marianne understand, in any dialect of feeling, that an unguarded laugh need not draw blood.

Colonel Brandon, the particular friend of Sir John, seemed no more adapted by resemblance of manner to be his friend than Lady Middleton was to be his wife, or Mrs. Jennings to be Lady Middleton’s mother. He was silent and grave. His appearance, however, was not unpleasing, in spite of his being, in the opinion of Marianne and Margaret, an absolute old bachelor—for he was on the wrong side of five-and-thirty. Though his face was not handsome, his countenance was sensible, and his address particularly gentlemanlike. In the pauses of conversation—those small clearings where a quieter spirit might have found a place—he seemed to listen, not merely to words, but to the room itself: to the scrape of a chair against the floor, the snap of a coal as it split, the faint rush of wind at a window. It gave him, to Elinor’s eye, a steadiness that the others lacked, as if he had learned the value of sound not only for entertainment, but for peace.

There was nothing in any of the party which could recommend them as companions to the Dashwoods; but the cold insipidity of Lady Middleton was so particularly repulsive that, in comparison, the gravity of Colonel Brandon, and even the boisterous mirth of Sir John and his mother-in-law, was interesting. Lady Middleton seemed to be roused to enjoyment only by the entrance of her four noisy children after dinner, who pulled her about, tore her clothes, and put an end to every kind of discourse except what related to themselves. Their shrieks—sharp as the squeal of a kettle left too long upon the boil—rang through the rooms. One child demanded sweetmeats with a cry; another thumped at a chair leg as if testing its endurance; a third made a drum of a book. Lady Middleton, far from checking them, surrendered her attention to them with the same neat, automatic patience she bestowed on the arrangement of her table. To Marianne, who heard in their disorder the ruin of every finer sound, the clamour was like a crude modern intrusion upon a landscape that ought to have been pastoral: as though the Park, with its older trees and older manners, were being conquered by a little army of senseless noise.

In the evening, as Marianne was discovered to be musical, she was invited to play. The instrument was unlocked; everybody prepared to be charmed. Marianne, who sang very well, at their request went through the chief of the songs which Lady Middleton had brought into the family on her marriage, and which perhaps had lain ever since in the same position on the pianoforte; for her ladyship had celebrated that event by giving up music—although by her mother’s account she had played extremely well, and by her own was very fond of it.

The first touch of Marianne’s fingers summoned a different air, as if the room—overcrowded with talk, with children’s outcries, with Sir John’s hearty interruptions—had at last remembered how to breathe. Her voice rose clear and warm, like wine poured into a glass that has waited too long empty; it carried the sweetness of ripe fruit and the faint bitterness of peel. Even those who could not have distinguished a well-made melody from a street tune felt the charm by mere contagion. Sir John was loud in his admiration at the end of every song, and as loud in his conversation with the others while every song lasted. He praised, he laughed, he exclaimed; and his praises, like his dinners, were abundant, if not nicely considered. Lady Middleton frequently called him to order, wondered how any one’s attention could be diverted from music for a moment, and asked Marianne to sing a particular song which Marianne had just finished—her requests repeating themselves with the same unconsciousness as her children’s demands.

Colonel Brandon alone, of all the party, heard Marianne without being in raptures. He paid her only the compliment of attention—steady, unbroken, as if he would not waste a single note. Marianne felt a respect for him on the occasion which the others had reasonably forfeited by their shameless want of taste. His pleasure in music, though it amounted not to that ecstatic delight which alone could sympathize with her own, was estimable when contrasted against the horrible insensibility of the rest. She was reasonable enough, too, to allow that a man of five-and-thirty might well have outlived all acuteness of feeling and every exquisite power of enjoyment. And with that charitable severity—so easy to bestow when one is young, so hard to accept when one is no longer—she was perfectly disposed to make every allowance for the Colonel’s advanced state of life which humanity required.
