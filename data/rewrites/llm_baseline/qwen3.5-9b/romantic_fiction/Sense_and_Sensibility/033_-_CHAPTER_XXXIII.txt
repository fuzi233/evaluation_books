After some initial resistance, Marianne eventually relented to her sister's earnest pleas and agreed to venture out with Elinor and Mrs. Jennings one morning for a brief half-hour excursion. However, she insisted on strict conditions: no social calls were to be made, and their sole purpose was to accompany Elinor to Mr. Gray's jewelry shop in Sackville Street, where negotiations regarding the exchange of her mother's antique jewels were underway.

As they approached the shop door, Mrs. Jennings suddenly remembered a lady residing at the opposite end of the street whom she felt compelled to visit. Since she had no personal business at Mr. Gray's, it was decided that while the younger ladies attended to their errands, Mrs. Jennings would pay her respects and return promptly. Upon entering the shop, the Dashwood sisters found themselves confronted by a crowded room teeming with customers. With no clerk available to assist them, they were forced to wait. Their only recourse was to take a seat at the end of the counter that appeared to promise the swiftest turnover. Only one gentleman stood nearby, and perhaps Elinor harbored a faint hope that his inherent politeness would expedite the process. Yet, his keen eye and refined taste proved superior to his courtesy. He was deeply engrossed in commissioning a toothpick-case, meticulously deliberating over its dimensions, form, and ornamentation. For nearly fifteen minutes, he examined and debated every option presented, guided solely by his own inventive whims. Until this intricate decision was finalized, he offered the two ladies merely a handful of wide, staring glances—a perfunctory acknowledgment that left Elinor with a vivid memory of a face possessing a striking, natural, and utterly insignificant charm, despite being dressed in the height of fashion.

Marianne, spared the pangs of contempt and irritation caused by this intrusive scrutiny and the dog-like manner in which he dismissed various "horrors" of toothpick-cases, remained blissfully unaware. She successfully retreated inward, collecting her thoughts until she was as oblivious to the chaotic scene unfolding in Mr. Gray's shop as she would be in her own bedroom. Finally, the transaction concluded. Ivory, gold, and pearls were assigned their places. The gentleman, having declared the final date by which his existence could endure without his new treasure, slowly donned his gloves. Bestowing one last look upon the Miss Dashwoods—a gaze that seemed to demand admiration rather than simply offer it—he departed with an air of genuine self-satisfaction and feigned indifference.

Elinor wasted no time advancing her own business and was on the verge of completing it when another gentleman approached her side. Turning to meet his gaze, she was surprised to recognize her brother, John Dashwood. "I am extremely glad to hear this, upon my word; truly glad. But so it ought to be; they are people of substantial means, related to you, and every civility and accommodation that serves to make your situation pleasant might reasonably be expected. And you are most comfortably settled in your little cottage, lacking for nothing!"

"Eleanor brought us a most charming account of the place," he continued. "He described it as the most complete residence of its kind ever seen, noting that you all seemed to enjoy it beyond measure. It was a great satisfaction to us to hear this, I assure you."

Elinor felt a twinge of shame toward her brother and was relieved when Mrs. Jennings's servant arrived to inform them that his mistress awaited them at the door. Mr. Dashwood escorted them downstairs, introducing them to Mrs. Jennings beside her carriage. Repeating his hope to call the following day, he took his leave. His subsequent visit was duly paid, accompanied by a pretextual apology from his sister-in-law for her absence, claiming she was entirely occupied with her mother and thus had no leisure to go anywhere. Mrs. Jennings, however, immediately assured him that she would not stand on ceremony, reminding him that they were all cousins or something akin to kin. She promised to visit Mrs. John Dashwood shortly, bringing her sisters along.

His demeanor toward the Dashwood sisters was calmly kind, while his treatment of Mrs. Jennings was notably attentive. Soon after, Colonel Brandon entered. John eyed him with a curious calculation that suggested he sought to know him primarily to ascertain his wealth, offering equal civility only once that condition was met. After remaining for half an hour, John invited Elinor to walk with him to Conduit Street to introduce him to Sir John and Lady Middleton. The weather was exceptionally fine, and she readily agreed.

Once outside, his inquiries commenced immediately. "Who is Colonel Brandon? Is he a man of fortune?"

"Yes; he possesses very good property in Dorsetshire."

"I am glad to hear that. He appears to be a most gentlemanlike man, and I think, Elinor, I may congratulate you on the prospect of a very respectable establishment in life."

"Me, brother? What do you mean?"

"He likes you. I observed him closely and am convinced of it. What is the extent of his fortune?"

"I believe it amounts to about two thousand a year."

"Two thousand a year!" he exclaimed, rising to a pitch of enthusiastic generosity. "Elinor, I wish with all my heart it were *twice* as much, for your sake."

"Indeed, I believe you," Elinor replied dryly, "but I am very certain that Colonel Brandon has not the slightest intention of marrying me."

"You are mistaken, Elinor; greatly mistaken. A very little effort on your part would secure him. Perhaps he is currently undecided; the modesty of your fortune may cause hesitation, or his friends may advise against it. But certain little attentions and encouragements—which ladies can so easily dispense—would fix him, regardless of his inclinations. There is no reason why you should not attempt to win him. It is not to be supposed that any prior attachment exists on your side—in short, you know as well as I do that such matters are quite out of the question; the objections are insurmountable. You possess too much sense not to see this clearly. Colonel Brandon must be the man, and no lack of civility on my part shall prevent him from becoming pleased with you and your family. It is a match destined to give universal satisfaction. In short, it is a matter of—"he lowered his voice to an important whisper—"which will be exceedingly welcome to *all parties*."

Recalling himself, he added, "That is, I mean to say—your friends are truly anxious to see you well settled; Fanny especially, for she holds your interests very close to her heart, I assure you. And her mother, Mrs. Ferrars, a woman of excellent nature, is sure it would bring her great pleasure; she mentioned as much the other day."

Elinor refused to offer any response.

"It would be somewhat remarkable, now," he continued, "perhaps even droll, if Fanny were to settle a brother while I settle a sister simultaneously. And yet, it is not entirely unlikely."

"Is Mr. Edward Ferrars," Elinor asked with resolve, "going to be married?"

"It is not officially settled, but there is talk of it. He has a most excellent mother. Mrs. Ferrars, with the utmost liberality, intends to provide him with an annual settlement of one thousand pounds if the union proceeds. The lady is the Honorable Miss Morton, the only daughter of the late Lord Morton, endowed with thirty thousand pounds. It is a highly desirable connection on both sides, and I have no doubt it will materialize in time. A thousand a-year is a significant sum for a mother to bestow permanently, yet Mrs. Ferrars possesses a noble spirit. To cite another example of her generosity: upon our arrival in town, realizing that funds were scarce for us at the moment, she placed banknotes totaling two hundred pounds into Fanny's hands. It was extremely welcome, for we must maintain a high standard of expenditure while here."

He paused, expecting her assent and compassion, forcing himself to reply, "Your expenses, both in town and country, must certainly be considerable; yet your income is substantial."
