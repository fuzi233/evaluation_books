A fire burned within Daniel Deronda, hotter and more urgent than ever before. Hans Cohen's words regarding Mirah's need for relief from jealousy had acted as the final spark, igniting a resolve that left him trembling with anticipation. On his next visit to Ezra Levin, Deronda arrived with a singular, unyielding purpose: to secure a private audience with Mirah. If she returned his love, he believed he possessed the courage to face whatever consequences followed. More importantly, as her betrothed husband, he envisioned gaining a protective authority over her—a shield against the inevitable storms that threatened to batter her fragile existence, particularly in her fraught relationship with her father.

Though Deronda observed no outward signs of restlessness in Lapidoth, nor any waning effort from the old man to court his daughter, a deep foreboding gnawed at Daniel's heart. He sensed a future struggle looming, a potential mortification or an intolerable surge of domestic discord that could render Ezra and Mirah helpless victims. These anxieties would have grown sharper had Deronda understood the turmoil churning beneath Lapidoth's surface. What appeared to Ezra as a petty, desultory torment was, for Lapidoth, a grueling submission to restraint, endured only because he viewed it as a temporary price for eventual, unbridled freedom.

Lapidoth had originally intended to wait for a favorable opportunity, perhaps an occasion to extract a substantial sum from Deronda. Yet, driven by an imperious gambling instinct that wove a relentless web through his life, he found his curiosity sharpening into a desperate hunt. He was scouring the household, his mind fixated on locating Mirah's hidden money and keys. Mirah, however, possessed a practical clarity that thwarted him; having entrusted all her savings except for immediate necessities to Mrs. Meyrick's care, she created a fortress of safety around her resources. To Lapidoth, this felt like the stifling security of a lunatic asylum, where every outlet was blocked. In his mind, secretly appropriating a few notes from a desk would have been a harmless domestic right, a liberty men often stretch far beyond the legal boundaries. He felt grievously treated, convinced he deserved access to her earnings as readily as he had once devoured her apple tarts. Yet, despite his grievances, he remained submissive. The temptation to appeal directly to Deronda, offering his services in exchange for funds to buy his way out, grew strong. He reasoned that clever people with nothing to sell could command high prices for their absence, and surely Deronda would welcome the chance to be rid of him. But a lingering, almost superstitious awe of Ezra's formidable friend held him back, causing him to defer his plans indefinitely.

That afternoon, however, circumstances aligned with a cruel irony. Deronda entered the room buoyed by a gladdened consciousness, his spirits so high they illuminated his very demeanor. Simultaneously, Lapidoth reached a crisis point, his mind consumed by schemes of escape, and Deronda's newfound amiability only fed these delusions. So preoccupied was the old man that he failed to engage with the reading material placed before him, eventually rising to smoke and wander in the square. This sudden silence freed the two friends. Though Mirah was absent, Deronda knew she would return before his departure, and a secret, glowing hope filled him. He anticipated seeing a sweet, newfound recognition in her eyes, a warmth previously denied to him. His manner toward Ezra became playfully affectionate.

"This little room is too close for you, Ezra," Deronda remarked, pausing his reading. "The heat of this season here rivals Genoa's worst, yet you sit in shade while I bake. You deserve a better home. As the stronger half, I shall arrange things as I please." He smiled warmly at Ezra, who replied, "I am straitened for nothing save breath. You, who might roam a spacious palace, find yourself in a narrow cage. Yet, I cannot bid you 'go.'"

"Oh, the country would feel like exile while you remain," Deronda said, rising to pace the cramped space, fanning himself with a handkerchief. "To me, this is the happiest room in the world. Besides, I shall imagine myself in the East, preparing for my journey there someday." He spoke with emphasis, removing his heavy cravat and a large ring, placing them on a small table behind Ezra, whose desk was cluttered with books and papers.

"I have worn this ring since returning home," Deronda continued, resettling himself. "Yet, I am such a Sybarite that I constantly cast it off as a burden during tasks. I understand why Romans wore summer rings—if they did. Now, I shall focus better."

Soon, they were lost in their work. Deronda read a piece of Rabbinical Hebrew under Ezra's correction, ignoring the subtle shift in the atmosphere until Lapidoth re-entered, seating himself inconspicuously in the background.

Lapidoth's wandering eyes snapped to the table, where the ring sparkled on the dark wood. During his walk, his mind had been rehearsing a fantasy: approaching Deronda privately to request a sum for travel, expecting an immediate inquiry about the amount. This scenario haunted him, torn between the fear of asking too little and the regret of asking too much. His desire knew no limits, and he lacked a gauge for Deronda's generosity. But now, amidst these airy preparations, the ring lay there, detached and within easy reach. Its value was modest compared to his imagined fortunes, yet it presented itself as a solid, tangible fact. A lightning thought struck him—not yet an intention, but a flicker of madness: if he quietly pocketed the ring and slipped away, he would possess the means for comfortable escape, free from trouble or danger. Since it was Deronda's property, and Deronda would never prosecute his own "children" for theft, the act would pass unnoticed. The images followed each other rapidly, coalescing into a single vision. Lapidoth had never committed larceny, though legally, taking this item from a virtual relative who might otherwise give freely would technically constitute theft. Still, the prospect of a heavier gift, obtained swiftly, outweighed the risk. The mental image of grasping the ring repeated like an internal melody, only to sink back into rejection. Satisfied his urgent longing by deciding to descend and watch for Deronda's departure, he rose and stood by the window. He calculated the short path to the door beside the table, resolved to execute his plan the moment he joined Deronda outside.

However, a dominance of desire, akin to a drinker's thirst, overrode his conscious resolve. As he passed the table, his fingers fell silently upon the ring. Before he realized it, he stood in the hallway, the ring clutched in his hand. Without hesitation, he donned his hat and fled the house. The possibility of returning to his children receded into the distant fog, replaced by a frantic concentration on selling the ring and boarding a ship immediately.

Inside, Deronda and Ezra barely noticed his exit. Soon after, Mirah entered, causing a genuine disruption. Her hat remained on her head. Rising to greet her, Deronda found her confused, troubled by a sensation she could not name.

"I only came to ensure Ezra has his new draught," she stammered. "I must go straight to Mrs. Meyrick's to retrieve something."

"Pray, allow me to walk with you," Deronda urged earnestly. "I tire Ezra enough already, and my brain feels like melted wax. I wish to accompany you to Mrs. Meyrick's."

"Oh, yes," Mirah murmured, blushing deeper. A vague, electric feeling moved through her, marking something new in Deronda. Turning away to prepare the medicine, she poured Ezra's draught while he leaned back, eyes closed, unable to shake the troubling ideas that had filled his mind during the reading session. For a moment, Deronda stood lost in thought, envisioning the walk, until Mirah returned with the medicine. Then, suddenly remembering his discarded cravat, he excused his disheveled state, retrieved it from the table, and cried out in violent surprise, "Good heavens! Where is my ring?"

He began searching frantically on the floor. Ezra glanced around the corner of his chair. Mirah, swift as thought, moved to where Deronda searched, asking, "Did you lay it down?"

"Yes," Deronda replied, blind to any alternative explanation save that the ring had fallen into the shadows of the variegated carpet. He moved furniture pieces, digging with his hands and squinting.

But a different truth had seized Mirah, draining the color from her cheeks. She leaned close to Ezra's ear and whispered, "Was my father here?" Ezra nodded, his expression terrible with understanding. Mirah darted back to Deronda, who was still casting his eyes in hopeless exploration. "You have not found it?" she asked hurriedly.

Meeting her terrified gaze, Deronda's alarm surged. "Perhaps I put it in my pocket," he lied, pretending to feel for it.

Mirah watched him closely. "It is not there? You put it on the table!" Her voice was sharp, piercing through his deception, refusing to let him pretend to find it in his pocket. Immediately, she rushed from the room.
