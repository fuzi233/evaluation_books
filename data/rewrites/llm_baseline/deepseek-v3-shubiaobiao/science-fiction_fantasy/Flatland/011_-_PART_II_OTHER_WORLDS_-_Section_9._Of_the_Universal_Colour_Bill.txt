As the grip of tradition loosened, the intellectual foundations of Flatland began to crumble. The once-venerated Art of Sight Recognition, now deemed obsolete, faded into obscurity. Geometry, Statics, Kinetics—disciplines that had once defined our scholarly pursuits—were dismissed as relics of a bygone era, even within the hallowed halls of our University. The simpler Art of Feeling soon met the same fate in our schools.

The Isosceles classes, long burdened by their subservient role, seized the moment. No longer compelled to contribute to the upkeep of education, they grew bolder, their numbers swelling unchecked. Without the tempering influence of intellectual discipline, their brutish instincts flourished. Meanwhile, the Soldiers and Artisans, emboldened by their newfound equality with the highest Polygons, declared that the complexities of life—whether Statical or Kinetical—could now be mastered through the effortless magic of Colour Recognition alone.

Dissatisfied with the natural decline of Sight Recognition, they demanded more: the outright abolition of all "elitist arts" and the defunding of mathematics, geometry, and sensory training. Equality, they argued, was not merely a social ideal but a natural law—Colour had erased the old hierarchies, and so too should the legal distinctions between classes.

The revolutionaries pressed further. If Colour was the great equalizer, then even the Priests and Women—long exempt from such markings—must submit to its rule. When met with resistance (for how could one paint beings with no sides?), they countered with cunning logic: *Should not the front of every person—the half bearing the eye and mouth—be distinguished from the rear?*

Thus, the Universal Colour Bill was born.

Under its provisions:
- **Women** would be painted red on the front half (containing eye and mouth) and green on the rear.
- **Priests**, though Circular, would follow the same scheme—red on the forward semicircle, green on the hinder.

The implications were insidious. From certain angles, a Woman’s line would mirror a Priest’s: half red, half green. In a society already losing its grasp on Sight Recognition, confusion was inevitable. Women, quick to exploit the ambiguity, would be mistaken for Priests—overhearing state secrets, issuing commands, basking in unearned reverence. The Circles, meanwhile, would suffer the erosion of their authority as the masses bowed to impostors.

But the Bill’s true malice lay deeper. The Circles, last bastions of intellectual rigor, relied on their colourless homes to hone their discernment. Now, forced into chromatic conformity, their children would grow up in a world of deceptive hues. The simplest act of recognition—distinguishing father from mother—would become a puzzle, vulnerable to maternal trickery. Trust in logic itself would fray.

And so, beneath the banner of equality, the architects of the Bill orchestrated the slow unraveling of the elite. The Irregular behind it—no crude Isosceles, but a cunning defect spared in folly—knew well: once the Circles faltered, the rest would follow. Aristocracy would collapse.

The Women, of course, rejoiced. What did they care for the ruin of the state? To them, the chaos was power.

And power, once tasted, is seldom surrendered.
