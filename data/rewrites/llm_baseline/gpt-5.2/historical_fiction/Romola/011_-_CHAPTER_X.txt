Three weeks had slipped away since Tito, with the easy confidence of a man who dislikes rough edges more than he loves any distant good, had poured his bright florins into Cennini’s palm. And on this day of San Giovanni, when Florence steamed in holiday heat and every narrow lane held a draught of warm dust and laughter, he carried himself as if the city were made expressly to agree with him. He turned out of the blaze into a shaded street, lifted off his black cloth berretta, and let the air touch his curls. His brow told no story—neither innocence nor guile—only youth, smooth and well-shaped; his eyes, wide and clear, moved with that untroubled glance which makes a face seem honest to those who want honesty, and harmless to those who want harmlessness. If admiration followed him, it did so as it follows a bright day: willingly, without asking why.

The festa had unknotted from the solemn lines of procession and was now streaming everywhere, seeking its next diversion. Such loosened moments were when a crowd’s appetite for sport turned peevish, when it wanted something living to poke and laugh at. Near San Martino, by the hostelry of the Bertucce, a clump had formed—bodies pressed shoulder to shoulder, heads craning, voices flinging their mirth like stones.

Tito would have slipped away; the Via de’ Bardi drew him, and time, like a creditor, was already tapping his shoulder. But under the laughter he caught a thin, shaken cry, a child’s voice cracked with fear: “Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!”

That sound tugged at the gentler seam in him. He pushed forward.

In the middle of the knot a man in parti-coloured cerretano dress held fast the wrists of a young contadina, laughing and coaxing as if her terror were only another trick. The onlookers sided with him in the quick, local cruelty of dialect—Florentine words for fool and simpleton spilling out with a nimble pride, as though the language itself were a cudgel they enjoyed swinging. The girl’s hood had fallen back; light-brown hair lay neatly plaited and pinned with a long silver clasp. Then the struggle turned her face toward Tito, and he saw, as suddenly as if the crowd had parted like water, Tessa—blue eyes flooded, lower lip trembling, baby features distorted by fright.

She saw him too. Hope flared through tears with the immediacy of a child recognizing a hand that has fed it.

Tito broke the circle. He caught her about the waist, drew her to him, and his voice cut through the merriment.

“Loose this child! By what right do you hold her against her will?”

The cerretano’s sharp-featured face—every angle tilted upward into impishness—showed small teeth in a grin that wanted to pass for good humour. He released her wrists, flung his hands behind him, shoulders rising in half apology, half protest.

“I mean the ragazza no evil in the world, Messere—ask this respectable company. I was only going to show a few samples of my skill, and she would have helped me well with that kitten face of hers—people trust what looks innocent. I promised her a lapful of confetti. But Messer has doubtless better confetti at hand, and she knows it.”

Laughter burst, greased by the sight of Tessa clinging to Tito’s arm as if it were a beam in floodwater. The laughs were not words to her; they were only the roar of beasts she had escaped. Tito, with irritation already pricking him—this delay, these eyes, these tongues—hastened to move.

The conjuror tossed the fallen hood back over Tessa’s head with a jaunty flourish. “Orsù, bear me no malice; come back when Messere can spare you.”

“Ay, Maestro Vaiano—she’ll come back presently, as the toad said to the harrow!” someone called, and the boys’ voices caracoled after the phrase, pleased with their own cruelty.

Tito thrust through toward a side-street, meaning to drop this troublesome incident as he would shake off dust. Yet the next street, too, greeted them with holiday remarks—some blunt, some leering, all carried on the loud common air like birds that had learned to sing only one coarse tune. He bent his head toward Tessa and spoke kindly but fast, as one speaks to a child who must be quieted on the way.

“Now, little one—where were you going? Are you come alone to the festa?”

Her fear surged back as soon as the cerretano was behind them. “Ah, no! I have lost my mother in the crowd—and my father-in-law. They will be angry—he will beat me. It was in San Pulinari—somebody pushed me and I couldn’t stop, and I got away from them. Oh, I don’t know where they’re gone! Please, don’t leave me!”

The sob at the end shook her like wind in a small tree.

Tito hurried on. The Badia was near; in a church one could speak quietly, and perhaps he could leave her with some orderly protection. But at that hour the doors were shut. They paused under the cloister’s shelter, where the sound of the festa dimmed into a distant murmur—like surf heard from behind a wall. The quieter acoustics soothed her only a little. Tito tried to shape the problem into a simple errand.

“Have you no cousin in Florence, my little Tessa? No friend whose house you can find? Or are you afraid to walk by yourself now that conjuror has frightened you? I am in haste to Oltrarno, but if I could take you near—”

“Oh, I am frightened—he was the devil, I know he was. And I don’t know where to go. I have nobody. My mother meant to have her dinner somewhere, and I don’t know where. Holy Madonna! I shall be beaten.”

Her mouth drooped. The beads above her green serge bodice rose and fell with her breath; tears began again, large and hot, falling as if they had been held back and now hurried to make up for lost time. She lifted her holiday apron to catch them, trying to be small, to be unseen.

It would have been brutal to leave her; and if Tito had a vice, it was not brutality but an almost childish need to keep the world smooth. He wished—briefly—that he had not been expected in the Via de’ Bardi, because duty, like harsh bread, is hardest when one is already warmed by something sweet.

He laid his hand on her apron, then, as one calms a frightened creature, rubbed her cheek and kissed its soft roundness.

“My poor little Tessa. Leave off crying. Let us see what can be done. Where is your home—where do you live?”

No answer at first; only the sobbing subsided into little catches of breath.

“Come. I’ll take you a little way, if you will tell me where you want to go.”

The apron fell. Her face cleared with the swift, unreasoning gratitude of a child: fear receded as if distance alone could undo it.

“I think I’ll go home, if you’ll take me,” she whispered, looking up at him with wide blue eyes that held something sweeter than a smile—an entire trust.

“Come then, little one,” Tito said, his tone softening as he offered his arm. “Which way is it?”

“Beyond Peretola—where the large pear-tree is.”

“Peretola? Out at which gate, pazzarella? I am a stranger—you must remember.”

“Out at the Por del Prato,” she said, clinging fast.

Tito did not know every turning well enough to choose quietness with certainty. Besides, where there were most people, there might be most chance of finding Monna Ghita, and so ending this accidental chivalry. He went straight toward Porta Rossa and Ognissanti, showing his bright, placating face to all observers who flung their jokes at the handsome young signor and his heavy-shod little maiden. The streets were full of mixed lives: decent holiday folk; apprentices with frolic that turned sour at envy; bold-eyed women under the yellow veil; beggars thrusting caps forward in a mockery of his haste; dicers and loungers whose voices carried a too-easy cruelty; boys whose tongues moved together like a flock trained to mob. Florence’s soundscape was not all bells and sacred singing; under the festa ran a coarse, persistent racket, a kind of public appetite that made a lost girl’s terror reasonable.

At Piazza d’Ognissanti Tito slackened. Heat pressed on both of them; the space opened wide, with stone benches against old walls. They sat.

“Holy Virgin,” Tessa breathed, “I am glad we have got away from those women and boys; but I was not frightened, because you could take care of me.”

“Pretty little Tessa,” Tito said, amused. “What makes you feel so safe with me?”

“Because you are so beautiful—like the people going into Paradise: they are all good.”

He saw nearby stalls with fruit and sweetmeats—their scent rising even above the human smell of sun and bodies: ripe apricots giving off that faint winey perfume; fresh-baked cakes smelling of warm flour and anise; comfits bright as beads. The thought of feeding her was easy kindness, and easier still than finding her mother.

“It is a long while since breakfast,” he said. “Are you hungry?”

“Yes, I think I am—if you will have some too.”

He bought apricots, little cakes, comfits—hands passing coins, paper and cloth rustling, the vendor’s singsong cry cutting through the air. He tipped the fruit into Tessa’s apron, which she gathered up like a pouch; the apricots lay there like small suns, downy and fragrant, their skins blushing where they had ripened most.

“Come,” he said. “Let us walk on to the Prato, and then perhaps you will not be afraid to go the rest of the way alone.”

“But you will have some,” she insisted, lifting the apron slightly as if offering a sacrament. “And we could eat together.”

“We will see,” he said aloud. And in his mind, warmed by the girl’s simple devotion and by the sight of her cradling food as if it were treasure, the fancy rose that she could have been the rustic heroine of some new pastoral—if only he had time to idle in the sun like poets and shepherds. The mischief, he told himself, was done: he was late already; another half-hour would hardly matter. And this—this was pleasant.

As they walked, she began to talk, words tumbling like grain from a loosened sack.

“We have a garden and plenty of pears, and two cows, besides the mules; and I’m very fond of them. But my father-in-law is a cross man. I wish my mother had not married him. I think he is wicked—he is very ugly.”

“And does your mother let him beat you, poverina? You said you feared it.”

“Ah, my mother herself scolds me. She loves my young sister better, and thinks I don’t do work enough. Nobody speaks kindly to me—only the Pievano when I go to confession. And the men in the Mercato laugh at me. Nobody ever kissed me and spoke to me as you do—only I talk so to my little black-faced kid, because I’m very fond of it.”

She did not seem to notice that Tito, in his fine dress and easy bearing, was no longer the same figure who had begged milk from her. To her he was still chiefly a face and a voice—something dropped out of brightness into her harsh day. In her world, language had always come weighted: commands, scoldings, coarse jests. Her own simple country speech, with its unguarded tenderness, did not stand in Florentine mouths. Here, in these streets, she heard words weaponized; she could not have said it so, but she felt it: the city’s dialect made sport of her, as if the very sounds told her she belonged beneath them.

They reached the Prato, a broad open space within the walls where young men played calcio and practised their strength. At midday it lay quiet, grass shimmering, the air thick and still, the distant preparation at the gate for the coming race like a muted promise. For a moment the noise of Florence seemed held back, as if the city inhaled here.

Tito stopped at the border of the open meadow.

“Now, Tessa, you will not be frightened if I leave you to walk the rest of the way by yourself. Addio! Shall I come and buy a cup of milk from you in the Mercato to-morrow morning—to see you are quite safe?”

He spoke soothingly, but he saw the instant sorrow in her widening eyes, the mouth corners sinking. She said nothing. She only opened her apron and looked down at the apricots and sweetmeats as if they had suddenly become too much—too heavy with the meaning of being given and not shared. Then she looked up, complaint trembling out of her.

“I thought you would have some, and we could sit down under a tree outside the gate, and eat them together.”

“Tessa, Tessa—you little siren,” he said, laughing as he kissed both her cheeks. “You would ruin me. I ought to have been in the Via de’ Bardi long ago. No—I must go back now. You are in no danger. There—I’ll take an apricot. Addio!”

He stepped away as he spoke. Tessa went pale; a sob rose like a wave. She turned, as if there were no use pleading, and began to walk on. She held her apron so forgetfully that the fruit rolled out onto the grass—soft thuds, the faintest bruising sound, as if even sweetness could be wasted.

Tito looked after her. He saw her shoulders lift with the sob she tried to swallow, saw the apricots tumble. He could not help himself; gentleness pulled him back as surely as vanity had pulled him forward into the crowd.

He went after her and stooped, gathering up the fallen fruit one by one, their skins warm from her body and the sun.

“See, my silly one,” he said. “Come—leave off crying. I will go with you, and we’ll sit down under the tree. Only remember, I must go back sometime.”

So it happened that not far beyond the gate they found a great plane-tree, its broad leaves making a net of shade. The air there was different: cooler, threaded with the whisper of leaves, the faint insect drone, the distant muffled rumble of the city like a sea held at bay. They sat under it, and the feast was spread on Tessa’s lap. She leaned against the trunk; Tito stretched opposite, elbows resting on the rough green growth that clung in the shade. Sunlight slipped through the boughs and played over them, moving and pausing like a living thing.

Tessa ate slowly, tasting with the reverence of someone for whom fruit was not a mere snack but a season’s gift. The apricots split under her small fingers, juice shining on the torn flesh; the smell rose richer as the pit was freed. The cakes crumbled, sweet with spice; the comfits clicked between teeth. Her contentment returned so completely it was as if fear had been only a bad dream.

“You pretty bird,” Tito said, watching her weigh in her mind whether to save the remains because he had said he would have no more. “To think of anyone scolding you! What sins do you tell at confession, Tessa?”

“Oh—a great many. I am often naughty. I don’t like work, and I can’t help being idle though I know I shall be beaten and scolded. And I give the mules the best fodder when nobody sees, and then when the Madre is angry I say I didn’t do it, and that makes me frightened at the devil. I think the conjuror was the devil. I am not so frightened after confession. And see—I’ve got a Breve here, that a good father who came to Prato preaching this Easter blessed and gave us all.”

She drew from her bosom a tiny bag, carefully tied, holding it up as solemnly as a relic. In that small gesture lay generations: poor folk binding comfort into scraps of parchment, faith made portable, language reduced to scrawls that promised protection even when read by none. It was a charm that belonged to her world, where the sacred had to be worn close because the day itself was hard.

“And I think the holy Madonna will take care of me,” she added, “she looks as if she would. And perhaps if I wasn’t idle, she wouldn’t let me be beaten.”

Tito, amused and moved by turns, leaned into the softness of the hour.

“If they are so cruel, Tessa—shouldn’t you like to leave them, and go live with a beautiful lady who would be kind to you, if she would have you to wait on her?”

She held her breath. Her eyes fixed on him, then wavered.

“I don’t know,” she said at last, doubtful because happiness offered too easily feels like a trick.

“Then should you like to be my little servant, and live with me?” Tito asked, smiling, meaning only to watch the pretty spark his words would make.

Joy flushed over her face at once. “Will you take me with you now? Ah—I shouldn’t go home and be beaten then.” She paused, then added with sudden practical sorrow, “But I should like to fetch my black-faced kid.”

“Yes, you must go back to your kid, my Tessa,” Tito said, rising, as if that settled it neatly. “And I must go the other way.”

Then, stepping from the shade into sun, he muttered with a half-laugh, “By Jupiter, it is not a pleasant time of day to walk from here to the Via de’ Bardi. I am more inclined to lie down and sleep in this shade.”

And that inclination—so often the true pilot of his life—won. He had risen early; had waited; had watched sights; had walked in sun; and the cool hush under the plane-tree, with Tessa near like an added softness in the air, tempted him past resistance. He lay down again, put his cap beneath his head on a tuft of grass by her side. It was not comfortable; he shifted, and then asked, as easily as if it were the most natural thing in the world, to rest his head against her lap. In that posture, cradled by her small warmth and by the leaf-dappled shade, he fell asleep.

Tessa sat still as a dove on its nest. The city’s sound was far away; here the world was leaf-whisper, breath, the occasional faint call from beyond the gate. When he slept deeply, she dared to touch the dark curls falling back from his ear, as if they were something sacred. She was too happy to sleep, too happy even to imagine waking would bring departure. In childhood—and in childish hearts that remain so—the new sweetness feels like a spell that will remake everything forever, leaving no path back to the old scolding voices.

Time slipped. The sun moved. Shade shifted in slow patterns along the grass.

At last Tito’s sleep broke into a doze in which he was again in the Via de’ Bardi, explaining why he had not appeared when expected. The image jolted him awake like a hand on the shoulder. He started up, sat straight, stretched his arms, shook out his cap.

“Tessa, little one—you have let me sleep too long. My hunger and the shadows together tell me the sun has travelled since I fell asleep. I must lose no more time. Addio.” He patted her cheek with one hand, settled his cap with the other.

She said nothing, but her face shifted toward misery; the tears gathered, ready to spill.

He spoke again, forcing into his voice a seriousness it did not naturally carry, the nearest he could come to sternness.

“Now, Tessa—you must not cry. I shall be angry; I shall not love you if you cry. You must go home to your black-faced kid; or if you like, go back to the gate and see the horses start. But I can stay with you no longer. If you cry, I shall think you are troublesome to me.”

The tears stopped as if struck down. Terror at the change in him turned her pale; she trembled, eyes widened with the grief she dared not let fall. The power of a word—love withdrawn, anger promised—was stronger than any charm she wore.

Tito’s gentleness required sorrow to be kept out of sight. He softened again at once, searching for a neat ending, a sweet plug to stop the ache.

“Look now,” he said, opening the wallet at his belt. “Here is a pretty charm I have had a long while—ever since I was in Sicily, a country far away.”

He fumbled among the small disorder of coins and trifles, unhooked the wallet, and tipped its contents onto Tessa’s lap. There, among odds and tokens, lay his onyx ring.

“Ah, my ring!” he exclaimed, slipping it onto his right forefinger. “I forgot to put it on again this morning. Strange—I never missed it!”

Then he found what he wanted: a small pointed bit of red coral, polished and vivid as a drop of sealed wax.

“See, Tessa,” he said, holding it up. “This little coral horn—like your goat’s horn, is it not? There is a hole, so you can put it on the cord round your neck with your Breve. Then the evil spirits cannot hurt you. If you ever see them coming in the shadow round a corner, point this at them—and they will run away. It is a buona fortuna, and will keep you from harm when I am not with you. Come—undo the cord.”

She obeyed, her fingers clumsy with haste, comfort flooding back as if the world were indeed being rearranged into safety. The coral hung beside the tiny bag of parchment; red against her skin, like a bright promise.

“And now you will give me a kiss,” Tito said, already sweeping the wallet’s contents back and hanging it at his waist, speaking as he worked to save time, “and look happy, like a good girl, and then—”

But she had already leaned forward and kissed his cheek, quick and obedient, mouth soft as a child’s blessing.

“Oh, you pretty pigeon!” Tito cried, laughing. He pressed her cheeks between his hands, crushing her features together and covering them with a general, impartial kiss, more playful than tender.

Then he sprang up and walked away. He did not look back until he was ten yards off; then he turned and gave a parting beck. Tessa watched him, still, not crying—her distress held down by fear and by the new weight of the coral charm at her throat. That was enough for him. His own ease depended on not seeing pain.

As he went, the distance to the Via de’ Bardi seemed longer than before, the sun heavier. The calm shade and the scent of fruit clung to him like a sweetness he had borrowed and must now pay for in hot steps.

“I wonder when Romola will kiss my cheek in that way?” he thought—half idly, half with the inward impatience of a man who wants the finest things without the rough labour of earning them.

No other excuse would be needed, he told himself. To Bardo and Romola he would say simply he had been unexpectedly hindered; their proud delicacy would ask no more. He hastened to Ognissanti, swallowed food quickly—bread and whatever the host could set before him—more to quiet hunger than to taste it, then crossed the Arno by the Ponte alla Carraja and made his way as directly as possible toward the Via de’ Bardi, leaving behind him, under a plane-tree, a girl with apricot-stained fingers and a charm against devils, listening to the fading of his footsteps as if the sound itself were the measure of her safety.
