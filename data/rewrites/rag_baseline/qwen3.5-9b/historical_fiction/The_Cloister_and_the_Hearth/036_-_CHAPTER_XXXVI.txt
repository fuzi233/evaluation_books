"In prison, sir? Good heavens, for what transgression?"

"She is a witness, and potentially a necessary one."

"Why, Messire Bailiff," interjected Denys, "you do not lay all your witnesses by the heels, I trust."

The alderman, pleased by the title of bailiff, grew communicative. "In cases of blood, we detain every testimony likely to provide legal leverage and thus thwart justice; hence we still hold the womenfolk. A man may hide in a single mindset for a week, but a woman, if she fulfills her duty to the realm on Friday, shall undo it before Sunday, or perish trying. Could you see that wench now, you would find her sobbing over having betrayed five males to the gallows. Had they been females, we might have trusted a subpoena. They despise one another, showing some sense. But now I recall, there were other reasons for binding this one. Hand me those depositions, young sir." He adjusted his spectacles. "Ah! She was implicated; she was part of the band."

A loud denial erupted simultaneously from Denys and Gerard.

"No need to deafen me," snapped the alderman. "It is written in black and white. 'Jean Hardy (one of the thieves), when questioned, confessed that—hmm? Yes, here it is. 'And that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; and hanged last month: and that she has been depressed ever since, openly blaming the band for his death, stating if they had not been rank cowards, he would never have been caught, and it is his opinion she betrayed them out of pure spite, and—'"

"'His opinion,'" Gerard cried indignantly. "What significance does the opinion of a cutthroat hold, burning to exact revenge on the woman who delivered him to justice? If you rely on that, what value is his testimony? Is a thief never a liar? Is he not always a liar? And here is a motive to lie: revenge, which is the strongest of all passions. Oh, sir, what madness to question a detected felon and listen to him fabricate an honest life—as if he were a truthful man swearing in broad daylight with his true hand on the Gospel!"

"Young man," the alderman said, "restrain your heat in the presence of authority! By your tone, I find you are a stranger. Know then that in this land we question everyone. We are not so weak as to hope to reach the truth by closing either our left ear or our right."

"And so you would listen to Satan defaming the saints!"

"Nonsense! The law deals only with men and women, and these cannot utter a story entirely of falsehoods, however hard they try. Therefore, we do not shut the barn-door against any man's grain. Once admitted, we merely winnow and sift. Who told you I swallowed the thief's story whole like clear water? Not so. I credited only so much as was corroborated by superior proof."

"Superior proof?" Gerard looked bewildered. "Who else but the thieves would speak against her?"

"Marry, herself."

"Herself, sir? Did you question her too?"

"I tell you we question everyone. Here is her deposition; can you read? Read it yourself, then."

Gerard glanced at Denys and handed him Manon's deposition.

"I am a native of Epinal. I left my home two years ago due to misfortune: I could not endure the man they forced upon me. My father beat me. I fled from my father. I went into service. I left service because the mistress was jealous of me. Her reason for dismissing me was that I was saucy. Last year I stood in the marketplace to be hired alongside other girls. The landlord of 'The Fair Star' employed me. I served him for eleven months. A young man courted me. I loved him. I discovered that travelers arrived and never departed again. I told my lover. He commanded me to hold my peace. He threatened me. I learned my lover belonged to a band of thieves. When travelers were to be robbed, the landlord went out and signaled the band to assemble. Then I wept and prayed for the travelers' souls. I never spoke. A month ago my lover died.

"The soldier reminded me of my lover. He was bearded like the one I had lost. I cannot say whether I should have intervened if he had lacked a beard. I regret speaking now."

The paper nearly slipped from Gerard's grasp. For the first time, he realized Manon's life hung by a thread. Knowing the doggedness of the law and the relentless men who enforced it, he threw himself suddenly to his knees at the alderman's feet. "Oh, sir! Consider the chasm between those cruel men and this poor, fragile woman! Could you possess the heart to send her to the same death as them? Could you have the heart to condemn us to watch her slaughter, who, risking her life for ours, had not otherwise been in peril? Alas, sir! Show me and my comrade some pity, if you have none for her, poor soul. Denys and I are true men, and you will rend our hearts if you kill that poor, simple girl. What can we do? What remains for us but to cut our throats at the foot of her gallows?"

The alderman was tough, yet mortal; the prayers and agitation of Gerard first astonished, then moved him. He displayed this in a peculiar manner, becoming peevish and fretful. "There, rise, do," he said. "I doubt anyone would speak so many words for me. What ho, Daniel! Fetch the town clerk." As the functionary entered from an adjoining room, the alderman continued, "Here is a foolish lad fretting about that girl. Can we stretch a point? Say we admit her to bear witness and question her favorably."

The town clerk was a man of "impossibilities."

"Nay, sir, we cannot do that: she was not involved in this affair. Had she been an accessory, we might have offered her a pardon to testify."

Gerard burst forth, "But she did better. Instead of being an accessory, she halted the crime; and she volunteered as a witness by rushing here with the tale."

"Tush, young man, 'tis a matter of law." The alderman and the clerk then engaged in a lengthy debate, the one maintaining, the other denying, that she stood as legally valid as if she had been an accomplice to the attempted robbery of the travelers. This proved fortunate for Manon: for the alderman, irritated by the clerk's repetition that he could not do this, nor that, nor the other, declared, "I will show him I can do anything I choose." He summoned Manon forth. Upon the landlord of "The White Hart" acting as her bondsman, and Denys depositing five gold pieces with him, and the girl promising, not without some coaxing from Denys, to appear as a witness, he released her, easing his conscience by explaining his leniency in his own terms.

"The town had to purchase a new rope for every hanged person and present it to the executioner, or compound with him in money: and she was, in his opinion, not worth this municipal expense, whereas determined characters like her late confederates certainly were." Thus, Denys and Gerard carried her off, Gerard dancing around her in joy, Denys bolstering her spirits by assuring her of the demise of a troublesome figure, while she wept ominously. However, on the road to "The White Hart," the public discerned her plight. Having heard the entire story from the archers, who naturally recounted it passionately in her favor, they followed, cheering and encouraging her until, finding herself backed by numbers, she plucked up courage. The landlord also saw at a glance that her presence in the inn would attract custom, and he received her politely, assigning her an upper chamber: here she secluded herself, and being alone, shed tears anew.

Poor little mind, it was like a ripple, rising and falling, falling and rising, endlessly oscillating. Instructing the landlord to be exceedingly kind to her and keep her confined without making her feel imprisoned, the friends departed: and behold! As they stepped into the street, they witnessed two processions approaching from opposite directions. One was a large throng, accompanied by noise, howls, and those indescribable cries by which rough natures occasionally reveal a kinship to the beasts of the field and forest—a connection we usually succeed in suppressing. The other, sparsely attended by a few nuns and friars, moved slowly and silently.

These were the prisoners destined for exposure in the marketplace. These were the gathered bones of the victims being transported to the churchyard.

The two met in the narrow street nearly at the inn door and could not pass each other for a considerable time, and the bier bearing the relics of mortality became wedged against the cart carrying the men who had turned those bones into what they were, men who in a few hours must die for the same sin. The mob lacked the quick wit to immediately grasp the grim symmetry of this encounter: but eventually, a woman cried, "Behold your work, ye dogs!" and the crowd seized the sentiment like wildfire. There ensued a horrific yell, and the culprits groaned, attempting to hide their heads upon their bosoms, but failing, their hands being bound.
