“SLEEP on, my love, in thy cold bed,
Never to be disquieted!
My last Good Night—thou wilt not wake
Till I thy fate shall overtake.”

The Hale house hung unnaturally, eerily quiet after the roar of the mob, the scuffling terror, and the breathless commotion of the night before. Mr. Hale had overseen every small preparation for Margaret’s return, seeing that the fire was stoked high, that warm tea and bread waited on the table, that every last trace of the riot’s intrusion had been swept away—then he sank back into his accustomed armchair and drifted off into one of his heavy, sad waking dreams, his thoughts turned entirely to the wife he had lost.

Dixon had dragged Mary Higgins into the kitchen to chivvy her along with household tasks, and her scolding was no less sharp for being delivered in a fierce, hushed whisper: to speak above her breath, while a dead woman lay still in the upstairs parlour, would have struck her as irreverent, unforgivable.

Margaret had long since resolved never to speak of that final, shattering fright on the platform to her father. There was no use. It had ended well, and Frederick was safely away—save for the small, gnawing fear that Leonards might scrape together enough borrowed money to follow him to London, to hunt him down there. But the odds were stacked heavily against such a plan succeeding. She would not torment herself over a threat she was powerless to stop. Frederick would be as wary as she could warn him to be, and in a day or two at most he would be safely across the Channel, out of England for good.

“I expect we shall hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow,” she said softly.
“Yes,” her father replied, hollow-voiced. “I suppose so.”
“If he can travel, he will be here tomorrow evening, I should think.”
“If he cannot come, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to walk with me to the funeral. I cannot face it alone. I should break down entirely.”
“Don’t ask Mr. Thornton, papa. Let me go with you,” Margaret burst out, impetuous.
“You? My dear, women do not usually attend funerals.”
“No—because they cannot master their own hearts. Women of our class stay away, for they have no control over their emotions, and yet are ashamed to show them. Poor working women go, and do not care if they are seen bowed and overwhelmed by grief. But I promise you, papa, if you will let me come, I shall be no trouble. Do not bring a stranger, and shut me out. Dear papa—if Mr. Bell cannot come, I shall go. I will not press the matter against your will if he arrives, though.”

Mr. Bell never came. A letter arrived soon after, warm with affection, full of genuine, deep regret: a severe gout attack had confined him to his home, and he could not travel an inch. He wrote that he planned to visit Milton soon regardless—his Milton estate required tending, his agent had written that his presence was unavoidable, and the only thing that reconciled him to the tedious journey he had long avoided was the thought of seeing his old friend, and doing what he could to comfort him in his loss.

Margaret fought to the last to persuade her father not to send for Mr. Thornton. There was an indescribable, sharp repugnance in her chest at the very thought. The night before the funeral, a stiff, formal note arrived from Mrs. Thornton, addressed to Margaret: at her son’s request, their carriage would be sent to attend the funeral, if the arrangement would not be disagreeable to the Hales.

Margaret tossed the note to her father. “Oh, do not let us have these empty formalities,” she said. “Let us go alone—you and me, papa. They do not care for us, or else he would have offered to come himself, instead of sending a useless empty carriage as a performative show of respect.”
“I thought you were so very averse to his attending at all, Margaret,” Mr. Hale said, bewildered.
“And so I am. I do not want him anywhere near us. I especially hated the thought that we might beg him to come. But this—this feels like a mockery of mourning. I never expected that from him.”

She startled her father by bursting into sudden, heavy tears. For days she had been so quiet and subdued in her grief, so thoughtful of everyone else, so gentle and patient, that he could not account for her sudden restlessness and agitation that night. The more he fussed over her, soft with his own sorrowful tenderness, the harder she cried.

She slept so poorly that night she was scarcely prepared for the new spike of anxiety that came with the post: a letter from Frederick. Mr. Lennox was out of town, his clerk wrote, and would not return until the latest Tuesday, perhaps not until Monday. After weighing his options, Frederick had decided to stay in London two days longer. The temptation to sneak back to Milton had been almost overwhelming, but the thought of Mr. Bell installed in his father’s home, and the final fright he had received at the railway platform, had made him resolve to stay put. He swore to Margaret he would take every possible precaution to avoid being tracked by Leonards.

Margaret thanked her lucky stars she received the letter while her father was upstairs tending to his wife’s things. If he had been there, he would have expected her to read it aloud, and the news of Frederick’s extended stay in London—coupled with references to the close call at the station, the very real risk of pursuit—would have plunged him into a state of nervous panic Margaret could never have soothed. More than once she found herself wincing at the memory of suggesting they consult Mr. Lennox. At the time it had seemed such a small, harmless step, barely adding to the tiny risks of detection—but every event since had proven it to be a terrible mistake. She fought that useless, bitter self-reproach hard, for there was no undoing what was done. Her father’s mind and body were far too weak to bear the weight of that secret, that fear; he would crumple entirely under it. She summoned every scrap of her fortitude to the surface, to stand strong for both of them.

Her father had forgotten all expectation of a letter from Frederick that morning. His whole world had narrowed to one single, aching thought: that the last living trace of his wife was about to be carried away from him, hidden from his sight forever. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker’s assistant arranged the crape draperies over his shoulders. He looked at Margaret with a lost, wistful gaze, and when the man was done, he tottered over to her, murmuring, “Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left. I cannot find the words to pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it, truly I do. I know it is God’s will. But I cannot understand why she had to die. Pray for me, Margaret, that I might find the faith to pray. It is such a hard strait, my child.”

Margaret sat beside him in the closed coach, almost holding him up in her arms, reciting every verse of holy comfort, every text of faithful resignation she could call to mind. Her voice never faltered, and in speaking the words, she drew strength into herself. Her father’s lips moved after hers, repeating the familiar texts as she spoke them. It was unbearable, watching him struggle so hard to grasp the resignation he was too broken to hold in his heart.

Margaret’s own fortitude nearly snapped when Dixon gave a tiny, quiet nod of her head, drawing her attention to Nicholas Higgins and his youngest daughter standing a little apart from the other mourners, watching the ceremony with grave, respectful attention. Nicholas wore his usual rough fustian work clothes, but a scrap of black cloth was stitched around the brim of his hat—a mark of mourning he had never worn even for his own lost daughter Bessy.

Mr. Hale saw none of it. He went on mechanically repeating the words of the funeral service as the clergyman read them. He sighed two or three times when the final words were spoken, then laid a hand on Margaret’s arm, mutely begging her to lead him away, as if he had gone blind, and she was his only faithful guide.

Dixon was sobbing openly, her face buried in her handkerchief, so lost in her own grief that she never noticed the crowd of onlookers dispersing until a man spoke close beside her. It was Mr. Thornton. He had been standing there the whole time, head bowed, hidden behind a group of workmen, so that no one had spotted him.
“I beg your pardon—but can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale, too? I should like to know how they fare.”
“Of course, sir. They are as well as can be expected. Master is terribly broken down. Miss Hale bears up far better than anyone would dare hope.”

Mr. Thornton would rather have heard that she was consumed by raw, natural sorrow. There was a selfish, hungry part of him that had clung to the fantasy that his great love could step in to comfort her, to console her— the same fierce, tender pleasure that stings a mother’s heart when her sick, drooping infant nestles close, dependent on her for everything. But that soft, glowing vision of what might have been, that he would have indulged in only days before, shattered completely at the memory of what he had seen near the Outwood railway station. “Shattered” was not half strong enough. The memory of the handsome young man standing so close to her, so familiar, so trusted, shot through him like a physical agony. He clenched his fists tight at his sides to subdue the pain.

It took a tremendous effort of will to try to resuscitate the perfect, unshakable trust he had once held in Margaret’s pure, delicate maidenliness. The second that effort slackened, the trust died again, powerless, and all kinds of wild, jealous fancies chased each other through his mind like half-formed dreams. Dixon’s words were a sharp, gnawing confirmation: she bore up too well under this grief. That meant she had some other bright hope to cling to, bright enough to cut through the dark of losing her mother. He knew full well how fiercely Margaret could love. He had loved her long enough to know the full depth of passion in her soul. Her whole world would glow in sunlight if a man worthy of that love won her heart, and she would rest peacefully, trustingly, on his sympathy. His sympathy? No. That other man’s.

The thought of that other man made Mr. Thornton’s already pale, grave face go twice as wan and stern.
“I may call, I suppose,” he said coldly. “On Mr. Hale, that is. He might admit me in a day or two, once he is more rested.” He spoke as if the answer meant nothing to him, but it was a lie. For all the pain gnawing at his chest, he ached to see the woman who had caused it. Even when he hated her, at the thought of that familiar, gentle pose by the railway, he felt a restless, unquenchable urge to see her again, to breathe the same air she breathed, to re-memorize every line of her face. He was caught in the Charybdis of his own passion, forced to circle closer and closer to the fatal centre he could not escape.
“I daresay master will see you, sir,” Dixon replied. “He was very sorry he had to turn you away the other day, but things were very unsettled then, very unpleasant.”

For some unaccountable reason, Dixon never mentioned this conversation, or the fact that Mr. Thornton had stood hidden at Mrs. Hale’s funeral, to Margaret. It was a small secret, held quiet between the two of them, that Margaret would never learn.
