And all this while, what had become of Rose Salterne, that same young breaker of hearts whom as yet no one had properly seen?

She was lodged, none too willingly, in the little farm-house by the mill, deep down in the green hollow of Combe, halfway betwixt Stow and Chapel, where the valley held its own weather and its own sound—water forever at work, wheel forever complaining, and the brook’s thin singing threading through the thicker hush of fern and ash. She had been sent there to be out of the way of all the brave stir at Bideford; and though her temper was too sweet to sour outright, she kept a kind of Martinmas Lent, abstaining from pleasure as if it were flesh, and taking her penance with a pout.

The days were long in that glen. Even the house’s homely business—skimming cream, turning cheeses in their cloths, warming yesterday’s loaf before the peat so the crust would crack and breathe again—could not fill all the hours. The mill had its own constant laboring music: the click and slap of the wheel-paddles, the low thunder of stones, the dusty cough of meal shaken through a sieve. It ought to have comforted her, that honest, old English sound; yet loneliness makes even good noises sharp.

So it came to pass that, though she had a Protestant’s suspicion of Eustace Leigh and something near an aversion besides, she could not bring herself to refuse him speech when he contrived, by one pretended errand or another, to visit the mill almost daily. At first her uncle and aunt looked stiff upon it, and her aunt took care to be a third voice at every talk; but Mr. Leigh’s son was a gentleman’s son, and a good customer besides, and it was ill-manners to be outright rude to the neighboring squire’s house. And Rose, for all her dutiful looks, carried that quiet wilfulness which often gets its own way by seeming only to ask. Indeed, she had been wise enough to beg her aunt never to leave her alone with him—because, as she put it, she “couldn’t a-bear the sight of Mr. Eustace, only she must have somebody to talk to down here.”

Her aunt, being a plain countrywoman with a head full of dairy reckonings and winter stores, concluded that townfolks’ fashions must be different, and that people knew their own minds best, and so let the matter slide.

Eustace, for his part, understood very well that the reef upon which his hopes were likeliest to split was the difference of creed between them. Therefore he kept his private opinions tucked away, like a knife in a boot, and showed instead the smooth side of himself: buying milk still warm from the cow, or flour newly bolted, and carrying it away only to give it to the old women of Moorwinstow, who agreed among themselves that, Papist or no, he was “a godly young man enough.” He even went so far, after taking counsel with Campian and Parsons upon certain plots then whispering through the country like wind under doors, as to decide that all three should go to church the next Sunday.

So Messrs. Evan Morgans and Morgan Evans, having crammed the rubrics as a scholar crams his Latin, behaved in a fashion orthodox and unexceptionable; and Eustace likewise, to the great wonder of the parish, answered and stood and knelt as if he had been bred to it. He went home afterward with a secret triumph, flattering himself that he had taken in parson, clerk, and people; not knowing—in his simple unsimplicity, and cunning foolishness—that each goodwife was saying to her neighbor, over her bread and small beer, “He turned Protestant? The devil turned monk! He’s only after Mistress Salterne, the young hypocrite.”

But if the two Jesuits found it expedient to reconcile themselves outwardly to the powers that were, they were none the less busy, in private, in contriving their overthrow.

Since April they had been hunted and hidden, playing at hide-and-seek from shire to shire, and now lay quiet as cats in a hedge, waiting for expected news from Ireland to give them their cue—waiting for a great rising of the West to sweep from her throne that stiff-necked, excommunicate usurper who called herself Queen of England. Their hope—like the rainbow’s foot that always seems to touch ground a field beyond your reach—skipped before them in those days as it has in many since: that England’s heart was truly theirs, and would return to the bosom of Rome, and that Elizabeth would be led in chains to the Old Man of the Seven Hills.

At times their language upon this point terrified old Mr. Leigh. One day, as Eustace came to his father’s private room after his usual descent to the mill, he heard voices lifted high. Parsons blustered as was his wont; Mr. Leigh answered peevishly, as men do when fear wears a thin coat of anger; and Campian—who was in truth the gentlest-tempered of men—tried to lay oil upon the troubled water. Eustace, for the good of the cause (as he would have named it), stopped outside and listened.

“My excellent sir,” Mr. Leigh was saying, “does not your very presence here show how I am affected toward the holy cause of the Catholic faith? But I cannot, in the meanwhile, forget that I am an Englishman.”

“And what is England?” said Parsons. “A heretic and schismatic Babylon, whereof it is written, ‘Come out of her, my people, lest you be partaker of her plagues.’ Yea, what is a country? An arbitrary division of territory by the princes of this world, who are naught, and come to naught. They are created by the people’s will; their existence depends on the sanction of him to whom all power is given in heaven and earth—our Holy Father the Pope. Take away the latter, and what is a king? The people who have made him may unmake him.”

“My dear sir, recollect that I have sworn allegiance to Queen Elizabeth!”

“Yes, sir, you have, sir; and, as I have shown at large in my writings, you were absolved from that allegiance from the moment that the bull of Pius the Fifth declared her a heretic and excommunicate, and thereby to have forfeited all dominion whatsoever. I tell you, sir, what I thought you should have known already, that since the year 1569, England has had no queen, no magistrates, no laws, no lawful authority whatsoever; and that to own allegiance to any English magistrate, sir, or to plead in an English court of law, is to disobey the apostolic precept, ‘How dare you go to law before the unbelievers?’ I tell you, sir, rebellion is now not merely permitted, it is a duty.”

“Take care, sir; for God’s sake, take care!” said Mr. Leigh. “Right or wrong, I cannot have such language used in my house. For the sake of my wife and children, I cannot!”

“My dear brother Parsons, deal more gently with the flock,” put in Campian. “Your opinion, though probable, as I well know, in the eyes of most of our order, is hardly safe enough here; the opposite is at least so safe that Mr. Leigh may well excuse his conscience for accepting it. After all, are we not sent hither to proclaim this very thing, and to relieve the souls of good Catholics from a burden which has seemed to them too heavy?”

“Yes,” said Parsons, half-sulkily, “to allow all Balaams who will to sacrifice to Baal, while they call themselves by the name of the Lord.”

“My dear brother, have I not often reminded you that Naaman was allowed to bow himself in the house of Rimmon? And can we therefore complain of the office to which the Holy Father has appointed us—to declare to such as Mr. Leigh his especial grace, by which the bull of Pius the Fifth (on whose soul God have mercy!) shall henceforth bind the queen and the heretics only; but in no ways the Catholics, at least as long as the present tyranny prevents the pious purposes of the bull?”

“Be it so, sir; be it so. Only observe this, Mr. Leigh, that our brother Campian confesses this to be a tyranny. Observe, sir, that the bull does still bind the so-called queen, and that she and her magistrates are still none the less usurpers, nonentities, and shadows of a shade. And observe this, sir, that when that which is lawful is excused to the weak, it remains no less lawful to the strong. The seven thousand who had not bowed the knee to Baal did not slay his priests; but Elijah did, and won to himself a good reward. And if the rest of the children of Israel sinned not in not slaying Eglon, yet Ehud’s deed was none the less justified by all laws human and divine.”

“For Heaven’s sake, do not talk so, sir! or I must leave the room. What have I to do with Ehud and Eglon, and slaughters, and tyrannies? Our queen is a very good queen, if Heaven would but grant her repentance, and turn her to the true faith. I have never been troubled about religion, nor any one else that I know of in the West country.”

“You forget Mr. Trudgeon of Launceston, father, and poor Father Mayne,” said Eustace, slipping in at last; and Campian added softly—

“Yes, your West of England also has been honored by its martyrs, as well as my London by the precious blood of Story.”

“What, young malapert?” cried Mr. Leigh, turning upon his son with sudden relief, glad to find a safer target for his vexation. “Are you too against me, with a murrain on you? And pray, what the devil brought Cuthbert Mayne to the gallows, and turned Mr. Trudgeon—he was always a foolish hot-head—out of house and home, but just such treasonable talk as Mr. Parsons must needs hold in my house, to make a beggar of me and my children, as he will before he has done.”

“The Blessed Virgin forbid!” said Campian.

“The Blessed Virgin forbid? But you must help her to forbid it, Mr. Campian. We should never have had the law of 1571, against bulls, and Agnus Deis, and blessed grains, if the Pope’s bull of 1569 had not made them matter of treason, by preventing a poor creature’s saving his soul in the true Church without putting his neck into a halter by denying the queen’s authority.”

“What, sir?” almost roared Parsons. “Do you dare to speak evil of the edicts of the Vicar of Christ?”

“I? No. I didn’t. Who says I did? All I meant was, I am sure—Mr. Campian, you are a reasonable man, speak for me.”

“Mr. Leigh only meant,” Campian soothed, “that the Holy Father’s prudent intentions have been so far defeated by the perverseness and invincible misunderstanding of the heretics, that that which was in itself meant for the good of the oppressed English Catholics has been perverted to their harm.”

“And thus, reverend sir,” Eustace added quickly, eager to stand again in his father’s good graces, “my father attaches blame, not to the Pope—Heaven forbid!—but to the pravity of his enemies.”

“And it is for this very reason,” said Campian, “that we have brought with us the present merciful explanation of the bull.”

“I’ll tell you what, gentlemen,” said Mr. Leigh, who, like other weak men, found courage as soon as his adversary seemed inclined to make peace, “I don’t think the declaration was needed. After the new law of 1571 was made, it was never put in force till Mayne and Trudgeon made fools of themselves, and that was full six years. There were a few offenders, they say, who were brought up and admonished, and let go; but even that did not happen down here, and need not happen now, unless you put my son here (for you shall never put me, I warrant you) upon some deed which had better be left alone, and so bring us all to shame.”

“Your son, sir, if not openly vowed to God, has, I hope, a due sense of that inward vocation which we have seen in him,” said Parsons, “and reverences his spiritual fathers too well to listen to the temptations of his earthly father.”

“What, sir, will you teach my son to disobey me?”

“Your son is ours also, sir. This is strange language in one who owes a debt to the Church, which it was charitably fancied he meant to pay in the person of his child.”

Those last words struck Mr. Leigh where he was tenderest. His face worked; and, breaking all bounds, he swore at Parsons, who stood foaming with righteous rage.

“A plague upon you, sir, and a black assizes for you, for you will come to the gallows yet! Do you mean to taunt me in my own house with that Hartland land? You had better go back and ask those who sent you where the dispensation to hold the land is, which they promised to get me years ago, and have gone on putting me off, till they have got my money, and my son, and my conscience, and I vow before all the saints, seem now to want my head over and above. God help me!” And the poor man’s eyes filled outright.

Now Eustace was stung at last; for, after all, he was an Englishman and a gentleman, and there is a point at which even devotion begins to smell like a bargain ill-kept. He spoke kindly enough, but firm.

“Courage, my dearest father. Remember that I am still your son, and not a Jesuit yet; and whether I ever become one, I promise you, will depend mainly on the treatment which you meet with at the hands of these reverend gentlemen, for whom I, as having brought them hither, must consider myself as surety to you.”

If a powder-barrel had burst in their faces, the Jesuits could scarcely have looked more blank. Campian stared at Parsons; Parsons at Campian; and then Parsons, recovering breath first, snapped—

“Sir! do you know, sir, the curse pronounced on those who, after putting their hand to the plough, look back?”

Eustace was one of those impulsive men who dare raise the devil and then fear him; and he tried to pass his boldness off by winks and signs toward his father, as much as to say he spoke only to quiet an old man’s terrors. But Campian was too startled, and Parsons too hot, to take his hint; and Eustace, having stepped out, must needs stand.

“All I read is, Father Parsons,” he answered, “that such are not fit for the kingdom of God; of which high honor I have for some time past felt myself unworthy. I have much doubt just now as to my vocation; and in the meanwhile have not forgotten that I am a citizen of a free country.” And with that he took his father’s arm and led him out.

Those last words hit hard. All their schoolmen’s subtlety, all their ciphers, dispensations, and indulgences, were on this side of the Channel but a cloud-castle, a painted mist, which one touch of the constable’s staff could scatter. “A citizen of a free country!” That was the stumbling-stone; and they looked at one another in real perplexity.

At last Parsons spoke, low and keen. “There’s a woman in the wind. I’ll lay my life on it. I saw him blush up crimson yesterday when his mother asked him whether some Rose Salterne or other was still in the neighborhood.”

“A woman!” said Campian. “Well, the spirit may be willing though the flesh be weak. We will inquire into this. The youth may do us service as a layman; and if aught should happen to his elder brother (whom the saints protect!) he is heir to some wealth. In the meanwhile, our dear brother Parsons will perhaps see the expediency of altering our tactics somewhat while we are here.”

And then began between them one of those long consultations in which two men, sent together by the wise method of their order, each served as bridle and spur to the other—Campian to soften, Parsons to drive; and each, if need be, could give counsel almost contradictory to his fellow, without seeming changeable, because “two are better than one.”

The upshot was this: that, in a day or two—during which Mr. Leigh and Eustace made their amends, and outward peace was kept—Father Campian asked Father Francis, the household chaplain, to grant him as a special favor the hearing of Eustace’s usual confession on the following Friday.

Poor Father Francis dared not refuse so great a name, and consented with an inward groan, knowing well what such favor meant: to be gently undermined, to have family secrets drawn out like a fishbone from the throat, and the Jesuits’ power increased as his waned. For between the regular priesthood and the Society in England there ran an armed neutrality, which wanted little to become open war.

Campian heard Eustace’s confession; and by questions such as the confessional breeds—questions that creep and circle, drawing closer each time like a hawk’s shadow—found, to his satisfaction, that the young man was what Campian would have called “in love.” He smiled, and set himself briskly to discover who the lady might be.

Had he said plainly, “I feel for you; if your desire be lawful, I will help you,” he might have had the whole of Eustace’s heart at once. But he had been trained to distrust, to take the basest motive as the likeliest spring of action, and therefore took the crooked road—expecting the worst and prying for it. He began his delicate tortures, and Eustace, knowing well the father’s cunning and yet delaying surrender as long as he could, dodged like a hare before the stoat: now pretending stupidity, now surprise, now scruple, now anger. Was she married or single? Catholic or heretic? English or foreign? Each question brought Campian a step nearer.

At last, when Campian—finding the case not so black as he had supposed—asked something of her worldly wealth, Eustace saw a door and sprang at it.

“Even if she be a heretic, she is heiress to one of the wealthiest merchants in Devon.”

“Ah,” said Campian thoughtfully. “And she is but eighteen, you say?”

“Only eighteen.”

“Ah. Well, my son, there is time. She may be reconciled to the Church: or you may change.”

“I shall die first.”

“Ah, poor lad! Well; she may be reconciled, and her wealth may be of use to the cause of Heaven.”

“And it shall be of use. Only absolve me, and let me be at peace. Let me have but her,” he cried, with a sudden raw hunger in his voice, as if he begged not for doctrine but for bread. “I do not want her wealth—not I! Let me have but her, and that but for one year, one month, one day!—and all the rest—money, fame, talents, yea, my life itself, hers if it be needed—are at the service of Holy Church. Ay, I shall glory in showing my devotion by some special sacrifice—some desperate deed. Prove me now, and see what there is I will not do!”

So Eustace was absolved. And afterward Campian, as if he had been waiting for that very cry, said quietly—

“This is indeed well, my son: for there is a thing to be done now, but it may be at the risk of life.”

“Prove me!” Eustace answered at once, impatient to be set to any task that might purchase peace.

“Here is a letter brought me last night; no matter from whence. You can understand it better than I, and I longed to show it you, but that I feared my son had become—”

“You feared wrongly, then, my dear Father Campian.”

Campian put the paper before him and translated the cipher.

“This to Evan Morgans, gentleman, at Mr. Leigh’s house in Moorwinstow, Devonshire. News may be had by one who will go to the shore of Clovelly, any evening after the 25th of November, at dead low tide, and there watch for a boat, rowed by one with a red beard, and a Portugal by his speech. If he be asked, ‘How many?’ he will answer, ‘Eight hundred and one.’ Take his letters and read them. If the shore be watched, let him who comes show a light three times in a safe place under the cliff above the town; below is dangerous landing. Farewell, and expect great things!”

“I will go,” said Eustace. “To-morrow is the 25th, and I know a sure and easy place. Your friend seems to know these shores well.”

“Ah—what is it we do not know?” said Campian, with a little mysterious smile, as if the sea itself were in their keeping. “And now?”

“And now,” said Eustace, with the sudden boyish daring of a man who wants approval as much as absolution, “to prove to you how I trust you, you shall come with me, and see this—the lady of whom I spoke—and judge for yourself whether my fault is not a venial one.”

“Ah, my son, have I not absolved you already? What have I to do with fair faces?” Campian answered; yet he rose. “Nevertheless, I will come; both to show you I trust you, and it may be to help toward reclaiming a heretic, and saving a lost soul. Who knows?”

So they set out together. The hill between Chapel and Stow mill lifted them above the valley’s steady water-speech, and the air there had that clean edge of late autumn, smelling of turned earth and woodsmoke. And, as it was appointed, they had scarce reached the top when up the lane came Mistress Rose Salterne herself, bright as a berry against the hedges, in all the glories of a new scarlet hood.

From beneath it her large dark languid eyes sent soft lightnings—no sermon, no rubric, no papal bull could have matched their swiftness—straight through poor Eustace’s heart and marrow. Up she tripped on delicate ankles and tiny feet, tall, lithe, and graceful, a true West-country lass; and as she passed them with a blush and courtesy, even Campian, who had vowed himself against vanity, turned to look back at the innocent creature. Her long dark curls, after the country fashion then, rolled down from beneath the hood below her waist; and it seemed to Eustace that those glossy nets had taken his very soul.

“There,” he whispered, trembling. “Can you excuse me now?”

“I had excused you long ago,” said Campian kindly. “Alas, that so much fair red and white should have been created only as a feast for worms!”

“A feast for gods, you mean!” Eustace cried; and the very homely absurdity of Campian’s pious phrase struck his common sense like a clap. Then, as if to escape the rebuke he knew he had earned, he burst out—

“Will you let me return for a moment? I will follow you. Let me go!”

Campian saw refusal would be wasted breath, and only nodded. Eustace darted away across a field, cut the corner of hedge and ditch, and met Rose full at the next bend of the road.

She started, and a little shriek slipped from her like a bird’s call.

“Mr. Leigh! I thought you had gone forward.”

“I came back to speak to you, Rose—Mistress Salterne, I mean.”

“To me?”

“To you I must speak, tell you all, or die!” He pressed up close. She shrank, startled by the heat in him.

“Do not stir; do not go, I implore you! Rose, only hear me!” And, fiercely and passionately, he caught her hand and poured out the whole story of his love, heaping her with every fantastic epithet that a young man, fed on sonnets and fever, can coin.

Much of it Rose had heard before—Bideford had its gallants, and posies, and verses enough—but there was a quiver now in his voice, and a hard fire in his eye, that made her draw back by instinct as from something that might burn.

“Let me go!” she said. “You are too rough, sir!”

“Ay!” he answered, and seized both her hands now, as if he could hold her by sheer will. “Rougher, perhaps, than the gay gallants of Bideford, who serenade you, and write sonnets to you, and send you posies. Rougher, but more loving, Rose! Do not turn away! I shall die if you take your eyes off me! Tell me—tell me, now, here—this moment—before we part—if I may love you!”

“Go away!” she cried, struggling, and bursting into tears. “This is too rude. If I am but a merchant’s daughter, I am God’s child. Remember that I am alone. Leave me; go! or I will call for help!”

Eustace had somewhere heard, or read, that such words in a woman’s mouth were but a fashion of speaking, and meant the very opposite of what they said; and he held her hands the more fiercely, staring into her face with hungry, blinded eyes. But Rose was in earnest. Her next shriek rang out sharp and clear along the lane, startling the birds in the hedge; and in that moment he knew—if he would keep his own good name—that he must let her go.

Yet there was one question, for the answer to which he would have risked his very life.
