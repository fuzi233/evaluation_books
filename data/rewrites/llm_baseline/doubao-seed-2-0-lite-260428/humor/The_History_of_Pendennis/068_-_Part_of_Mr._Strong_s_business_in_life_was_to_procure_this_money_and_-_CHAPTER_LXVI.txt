The great drafty mausoleum that was Clavering Park had been pretty dismal back in the days when its bankrupt owner was hiding out as a refugee abroad, but somehow it managed to be even more joyless now that Sir Francis Clavering was actually living in it. 90% of the mansion was boarded up and locked tight, and the baronet camped out in just a handful of ground-floor rooms, waited on by his housekeeper and a part-time helper from the lodge gate, who cooked half the game he wasted entire dreary mornings wandering the grounds shooting. His valet Lightfoot had abandoned him for a post in Lady Clavering’s household, and as Pen learned in a gushing note from Mr Smirke who officiated the whole thing, the clever lad had followed through on his plan to marry Mrs Bonner, Lady Clavering’s head maid. The mature, thrifty Bonner had fallen head over heels for the young man’s charm, handed over all her life savings, and the pair of them were dead set on running the Clavering Arms pub as soon as they finished their current jobs at quarter day. Pen graciously promised he’d host his victory election dinner there the second Sir Francis stepped down and handed the borough seat over to him, so naturally when Pen showed up for his September visit, courtesy of his uncle who could get Clavering to agree to literally anything, the baronet was over the moon. Finally, a companion to end his loneliness! And probably a guy who’d lend him a bit of spare cash!

Pen handed over that very desirable cash just two days after he arrived, and no sooner was the money in Sir Francis’s pocket than he suddenly remembered he had urgent business in Chatteris and all the fancy nearby watering holes the shire had to offer. Off he trotted to sort his “affairs” – which, for anyone who knew him, meant binging at the county races and the local billiard halls for days on end. Pen didn’t mind being left alone at all; he had stacks of books, a fat cigar, and a pile of blank paper to keep him occupied through the long nights, and in all honesty he’d found three days of Sir Francis’s sloshed rambling unbearable enough that he’d practically jumped at the chance to hand over the loan just to send the man packing.

What’s more, he had work to do: he needed to win over the Clavering townsfolk and all the borough voters, and he was extra motivated to shake off the old reputation he’d earned as a snooty, sulky young dandy ten years prior. His mischievous sense of humor made the whole game a blast. The usually reserved, quiet Pen suddenly turned into Captain Strong’s carbon copy: loud, jovial, ready to laugh at literally anyone’s joke, shaking hands so hard and warmly you’d swear he’d known every farmer and tradesman his whole life. He showed up for market day and the farmers’ lunch at the pub, schmoozed every person who would talk to him, and behaved exactly like a perfectly seasoned politician – the exact sort of upstanding high-birth gentleman who acts like your best friend the second he wants something from your vote. It’s a funny little quirk of human nature, isn’t it? We all know that over-the-top charm is fake, cheap as counterfeit coin, that the flattery costs them nothing to hand out to every single voter – but we all take it anyway, rather than turn down free nice things.

Pen popped in on the new liberal Clavering Athenaeum the progressive lot had built specifically to spite the stuffy old upper-class reading room that banned tradesmen and once nearly refused to even let copies of the *Edinburgh Review* cross its threshold. The good Doctor Portman was off at Harrogate treating his gout with his whole family, which Pen pretended to be devastated about in a very polite, heartfelt letter begging for the old reverend’s future advice – but privately he was thrilled, because it meant he could cozy up to the opposition preacher Mr Simcoe, the two cloth-factory partners from Chatteris, and the local Independent minister all with zero pushback. He invited the younger cloth-factory partner over for a casual dinner at the park, dropped off hares and partridges at the Simcoes’ house and made a big show of begging to read Simcoe’s latest sermon, and even faked a little mild upset stomach so he could go pay a visit to local apothecary Mr Huxter, who was over the moon to get a fancy young gentleman as a patient. Old Pendennis would have been so proud of his star pupil, Pen cackled to himself, and his little wins had him in a wicked, excellent mood.

But when he wandered home late one night after chairing an Athenaeum meeting, crossed the old familiar bridge over the rushing Brawl, and spotted his old childhood home Fairoaks peeking through the trees, all that glee drained right out of him. He could see a faint light glowing in the upstairs bedroom window where his mother had sat up for hours worrying, praying, and waiting for him when he was a boy. He averted his eyes fast, like the light was the old lady’s ghost watching him, disappointed in all the silly politician games he was playing. The midnight toll from the town clock jolted him out of his sad reverie, and he trundled back through the lodge gates, under the rustling lime tree arcade back to the park.

The next afternoon he called on Fairoaks’ new tenant, Captain Stokes of the Bombay Artillery, who gave him a full tour of the grounds, bragged about the new duck pond he’d dug in the back garden, and begged Pen and his wife to come over for dinner as soon as they had time. Pen admitted straight up he’d been putting the visit off for two weeks because he couldn’t bear to see the place he’d grown up in full of strangers. The captain got it, no questions asked, and soon his wife came down in her best dress, surrounded by their kids, who were clambering all over the old worn armchair that used to belong to Pen’s dad – the chair Pen never would have dared climb in as a kid, not even if you’d offered him a king’s ransom. When Stokes’ daughter sat down at the old piano to play, Pen didn’t listen to a single note she played; all he could hear was Laura singing, and his mom tapping her foot along behind her in the old days. The dinner that followed was dreadfully awkward and dull, until the local barber doubling as a part-time waiter Hodson dropped a whole plate right over Pen’s shoulder. Squire Hobnell cackled that Hodson’s hands were obviously slippery with bear grease, that he’s always dropping crockery, and Pen burst out laughing so hard the whole table loosened up. For the rest of the night everyone took turns making terrible, unfunny jokes about making Hodson cut the hare for the second course, and every single guest thought it was the funniest bit of banter they’d ever heard in their lives.

Meanwhile, back in London, Blanche Amory was in town from her Tunbridge villa on a big shopping trip, chaperoned by none other than Mrs Bonner, who was gearing up to quit service, marry Lightfoot, and become landlady of the Clavering Arms. The old nurse wanted to buy all her favorite loved ones goodbye presents before she left, so she dragged Blanche along to help pick stuff out. She bought Blanche a massive fancy Bible, and Blanche talked her into buying an equally huge copy of Johnson’s Dictionary for her mom, both of them very pleased with how thoughtful those gifts were. Bonner also stocked up on bargains of linen for the pub, and picked out a bright red and yellow neck handkerchief that was very obviously meant for Lightfoot – who was already dripping with cheap pins, rings, shirt studs, and chains Bonner had lavished on him, despite the 25 year age gap between them.

When they were done in the shop, Blanche smiled sweetly at the shop boy and asked him to point them the way to Shepherd’s Inn. Bonner was confused the second they stepped out the door. “Shepherd’s Inn? What on earth do you want there, Miss Blanche? Captain Strong lives there, are you going to pay him a visit?” Blanche said no, she wanted to go thank that nice Fanny Bolton girl who’d nursed Pen back from the brink of death the year before, and she’d even dug out a whole pile of her old dresses to give her as a thank you. Bonner immediately started mentally staffing her future pub with the ugliest, most hunchbacked 70-year-old barmaids imaginable, the kind that could never steal her sweet Lightfoot away, and mumbled something about not liking pretty young girls hanging around single men.

Blanche, all dolled up in her frills, shot-silk dress, fancy mantle, and parasol, floated into Shepherd’s Inn like Queen Victoria making a royal charity tour, making the scrubbing woman at the lodge gape. She asked for Fanny Bolton, only to be told Fanny had popped up to Captain Strong’s rooms, so off they went up the stairs. Loitering in the courtyard, old swaggering Captain Costigan caught a sight of Blanche peeking out from under her bonnet and whistled to himself: “Begor, that’s a foine young gyurll, heading up to Sthrong and Altamont’s rooms. They do always have a foine line of visitors up them stairs.” A second later he heard bloodcurdling shrieks coming from the second floor, and he charged up the stairs as fast as his old wobbly legs could carry him, banged so hard on Strong’s door the whole frame shook. Strong cracked the door half an inch, growled “Go to hell, Cos!” and slammed it right in his face, leaving the old soldier stomping back down the stairs muttering about duels and how he’d get his revenge for that insult.

What he didn’t know, and you lucky readers do, was this: Blanche’s long-presumed-dead father Amory, who was now going by the name Altamont, had arranged to meet a pair of actresses from a local theatre at the chambers that exact same afternoon, to take them on a day trip down the river to Greenwich. His servant Grady saw two ladies walk through the door, assumed they were the thespians, and ushered them straight inside without a single question. The two fancy bouquets sitting on the table were for the actresses, and Blanche was happily sniffing the roses, peeking behind the curtains, cooing over the prints and the Clavering estate map on the wall, when a roar boomed out of the back bedroom: “Grady! My coat!”

Mrs Bonner froze, that voice was all too familiar. The bedroom door flew open, and out stepped Amory in his shirtsleeves, no coat, very confused to see two women he didn’t recognize. Mrs Bonner shrieked so loud the windows rattled, screamed his name “Amory! Amory!” and fainted dead away right in her chair. He stared for a second, then rushed over to Blanche, grabbed her, and planted a big smacker on her cheek. “That’s right, Betsy, it’s me! Don’t tell your mom I’m alive, it’s a big secret, everyone thinks I’m dead! Look how pretty you’ve grown, come on, give your old dad a kiss!” Blanche screamed too, over and over, those are the exact shrieks Costigan heard wafting down the stairs. Strong came rushing in the second he heard the noise, saw their faces, understood immediately what had happened, and sent Grady down to stand in the courtyard and lie to everyone who knocked that no one was home, the whole day trip party had already left hours ago. When Costigan later buttonholed Grady to ask what all the screaming was about, Grady just shrugged. “Oh, it was some woman. And if you ask me, women are the cause of almost every bit of mischief that ever happens in the whole world.” Blanche never got to visit Fanny Bolton that day, and the two actresses never got their fancy Greenwich river trip, either.
