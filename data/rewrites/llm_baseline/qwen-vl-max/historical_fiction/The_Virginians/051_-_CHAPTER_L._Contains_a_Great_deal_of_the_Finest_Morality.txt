When we were first introduced to Sir Miles Warrington at the King’s drawing-room in St. James’s Palace, I must confess that, upon seeing his jolly round face, broad waistcoat, and hearty country manner, I immediately thought I had found an admirable acquaintance—perhaps even the noblest specimen of humanity: the celebrated English country gentleman. To be such a man is, indeed, to stand close to the divine, perched atop the summit of earthly happiness. To possess a generous estate, free from encumbrances, with rents paid promptly by farmers who bless their stars for having such a landlord; to have only one tenant perhaps holding back, not out of malice but to afford the good old gentleman the opportunity to display his boundless charity; to hunt three days a week, adore the sport above all else, and enjoy perfect health and appetite as a result; to have not just appetite, but a sumptuous dinner; to sit in church amid a chorus of blessings from villagers, the foremost man in the parish, its benefactor, aware of his own worth—though he does mutter “Have mercy upon us, miserable sinners” only because the book demands it, and to set an example—yet how can a man so healthy, wealthy, and beloved truly believe himself a wretched sinner? Allons donc! Give any man robust health, temper, five thousand pounds a year, the admiration of his community, and the love of his family, and you’ll find it impossible to make him feel deeply dissatisfied with his spiritual state. If you were a Royal Highness, arriving at church in perfect comfort, the parson waiting to begin until your grace entered, would you consider yourself a miserable sinner? Perhaps only when afflicted with gout, isolated, death looming, your doctor having forbidden claret and prescribed arrowroot and sherry—then, yes, humility might come. But under the sun, in high spirits, the word *miserable* is mere form. You don’t truly believe you’re pitiable. If misery must be endured, what then of Colin Ploughman, suffering from ague, supporting seven children, paying two pounds a year rent, and earning eight shillings weekly? No—such a man is far more deserving of pity than the rich, jovial country gentleman, who, if he is a sinner, has few to tell him so.

He may grow selfish, but at least he is content with himself. For miles around, none rival him in goodness or greatness. His excellent wife serves him—and the entire parish. His children revere him. The vicar respects him. He commands authority at quarter-sessions. Poachers tremble before him. At market, hats fly off in his presence. Around his grand coach, where his spotless daughters and noble lady sit, tradesmen bow bareheaded, farmwives curtsey endlessly. From their seats, the ladies look down with benevolent smiles, nodding kindly to the poor. They buy a yard of ribbon with graciousness, purchase an ounce of salts or a packet of flower-seeds with condescension, deign to bargain over a goose. Their journey resembles a royal progress. A happy people presses about them, blessing them. Tradesmen bow, farmers’ wives bob, town boys wave ragged hats and cheer the red-faced coachman driving the fat bays, shouting, “Sir Miles for ever! Throw us a halfpenny, my lady!”

But suppose the market-woman hides her plump goose when the coach approaches, fearing the lady might insist on buying it cheaply? Suppose no coppers ever emerge from the coach window? Suppose Sir Miles offers his tenants thin beer and his poor a watery broth? This may well be the way of our ideal English gentleman. There have been many such gentlemen and ladies—those who patronized the poor without relieving them, who shouted “Amen!” at church as loudly as the clerk, who observed every ritual of piety and every custom of English gentility, who bought virtue as though it were a bargain, believing they honored it through purchase.

Poor Harry, in distress, turned to his relatives for help. His aunt sent him a tract and her blessing. His uncle was out of town and could not reply. How common is this kind of behavior in respectable society? One might imagine Lord and Lady Macbeth plotting murder, meeting afterward with awkwardness. But what of Lord and Lady Skinflint, consulting in their bedroom about aiding their unfortunate nephew, deciding against it, then descending to family prayers, greeting their children and servants, speaking virtuously before them, and retiring to talk privately—what thoughts pass between them? What do they think of the kinsman left to suffer, groaning unaided? How can they maintain their pious airs? How dare they look each other in the eye? Do they believe they’ve done wrong? Is Skinflint tormented by guilt over the plea ignored, the hunger sent away empty? Not at all. He is angry at Prodigal for being foolish. He feels no shame for being stingy. “A young man with such prospects throwing them away?” he thinks. “A fortune wasted on gamblers and wastrels? Horrible, horrible! Take warning, child, from this young man’s folly—see the ruin of extravagance.”

According to the great and ever-established Church of the Pharisees, this is a perfect moment for moral instruction and the assertion of virtue. “And to think he deceived us so!” exclaims Lady Warrington. “Very sad, very sad, my dear,” says Sir Miles, wagging his head. “To think of so much extravagance in one so young!” cries Lady Warrington again. “Cards, bets, tavern feasts of wicked profusion, fine horses, the company of the wealthy and profligate of his sex, and, I fear, of the most iniquitous women.” “Hush, my Lady Warrington!” interrupts her husband, glancing toward the spotless Dora and Flora, who lower their blushing heads at mention of the last. “No wonder my poor children hide their faces!” continues mamma. “My dears, I wish even the existence of such creatures could be kept from you!” “They can’t go to an opera or the park without seeing them,” says Sir Miles. “To think we should have introduced such a young serpent into our family! And left him with that guileless darling!” She points to Master Miles. “Who’s a serpent, mamma?” asks the boy. “First you said cousin Harry was bad, then good, now bad again. Which is he, Sir Miles?” “He has faults, like all of us, Miley, my dear. Your cousin has been wild—you must take warning by him.” “Was not my elder brother, who died—my naughty brother—was not he wild too? He never gave me money, nor toys, nor rode with me, nor—why do you cry, mamma? Sure I remember how Hugh and you were always fighting—” “Silence, sir!” cry papa and the girls together. “Don’t you know you’re never to mention that name?” “I know I love Harry, and I didn’t love Hugh,” says the bold little rebel. “And if cousin Harry is in prison, I’ll give him my half-guinea that my godpapa gave me, and anything I have—yes, anything, except—except my little horse—and my silver waistcoat—and—Snowball and Sweetlips at home—and—yes, my custard after dinner.” This was in response to a hint from sister Dora. “But I’d give him some of it,” adds Miles after a pause. “Shut thy mouth with it, child, and then go about thy business,” says papa, amused. Sir Miles Warrington possessed a considerable fund of easy humor.

“Who would have thought he’d ever be so wild?” murmurs mamma. “Nay, youth is the season for wild oats, my dear.” “That we should have been so misled in him!” sigh the girls. “That he should kiss us both!” cries papa. “Sir Miles Warrington, I have no patience with that sort of vulgarity!” declares the majestic matron. “Which of you was the favourite yesterday, girls?” continues the father. “Favourite, indeed! I told him over and over again of my engagement to dear Tom—I did, Dora—why do you sneer?” says the handsome sister. “Nay, to do her justice, so did Dora too,” says papa. “Because Flora seemed to wish to forget her engagement with dear Tom sometimes,” remarks the sister. “I never, never, never wished to break with Tom! It’s wicked of you to say so, Dora! It is you who are always envious because I happen—because gentlemen imagine I’m not ill-looking, and prefer me to some folks, in spite of all their learning and wit!” cries Flora, tossing her head and looking at the mirror. “Why are you always looking there, sister?” asks artless Miles junior. “Sure, you must know your face well enough!” “Some people look at it just as often, child, who haven’t near such good reason,” says papa gallantly. “If you mean me, Sir Miles, I thank you,” cries Dora. “My face is as Heaven made it, and my father and mother gave it me. ’Tis not my fault if I resemble my papa’s family. If my head is homely, at least I have brains in it. I envious of Flora, indeed, because she has found favor in the sight of poor Tom Claypool! I should as soon be proud of captivating a ploughboy!” “Pray, miss, was your Mr. Harry, of Virginia, much wiser than Tom Claypool? You would have had him for the asking!” exclaims Flora. “And so would you, miss, and have dropped Tom Claypool into the sea!” cries Dora. “I wouldn’t.” “You would.” “I wouldn’t”—and da capo goes the conversation—the shuttlecock of wrath briskly passed between the sisters.

“Oh, my children! Is this the way you dwell together in unity?” exclaims their excellent mother, laying down her embroidery. “What an example you set to this Innocent!” “Like to see ‘em fight, my lady!” cries the Innocent, rubbing his hands. “At her, Flora! Worry her, Dora! To it again, you little rogues!” says facetious papa. “’Tis good sport, ain’t it, Miley?” “Oh, Sir Miles! Oh, my children! These disputes are unseemly. They tear a fond mother’s heart,” says mamma, with majestic gesture, though bearing the wound with remarkable calm. “What cause for thankfulness ought we to have that watchful parents have prevented any idle engagements between you and your misguided cousin. If we have been mistaken in him, is it not a mercy we discovered our error in time? If either of you had any preference for him, your excellent good sense will teach you to overcome, to eradicate, the vain feeling. That we cherished and were kind to him can never be a source of regret. ’Tis a proof of our good-nature. What we have to regret, I fear, is that your cousin should have proved unworthy of our kindness, and, coming away from the society of gamblers, play-actors, and the like, should have brought contamination—pollution, I had almost said—into this pure family!”

“Oh, bother mamma’s sermons!” says Flora, as my lady begins another harangue—of which we only record the beginning—but during which papa, whistling, quietly slips from the room on tiptoe, while the artless Miles winds up his top and pegs it beneath his sisters’ robes. The top hums, stumbles, tumbles, and expires in its usual drunken manner long before Lady Warrington finishes her sermon. “Were you listening to me, my child?” she asks, placing her hand on her darling’s head. “Yes, mother,” says he, whipcord in mouth, winding up his toy. “You was a-saying that Harry was very poor now, and that we oughtn’t to help him. That’s what you was saying; wasn’t it, madam?” “My poor child, thou wilt understand me better when thou art older!” says mamma, turning toward the ceiling, her habitual refuge. “Get out, you little wretch!” cries one sister. The artless one has pegged his top at Dora’s toes and laughs at her discomfort.

But what is this? Who comes here? Why does Sir Miles return to the drawing-room, and why does Tom Claypool, striding behind him, wear such a disturbed countenance?

“Here’s a pretty business, my Lady Warrington!” cries Sir Miles. “Here’s a wonderful wonder of wonders, girls!” “For goodness’ sake, gentlemen, what is your intelligence?” asks the virtuous matron. “The whole town’s talking about it, my lady!” says Tom Claypool, puffing for breath. “Tom has seen him,” continues Sir Miles. “Seen both of them, my Lady Warrington. They were at Ranelagh last night, with a regular mob after them. So alike, that but for different ribbons you’d hardly tell one from the other. One in blue, the other in brown; but I’m certain he’s worn both suits here.” “What suits?” “What one, what other?” call the girls. “Why, your fortunate youth, to be sure.” “Our precious Virginian, and heir to the principality!” says Sir Miles. “Is my nephew, then, released from his incarceration?” asks her ladyship. “And is he again plunged in the vortex of dissip----” “Confound him!” roars the Baronet, with an expression I fear was stronger still. “What should you think, my Lady Warrington, if this precious nephew of mine should turn out to be an impostor; by George! no better than an adventurer?” “An inward monitor whispered me as much!” cries the lady; “but I dashed from me the unworthy suspicion. Speak, Sir Miles, we burn with impatience to listen to your intelligence.” “I’ll—speak, my love, when you’ve done,” says Sir Miles. “Well, what do you think of my gentleman, who comes into my house, dines at my table, is treated as one of this family, kisses my—” “What?” asks Tom Claypool, flushing as red as his waistcoat. “—Hem! Kisses my wife’s hand, and is treated in the fondest manner, by George! What do you think of this fellow, who talks of his property and his principality, by Jupiter!—turning out to be a beggarly SECOND SON! A beggar, my Lady Warrington, by——” “Sir Miles Warrington, no violence of language before these dear ones! I sink to the earth, confounded by this unutterable hypocrisy. And did I entrust thee to a pretender, my blessed boy? Did I leave thee with an impostor, my innocent one?” the matron cries, fondling her son. “Who’s an impostor, my lady?” asks the child. “That confounded young scamp of a Harry Warrington!” bawls papa; whereupon the little Miles, after a puzzled look and some hidden emotion, bursts into tears. His admirable mother tries to clutch him to her heart, but he rejects the embrace, crying louder and kicking wildly in her lap, as the butler announces: “Mr. George Warrington, Mr. Henry Warrington!”

Miles is dropped from his mother’s lap. Sir Miles’s face matches Mr. Claypool’s waistcoat. The three ladies rise and make three frigid curtseys as our two young men enter. Little Miles runs to them, holds out a hand. “Oh, Harry! No! which is Harry? You’re my Harry,” and he chooses rightly this time. “Oh, you dear Harry! I’m so glad you are come! And they’ve been abusing you so!” “I am come to pay my duty to my uncle,” says the dark-haired Mr. Warrington; “and to thank him for his hospitalities to my brother Henry.” “What, nephew George? My brother’s face and eyes! Boys both, I am delighted to see you!” cries their uncle, grasping each hand affectionately, his honest face glowing with pleasure. “This indeed hath been a most mysterious and a most providential resuscitation,” says Lady Warrington. “Only I wonder that my nephew Henry concealed the circumstance until now,” she adds, casting a sidelong glance at both young gentlemen. “He knew it no more than your ladyship,” says Mr. Warrington. The young ladies exchange glances, lowering their eyes. “Indeed, sir! A most singular circumstance,” says mamma, with another curtsey. “We had heard of it, sir; and Mr. Claypool, our county neighbour, had just brought us the intelligence, and it even now formed the subject of my conversation with my daughters.” “Yes,” cries a little voice, “and do you know, Harry, father and mother said you was a—a imp----” “Silence, my child! Screwby, convey Master Warrington to his own apartment! These, Mr. Warrington—or, I suppose I should say nephew George—are your cousins.” Two curtseys—two cheeses—two hands extended. Mr. Esmond Warrington makes a profound low bow, embracing (and it is the only embrace he offers) all three ladies. He lays his hat to his heart. “It is my duty, madam, to pay my respects to my uncle and cousins, and to thank your ladyship for such hospitality as you have been enabled to show to my brother.” “It was not much, nephew, but it was our best. Ods bobs!” cries the hearty Sir Miles, “it was our best!” “And I appreciate it, sir,” says Mr. Warrington, looking gravely around the family. “Give us thy hand. Not a word more,” says Sir Miles. “What? Do you think I’m a cannibal, and won’t extend the hand of hospitality to my dear brother’s son? What say you, lads? Will you eat our mutton at three? This is my neighbour, Tom Claypool, son to Sir Thomas Claypool, Baronet, and my very good friend. Hey, Tom! Thou wilt be of the party, Tom? Thou knowest our brew, hey, my boy?” “Yes, I know it, Sir Miles,” replies Tom, with no particular rapture. “And thou shalt taste it, my boy! Thou shalt taste it! What is there for dinner, my Lady Warrington? Our food is plain, but plenty, lads—plain, but plenty!” “We cannot partake of it to-day, sir. We dine with a friend who occupies my Lord Wrotham’s house, your neighbour. Colonel Lambert—Major-General Lambert he has just been made.” “With two daughters, I think—countrified-looking girls—are they not?” asks Flora. “I think I have remarked two little rather dowdy things,” says Dora. “They are as good girls as any in England!” breaks out Harry, to whom no one had spoken a word. His reign was over. He was nobody. What wonder, then, that he should not be visible? “Oh, indeed, cousin!” says Dora, glancing at the young man, who sits with burning cheeks, chafing at the humiliation, yet unsure how or whether to respond. “Oh, indeed, cousin! You are very charitable—or very lucky, I’m sure! You see angels where we only see ordinary little persons. I’m sure I could not imagine who those odd-looking people were in Lord Wrotham’s coach, with his handsome liveries. But if they were three angels, I have nothing to say.” “My brother is an enthusiast,” interposes George. “He is often mistaken about women.” “Oh, really!” says Dora, looking slightly uneasy. “I fear my nephew Henry has indeed met with some unfavourable specimens of our sex,” remarks the matron, groaning. “We are so easily taken in, madam—we are both very young yet—we shall grow older and learn better.” “Most sincerely, nephew George, I trust you may. You have my best wishes, my prayers, for your brother’s welfare and your own. No efforts of ours have been wanting. At a painful moment, to which I will not further allude—” “And when my uncle Sir Miles was out of town,” says George, looking toward the Baronet, who smiles with affectionate approval. “—I sent your brother a work which I thought might comfort him, and I know might improve him. Nay, do not thank me; I claim no credit; I did but my duty—a humble woman’s duty—for what are this world’s goods, nephew, compared to the welfare of a soul? If I did good, I am thankful; if I was useful, I rejoice. If, through my means, you have been brought, Harry, to consider—” “Oh! The sermon, is it?” breaks in straightforward Harry. “I hadn’t time to read a single syllable of it, aunt—thank you. You see I don’t care much about that kind of thing—but thank you all the same.” “The intention is everything,” says Mr. Warrington, “and we are both grateful. Our dear friend, General Lambert, intended to give bail for Harry; but, happily, I had funds of Harry’s with me to meet any demands upon us. But the kindness is the same, and I am grateful to the friend who hastened to my brother’s rescue when he had most need of aid, and when his own relations happened—so unfortunately—to be out of town.” “Anything I could do, my dear boy, I’m sure—my brother’s son—my own nephew—ods bobs! you know—that is, anything—anything, you know!” cries Sir Miles, clasping George’s hand with a generous smack. “You can’t stay and dine with us? Put off the Colonel—the General—do, now! Or name a day. My Lady Warrington, make my nephew name a day when he will sit under his grandfather’s picture and drink some of his wine!”

“His intellectual faculties seem more developed than those of his unlucky younger brother,” remarked my lady, when the young gentlemen had taken their leave. “The younger must be reckless and extravagant about money indeed, for did you remark, Sir Miles, the loss of his reversion in Virginia—the amount of which has, no doubt, been grossly exaggerated, but, nevertheless, must be something considerable—did you, I say, remark that the ruin of Harry’s prospects scarcely seemed to affect him?” “I shouldn’t be at all surprised that the elder turns out to be as poor as the young one,” says Dora, tossing her head. “He! he! Did you see that cousin George had one of cousin Harry’s suits of clothes on—the brown and gold—that one he wore when he went with you to the oratorio, Flora?” “Did he take Flora to an oratorio?” asks Mr. Claypool, fiercely. “I was ill and couldn’t go, and my cousin went with her,” says Dora. “Far be it from me to object to any innocent amusement, much less to the music of Mr. Handel, dear Mr. Claypool,” says mamma. “Music refines the soul, elevates the understanding, is heard in our churches, and ’tis well known was practised by King David. Your operas I shun as deleterious; your ballets I would forbid to my children as most immoral; but music, my dears! May we enjoy it, like everything else in reason—may we—” “There’s the music of the dinner-bell,” says papa, rubbing his hands. “Come, girls. Screwby, go and fetch Master Miley. Tom, take down my lady.” “Nay, dear Thomas, I walk but slowly. Go you with dearest Flora downstairs,” says Virtue. But Dora took care to make the evening pleasant by talking of Handel and oratorios constantly during dinner.
