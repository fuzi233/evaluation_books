You, who are blessed with the omnivorous gaze of Three Dimensions, who can feast your eyes upon the complete circumference of a spiced holiday pie in a single glance and parse the complex geometry of a bustling kitchen from above—how shall I convey the extreme difficulty we in Flatland experience in recognizing one another’s configuration? To us, the visual realm is as stark and unyielding as an unleavened cracker. As I have recorded in my extensive histories of our regional foodways, all beings in Flatland, animate or inanimate, present to our eyes the exact same profile: a single, featureless straight Line. How, then, without the rich visual plating you enjoy, do we distinguish the agrarian elder from the aristocrat? 

The answer is threefold, rooted deeply in our sensory and cultural traditions. The first method relies entirely upon our acoustic ecology. With us, the ear is as meticulously trained as a master chef’s palate. In quieter, bygone eras, before the clatter of modernization, our sonic environment was a critical determinant of our psychological well-being. By tuning into the resonant frequencies of our neighbors, we could not only recognize the familiar vocal timbre of a friend but discriminate between the three lowest orders: the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. (The Isosceles, I confess, barely register in these refined auditory landscapes). 

Yet, as we ascend the social scale and move toward the urban centers, this acoustic heritage faces a tragic erosion. The dominant, heavily-engineered architecture of our modern cities creates an industrial noise pollution that muddies our delicate sonic terroir. The Aristocracy, increasingly disconnected from the land, views voice-discrimination as a plebeian virtue, their own ears dulled by the superficial commercialization of their insulated lives. Furthermore, the acoustic method is uniquely vulnerable to imposture. Among our lowest orders, the vocal organs are robustly developed, heavily steeped in native dialects. An Isosceles, drawing upon the deep, earthy cadence of his agrarian roots, can easily feign the resonant baritone of a Polygon, or even the perfect, humming pitch of a Circle. Because the subjective experience of hearing is inherently untranslatable, we cannot trust it in matters of high society. 

Thus, a second method is more commonly practiced: the ancient ritual of Feeling. Among our Women and the lower classes, touch remains the principal test of recognition. What a formal introduction is to Spaceland, the intricate, reciprocal process of tactile greeting is to us. In the remote agrarian districts, where elders still tend to slow-fermenting root-spores and honor the rhythms of the seasons, the greeting retains its profound, traditional cadence: *"Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so."* This phrase is a beautifully balanced exchange, acknowledging the mutual vulnerability and shared sustenance of community. 

Tragically, this rich cultural memory is actively eroding under the pressure of a dominant, hurried lexicon. In the towns, among men of commerce who view time merely as currency and meals as utilitarian fuel, the words *"be felt by"* are ruthlessly excised. The greeting is starved down to: *"Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so."* Worse still, among our dashing, hyper-modern young gentlemen—who possess no appreciation for the sensory depth of their native language—the formula is butchered into a sterile, commercialized slang. They deploy *"to feel"* in a cold, technical capacity, sanctioning such barbarisms as, *"Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones."* In this subtle shift of power dynamics, the profound reciprocity of the encounter is lost, replaced by an extractive, one-sided transaction. 

Do not suppose, however, that Feeling is a tedious, exhaustive groping. Just as a seasoned food historian can discern the origin of a wild-foraged mushroom with a single brush of the thumb, our long practice enables us to ascertain a class by feeling but a single angle. The dull, brainless vertex of an acute-angled Isosceles is as unmistakable as a spoiled broth, just as the wider, more stable angles of the Equilateral and the Pentagon offer a distinct, refined tactile flavor. Only among the highest sections of the nobility does the palate falter; even a Master of Arts in our University of Wentbridge has been known to confuse the subtle edge of a ten-sided Polygon with a twelve-sided one. 

This tactile communion requires extreme care, for the angles of our bodies can inflict irreparable injury upon an unwary Feeler. It is essential that the Felt stand as perfectly still as a resting dough. A sudden start, a fidgety shift, or a violent sneeze can prove fatal. This is particularly true when dealing with the lower classes of the Triangles. Being rough-hewn foragers and laborers, their eyes are situated so far from their vertices that they remain tragically oblivious to their own extremities. They lack the delicate, sensory appreciation of the highly organized Polygon.

This brings to mind a multi-generational sorrow that haunts my own family’s timeline, a mythic tragedy seamlessly woven into our everyday domestic routines. I first heard the tale from my excellent Grandfather. We were in the kitchen, tending to a slow-simmering, fragrant stem-broth, when he recounted the fate of his great-great-great-Grandfather, a respectable Working Man with an angle of 59 degrees 30 minutes. My ancestor, an agrarian who spent his days harvesting damp mosses, was afflicted with terrible rheumatism. During a formal introduction, while in the act of being felt by a Polygon, my ancestor suffered a sudden, uncontrollable spasm and accidentally transfixed the Great Man through the diagonal. The ensuing moral shock and subsequent imprisonment threw our entire lineage back a degree and a half in our generational ascent. It took five full generations of circular, rhythmic pacing—marrying, laboring, and cultivating our family’s standing like a precious, carefully nurtured sourdough starter—to recover the lost ground and finally achieve the 60 degrees required to enter the class of Regulars. All this calamity stemmed from one involuntary twitch during the ritual of Feeling.

At this juncture, you might wonder how we, who cannot see two intersecting lines, manage to register angles of different sizes with such minute precision. We do not see them; we *infer* them. Our sense of touch is a highly developed instrument, honed by the absolute necessity of our environment. Moreover, Nature herself supplies us with an ascending scale, an alphabet of angles. It is a Law of Nature that the brain of the Isosceles class begins at half a degree and increases, through generational cultivation, by half a degree at a time. 

Because of moral stagnation and the excessive fecundity of the Vagabond classes, there is always a tragic superfluity of individuals of the half-degree and single-degree class. Absolutely destitute of civic rights, these unfortunate souls are utilized by the Board of Education as tactile "Specimens." Bound immovably in the classrooms of our Infant Schools, they serve as living protractors, imparting tactile intelligence to the offspring of the Middle Classes. 

As one who finds profound meaning in the nurturing power of sustenance, I find the State’s treatment of these Specimens to be a stark reflection of a worldview that reduces life to grim utility. In cheaper schools, the Specimens are fed and allowed to exist for years, though their angles grow dull and inaccurate from constant handling. In the more "regulated" regions, however, the State finds it more advantageous to dispense with food entirely. The Specimens are systematically starved—denied the very culinary rituals that bind a culture together—and renewed every month when they expire. Politicians argue that this "expensive system" maintains the sharp accuracy of the angles while simultaneously diminishing the redundant Isosceles population. Though popularly elected School Boards often push for the "cheap system," the chilling reality is that the profound, life-giving act of nourishment is entirely weaponized by the dominant lexicon of State economy.

Enough has been said, I trust, to demonstrate that Recognition by Feeling is far more trustworthy than the corrupted, noise-polluted method of Hearing. Yet, because of the physical dangers inherent in tactile introduction, many in the Middle and Lower classes—and all without exception in the Polygonal and Circular orders—prefer a third method of recognition, the mechanics of which I shall prepare for you in the following section.
