Now that this book is ready to be shared with the world, I feel a strong sense of its limitations—both in how it’s written and in what it includes. I must admit, it does not aim to be a complete record of everything we saw or did during our journey into Kukuanaland. There are many things I would have liked to explore in greater depth, but which have only been briefly mentioned here.

For instance, I gathered fascinating legends about the chain armor that saved us from death in the great battle of Loo—and also about the “Silent Ones,” the towering figures found at the entrance to the stalactite cave. These stories could have filled pages with mystery and wonder. I also wanted to examine the differences between the Zulu and Kukuana languages—some of which struck me as deeply meaningful. A few pages on the plants and animals native to Kukuanaland might have added richness too. I discovered eight new kinds of antelope and countless unfamiliar plants, mostly bulbs, none of which I had seen before.

Then there’s the remarkable military system of the Kukuanas—a topic barely touched upon. In my view, it surpasses even Chaka’s famed Zulu army, allowing faster mobilization without the harsh rule of forced celibacy. And yet I’ve said little about their family customs, which are often strange and intriguing, or their advanced skill in metalworking. Their “tollas”—heavy throwing knives—are made with iron backs and edges forged from fine steel, welded with astonishing precision. This craft shows a deep mastery of metallurgy.

But Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good and I agreed: the best approach was to tell the story simply and directly. We decided to leave deeper explorations for another time, should the need arise. For now, I’m happy to share any details I can with those who are curious.

I must also apologize for my plain style of writing. I’ve spent more time handling a rifle than a pen, so I cannot pretend to the elegance and flourishes seen in novels—though I do enjoy reading them sometimes. Perhaps such stylistic touches are expected. I regret not being able to offer them. Still, I believe truth told plainly carries more weight. Simple words can speak louder than fancy ones.
