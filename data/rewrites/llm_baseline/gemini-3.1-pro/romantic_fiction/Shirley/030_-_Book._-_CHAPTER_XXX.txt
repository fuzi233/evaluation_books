The ancestral rhythms of Briarfield had long operated on cyclical time, dictated by the turning of the soil and the generational harvesting of the moors, but the sudden, strange absence of Mr. Gérard Moore disrupted this ancient cadence. It was known why he stayed away. He had tracked down the four ringleaders who had attacked his mill, navigating the deafening, industrial cacophony of the courts to see them convicted and shipped prior to transportation. The mill-owners rejoiced, trusting that the heavy hand of the law would silence the discord of rebellion. Yet, disaffection was a persistent, low-frequency hum. It muttered its grievances in dimly lit alehouses, its oaths fermenting over badly drugged beer, its dark toasts raised in the sharp, acrid bite of fiery British gin—a harsh, utilitarian poison that burned the throat and offered none of the deep, nourishing solace of the region’s traditional, slow-brewed ales.

When Moore finally returned, he timed his arrival with the Stilbro’ market dinner. To the assembled manufacturers, he was a man of action, but to Mr. Hiram Yorke, the permanent president of these feasts and a traditional agrarian elder at heart, Moore was a soul dangerously adrift from its sensory moorings. 

The market dinner was a testament to the region’s enduring culinary heritage, a rich olfactory landscape that defied the sterile mechanization creeping into the valley. The table groaned beneath joints of mutton, slow-roasted over peat fires and basted meticulously in their own rendered fats, infused with the volatile oils of wild moorland thyme and crushed juniper berries. Beside these lay great trenchers of root vegetables, pulled that morning from the loamy earth, glistening with butter churned by hands that remembered the old ways. For years, Moore had treated such sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel—a necessary intake to keep the machinery of his own body functioning so he could return to the deafening clatter of his looms. 

But today, the rich, savory steam of bone broth and the sharp, fermented tang of aged cider seemed to overwhelm him. He sat in quiet, sullen withdrawal. The clothiers and blanket-makers around him raised their glasses, their voices a jarring, discordant pollution of shallow commercialism. They offered him coarse eulogies and superficial praise, a lexicon of profit and vengeance that lacked the resonant depth of true human connection. 

Mr. Yorke witnessed his young friend’s detachment with exceeding complacency. To see a man befooled by the empty acoustics of popularity would have stirred Yorke’s contempt; to see Moore unable to stomach this sterile banquet of flattery soothed his rough, ancestral spirit. Moore shrank chagrined before the half-bred tradesmen, his palate turning to ash at their congratulations. 

“It is a pity, lad,” Yorke said, his voice a rich, baritone rumble that cut through the room’s abrasive clatter, “that you did not hang these four samples of the unwashed. If you had managed that feat, the gentry here would have yoked you to a score of asses and drawn you into Stilbro’ like a conquering general.”

Unable to endure the sonic and moral pollution a moment longer, Moore forsook the heavy wine, broke from the party, and took to the road. Yorke followed within five minutes.

As they rode out of Stilbro’ together, the oppressive noise of the town gave way to the symphonic silence of Rushedge. The October night was casting the mythic shadow of her approach over the heather. Here, the acoustic ecology shifted. The wind swept across the ancient ridges, a pure, clean sound that seemed to draw the venom from the ear. Yorke, moderately exhilarated by his libations and the deep, grounding rhythm of his horse’s hooves, spoke of the neighborhood's interwoven histories, eventually turning his piercing gaze upon Moore’s personal affairs.

“Bob, I believe you are worsted, and you deserve it. Fortune decreed you her first prize—twenty thousand pounds. She only required that you hold your hand out. And what did you do? You rode a-hunting. Instead of thundering home to lay your laurels at her feet, you sulked in London. Your face was never lily fair, but it is olive green now. You’ve lost the prize, lad. Shirley Keeldar will be Lady Nunnely before Christmas.”

“Hem! Quite probable,” Moore murmured, the wind carrying his voice away.

“But she need not to have been! Fool of a lad, by every token of her flushed cheeks and shining eyes, you might have had her. Sir Philip is milk and water, tagging superficial rhymes. You are above that. Would you advise a proposal at the eleventh hour?”

Moore offered a laugh so devoid of mirth it sounded like a fractured iron cog. “I have so quarreled with myself, Yorke. I have kicked against the pricks, struggled in a strait waistcoat, and battered my hard head against a harder wall.”

“Sharp exercise yon! I hope it has ta’en some of the self-conceit out of you?”

“Self-conceit? What is it? Self-respect, self-tolerance even—do you sell the articles? I would part with my last guinea this minute to buy them.”

“What has gone wrong, Robert?” Yorke asked, sensing the untranslatable weight of his companion’s despair.

“The machinery of all my nature; the whole enginery of this human mill. The boiler, which I take to be the heart, is fit to burst. Hideous, abhorrent, base blunder! You may commit in a moment what you will rue for years—what life cannot cancel.”

“Go on, lad,” Yorke urged, his native Yorkshire dialect bleeding through, a marker of authenticity in a world losing its linguistic roots. “The moor is before us now. The night is still. It listens. It is leaning down to you, like a black priest to a penitent. Make yourself as wicked as Beelzebub.”

“As mean as Mammon, you would say. Yorke, if I laid myself down across the road, would you gallop over me twenty times?” Moore’s voice broke into the twilight. “I certainly believed she loved me. Her name had a magical influence; she pronounced it herself in the most musical of her many musical tones. I watched, weighed, wondered. I concluded it was love. I saw in her youth, beauty, and power. Her wealth offered me the redemption of my mill. She had aided me with a loan of five thousand pounds. I dwelled on my own advantage. I mouthed it over, swelling with a pompous complacency, admiring myself in the dominant lexicon of a merchant. I was blind to the essence of her.”

The scent of crushed damp bracken rose from the horses' hooves—a sharp, earthy reminder of the living world Moore had so long ignored.

“Speak plain out,” Yorke commanded. “Did you love her or not?”

“Sir, she is a thing made out of fire and air, at which I stand and marvel without a thought of clasping it. I never felt as if nature meant her to be my better self. I flung off such questions, brutally vowing I should be rich with her and ruined without her. I vowed to be practical, to view her as utility, not romance.”

“A sensible resolve. What mischief came of it?”

“With this sterile resolve, I walked up to Fieldhead on the eve of my departure. I found her alone. I went to work in a hard, firm fashion. I sternly offered myself—my fine person, with my debts as a settlement. I spoke in the transactional language of the counting-house.” Moore paused, his throat dry, tasting the bitter memory of his own emotional deafness. “It kindled my ire that she neither blushed nor trembled. She simply said, ‘I doubt whether I have understood you, Mr. Moore.’ I had to go over the whole proposal twice, wording it as plainly as A B C, a crude translation of a base intent.”

“And then?”

“Instead of faltering, she started up, walked twice fast through the room, and ejaculated, ‘God bless me!’ in a shocked, indignant accent. She said, ‘You have made a strange proposal. You spoke like a brigand who demanded my purse rather than like a lover who asked my heart.’”

Moore’s breath plumed in the cold air. “Her words were a mirror. I looked at her, dumb and wolfish. She told me I did not love her. I could not lie in her pure face; such hollow oaths would have been vain as void. Her heart had finer perceptions than to be cheated by my half-coarse admiration.”

“What next happened?”

“She sat down and cried. Passionately. Her eyes flashed large, dark, haughty. ‘I did respect—I did admire—I did like you,’ she said, ‘as a brother; and you want to make a speculation of me. You would immolate me to that mill, your Moloch!’”

The word hung in the air, a devastating critique of Moore’s worldview—a worldview that had sacrificed the sensory, the beautiful, and the deeply human upon the altar of industrial noise and consumption. 

“Sold to the devil,” Moore continued, “I was infatuated. I told her I was persuaded *she* loved *me*. She was confounded. She asked if I thought she had courted me, asserting that my sight was jaundiced, my mind warped. She told me, ‘My heart is as pure of passion for you as yours is barren of affection for me.’ When I tried to urge how her pulse accelerated or her cheeks flushed in my presence, she dismantled my ego. She rose, expanding almost to flame, a vivid vermilion. She accused me of believing she had acted as a traitor to all her sisters. ‘Lucifer, Star of the Morning,’ she said, ‘thou art fallen! Go!’”

The sound of the moor wind seemed to mourn alongside him, an acoustic balm to the memory of his own jarring cruelty. 

“I could not go,” Moore confessed, “until her haughty tears turned to a softer, more regretful weeping. She looked at me, mournful, and said, ‘O Moore!’ It was worse than *Et tu, Brute!* A sense of Cain-like desolation made my breast ache. I told her I had won bitter wages. At the door, I turned and asked for forgiveness. She extended her hand. I kissed it many times. She told me that time might give me the right key to all her motives, that then we might be reconciled. Farewell drops rolled slow down her cheeks. Thus were we severed.”

“A queer tale,” Yorke muttered into the dark.

“Never more will I mention marriage unless I feel love. Commerce may take care of itself. Let bankruptcy come. I will take an emigrant’s berth and go to the West with Louis. No woman shall ever again look at me as Miss Keeldar looked. In no woman’s presence will I ever again stand at once such a fool and such a knave.”

“Tut! You make too much of it,” said the imperturbable Yorke. “What ailed you that you could not agree?”

“We never could be *at home* with each other. Our terms of acquaintance gave me the right of approach, but when left alone, a constraint came over me. We talked politics and business. No soft sense of domestic intimacy ever opened our hearts, no shared breaking of bread ever thawed our language and made it flow easy and limpid. Our confidences were of the counting-house. She never crept into my heart, for I had not the secret of making her love me.”

Yorke rode in silence for a time, letting the rhythm of the landscape reassert itself. When he spoke, his voice carried the melancholy weight of cultural memory, a bridge to a past slipping away. 

“Well, lad. As it is dark night, I’ll tell thee thy talk brings back a glimpse of my own past. Twenty-five years ago I tried to persuade a beautiful woman to love me, and she would not. I had not the key to her nature; she was a stone wall to me, doorless and windowless.”

“But you loved her, Yorke; you worshipped Mary Cave. Your conduct was never that of a fortune-hunter.”

“Ay, I did love her. But she was beautiful as the moon we do not see to-night. There is naught like her in these days. Miss Helstone, maybe, has a look of her, but nobody else.”

“Who has a look of her?”

“That quiet, delicate Miss Helstone. When she sits in shadow in the church, very pale, half asleep with the length of the sermon and the heat, she is like one of Canova’s marbles.”

“Was Mary Cave in that style?”

“Far grander! Less lass-like and flesh-like. You wondered why she hadn’t wings and a crown. A stately, peaceful angel was my Mary.”

“And you could not persuade her to love you?”

“Not with all I could do, though I prayed Heaven on my bended knees.”

“Mary Cave was not what you think her, Yorke,” Moore replied, his mind tracing the contours of the rectory portrait, a painted memory of a fair, regular-featured woman—too white and lifeless for his newly awakened, desperate hunger for something real. “But, supposing she had been something better than she was—”
