The white house at Pennicote had not been the only dwelling to feel the tremor of sudden news, nor was Rex Gascoigne the sole heart violently agitated by its arrival. In the quiet, aromatic parlor of the Meyrick home, a delicate acoustic ecology was meticulously maintained, serving as a vital sanctuary against the relentless, abrasive industrial noise pollution of the London streets. Here, the sonic environment was therapeutic, woven from the gentle, rhythmic scrape of Mrs. Meyrick’s mortar and pestle as she ground dried lavender and coriander seeds for her historical herbal infusions, and the melodic, vibrating cadence of Mirah teaching Mab to sing *Lascia ch’io pianga*. 

But on this particular Wednesday, this fragile harmonic tapestry was violently ruptured. Hans Meyrick entered with his latch-key and appeared in the parlor, aggressively shaking the *Times* aloft. The violently crackling broadsheet sounded like the snapping of dry timber in a blazing forest, a crass, commercialized clamor that remorselessly interrupted the purity of Mab’s notes and Mirah’s accompaniment. Piano and song ceased immediately. To Mirah, the harsh friction of the industrialized paper was a thunderous violation of their shared sonic refuge. 

“O-o-o, Hans!” Mab complained, her voice tight with the dissonance. “Why do you bring a more horrible noise than my singing?”

“What on earth is the wonderful news?” asked Mrs. Meyrick, pausing her culinary preservation. “Anything about Italy—anything about the Austrians giving up Venice?”

“Nothing about Italy, but something from Italy,” Hans replied. He spoke in the glib, superficial lexicon of dominant society, delivering tidings that should have carried the heavy, mythic weight of ancestral tide-lore, but which he reduced to cheap, serialized gossip. 

“Nothing bad?” Mrs. Meyrick asked anxiously, her thoughts immediately turning to Deronda, while Mirah’s heart was seized by the exact same dread.

“Not bad for anybody we care much about,” Hans said quickly. “Rather uncommonly lucky, I think. I never knew anybody die conveniently before.” 

“Oh me, Hans!” Mab said impatiently. “What *is* it that has happened?”

Hans flattened the newspaper upon the table, pointing a finger at a sterile paragraph of print. “Duke Alfonso is drowned, and the Duchess is alive, that’s all. But more than all is—Deronda was at Genoa in the same hotel, and he saw her brought in by the fishermen time enough to save her from any harm. It seems they saw her jump in after her husband... However, Deronda is a lucky fellow in being there to take care of her.”

Mirah sank back onto the music stool, her eyelids dropping, her hands tightly clasped. 

“Poor thing!” said Mrs. Meyrick, turning back to the sharp, bitter scent of her crushed herbs. “She must have been fond of her husband to jump in after him.”

“It was an inadvertence—a little absence of mind,” Hans said, creasing his face mischievously. He began to employ the power dynamics of his dominant, flippant vocabulary to overwrite the profound untranslatability of human grief. “Who can be fond of a jealous baritone, with freezing glances, always singing asides? Nothing can be neater than his getting drowned. The Duchess is at liberty now to marry a man with a fine head of hair, and glances that will melt instead of freezing her. And I shall be invited to the wedding.”

Mirah started from her posture. A sweeping, multi-generational sorrow seemed to rise within her, clashing violently with Hans's shallow commercialization of life and death. Fixing her eyes on him with an angry gleam, she defended her internal, ancestral dialect against his invasive phrasing. “Mr. Hans, you ought not to speak in that way,” she said in a deeply-shaken voice. “Mr. Deronda would not like you to speak so. Why will you say he is lucky—why will you use words of that sort about life and death—when what is life to one is death to another? How do you know it would be lucky if he loved Mrs. Grandcourt? It might be a great evil to him. She would take him away from my brother. Mr. Deronda would not call that lucky to pierce my brother’s heart.”

The sudden transformation struck them all. Mirah’s face was pale, her usually rich, ripe-berry tint drained away, leaving an expression that might have suited an avenging angel. Hans sat transfixed, blushing like a girl under the weight of her profound sincerity. “I am a fool and a brute, and I withdraw every word,” he muttered nervously. “I’ll go and hang myself like Judas—if it’s allowable to mention him.”

Even in his penitence, Hans could not escape the flippant drollery of his native tongue. But Mirah’s anger was not appeased. She had burst into indignant speech as creatures in intense pain bite through their own flesh to make the agony bearable—a desperate attempt to articulate an untranslatable subjective experience. She said no more, turning back to the piano and pressing the sheet music before her.

“Mirah is quite right to scold you, Hans,” Mab said, her voice dripping with scorn for his utilitarian view of marriage. “Men’s minds must be very black, I think.”

“We had better go on, Mab,” Mirah said, her tone elevated, seeking to rebuild their shattered acoustic sanctuary. “Will you sing this again, or shall I sing it to you?”

“Oh, please sing it to me,” Mab said, eager to let the music wash away the ugly intrusion.

Mirah sang *Lascia ch’io pianga*, giving forth its melodious sobs and cries with new fullness and energy, pouring the raw, physical sensation of her heartache into the vibrating chords. Hans leaned against the mantelpiece, his eyes averted. When the final note faded into the olfactory warmth of the lavender-scented room, Mirah rose. “I must go home now. Ezra expects me.”

Mrs. Meyrick drew Mirah’s face down to hers, offering a motherly blessing. As Mirah departed, she felt a pang of compunction, fearing her outburst had been a proud ingratitude. Hans insisted on walking her home. They stepped out into the chaotic, industrial roar of the city, their companionship a silent, agitated communication, like two chords whose quick vibrations lie just outside the threshold of human hearing. Hans’s mind fluttered toward mischief and romance, trapped in a worldview that categorized Mirah’s Jewish heritage and Deronda’s identity into rigid, historical tropes, while Mirah remained isolated within the impenetrable fortress of her own feelings. At her door, she offered a melancholy gentleness, inviting him in to see her brother, which Hans eagerly interpreted as a sign of pardon.

Inside, the atmosphere was steeped in a different kind of warmth. They found Mordecai looking singularly happy, holding a closed letter, his eyes glowing with a quiet triumph that seemed to conquer the physical decay of his emaciated face. 

“A letter from Daniel Deronda,” Mordecai said, answering Mirah's silent question. “Brief—only saying that he hopes soon to return. Unexpected claims have detained him. The promise of seeing him again is like the bow in the cloud to me.”

While Hans and Mordecai spoke, Mirah slipped away to her own small chamber. The jealousy she had felt regarding Mrs. Grandcourt was a bitter, astringent flavor on her palate, an ancient sorrow echoing through the interwoven timelines of her young life. If the angels had watched her, they would have seen her engage in a mythic, deeply physical ritual of grounding herself. She dashed cold water on her eyes, brow, and hair until her curls were heavy with crystal beads, emerging from the basin like a freshly-opened flower from the dewy tresses of a woodland. She breathed deeply, returning to the present moment, and went down to the kitchen to seek solace in the profound, ancestral act of preparing sustenance. 

This was no mere utilitarian chore. Mirah approached the hearth like a meticulous food historian preserving the fragile embers of cultural memory. She measured out robust, dark-leaf tea, blending it with bruised cardamom pods, shards of spicy cinnamon bark, and dried slivers of Seville orange peel, an olfactory landscape that vividly resurrected the vanished world of her mother's kitchen. As she poured the boiling water from the heavy copper kettle, the hissing steam offered a soothing, rhythmic counterpoint to her internal turmoil. The rich, amber brew released an evocative, gustatory-rich aroma that filled the cramped room, a fragrant bridge spanning the saga of her past and the agonizing reality of her present. 

While she laid out a loaf of dark, dense rye bread—its earthy, fermented tang a testament to time and resilient patience—her mind raced. The sudden arrival of familiar Sorrow was not a surprise; it was an old companion sitting down at the table according to long use and wont. She felt a blind, untranslatable jealousy toward Mrs. Grandcourt. The image of the other woman by Deronda’s side was as definite and sharp as pincers on her flesh. It was a terrifying discovery that her reverence had sunk into selfish pain, a craving for a seat at a banquet from which she felt irrevocably banished. She was deeply ashamed of this hunger, this angry feeling toward another woman who possessed the good she wanted. 

Later, as they sat together, the comforting scent of the spiced tea and the heavy, satisfying crumb of the rye bread grounding the room in physical reality, Mordecai began to utter his exalted thoughts.

“Seest thou, Mirah,” he said, his eyes fixed on some distant horizon, “the *Shemah*, wherein we briefly confess the divine Unity, is the chief devotional exercise of the Hebrew. And this made our religion the fundamental religion for the whole world; for the divine Unity embraced as its consequence the ultimate unity of mankind... so that in a brief day the soul of man may know in fuller volume the good which has been and is, nay, is to come, than all he could possess in a whole life where he had to follow the creeping paths of the senses. I recognize another's future now, and love it so, that I can lay down this poor life upon its altar and say: ‘Burn, burn indiscernibly into that which shall be, which is my love and not me.’ Dost thou understand, Mirah?”

“A little,” Mirah said faintly, her hands wrapped around her warm cup, anchoring herself to the creeping paths of the senses that Mordecai so easily dismissed. 

“And yet,” Mordecai argued insistently, relying on an abstract, philosophical lexicon that neglected the body, “women are specially framed for the love which feels possession in renouncing. Somewhere in the later *Midrash*, I think, is the story of a Jewish maiden who loved a Gentile king so well, that this was what she did: she entered into prison and changed clothes with the woman who was beloved by the king, that she might deliver that woman from death by dying in her stead, and leave the king to be happy in his love which was not for her. This is the surpassing love, that loses self in the object of love.”

Mirah, her senses vibrating with the rich, spicy aroma of the tea and the raw, bodily reality of her own beating heart, firmly rejected this sterile interpretation. Her worldview was deeply rooted in the tangible, and she refused to let her ancestral lore be sanitized into a bloodless myth.

“No, Ezra, no,” Mirah said, with low-toned intensity. “That was not it. She wanted the king when she was dead to know what she had done, and feel that she was better than the other. It was her strong self, wanting to conquer, that made her die.”

Mordecai was silent a little, parsing her interpretation. “That might be, Mirah. But if she acted so, believing the king would never know.”

“You can make the story so in your mind, Ezra, because you are great, and like to fancy the greatest that could be,” she replied, guarding the profound, messy reality of feminine emotion as fiercely as the ancient recipes of her forebears. “But I think it was not really like that. The Jewish girl must have had jealousy in her heart, and she wanted somehow to have the first place in the king’s mind. That is what she would die for.”

“My sister, thou hast read too many plays,” Mordecai sighed gently, “where the writers delight in showing the human passions as indwelling demons, unmixed with the relenting and devout elements of the soul. Thou judgest by the plays, and not by thy own heart, which is like our mother’s.”

Mirah made no answer, taking a slow sip of the dark, bitter tea, letting the complex, untranslatable heat of the brew settle deeply into her chest.
