Some mornings, when I’m propped up in bed sipping that first steamy cuppa, watching my man Jeeves drift around the room laying out my threads for the day, I’ve had full-on panic attacks thinking what on earth I’d do if the bloke ever decided to quit. Things are manageable enough now I’m in New York, but back in London? The anxiety was crippling. All sorts of scummy low-lifes kept trying to poach him right out from under my nose. Young Reggie Foljambe, I know for a fact, offered him double what I was paying, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves – the chump whose own valet is famous for pressing his trousers sideways instead of down the proper crease – used to stare at Jeeves when he popped round like he was a starving man eyeing a truffle sandwich. Absolute pirates, the lot of them.

The thing is, Jeeves is so impossibly good at his job you can spot it in the tiny stuff, even the way he shoves a shirt stud in without fumbling once. I rely on him completely for every crisis, and he never ever drops the ball. Even better? He’ll always go the extra mile for any of my pals who happen to be neck-deep in trouble. Take that daft little saga of dear old Bicky and his uncle, the human hard-boiled egg, for example.

It went down a few months after I’d touched down in America. I stumbled back to the flat pretty late one night, and when Jeeves brought me that final nightcap, he dropped the news: “Mr. Bickersteth called for you twice this evening, sir, while you were out. He seemed rather agitated.”

“Pipped, you mean?” I asked, swirling my whisky around the glass. I felt bad for Bicky, but truth be told, I was glad to have a safe topic to chat about. See, things had been frosty between Jeeves and me for weeks, ever since I made the deeply controversial decision to grow a moustache. The man was gutted. He couldn’t stand the thing at any price, and I’d been living in a fog of quiet, seething disapproval ever since, which was getting extremely tedious. Don’t get me wrong – I know Jeeves’s judgment on suits and ties is 100% correct, and I’ve caved to his demands plenty of times like a compliant lamb. But a valet claiming jurisdiction over your upper lip? That’s a step too far. I’d dug my heels in, defiant as a bulldog.

“Said he’d be back later, sir,” Jeeves added. “I saw in the paper this morning that his grace the Duke of Chiswick, Mr. Bickersteth’s uncle, is arriving on the *Carmantic*.”

Wait a second. Bicky’s uncle was a duke? I’d met Bicky at a messy booze-up in Washington Square not long after I arrived, homesick and craving other English blokes to hang out with. We’d bonded over a quiet snort in the corner away from all the pretentious artists and sculptors, and he’d sealed the deal by doing the most perfect impression of a bull terrier chasing a cat up a tree I’d ever seen. Even after we got thick as thieves, all I knew about him was he was usually skint, and some uncle back home sent him a bit of cash every month to keep the lights on. I had no idea the uncle was a flipping duke.

Jeeves, naturally, knew every single detail. Bicky was the son of the duke’s late sister, who’d married a Coldstream Guards captain who died broke, no inheritance at all. And the Duke of Chiswick? Half owner of London, lord of five whole northern counties, and the cheapest man to ever draw breath. The kind of tightwad Americans call a hard-boiled egg.

Just then the doorbell rang, and Jeeves drifted out to answer it, and in trundled Bicky, looking like he’d just been dragged through a hedge backwards.

“Halloa, Bicky, old man!” I said. “Jeeves, grab another glass, let’s get this party started. What’s the trouble?”

“I’m in the soup, Bertie,” he moaned. “I need your help. My uncle’s turning up tomorrow out of the blue, wants to stay with me to skip paying for a hotel. He sent me off to some godforsaken ranch in Colorado to learn farming, right? I bailed, came to New York, told him I started a booming business here, and now he’s showing up unannounced. I can’t put him up in my crummy boarding house, the landlady serves boiled cabbage for every meal!”

I turned to Jeeves. “Got any ideas, Jeeves?”

“With your permission, sir,” he said, smooth as silk, “you might lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat for the duration of his grace’s stay. I will pose as Mr. Bickersteth’s personal valet, and you will be his house guest. His grace will be none the wiser.”

Bicky stared at him like he’d just pulled a rabbit out of a top hat. “Bertie, how does he do it? His head sticks out funny at the back, that’s what it is! All the great geniuses have lumpy heads like that.”

The next morning I hopped out of bed at the crack of dawn to be there when the duke turned up. Sure enough, not long after 9, I looked out the window and saw a top-hatted old boy throwing a full-on wobbly over his taxi fare, yelling that in London the ride would’ve cost eightpence, and the cabbie was having none of it. It was Chiswick, no two ways about it. I dragged him inside, poured him tea, and before you knew it he was fully convinced Bicky was a millionaire business tycoon, rolling in Rockefeller levels of cash. Bicky showed up ten minutes later, chuffed to bits that the con was working, and took his uncle off to see the sights, starting with Grant’s Tomb.

I waltzed back to the flat that evening to get changed for dinner, and Jeeves dropped the bomb that made my blood run cold. “The scheme has developed an unforeseen snag, sir. His grace has decided to cancel Mr. Bickersteth’s monthly allowance entirely, on the logic that a man doing so fantastically well in New York business can’t possibly need financial support from home.”

Great Scot! Poor Bicky walked in not long after, hunched in an armchair, staring at nothing, rambling madly about a get-rich-quick chicken farm scheme he’d seen in a magazine ad. “You start with one hen! It lays one egg a day, sell seven for 25 cents, no feed costs! In no time you’re wading up to your knees in hens, all making you money! But I don’t have the five bucks startup cash, and I’m not borrowing from you, Bertie, I refuse to owe my pal money.”

I turned desperately to Jeeves. “Come on, Jeeves, we’ve got to fix this.”

“Ah, sir,” he said, calm as ever. “It just occurred to me that his grace’s status as a famous British duke is a very valuable untapped resource. Americans are obsessed with meeting nobility. I’m certain plenty of people would happily pay two or three dollars for the privilege of shaking his hand and getting an introduction.”

We tried it for a whole week, and it was a total flop. The only person we lined up was a deli owner who wanted to pay us entirely in sliced ham, and an anarchist bloke who ran a pawnbroker shop who offered ten bucks just to kick the old duke in the shin. Bicky was this close to taking him up on it. We were about to call the whole thing off, when Jeeves drifted in with my morning tea the next day, calm as you please.

“Might I speak to you about the his grace project, sir? I’ve arranged the interested parties.”

“Wait,” I said, sitting straight up and spilling tea all over my duvet. “You got people to pay? How many?”

“Eighty-seven gentlemen, sir. From Birdsburg, Missouri.”
