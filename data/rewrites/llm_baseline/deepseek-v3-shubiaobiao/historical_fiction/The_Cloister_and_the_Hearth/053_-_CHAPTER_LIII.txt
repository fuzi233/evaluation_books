The tension in the room was thick enough to cut with a knife. Catherine, her voice strained with weariness, finally broke the silence.

"Richart, for Heaven’s sake, don’t let this wretched girl turn us all against one another. Hasn’t she caused enough trouble already?"

Richart exhaled sharply. "Aye, she has. But fear not, Mother. Let her come, read the letter from the boy she’s ensnared with her devilish charms, and then be gone. You’ve all promised civility—no more, no less. And I’ll hold my tongue."

And so they waited, a household divided.

Margaret, for her part, arrived with her own bitterness simmering beneath a carefully constructed mask of dignity. When Denys had told her they refused to come to her—that she must go to them—her lips curled in disdain.

"Of course," she muttered. "Even in this, their pride outweighs their love for Gerard. But I’ll go—for his sake."

She dressed as if for battle, each garment chosen to defy their judgment. A dark blue kirtle, roan hose, and a fitted hood of the same hue, hemmed with silver—plain yet elegant, a quiet rebuke to their assumptions. Beauty, she knew, was a weapon, and she wielded it well.

Yet when the moment came, her courage faltered. Halfway there, she stopped abruptly.

"Denys," she whispered.

"Aye?"

"I—I must go back."

"What, forgotten something?"

"My nerve," she admitted, voice trembling. "Oh, God, I can’t do this."

Denys squeezed her shoulder. "Steady, lass. I’ll be right beside you."

With that reassurance, she pressed forward, chin high but heart pounding.

Inside the house, the family sat in stiff silence, a volatile mix of curiosity, resentment, and reluctant pity swirling among them. When Denys slipped in to announce her arrival, Eli gave a terse nod.

"Bring her in. And remember—no outbursts."

The door creaked open.

Margaret stood framed in the threshold, pale but composed, her gaze steady despite the hostility radiating from the room. She moved with deliberate grace, her condition evident in the slight heaviness of her step, the way her hand occasionally drifted to her waist.

The women softened slightly at the sight. The brothers scowled.

She withdrew the letter from her bosom, kissed it as if in private devotion, then unfolded it with hands that barely trembled.

But then she noticed—they had seated her apart, like a leper. A flicker of hurt crossed her face before she masked it. With a subtle gesture, she summoned Denys to her side, and he stood sentinel behind her, solid as a bulwark.

Then she began to read.

Her voice, rich and warm, filled the room, weaving Gerard’s words into the air like a spell.

*"I do nothing doubt, my Margaret, that long ere this shall meet thy beloved eyes, Denys, my most dear friend, will have sought thee out..."*

At the mention of his name, Denys groaned, bowing his head. Margaret, without looking, reached back and grasped his hand—a silent pact between the two who loved Gerard most.

She read on, recounting Gerard’s trials—robbed, left destitute, saved by strangers who showed him kindness when his own family had turned him away. The room listened, rapt, as his voice echoed through hers.

When she reached the tale of the beggar—the cripple who was no cripple at all—Sybrandt snorted with laughter.

Margaret’s eyes flashed. She snapped the letter shut.

Eli rounded on his son. "One more word, and you’re out the door!"

Catherine, ever the peacemaker, murmured, "Read on, child. Pay no mind to fools."

Margaret took a steadying breath and continued. Gerard’s words painted a picture of hardship and resilience, of a boy forced to rely on wit and grit to survive. And through it all—his longing for home, for her.

By the time she finished, the room was silent, the air thick with unspoken emotions.

Margaret carefully folded the letter, her hands steady now. She had done what she came to do.

The rest was up to them.
