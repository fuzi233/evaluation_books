It has often struck me—though I speak with humility before those who have worn the sword—that the entrenchments of Breed’s Hill served as a lasting shield for the Continental army throughout the entire American war. The toll exacted upon us from behind those lines was so great, and the enemy’s resolve so unyielding, that the British commanders henceforth held the Americans’ barricades in high regard. Indeed, even had they been firing from behind mere blankets, certain generals would hesitate to press forward. At White Plains, when Washington’s army was once again at the mercy of victory, we learned afterward that our own troops had paused before a barrier made not of stone or timber, but of cornstalks and straw. Another chance was given to us—and it lasted through an entire winter. During that time, the dwindling, disheartened forces of Congress lay starving and nearly unarmed within our grasp, yet the magnanimous General Howe left Valley Forge untouched. While his well-supplied, disciplined army feasted and gambled in Philadelphia, he allowed the American cause to endure, untouched by conquest.

And what of the celebrations? Triumphal arches rose in London; tournaments were staged in mock medieval splendor; ladies offered garlands and verses to this noble commander, proclaiming his laurels eternal! Why, then, have my fellow countrymen in America never raised statues to such a man? Among all their officers, none fought their battles more wisely, none helped them recover from defeat with greater skill, none ensured their losses were less costly. And when fortune finally turned, and strength prevailed, none showed more patience, compassion, or quiet dignity in helping the fallen rise again.

Ah, think back eighteen years—of the young warrior England sent across the sea, burning with ambition and fire. Picture him pacing endlessly around the enemy’s defenses, sleepless by night and day, consumed by fury and longing, eyes fixed on every shadow, scenting battle like a hound. He crept through midnight forests, scaled cliffs before dawn, waited until the foe revealed himself—then leapt upon him, grappled, and died still striking. That was Wolfe at Quebec. Now listen: here is Howe, smiling amid dancers in Philadelphia, while fiddles play and wine flows freely.

One of the grand schemes devised by our ministers and generals was to sever New England from the rest of the colonies by establishing a link between Canada and New York. Burgoyne was entrusted with this task, and he set out from Quebec with bold confidence, promising success. His march began in full military pomp—trumpets blared proclamations before him, summoning the colonists to remember England’s might and lay down arms. He led a formidable force: English regulars, German veterans, Indian warriors, and a brilliant artillery train. It was believed the people along his path would rally to the Crown. But the Continental resistance, though initially small, grew stronger. Washington’s army was weakened as troops were diverted to meet this northern threat. A detachment from New York was meant to ascend the Hudson, clear the way, and join Burgoyne at Albany. That was the moment to strike Washington’s diminished force—but fate intervened. Nor do I regret the war’s end. Looking back now, how clear the errors of the losing side appear!

From beginning to end, we arrived too late. Our reinforcements came too late. Our fleet appeared off Yorktown just after Cornwallis had surrendered. A route of escape opened for Burgoyne—but he chose retreat too late. I’ve heard defeated officers argue, years later, that a different wind could have saved America for us; that a storm spared the French fleet; that three times, but for darkness, Washington and his army were ours. Who hasn’t wondered, reading history, about what *might* have been?

I take down my old, tattered map-book and trace the battlefield. I marvel at the strange turns that decided the outcome. Setting aside talk of courage—both sides fought bravely—we held the better hand and still lost the game. Yet the contest stirred something deep in me. I felt its pulse.

My brother Hal, settled on his Virginia plantation, found perfect contentment in simple life. The company of neighbors, tending crops, fishing, hunting, playing cards—these were his joys. Happy Hal, beneath his wide porches, dogs at his feet, Will Wimbles living freely in his house, negroes fat and idle, his sharp little wife ruling over all, her husband obeying without question. She died, and he mourned briefly—then moved on. Happy, truly, though such a life would have driven me mad.

My income was fivefold his, my house in England large and stately, built of brick and stone. My wife—would I trade her for any other? My children—I accept Lady Warrington’s judgment. Yet with all these blessings, I fear I’ve been ungrateful. Hodge, my gatekeeper, shares his bread and bacon with a family as large as mine, and seems to enjoy his meal as much as I do—though Mrs. Molly prepares her best dishes, and Mr. Gumbo opens the finest bottle. Ah, sweetmeats no longer taste sweet to me, though they delight the nursery. Claret’s perfume grows stale with age.

Our parson has preached to me many times. I may not have listened at first—but now I understand. Do you remember, honest Joe? (He does—he’s repeated it as often as his sermons, and Lady Warrington pretends she’s never heard it.) That October evening when we scrambled up an embrasure at Fort Clinton, and a clubbed musket nearly ended my life—had not your sword cut through the rebel’s throat, I’d be gone. You wore red then—the uniform of the brave Sixty-third, under the gallant Sill, who fell beside you pierced by many wounds. You traded red for black and took the pulpit. Your sermons are sound and brief. We read the papers together over wine. Not two months ago, we read of Howe’s triumph on June 1st. We heard of Rawdon, who fought with us at Fort Clinton, joining the Duke of York. Now, the Duke is retreating before Pichegru, and my son Miles has captured Valenciennes. Ah, parson—would you not don your old Sixty-third coat again? (Though I doubt Mrs. Blake could fit the buttons.)

The boys played a play with my militia sword. Oh, that I were young again, Mr. Blake! That I had no gout in my toe! I’d saddle Rosinante and ride into the world once more, feel the blood quicken, and play life’s glorious game!

The last decisive move I witnessed was won gallantly by our side—though the Americans claimed the final rubber. We gained only the ground we stood on, and the guns, stores, and ships we destroyed. But our rescue attempt came too late to save Burgoyne’s doomed army.

After one of those delays that plagued our campaigns, an expedition sailed from New York in late September 1777. Had it advanced just two weeks earlier, it might have saved Burgoyne. But fate decreed otherwise. The delay wasn’t Sir Henry Clinton’s fault—he couldn’t leave New York undefended. It was the winds and weather that delayed reinforcements from England. The fleet brought letters from home—long, loving missives from Aunt Hetty and Grandfather, with news of the children. My wife’s heart ached for them. She said nothing, but I saw her plea in her eyes—her fears, her longing. “Why stay?” she seemed to say. “You have no duty here. Why risk your life, my happiness?”

I understood. We weren’t going into immediate danger. I told her Sir Henry would only accompany part of the way. I’d return with him, and Christmas, God willing, would see us reunited in England.

Three thousand men—including two weak regiments of Loyalists and New York Militia (among whom was my cousin, Captain Will Esmond)—embarked at New York. Our fleet sailed up the Hudson River, which, to my mind, rivals the Rhine in beauty. No fire greeted us from the steep cliffs known as the Palisades—strangely, the enemy was unaware of our movement.

We landed first on the eastern bank at Verplancks Point. The Continental troops withdrew after slight resistance. Their commander, the seasoned Putnam, assumed we were advancing toward the Eastern Highlands. He fell back to guard the passes, sending a small detachment forward as if pursued—believing the main force would follow.

Meanwhile, before dawn, two thousand men crossed to Stoney Point on the western shore, opposite Verplancks. There loomed the Dunderberg—a great hill, named by Dutch lords, rising sharply over the river. A stream ran at its base, separating it from the height where Fort Clinton stood—not named for our general, but for a respected New York gentleman, now commanding against us. Above Fort Clinton rose Fort Montgomery; behind them, Bear Hill. Across the river, Saint Antony’s Nose towered—named whimsically by the Dutch.

The assaults began almost simultaneously. Half our force, under Campbell, attacked Fort Montgomery. He fell before the ramparts. Sir Henry, ever seeking danger, led the rest. He hoped we’d fare better than at Sullivan Island.

A narrow path led up the Dunderberg—so tight that only three men could pass abreast. In silence, we climbed, amazed at each step that no one challenged us. The enemy had no watch there. We weren’t seen until descending the slope, where a small force rushed to oppose us. The gunfire spoiled surprise. But the fort was before us.

With no artillery, we had to assault. Silently, swiftly, the men climbed the hill under cannon fire. Orders forbade firing. Sir Henry took the colors of the Sixty-third and led the stormers upward. The incline was so steep that soldiers pushed each other over walls and through embrasures. There, Lieutenant Joseph Blake—father of the young Joseph Clinton Blake, who gazes fondly at a certain lady—saved the life of his unworthy patron by striking down a rebel aiming at him.

About a quarter of the garrison escaped. The rest were killed, wounded, or captured. Fort Montgomery was taken in similar fashion. That night, from the heights where the king’s flag flew, we watched a magnificent spectacle below. A boom and chain stretched from Fort Montgomery to Saint Antony’s Nose, protecting several American frigates and galleys. With the fort lost, the ships tried to flee upstream in darkness. But the wind turned against them. Escape was impossible. Crews abandoned ship, setting them ablaze with sails unfurled. We saw towering pyramids of flame reflected in the river, exploding in thunderous blasts before sinking beneath the water.

The next morning, a parlementaire arrived to inquire about wounded and prisoners. An officer handed me a note—my heart stopped. It was dated October 7, from General George Clinton’s headquarters. It read: “Colonel H. Warrington, of the Virginia line, hopes Sir George Warrington escaped unhurt in the assault of last evening, from which the Colonel himself was fortunate enough to retire without injury.”

Never did I pray more fervently. I thanked Heaven my brother lived—and vowed to withdraw from this fraternal war, entered only because duty called. I longed to see him before leaving. I requested, and received, a mission to General Clinton’s camp.

His headquarters lay miles upriver. A boat with a flag of truce carried me to the shore, where pickets met me. Soon, my brother stood before me. He had recently joined Clinton’s division, bearing orders from Philadelphia. He’d heard I’d been present at the attack.

We spent a short, joyful night together. Mr. Sady, always loyal to Hal, cooked a feast for both of us. Only one straw bed existed in our hut—Hal and I slept side by side, as we had in boyhood. We spoke of everything—past, present, future.

When I told him I planned to retire, his face lit with joy. “Praised be God!” he cried. “Oh, George—think what might have happened had we met in that fight two nights ago!” He turned pale at the thought.

He reassured me about our mother. Though a staunch Tory, the Chief had ordered her protection. “And Fanny,” he added proudly, “watches over her—she’s as good as a company!” Then, with enthusiasm: “Isn’t she clever? Isn’t she beautiful? Isn’t she kind?”—never waiting for an answer. “And to think I almost married Maria! Mercy, what an escape!”

He spoke of Hagan, who prayed daily for the King at Castlewood—though the doors were bolted, and no one heard. “Gracious powers! His wife is sixty, and oh, George—what she drinks!” But why dwell on faults? I’m glad she lived to toast King George long after his dominion was lost.

Morning came. My mission ended. My brother walked with me to the riverside, where my boat waited. We embraced. His hand lingered in mine as I stepped aboard.

“Shall I ever see thee again, dearest companion of my youth?” I asked myself. “Among cold Englishmen, can I find a friend like thee? When did you ever harbor a thought that wasn’t kind? When a desire, that you wouldn’t give up for me? How brave, how modest, how gentle, how strong—how simple, unselfish, humble—you who see others’ merit and shrink from your own!”

He stood on the shore until his figure faded. Tears blurred my vision.

Sir Henry’s victory, brilliant as it was, came too late. It could not prevent Burgoyne’s disaster at Saratoga, which followed soon after. Our advance was insufficient to relieve him. Secret messengers were sent—none reached him. One, carrying a letter from Sir Henry, fell into the hands of General Clinton’s troops, mistaken for British soldiers due to their red uniforms. The spy was discovered and executed.

Ten days after the forts fell, we learned of Saratoga’s ruin. We carried the grim news home. I feared my wife could not mourn. She had her children, her father, her sister to greet—and nightly prayers of thanksgiving that her husband returned alive.
