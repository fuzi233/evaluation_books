The cry of love torn from Phaidor by her fright struck straight through me, and left me tangled in a mess of sharp, awkward guilt. I’d never meant to lead her on, never so much as hinted I cared for her that way, and yet some careless word or small, kind gesture I’d long forgotten had convinced her I returned her affection.

I was never the sort of man to moon over scented gloves too small for my hand, or tuck a dead flower that stank worse than rotting Martian cabbage in my tunic pocket. My days were for fighting, for drilling, for the rough, honest work of a warrior—all the soft, tangled games of romance had never come naturally to me. I stood there frozen, far more afraid of meeting that beautiful girl’s eyes to tell her the truth than I ever was to face a charging horde of green Tharks on the dead sea bottoms. But there was no way out, so I did the clumsy best I could.

I unclasped her arms from around my neck gently, held her hands in mine, and told her the whole, unvarnished truth of Dejah Thoris. That across two entire worlds, across a hundred long years of life and battle, I had only ever loved one woman, and I would never love another.

The words did not go over well.

She sprang to her feet like a snarling tigress, chest heaving, her perfect face twisted into a mask of vicious, unholy rage. Her eyes blazed bright enough to burn. “Dog!” she hissed. “Blasphemer! Do you imagine Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, begs for a man’s affection? I command it. What do I care for your trivial, outer-world fancy for that silly little Heliumite you chose in some dead old life?

“I honored you with my love, and you spat it back in my face. Ten thousand deaths too foul to name could never atone for that insult. That thing you call Dejah Thoris will die the cruelest of them all. You signed her death warrant just now.

“And you? You’ll be the lowest, most abused slave in the service of the goddess you dared to mock. Tortures and humiliations will be heaped on you until you grovel in the dirt begging for the tiny mercy of death. And when at last I grant your prayer, I’ll stand on the high balconies of the Golden Cliffs and laugh as the great white apes tear you limb from limb.”

She had the whole revenge plotted out, every last gory detail. I could scarce believe that a creature so divinely beautiful could be so steeped in venom. It struck me, though, that she’d forgotten one small, inconvenient fact that put a crimp in her plans, so I nodded at the nearest port-hole, no intent to taunt her, just to help her adjust her little scheme to the reality of where we were.

She’d been so caught up in her rage she’d entirely forgotten we were still in the sinking submarine. One glance at the dark, churning, crushing waters outside the glass sent her crumpling to a low bench, her face buried in her arms, sobbing like a lost, miserable little girl rather than the proud, untouchable daughter of the Thern’s ruling god.

Down we sank, deeper and deeper, until the thick, pressure-forged glass of the port-holes grew warm against my palms, heated by the superheated water far beneath Barsoom’s outer crust. We must have been miles below the surface of the planet. At last our downward motion stopped, the propellers at the stern whirred to life, and we shot forward at high speed. It was black as pitch down there, but the light spilling from our port-holes and the blazing beam of the submarine’s nose searchlight showed we were racing through a narrow, tube-like tunnel hewn straight out of solid red volcanic rock.

A few minutes later the propellers fell silent. We drifted to a full stop, then shot upward fast toward the surface. The light outside grew brighter by the second, until we halted entirely. Xodar stepped through the cabin hatch with a squad of his black guards. “Come,” he ordered, and we followed him out.

We stood in a small, domed subterranean vault, a pool of still water at its center holding our submarine, only its matte black hull peeking above the surface. A flat stone platform ringed the pool, and the cave walls rose straight up a dozen feet before curving over into a low, vaulted roof. Dim, glowing archways lined the ledge, leading off into unlit tunnels. Our guards herded us to one of these, and after a short walk we stopped before a steel elevator cage at the bottom of a shaft that stretched up so far we could not see its top.

The car was the same magnetic elevator common across Barsoom, powered by giant electromagnets strung at the top of the shaft, their pull regulated by a simple electrical dial to control speed. With Mars’ low gravity offering barely any resistance to the magnet’s force, these lifts shot upward so fast the ride left your stomach lurching. The door had barely clicked shut behind us before we were slowing to a stop at the landing at the top of the shaft.

When we stepped out of the small stone terminal building, we found ourselves in a fairyland of splendor no words from Earth could ever properly describe. You could talk all you want about crimson Martian grass, ivory-trunked trees draped in vivid purple blooms, walkways paved with crushed rubies and emeralds and turquoise and raw diamonds, a temple of burnished gold carved over every inch with impossibly fine filigree. But where are the words to name the colors that no human eye on Earth has ever seen? Where is the imagination that can picture the scintillating glow of unheard-of light rays spilling from the thousands of strange, nameless gemstones native to Barsoom? Even my eyes, long used to the barbaric opulence of a Jeddak of Helium’s court, went wide at the sight. Phaidor stared, lips parted. “The Temple of Issus,” she whispered to herself. Xodar watched us with his grim half-smile, half amusement, half gloating over the shock we felt.

The gardens swarmed with black First Born men and women draped in rich, brilliant silks, waited on hand and foot by red and white women stolen as princesses from the outer world and Thern temples, reduced to nothing but slaves. We marched past them all toward the great gilded temple doors, where a cordon of armed black guards halted us. Xodar spoke a few quiet words to their officer, the two of them vanishing inside the temple for a long stretch before they returned, announcing Issus wished to see Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, and the strange man from another world who had once been a Prince of Helium.

We moved through endless corridors and great, soaring halls of impossible beauty, until we stopped in a vast antechamber at the very heart of the temple. One of our guards walked to a massive carved door at the far end, gave a secret signal, and a richly robed courtier stepped out. We were ordered to drop to our hands and knees, our backs turned to the chamber beyond. The doors swung open. We were warned that turning our heads even a fraction would mean instant death, and commanded to crawl backwards into Issus’s presence.

It was the most humiliating moment of my whole life. Only the thought of Dejah Thoris, the tiny spark of hope that I might still find her alive, stopped me from leaping to my feet, drawing my sword, and charging into that room to die like a warrior, facing my enemies with their blood soaking my own before I fell.

We crawled that disgusting, humiliating way for two hundred feet before our escort halted us. “Let them rise,” a thin, wavering old voice called out from behind us, a voice that had spent thousands of years barking orders that no one dared disobey. “Rise,” our guard echoed, “but never turn your face toward Issus.”

“The woman pleases me,” the thin voice said after a long, quiet pause. “She will serve me for her allotted time. The man may be sent to the Isle of Shador, on the northern shore of the Sea of Omean. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus. All those of lower rank who gaze upon my radiant, holy face may live no longer than one year afterward.”

I watched Phaidor out of the corner of my eye. She blanched so pale her skin looked almost white as bleached ivory. Slow as if pulled by an invisible, unbreakable string, she turned, her bare arm brushing mine as she faced the supposed goddess of life eternal. I could not see her face, but the violent shudder that wracked her body traveled straight through the arm pressed to mine, hard enough to feel. “That must be some blinding, unearthly loveliness,” I thought, “to shake a woman as beautiful as Phaidor that badly.”

“Leave the woman here. Take the man away. Go,” Issus ordered. The guard’s heavy hand fell on my shoulder, and I dropped back to my hands and knees to crawl back out of the audience chamber. It was my first audience with a “living goddess,” and I left entirely unimpressed—aside from the deep embarrassment of scrambling around on my knees like a whipped cur.

Once outside the doors they let me stand, and Xodar fell into step beside me as we walked slowly back toward the gardens. “You spared my life when you could have killed me easily,” he said after a long silence, “and I would help you if I can. I can make your bondage far easier to bear, but your fate is sealed. You will never go back to the outer world.”

“What fate?” I asked.

“It all depends on Issus. As long as she never calls you to look on her face, you might live for years, with me arranging the softest possible posts for you. But she often calls in strong, skilled fighters like you for her monthly temple rites. Men pitted against men, or against great beasts, for her amusement—and to stock her larder.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked. I felt no shock. After what I’d seen of the Holy Therns, nothing in their hidden “heaven” could surprise me. Ages of brutal fanaticism and absolute power had stripped every last trace of mercy and humanity from the First Born. They saw all the rest of Barsoom’s inhabitants the same way we see cattle on Earth: meat, no more. So why wouldn’t they eat their flesh?

“Only the flesh of the best-bred Therns and red Barsoomians is fit for her. The rest go to our tables. The beasts go to the slaves. She has other rare delicacies, too.”

I did not grasp how deep their depravity ran then, I thought cannibalism was the worst they could possibly do. I had no idea how far absolute, unchallenged power could twist a creature to cruelty.

We were almost at the gardens when an officer ran up behind us, breathless. “Issus demands to see the man again,” he said. “The girl told her he is of unmatched strength and beauty, that he killed seven of the First Born all alone, and took Dator Xodar captive with his bare hands, binding him with his own harness.”

Xodar’s jaw tightened. He clearly hated that his humiliating defeat had reached Issus’s ears. Without a word, we followed the officer back to the closed doors of the audience chamber, went through the whole crawling ritual again, and Issus bade me stand. The silence hung thick as a tomb for minutes, while that ancient goddess stared at me. At last that thin, wavering voice droned out the same line that had sealed the fate of thousands of victims before me. “Let the man turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of lower order who gaze upon the holy vision of my radiant face survive no longer than a single year.”

I turned, ready for the sight of divine, unearthly glory that could burn a mortal’s eyes. What I saw was a line of armed guards standing between me and a carved sorapus wood dais. Perched on that throne was a shriveled old black woman. Not a single hair grew on her wrinkled, bald skull. Save for two sharp, yellow fangs, she had no teeth left at all. Her eyes burned deep in sunken sockets flanked by a thin, hawk-like nose. Her face was crisscrossed with a million deep, cracked wrinkles, her body as emaciated and repulsive as her face. Stick-thin arms and legs jutted from a torso that was little more than a distended, sagging belly. That was the “holy, radiant divine beauty” everyone worshipped. Phaidor stood among the group of trembling female slaves at her side, white as a sheet.

“Is this the man who killed seven of the First Born, and bound Dator Xodar with his bare hands?” Issus squealed. “Most glorious divine vision, it is,” the officer at my side answered. “Bring Dator Xodar to me,” she ordered. They dragged Xodar into the room. She glared at him, malice burning in her hideous little eyes. “And you call yourself a Dator of the Immortal Race?” she shrieked. “For the shame you have brought on us, you are stripped of all rank. You are no longer a Dator. You are the lowest of all slaves, to fetch and carry for the garden slaves. Strip his harness from him. Cowards and prisoners wear no fine trappings.”

Xodar stood rigid and tall, not a single muscle twitching, as the guards tore his gorgeous ceremonial trappings off him. “Begone!” she screamed. “And you will not serve in the gardens. You will go to the Isle of Shador, to be the personal slave of the man who defeated you. Fetch and carry for him for the rest of your days. Get out of my sight.” Xodar walked out of the chamber, head held high, his pride unbroken.

Issus turned and made to leave through a side exit. Over her shoulder she called to me: “You will be sent to Shador for now. Later, Issus will watch you fight. Go.” She vanished, her whole retinue trailing after her. Only Phaidor lagged behind. As I turned to follow my guards back toward the gardens, she ran after me.

“Don’t leave me here,” she begged, breathless, half incoherent. “Forgive what I said. I did not mean it. Take me with you to Shador. Let me share your prison. We both looked on Issus—we both only have a year left to live. We can spend that whole year together, happy, while we still can. You never understood. We Therns do not marry the way outer world red men do. We could have loved each other forever.”

“Phaidor,” I said, gentle as I could, “you cannot blame me for not understanding your ways, when you do not understand mine. I do not wish to hurt you, but what you ask can never be. While I draw breath, my heart belongs only to Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium. When I die, my heart will stop beating, and I know nothing of what comes after. Not even Matai Shang or the great Issus know any more than that.”

Phaidor stared at me for a long moment. No rage burned in her eyes now, only quiet, hopeless grief. “I do not understand,” she whispered, and turned, walking slowly back through the door where Issus’s retinue had vanished, until she was gone from my sight.
