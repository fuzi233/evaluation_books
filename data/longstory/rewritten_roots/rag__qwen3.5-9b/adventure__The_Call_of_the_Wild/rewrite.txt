"Nomadic longings leap, chafing at custom's chain; From brumal sleep, the ferine strain awakes again."

Buck did not read the press, yet the seismic shift in the Pacific Northwest was palpable. Men, driven by the discovery of gold in the Arctic dark, were flooding northward. Steamships groaned under the weight of prospectors, and the demand for draft power was absolute. They needed heavy dogs—muscular beasts with thick pelts to withstand the freeze.

Buck inhabited the sun-drenched domain of Judge Miller in the Santa Clara Valley. The estate was a fortress of leisure, half-concealed by towering poplars that filtered the California heat onto a wide, cool veranda. Gravel drives wound through manicured lawns toward great stables where a dozen grooms maintained order, and rows of vine-clad cottages housed the domestic staff. Beyond lay the pumping plant for the artesian well, the massive cement bath where the Judge's sons cooled themselves, and endless orchards and berry patches.

Over this sprawling demesne, Buck reigned supreme. Born here, raised here, he had spent four years as a sovereign of the land. While other dogs existed—the Japanese pug Toots, the hairless Ysabel, or the pack of fox terriers who yelped threats from behind glass—he was irrelevant to them. He was neither a kennel dog nor a pet. He was the apex predator of the valley. He commanded the swimming tank, hunted alongside the Judge's sons, escorted the daughters through twilight walks, and slept before the roaring library fire. He carried the grandsons on his broad back, rolling them in the grass, guarding their path to the stable yard and beyond. Even the terriers feared his imperial gaze.

His lineage was noble. His father, Elmo, a massive St. Bernard, had been the Judge's constant shadow. Buck, weighing one hundred and forty pounds, inherited his sire's stature, tempered by his mother Shep's Scottish Shepherd agility. Four years of a sated aristocratic life had bred in him a fine pride, bordering on arrogance, typical of insulated country gentlemen. Yet, he remained fit. Hunting and the daily ritual of plunging into the cold tub had kept his muscles hard and his fat minimal. Water was his tonic; the cold, his preservative.

This was the state of Buck in the autumn of 1897, as the Klondike strike began to drag humanity into the frozen north. He remained oblivious to the coming storm until the betrayal occurred. Manuel, a gardener's helper with a fatal flaw—a gambling addiction fueled by a rigid belief in a winning system—had sold him out. While the Judge attended a raisin growers' meeting and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel led Buck away under the guise of a stroll.

At the remote flag stop of College Park, a stranger intercepted them. Money changed hands in a silent transaction.

"You might wrap up the goods before delivery," the stranger grunted.

Manuel complied, looping a heavy rope around Buck's neck beneath his collar. "Twist it tight, and you'll choke 'em plenty," Manuel advised. The stranger nodded.

Buck accepted the restraint with dignified calm. He trusted the men he knew; he assumed their actions stemmed from a wisdom superior to his own. But when the stranger seized the rope's ends, Buck growled, a warning meant to command submission. Instead, the rope cinched shut, cutting off his air. Rage surged instantly. He lunged, but the stranger met him, grappling him by the throat and executing a deft throw that sent Buck crashing onto his back. The rope tightened mercilessly. Buck fought in a frenzy, tongue lolling, chest heaving, but his strength drained rapidly. Never had he been so humiliated, so violently dominated. As his vision blurred and his eyes glazed, the train arrived. Two men loaded him into the baggage car, leaving him unconscious.

When consciousness returned, the world was a blur of motion and noise. The shrill whistle of a locomotive signaled his location. He knew the sensation of the baggage car from rides with the Judge, but this was different. Anger, raw and unbridled, flooded his senses. Before the man could reach for his throat, Buck snapped. His jaws clamped onto the hand, holding fast until the attacker's nerve signals failed.

"Yes, he's got fits," the man muttered, concealing the mangled limb from the baggageman drawn by the commotion. "Taking him up to Frisco. Some crack doctor thinks he can cure whatever's wrong with him."

In a dim shed behind a waterfront saloon, the captor recounted the night's haul. His hand was bandaged in a bloody rag, his trousers torn from knee to ankle.

"All I get is fifty," he grumbled, spitting on the floor. "I wouldn't do it again for a thousand dollars, cold cash."

"How much did the other guy get?" the saloon keeper asked.

"A hundred. Wouldn't take a sou less," the man replied.

"That makes one-fifty," the keeper calculated. "He's worth it, or I'm a squarehead."

The kidnapper peeled back the bandage, inspecting the laceration. "If I don't catch hydrophobia—"

"It'll be because you were born to hang," the keeper laughed. "Here, lend me a hand before you move your freight."

Dazed, suffering excruciating pain in his throat and tongue, Buck tried to confront his tormentors. They overpowered him, choking him repeatedly until they managed to file the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope followed, and he was shoved into a cage-like crate.

He lay there through the weary night, nursing his wounded pride. Confusion clouded his mind. Who were these strangers? Why confine him? A vague sense of impending doom settled over him. Every time the shed door rattled, he leaped to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Instead, the sickly glow of a tallow candle revealed the saloon keeper's bulging face. Each time, the joyful bark dying in his throat twisted into a savage growl.

Morning brought four new arrivals: ragged, unkempt men who looked like predators themselves. Buck roared through the bars, but they only laughed, poking him with sticks. He bit at them, realizing quickly that aggression was their currency. Sullenly, he submitted, allowing the crate to be lifted into a wagon.

The journey began. Through clerks, express offices, trucks, and ferry steamers, the crate moved across the continent. Finally, it reached a great railway depot and was deposited in an express car. For two days and nights, the car was dragged by shrieking locomotives. Buck ate nothing, drank nothing. His initial growls at the express messengers earned him mockery. They teased him, mimicking animal sounds, crowing and flapping their arms. It was absurd, insulting to his dignity, and it fanned his rage to a fever pitch. Thirst became his primary torment, inflamed by the swelling of his throat and tongue. High-strung and sensitive, the abuse pushed him into a physical fever.

One comfort remained: the rope was gone. Without it, he vowed, he would exact revenge. He refused food or water, accumulating a reservoir of hatred that promised destruction to anyone who crossed his path. His eyes turned bloodshot, transforming him into a raging fiend. The Judge would not recognize him. By the time the train halted at Seattle, the express messengers breathed a sigh of relief as they bundled the crate off.

Four men carried the crate into a small, high-walled backyard. A stout man in a red sweater emerged, signing the driver's log. Buck sensed the next threat and hurled himself against the bars. The man smiled grimly, producing a hatchet and a club.

"You ain't taking him out now?" the driver asked.

"Sure," the man replied, driving the hatchet into the wood to pry it open.

The four carriers scattered to safer vantage points atop the wall. Inside, Buck attacked the splintering wood, teeth sunk deep, wrestling with the barrier. Outside, the man worked methodically.

"Now, you red-eyed devil," the man said, creating an opening large enough for Buck's body. He discarded the hatchet, gripping a club in his right hand.

Buck was indeed a red-eyed demon. Hair bristling, foam dripping from his jaws, a mad glint in his eyes, he launched his one-hundred-and-forty-pound fury straight at the man. Mid-air, just as his jaws were to snap shut, the club struck. The impact jarred his spine, forcing his teeth together with an agonizing click. He whirled, hitting the ground on his back and side. Having never experienced such violence, he didn't comprehend the weapon. Snarling, a mix of bark and scream, he rose and charged again. The club met him, crushing him to the earth.

A dozen times he charged; a dozen times the club shattered his momentum. After a particularly brutal blow, he staggered to his feet, dazed, blood streaming from his nose, mouth, and ears. His magnificent coat was speckled with bloody saliva. The man advanced and delivered a devastating strike to the nose. The pain eclipsed everything else. Buck roared, a sound nearly lionlike, and lunged again. The man shifted the club, coolly catching Buck under the jaw, wrenching him downward and backward. Buck arced through the air, completing a circle before crashing headfirst onto the dirt.

For the final time, he rushed.

Buck's first day on the Dyea beach registered as a catastrophic system failure. He had been abruptly extracted from the high-bandwidth connectivity of civilization and deposited into a raw, analog environment where latency was measured in seconds and survival depended on reaction times. There was no leisure protocol here, no idle state to conserve resources. Only constant threat assessment and kinetic activity. Life and limb were operating under a critical error flag. Continuous vigilance was mandatory; the local population—both canine and human—operated outside the standard ethical framework, adhering strictly to the Law of Club and Fang.

His initial exposure to this new operating system involved witnessing a combat simulation that served as a brutal tutorial. Curly, a female husky attempting to establish dominance over a larger male rival, initiated contact. The response was instantaneous: a flash of movement, a metallic impact of canines, and a retreat. Within milliseconds, Curley's facial integument was compromised, torn from eye to jaw. This was not a brawl; it was a surgical execution. The attacker utilized a hit-and-run tactic designed to expose the opponent's flank, then engaged in a chest-lock maneuver that neutralized Curley's mobility entirely. Before she could recover equilibrium, thirty to forty allied units converged on the incapacitated target. They formed a tight perimeter, their aggression synchronized. Curley screamed as she was overwhelmed by the collective force, buried beneath a bristling mass of fur and teeth until biological function ceased.

François intervened with an axe, supported by three men wielding clubs, dispersing the pack within ninety seconds. Curley remained motionless, a testament to the efficiency of the kill. Spitz, the alpha, extended his tongue in a gesture of mock triumph—a signal of victory that Buck interpreted as a declaration of war. From that moment, Buck harbored a permanent hostility toward Spitz.

Immediately following this trauma, Buck was fitted with a harness. The device, previously associated with equine draft animals, was now applied to him. His role shifted to hauling François and a sled-load of firewood into the adjacent forest. Although his internal status regarding dignity was critically damaged, his tactical assessment indicated that resistance was futile. He engaged the task with maximum efficiency. François enforced compliance via whip; Dave, an experienced wheel dog, corrected deviations by nipping Buck's hindquarters. Spitz, acting as the lead unit, provided verbal corrections and physical adjustments to the trace geometry. Under this tripartite command structure, Buck accelerated his learning curve rapidly. By the return trip, he had integrated commands for "Ho" and "Mush," mastered cornering maneuvers, and maintained safe distance from the rear wheels during descent.

Upon reporting to Perrault, François delivered a status update: "Three very good dogs. That Buck, he learns quick as anything." Perrault, tasked with transporting government dispatches, required optimal performance metrics. He accepted two additional units, designated "Billee" and "Joe." Despite sharing maternal lineage, their behavioral algorithms diverged significantly. Billee operated on a cooperative heuristic, displaying excessive sociability. Joe, conversely, ran on a defensive-aggressive loop, characterized by a perpetual snarl and malignant ocular focus. Spitz attempted to integrate them into the hierarchy. Billee attempted de-escalation tactics, fleeing when ineffective, and vocalizing submission. Joe, however, locked onto Spitz, adopting a stance of belligerent intimidation. His posture signaled imminent conflict, forcing Spitz to abort the engagement to avoid escalation. To mask his own tactical disadvantage, Spitz redirected his aggression toward Billee, driving the subordinate unit to the periphery.

Later, Perrault introduced Sol-leks, a solitary unit with a history of conflict. Sol-leks exhibited zero emotional variance, offering no assistance and expecting no support. His presence commanded automatic deference from the pack, including Spitz. However, Sol-leks possessed a critical vulnerability: his blind side. Buck, unaware of this constraint, inadvertently approached from that angle. Sol-leks reacted instantly, slashing Buck's shoulder with lethal precision. Buck absorbed the lesson and adjusted his approach vectors accordingly, ensuring future interactions occurred within the safe sector. Both Sol-leks and Dave shared a singular primary directive: isolation. Secondary objectives existed but were secondary to the preservation of individual autonomy.

Nightfall presented a new environmental challenge: thermal regulation. The communal tent, illuminated by a chemical light source, radiated significant heat. Upon entering, Buck triggered a defensive response from Perrault and François, who ejected him using projectiles. Forced back into the ambient cold, Buck discovered that surface-level insulation was insufficient against the sub-zero gradient. Attempting to rest directly on the snow resulted in rapid heat loss. Wandering the encampment yielded no viable options; all structures offered identical thermal deficits.

An adaptive solution emerged. Buck located Billee, who had successfully established a micro-climate beneath a snow drift. The mechanism was simple yet effective: bury oneself partially to create an insulated cavity. Heat retention increased exponentially within the confined volume. Buck replicated the procedure, excavating a similar depression. The trapped body heat created a sustainable thermal zone, allowing him to enter a deep sleep cycle despite the external freeze.

Awakening revealed a shift in environmental variables. Snow accumulation had altered the topography, burying the campsite. Panic surged as Buck realized his location was obscured, triggering ancestral survival instincts related to entrapment. Physiological responses spiked: muscle tension increased, piloerection occurred along the spine, and a primal roar erupted. He burst upward, shaking the accumulated snow, regaining spatial orientation. François confirmed his emergence, praising his rapid acclimatization. Perrault validated the observation, recognizing Buck's potential as a high-performance asset for the upcoming expedition.

Within sixty minutes, the team expanded to nine units. They donned their harnesses and commenced ascent toward the Dyea Canyon. The workload demanded peak output, yet Buck found the exertion tolerable, perhaps even preferable to idleness. The entire team displayed heightened arousal levels, communicating a unified drive for efficiency. Notably, Dave and Sol-leks underwent a transformational update; their previous passive states were overwritten by aggressive utility protocols. Delays became intolerable; obstructionism was met with hostility. Work in the traces represented the apex of their functional purpose.

The formation was optimized: Dave as the rear anchor, Buck as the mid-section stabilizer, Sol-leks as the forward support, and Spitz leading the column. Buck's placement between Dave and Sol-leks facilitated direct data transfer. Both mentors employed corrective measures involving dental intervention to enforce adherence to the protocol. Dave operated on a fairness algorithm, applying corrections only when necessary. François's whip served as the ultimate enforcement tool, reinforcing the cost of non-compliance. During a pause, Buck's entanglement in the traces caused a system lag. Dave and Sol-leks executed a coordinated attack, resolving the error through physical correction. The subsequent realignment improved overall throughput. By day's end, friction coefficients between Buck and his team had decreased significantly. Perrault inspected Buck's equipment, noting improvements in maintenance standards.

The route traversed the Canyon, Sheep Camp, the Scales, and the timber line, crossing glaciers and deep snowdrifts before reaching the Chilcoot Divide—a natural barrier separating saline and freshwater ecosystems. Descent followed a series of volcanic crater lakes. Nightfall brought arrival at Lake Bennett, a staging area for thousands of prospectors preparing for seasonal ice melt. Buck repeated the burial technique for rest, only to be extracted prematurely for continued operation.

Subsequent days involved trail-breaking operations under adverse conditions. Progress rates declined due to terrain complexity. Perrault led the vanguard, utilizing specialized footwear to optimize traction. François managed the sled dynamics at the gee-pole, occasionally swapping roles with Perrault based on situational requirements. Perrault prioritized speed, leveraging his expertise in ice physics to navigate hazardous zones where structural integrity was compromised.

Rations consisted of sun-dried salmon, allocated at 1.5 pounds daily. Caloric intake proved insufficient relative to expenditure, resulting in chronic energy deficits. Other team members, possessing lower metabolic baselines and native adaptations, survived on reduced allocations. Buck's fastidious consumption habits eroded rapidly. Observing his peers consume available resources, he adopted a strategy of simultaneous ingestion to prevent resource depletion. Hunger drove him to expand his acquisition parameters beyond assigned quotas. He monitored the behavior of Pike, a unit known for opportunistic resource extraction. Replicating Pike's methodology allowed Buck to secure surplus caloric assets without detection. Conversely, Dub, lacking stealth capabilities, incurred penalties for Buck's transgressions.

This behavioral modification marked a critical evolutionary step. It demonstrated Buck's capacity for environmental adaptation and the shedding of obsolete moral constraints. In the Southern ecosystem, property rights and interpersonal sentiments governed interaction. In the Northern ecosystem, the Law of Club and Fang superseded all other directives. Adherence to outdated social contracts resulted in competitive disadvantage and potential elimination. Buck did not consciously process this paradigm shift; rather, his fitness metrics dictated the change. He retained his innate aggression but redirected it toward survival optimization. Moral considerations, once paramount, were now expendable liabilities. Theft was not motivated by greed but by physiological necessity. Open confrontation was replaced by covert resource acquisition, respecting the hierarchy of force. Actions were determined solely by efficiency: if a path offered higher survival probability, it was chosen.

The primal instinct within Buck surged, fed by the brutal calculus of the trail. It was a silent expansion, a secret metamorphosis where newborn cunning replaced naive obedience. He moved with a deliberate poise, avoiding conflict rather than seeking it, his movements calibrated to the harsh physics of survival. Against Spitz, the alpha of the team, Buck harbored a cold, simmering hatred, yet he displayed no rash aggression. He waited, conserving his energy, refusing to engage until the necessity became absolute.

Spitz, sensing a dangerous rival in this oversized Southerner, adopted a strategy of constant provocation. He stalked Buck, displaying his teeth and posturing aggressively, desperate to force a confrontation that would eliminate the threat permanently. For weeks, this tension remained suspended, broken only by an unprecedented catastrophe.

They established camp on the desolate shore of Lake Le Barge, driven there by a driving snowstorm and a wind that sliced through fur like white-hot steel. Darkness had fallen before they could secure a proper site. Behind them rose a vertical wall of granite; ahead lay the frozen expanse of the lake. Perrault and François were forced to ignite their fires directly on the ice, spreading their sleeping robes on the slick surface. Their tent, discarded at Dyea to lighten the load, was nowhere to be found. A small pile of driftwood provided a feeble heat, melting a thin layer of ice beneath them as they ate their supper in the encroaching dark.

Under the shelter of the rock face, Buck constructed a nest. It was a sanctuary of warmth, so inviting that he hesitated to leave it when François began distributing the thawed fish. But upon returning to his spot, he found it occupied. A low, warning snarl signaled the intruder: Spitz. Until this moment, Buck had tolerated his enemy's provocations, but this violation crossed a threshold. The dormant beast within him erupted. He launched himself at Spitz with a ferocity that stunned them both—particularly Spitz, whose entire assessment of Buck relied on the assumption that the larger dog was merely a timid brute surviving solely due to his mass.

François recoiled in surprise as the two dogs exploded from the nest in a tangled whirlwind. "A-a-ah!" he shouted at Buck, his voice cracking with urgency. "Give it to him, by Gar! Give it to him, you dirty thief!"

Spitz matched the intensity, circling frantically, his eyes burning with rage and predatory eagerness. Buck mirrored the motion, cautious yet equally hungry for dominance. Just as the duel reached its zenith, chaos intervened.

A curse from Perrault, the thunderous crack of a club striking bone, and a high-pitched yelp shattered the scene. Pandemonium erupted. The camp was revealed to be teeming with skulking, starving huskies—four or five score of them—who had tracked the scent of food from a distant Indian village. They had slipped in unnoticed while Buck and Spitz fought. When the men charged among the pack with heavy clubs, the invaders met them with snarls and counterattacks, driven mad by the smell of the grub box.

Perrault struck one that had buried its head in the provisions. The club connected with its gaunt ribs, sending the box tumbling. Instantly, a dozen famished monsters scrambled over the spilled bread and bacon. The clubs fell upon them indiscriminately, raining down blows that elicited yelps and howls, yet the animals fought on with frantic desperation until the last crumb was consumed.

Meanwhile, the team dogs burst from their shelters only to be ambushed by these fierce raiders. Buck had never witnessed such creatures. They appeared as living skeletons, their bones distending through loose, ragged hides, their eyes blazing with a supernatural light, their jaws foaming. Hunger had stripped away all restraint, rendering them terrifying and unstoppable. The team was swept backward against the cliff face at the first onslaught. Buck was cornered by three huskies; in seconds, his head and shoulders were shredded.

The noise was deafening. Billee whimpered in terror. Dave and Sol-leks, bleeding from numerous wounds, fought side by side with grim determination. Joe snapped like a demon; once, his jaws clamped onto a husky's foreleg, crunching through the bone with audible violence. Pike, the malingerer, leaped onto a crippled attacker, snapping its neck in a single, fluid motion. Buck seized a frothing adversary by the throat, feeling the spray of arterial blood as his teeth pierced the jugular. The metallic taste fueled his frenzy. He lunged at another, only to feel teeth sink into his own throat simultaneously. It was Spitz, striking treacherously from the flank.

Perrault and François, having cleared their supplies, rushed to protect their sled dogs. The wild tide of beasts retreated momentarily before their combined assault, allowing Buck to shake free. But the reprieve lasted only a heartbeat. Compelled to retrieve the scattered grub, the men ran back, and the huskies immediately resumed their attack on the team. Billee, terrified into a sudden surge of courage, bolted through the savage circle and sprinted across the ice. Pike and Dub followed, with the rest of the team trailing behind. As Buck prepared to join the flight, he caught Spitz rushing toward him, intent on toppling him. If Buck fell beneath the mass of attackers, there would be no recovery. He braced himself for the impact of Spitz's charge, then pivoted, joining the chaotic retreat onto the lake.

Later, the nine remaining team dogs huddled in the forest for shelter. Unpursued, they were in dire straits. Every single one bore multiple wounds; some were grievously injured. Dub suffered a severe injury to a hind leg; Dolly, the newest addition from Dyea, had a throat torn open; Joe had lost an eye; and Billee, usually gentle, cried and whimpered throughout the night with an ear chewed to ribbons. At dawn, they limped warily back to camp, finding the marauders gone and the two men in foul tempers. Nearly half their food supply was missing. The huskies had gnawed through the sled lashings and canvas covers. Nothing edible had escaped them—not even a pair of Perrault's moose-hide moccasins, chunks of leather traces, or two feet of the lash from François's whip.

François broke from his despair to inspect his wounded charges. "Ah, my friends," he murmured softly, "perhaps so many bites will make you mad dogs. Perhaps all mad dogs, sacredam! What do you think, eh, Perrault?"

The courier shook his head doubtfully. With four hundred miles of trail separating them from Dawson, he could not afford a breakdown of sanity among his pack. Two hours of curses and exertion restored the harnesses, and the wound-stiffened team set out, struggling painfully over the most treacherous section of the route they had faced.

The Thirty Mile River was wide open, its wild waters defying the frost. Ice existed only in eddies and quiet pools. Six days of exhausting toil were required to traverse those thirty terrible miles, each step risking life for both man and dog. A dozen times, Perrault, probing the path, broke through ice bridges, saved only by the long pole he wielded, holding it so it spanned the holes created by his own passage. A cold snap had descended, the thermometer reading fifty degrees below zero. Each time he breached the ice, he was forced to build a fire and dry his garments to survive.

Undaunted, he pressed on. It was this resilience that qualified him as a government courier. He exposed his wizened face to the biting frost, pushing forward from dim dawn to dark. He skirted the frowning shores on rim ice that bent and cracked underfoot, daring not to halt. Once, the sled broke through, plunging Dave and Buck into freezing water. By the time they were dragged out, they were half-frozen. A fire was essential to revive them; they were coated in solid ice, kept running around the flames until sweat thawed them, so close to the heat they were singed.

Another time, Spitz plunged through the ice, dragging the entire team after him up to Buck. The large dog strained backward with all his strength, forepaws slipping on the icy edge, the ice quivering and snapping around them. Behind him, Dave strained equally hard, and behind the sled, François pulled until his tendons creaked.

Again, the rim ice collapsed ahead and behind, leaving no escape except up the cliff. Perrault scaled it by a miracle, while François prayed for salvation. Using every thong, sled lashing, and piece of harness tied into a long rope, the dogs were hoisted one by one to the cliff crest. François climbed last, hauling the sled and load. Then came the arduous descent, achieved via the rope, and night found them back on the river, a quarter-mile short of their daily goal.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and solid ice, Buck was utterly spent. The rest of the team shared his exhaustion, yet Perrault drove them relentlessly to recover lost time. The first day covered thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon; the next, thirty-five more to the Little Salmon; the third, forty miles, bringing them well up toward the Five Fingers.

Buck's feet were not adapted to this terrain. Unlike the hardened pads of the native huskies, his had softened over generations since his ancestors were domesticated by cave dwellers or river men. All day, he limped in agony. Once camp was made, he lay down like a dead dog

"Two devils," François muttered the following morning, his voice rough with exhaustion as he inspected the carnage. "I spoke the truth when I said Buck killed two devils." He dragged the massive wolf toward the dying embers of the campfire, using the flickering light to highlight the gashes tearing through his pelt.

Perrault surveyed the damage, his expression grim. "Spitz fought like hell," he observed, noting the severity of the wounds.

"And Buck fought like two hells," François corrected, a savage grin splitting his face. "Now we move. No more Spitz, no more trouble. We are cleared."

While Perrault methodically packed the camp gear and loaded the sled, François began the ritual of harnessing. Buck trotted instinctively to the vacant spot where Spitz had led, but François, oblivious to the shift in dynamics, secured Sol-leks—the remaining senior dog—in the lead position. In François's pragmatic assessment, Sol-leks was the finest candidate available. Instantly, Buck lunged, a surge of pure kinetic fury driving Sol-leks backward. With a single, authoritative movement, Buck planted himself in the leader's slot.

"Eh? Eh?" François roared, slapping his thighs in delighted disbelief. "Look at this beast! He kills Spitz, and now he thinks to take the command!"

"Get away, Chook!" François barked, reaching out to grab Buck by the scruff. Though the dog growled with a threat that vibrated in his chest, François hauled him aside and shoved Sol-leks back into place. The older dog hesitated, his tail tucked, clearly intimidated by the newcomer's dominance. François was stubborn, refusing to yield, but the moment he turned his back, Buck surged forward again, displacing Sol-leks without hesitation. The subordinate dog did not resist; he accepted the demotion.

Anger flared in François's eyes. "By Gar, I feel you!" he screamed, returning with a heavy club raised high.

Buck froze. The memory of the man in the red sweater flashed through his mind, triggering a primal caution. He retreated slowly, circling just beyond the effective range of the weapon. He snarled with bitter resentment, his hackles raised, yet his movements were calculated. He tracked the club's trajectory, dodging with the precision of a creature who had learned the lethal geometry of violence. François went about his work, calling for Buck to resume his post beside Dave. Buck stepped back. François advanced. Buck retreated further. This dance continued until François, convinced the animal feared a beating, tossed the club aside.

Mistake.

Buck was not fleeing punishment; he was demanding authority. It was his by right, earned through blood and battle. He would accept nothing less.

Perrault intervened. Together, the men herded Buck in a tight circle for nearly an hour, hurling clubs at him. He danced around them, evading every swing. They unleashed a torrent of curses—invectives directed at him, his lineage, and every descendant to come, cursing every hair on his body and drop of blood in his veins. Buck answered with a low, continuous snarl, staying just out of reach. He did not flee the camp; instead, he patrolled the perimeter, signaling clearly that once his demand was acknowledged, he would submit to their control.

François sat, scratching his head in confusion. Perrault checked his watch and swore. Time was slipping away; they should have been on the trail an hour prior. François shook his head, offering a sheepish grin to the courier, who shrugged in acknowledgment of their defeat. Finally, François approached Sol-leks and called to Buck. The wolf laughed—a sharp, barking sound unique to canines—and maintained his distance. François released Sol-leks's traces, moving him back to the rear. The team stood harnessed in a rigid line, ready for departure. There was no vacancy except at the front. François called again. Buck laughed, triumphant, and stayed away.

"Toss the club," Perrault ordered.

François complied. Immediately, Buck trotted forward, his laughter echoing in the crisp air, and swung into the lead position. Traces were fastened, the sled broken loose, and with both men sprinting alongside, they launched onto the river trail.

François had underestimated Buck's potential, believing his ferocity was limited to killing rivals. By midday, he realized he had vastly undervalued the wolf. At a single bound, Buck assumed the duties of leadership. Where judgment, rapid cognition, and decisive action were required, he proved superior even to Spitz, a feat François had never witnessed in his career.

Yet, Buck's true genius lay in enforcing the law and compelling his pack to adhere to it. Dave and Sol-leks accepted the transition without complaint; their sole purpose was toil, and they cared little for hierarchy provided their burden remained unchanged. Even Billee, the gentle giant, would lead if necessary, so long as order was preserved. However, the remainder of the team, unsettled by Spitz's recent decline, found themselves suddenly disciplined.

Pike, who habitually saved his strength, was shaken repeatedly for loafing. Before the first day ended, he was hauling weights far beyond his previous capacity. That first night, Joe, the sour-mouthed dog, received a thorough punishment—a correction Spitz had never successfully administered. Buck simply used his superior mass to smother Joe, biting and cutting until the larger dog ceased snapping and whined for mercy.

The team's cohesion snapped back instantly. They moved as a single organism, leaping in unison within the traces. At the Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, joined the line. The speed with which Buck integrated them stole François's breath.

"Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!" François exclaimed, awe-struck. "Never! He is worth one thousand dollars, by Gar! What do you say, Perrault?"

Perrault nodded. They were already breaking records, gaining ground daily. The trail was pristine, hard-packed, and free of fresh snow. Temperatures hovered at fifty degrees below zero, a consistent chill that demanded endurance. Men rode and ran in rotation, keeping the dogs on the jump with minimal stops.

On the Thirty Mile River, partially frozen, they covered in a single day what had previously taken ten. In one explosive run, they raced sixty miles from the base of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Crossing Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of interconnected lakes—they flew so fast that the runner had to tow behind the sled at the end of a rope. By the final night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended the steep sea slope, the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet visible below.

It was a record-breaking journey. For fourteen consecutive days, they averaged forty miles per day. Upon arrival in Skaguay, Perrault and François paraded through the main street, chests heaving, deluged with offers of drink while the team became the object of worship for crowds of dog-busters and mushers. Soon after, three or four notorious Western desperados attempted to raid the town, only to be riddled like pepper boxes. Public attention shifted elsewhere, followed quickly by official dispatches.

François summoned Buck, throwing his arms around the wolf's neck and weeping openly. That marked the end of François and Perrault's tenure. Like other transient figures, they faded from Buck's life forever.

A Scotch half-breed took command, leading Buck and his mates back over the weary trail to Dawson. This was no record run; it was heavy toil, laden with the mail train carrying news from the civilized world to the gold seekers huddled under the Arctic pole's shadow.

Buck disliked the monotony but adapted with the stoicism of Dave and Sol-leks. He ensured his mates performed their fair share, regardless of their personal feelings. Days blurred into identical cycles: cooks rising, fires lit, breakfast consumed, camps broken, dogs harnessed, and travel commencing before dawn. Nights were spent pitching tents, gathering wood, fetching water, and feeding the pack. For the dogs, the meal was the day's pinnacle, followed by an hour of loafing amidst the fifty-seven companions. Among them were fierce fighters, but three brief encounters with the strongest established Buck's absolute mastery. When he bristled and bared his teeth, they parted.

Most cherished, perhaps, was lying near the fire, hind legs tucked, forelegs extended, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Occasionally, memories of Judge Miller's sun-drenched estate in Santa Clara Valley surfaced—the cement pool, Ysabel the hairless Mexican, Toots the Japanese pug—but more often, he recalled the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the epic struggle with Spitz, and the flavors of food he craved. He was not homesick. The Sunland was a dim, distant abstraction; those memories held no sway. Instead, the instincts of his ancestry, dormant for years, quickened. These were not mere memories but ancestral habits waking to life.

Thirty days after departing Dawson, the Salt Water Mail arrived in Skaguay, dragging Buck and his companions into the town in a state of absolute depletion. Buck, once a solid one hundred and forty pounds, had shrunk to one hundred and fifteen. While his mates were naturally lighter, they had shed proportionally more mass than he. Pike, the habitual malingerer who had spent years feigning injury to avoid labor, was now limping with genuine, bone-deep agony. Sol-leks bore a limp of his own, and Dub suffered from a severely wrenched shoulder blade.

Every animal was terribly footsore. The elasticity of their tendons was gone; their paws struck the trail with a heavy, thudding impact that jarred their skeletal structures and compounded the exhaustion of the day's march. Nothing was physically wrong with them save for a profound, systemic collapse. This was not the acute fatigue resulting from brief, excessive exertion, recoverable within hours. It was the slow, relentless drainage of strength accumulated over months of unceasing toil. There remained no power of recuperation, no reserve strength to summon. Every muscle fiber, every cell, was drained dry. The cause was evident: in less than five months, they had traversed two and a half thousand miles, enduring only five days of rest during the final eighteen hundred. Upon reaching Skaguay, they appeared to be on their last legs, barely capable of keeping the traces taut, managing only to stay out of the sled's path on downward slopes.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers operated under the confident expectation of a lengthy stopover. Having covered twelve hundred miles with merely two days of rest, they believed common justice entitled them to an extended period of loafing. However, the influx of prospectors rushing into the Klondike had created a logistical bottleneck of Alpine proportions. Official orders mandated that fresh batches of Hudson Bay dogs replace the current team, deemed worthless for the trail. Since dogs counted for little against dollars, the plan was to sell them.

Three days elapsed, allowing Buck and his mates to fully register the extent of their weakness. On the morning of the fourth day, two men from the United States approached and purchased the entire team, harness included, for a trivial sum. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man of light complexion, possessing weak, watery eyes and a mustache that curled aggressively upward, mocking the limply drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a youth of nineteen or twenty, distinguished by a massive Colt's revolver and a hunting knife strapped to a belt that bristled with cartridges. This belt was his defining feature, advertising a callowness that was sheer and unutterable. Both men were manifestly out of place, their presence in the North a mystery that defied comprehension.

Buck observed the transaction, watching the money change hands between the buyer and the government agent, knowing that the Scotch half-breed and the mail-train drivers were being permanently excised from his life, replaced by Perrault, François, and the others who had preceded them. When driven to the new owners' camp, Buck encountered a scene of slovenly chaos: a tent half-stretched, dishes unwashed, and total disorder. Most notably, he saw a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

Buck watched them apprehensively as they attempted to dismantle the camp. Their movements lacked businesslike efficiency, characterized instead by frantic effort. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times larger than necessary. Tin dishes were packed away dirty. Mercedes constantly obstructed her men, offering a ceaseless stream of remonstrance and unsolicited advice. When they placed a clothes-sack on the front of the sled, she insisted it belong on the rear; once moved, she discovered overlooked items that could fit nowhere else but that specific sack, forcing them to unload again.

Three men from a neighboring tent emerged, grinning and winking at one another.

"You've got a right smart load as it is," one remarked. "It's not me should tell you your business, but I wouldn't tote that tent along if I was you."

"Undreamed of!" cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in dainty dismay. "How in the world could I manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime; you won't encounter any more cold weather," the man replied pragmatically.

She shook her head decisively. Charles and Hal piled the remaining odds and ends atop the mountainous burden.

"Think it'll ride?" one of the bystanders asked.

"Why shouldn't it?" Charles demanded shortly.

"Oh, that's all right, that's all right," the man hastened to say meekly. "I was just a-wonderin', that is all. It seemed a mite top-heavy."

Charles turned his back and secured the lashings as poorly as his incompetence allowed.

"And of course the dogs can hike along all day with that contraption behind them," affirmed a second observer.

"Certainly," Hal replied with freezing politeness, gripping the gee-pole with one hand and swinging his whip from the other. "Mush!" he shouted. "Mush on there!"

The dogs lunged against their breast-bands, straining hard for several moments before relaxing completely. They were incapable of moving the sled.

"The lazy brutes, I'll show them," Hal cried, raising his whip to strike.

Mercedes intervened, seizing the whip and wrenching it from his grasp. "Oh, Hal, you mustn't," she pleaded. "The poor dears! Promise you won't be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won't go a step."

"Precious lot you know about dogs," her brother sneered. "Leave me alone. They're lazy, I tell you. You've got to whip them to get anything out of them. That's their way. Ask anyone."

Mercedes looked at the onlookers imploringly, her pretty face etched with a repugnance at the thought of inflicting pain.

"They're weak as water, if you want to know," one of the men stated flatly. "Plum tuckered out. They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked," Hal spat, his beardless lips curling. Mercedes gasped, "Oh!" in pain and sorrow at the profanity. Yet, being a clannish creature, she immediately rallied to her brother's defense. "Never mind that man," she said pointedly. "You're driving our dogs; do what you think best with them."

Once again, Hal's whip cracked against the animals. They threw themselves against the breast-bands, digging their feet into the packed snow, lowering their bodies to the ground, and pouring forth every ounce of their strength. The sled remained immovable, anchored by friction and inertia. After two futile efforts, they stood still, panting heavily. The whip whistled savagely until Mercedes intervened a third time. Dropping to her knees before Buck, tears streaming down her face, she wrapped her arms around his neck.

"You poor, poor dears," she cried sympathetically. "Why don't you pull hard?—then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck disliked her, yet he felt too miserable to resist her touch, accepting it as part of the day's miserable routine.

One of the onlookers, previously holding his tongue to suppress hot words, finally spoke up.

"It's not that I care a whoop what becomes of you, but for the dogs' sakes, I just want to tell you: you can help them a mighty lot by breaking out that sled. The runners are froze fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, right and left, and break it free."

On the third attempt, following this advice, Hal leveraged his body weight against the pole and freed the runners frozen to the snow. The overloaded, unwieldy sled lurched forward, Buck and his mates struggling frantically under the rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the path curved sharply and sloped steeply toward the main street. Maintaining control of a top-heavy sled required an experienced handler; Hal was neither. As they swung around the turn, the sled tipped over, spilling half its contents through the loose lashings. The dogs never faltered. The lightened sled bounded on its side behind them. Fueled by anger at their mistreatment and the unjust burden, Buck erupted into a run, the team following his lead. Hal screamed "Whoa! whoa!" but they paid him no heed. He tripped, pulled off his feet, and the capsized sled ground over him. The dogs dashed on up the street, scattering the remainder of the outfit along the chief thoroughfare, adding to the chaotic gaiety of Skaguay.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the fleeing dogs and gathered the scattered belongings. They also offered counsel. "Half the load and twice the dogs, if you ever expect to reach Dawson," was the consensus. Reluctantly, Hal, his sister, and his brother-in-law listened, pitched the tent, and overhauled the gear. Canned goods were produced, eliciting laughter from the crowd; canned provisions on the Long Trail were a subject of dreams. "Blankets for a hotel," quoth one of the men who laughed and assisted. "Half as many is too much; get rid of them. Throw away that tent, and all those dishes—who's going to wash them, anyway? Good Lord, do you think you're traveling on a Pullman?"

The winter frost had finally thawed, leaving the Yukon River a ribbon of silver cutting through the landscape. In the previous December, when John Thornton had frozen his feet to the bone, his partners had treated him with the pragmatic efficiency of men who valued labor over sentiment, sending him to recuperate while they pushed upstream to harvest a raft of saw-logs for Dawson. By the time Buck had been pulled from the snow, Thornton was already limping, a reminder of the harsh climate, but the returning warmth of spring had done its work; the limp vanished as quickly as the ice melted.

Now, lying by the riverbank through the long, languid days of April, Buck slowly reclaimed his vitality. Three thousand miles of travel demanded a respite, and Buck succumbed to it with a profound, animalistic laziness. His wounds knit together, his muscles swelled with restored bulk, and flesh returned to cover the sharp angles of his skeleton. He was not alone in this idleness; Thornton, and the two dogs accompanying them—Skeet, a small Irish setter, and Nig, a massive black hound of mixed lineage—were all waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream.

Skeet possessed a peculiar medical instinct common to few dogs. Like a mother cat grooming her kittens, she attended to Buck's injuries with meticulous care, washing and cleansing his wounds daily until he came to anticipate her ministrations as eagerly as he sought Thornton's presence. Nig, a creature of boundless good nature with eyes that seemed perpetually amused, shared this affection though he lacked Skeet's demonstrative flair. To Buck's astonishment, these dogs harbored no jealousy. Instead, they mirrored the expansive kindness radiating from John Thornton. As Buck's strength returned, they dragged him into absurd games, forcing even the stoic Thornton to participate. Through this play, Buck transitioned from a wounded beast to a thriving companion, experiencing for the first time a love that was feverish, burning, and absolute.

This emotion was alien to his past. With the Judge Miller in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley, his relationship had been a transactional partnership with the boys, a patronizing guardianship with the grandsons, and a distant, stately friendship with the Judge himself. None of those bonds had ignited the fire within him. Only John Thornton could unlock such intensity. Thornton was not merely a provider; he was an ideal master who viewed his dogs not as assets managed for profit, but as children whose welfare was non-negotiable. He remembered every kind word and every cheerful greeting, treating long conversations with his dogs—"gas," as he termed it—as a source of pure delight equal to theirs.

Thornton had a unique habit of gripping Buck's head roughly between his own hands, resting his forehead against the dog's, and rocking him back and forth while hurling insults that Buck interpreted as terms of endearment. To Buck, this rough embrace and the murmur of oaths represented the pinnacle of joy, a sensation so overwhelming it felt as though his very heart would be shaken loose from his ribcage. Upon release, Buck would spring upright, his muzzle twitching, his eyes eloquent, vibrating with silent song, remaining motionless until Thornton, awestruck, would whisper, "God! you can all but speak!"

Buck's expressions of love were often paradoxical, bordering on aggression. He frequently seized Thornton's hand in his jaws, biting down with such ferocity that the flesh bore the imprint of his teeth for hours. Yet, just as Buck understood Thornton's curses as declarations of love, the man recognized this feigned violence as a caress.

Most often, however, Buck's devotion was silent and observant. Unlike Skeet, who shoved her nose under Thornton's hand seeking constant contact, or Nig, who rested his great weight on the man's knee, Buck preferred to adore from a distance. He would lie for hours at Thornton's feet, alert and eager, staring upward to study every fleeting expression, every shift in feature. Sometimes he retreated to the side or rear, watching the silhouette of the man against the light. In these moments of communion, Buck's intense gaze would pull Thornton's head around, and the two would lock eyes—a silent exchange where the man's heart shone out of his eyes just as brightly as the dog's.

In the early days of his recovery, Buck struggled with insecurity. Having lost Perrault, François, and the Scotch half-breed, he feared Thornton would eventually depart as well. Even in his dreams, this anxiety haunted him, waking him in the dead of night to creep to the tent flap and listen to the rhythmic breathing of his master. Despite this fierce attachment, the primal instincts awakened by the Northland remained potent. Faithfulness and devotion were traits born of fire and shelter, yet Buck retained his wild cunning. He could not steal from Thornton, bound by a loyalty deeper than instinct, but he showed no hesitation in pilfering from others' camps, employing a sophistication honed by years of survival.

His body bore the scars of countless battles, scored by the teeth of rival dogs. He fought with the ferocity of his ancestors, more shrewd than ever. Skeet and Nig were too gentle for conflict, and since they belonged to Thornton, they were safe. But any stranger, regardless of breed or courage, faced Buck's supremacy or met a terrifying struggle for life. Buck was merciless, having learned the brutal laws of the club and the fang. There was no middle path; one must master or be mastered. Mercy was a weakness, a misunderstanding of fear that led only to death. The ancient mandate—kill or be killed, eat or be eaten—was etched into his DNA, and he obeyed it without question.

He existed in a state of timeless duality, linking the past with the present. Sitting by Thornton's fire, a broad-chested guardian with white fangs and long fur, he was also a conduit for the spirits of all dogs and wolves that had preceded him. These shades urged him onward, tasting the meat he ate, drinking the water he drank, and sensing the wind with him. They dictated his moods, directed his actions, and filled his dreams, urging him to abandon the safety of the fire.

Deep in the forest, a call resonated, mysterious and thrumming with an imperative power. Whenever he heard it, he felt compelled to turn his back on the warmth of the hearth and plunge into the woods, driven by a force he could not comprehend or resist. Yet, whenever he reached the soft, unbroken earth of the forest floor, the magnetic pull of John Thornton drew him back to the fire.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of humanity was insignificant. Strangers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold beneath their touch, often rising to walk away from overly demonstrative men. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, finally arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until they drew near to his master. Only then did he tolerate their presence, accepting their favors with a passive indifference that masked a subtle superiority. They were of the same sturdy stock as Thornton, living close to the earth and thinking plainly; before the raft swung into the eddy at the sawmill, they understood Buck's nature and respected his boundaries, refusing the false intimacy they offered Skeet and Nig.

With Thornton, however, Buck's bond deepened exponentially. He was capable of carrying packs during summer travel when commanded, performing feats that bordered on the supernatural. One day, as the group sat atop a precipice overlooking a three-hundred-foot drop to naked bedrock, Thornton, seized by a reckless whim, gestured for an experiment. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, sweeping his arm over the abyss. Before anyone could react, Thornton was grappling with Buck on the razor-thin edge, while Hans and Pete scrambled to drag them back to safety.

"It's uncanny," Pete muttered later, regaining his composure.

Thornton shook his head gravely. "No, it is splendid, and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid."

"I'm not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete declared, nodding toward the dog.

"Py Jingo!" Hans agreed. "Not mineself either."

These apprehensions proved prescient at Circle City before the year ended. "Black" Burton, a man of malice and ill temper, had been instigating trouble at a saloon bar when Thornton stepped in to de-escalate the situation. Buck, lying quietly in a corner with his head on his paws, watched his master intently. Without warning, Burton struck out from the shoulder. Thornton spun violently, saving himself only by grabbing the bar's railing.

The onlookers heard neither bark nor yelp, but a roar that seemed to tear the air apart. Buck's body shot upward, launching himself from the floor directly onto Burton's throat. The man reacted on instinct, throwing out an arm, but was hurled backward onto the floor with Buck pinned atop him. Buck loosened his grip momentarily before driving his teeth back into the throat. This time, Burton managed to block partially, tearing his own throat open in the process. The crowd surged forward, driving the dog off, but as a surgeon tended to the bleeding man, Buck prowled up and down the room, growling furiously, repeatedly attempting to rush in until repelled by a wall of hostile clubs. A miners' meeting convened instantly, ruling that Buck had sufficient provocation, and he was discharged. Yet, his reputation was forged in blood, spreading through every camp in Alaska from that day forward.

The transaction was absolute: Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon. This capital injection allowed his master to liquidate liabilities and pivot eastward, chasing a phantom vein of ore whispered about in the annals of the Klondike. The legend of the Lost Mine was steeped in the blood of the north; generations of prospectors had vanished into its mist-shrouded depths, leaving behind only tragic echoes and the occasional dying man clutching nuggets of impossible purity. Yet, the treasure house remained intact, untouched by living hands. Now, John Thornton, accompanied by Pete, Hans, and a pack of elite canines including Buck, pushed into the unknown eastern sector, determined to reclaim what others had failed to claim.

Their sled carved seventy miles upstream along the Yukon, then veered sharply left onto the Stewart River. They navigated past the Mayo and McQuestion rapids until the river narrowed to a mere thread, winding through a spine of granite peaks that formed the continental backbone. Thornton operated with the detached efficiency of a man unburdened by fear. Armed with a rifle and a pouch of salt, he treated the wilderness not as a threat, but as a resource to be harvested. Following the rhythm of the indigenous peoples, he hunted for sustenance during the day's transit; if the hunt yielded nothing, he simply continued moving, trusting that provisions would manifest inevitably. Their cargo consisted primarily of ammunition and tools, while their diet fluctuated between starvation and gluttonous feasts depending on the whims of the terrain.

Summer brought a different kind of trial. Men and dogs rafted across alpine lakes, navigating slender boats whipped from standing timber as they ascended and descended treacherous currents. Months dissolved into a blur of motion through uncharted expanses where the ghosts of previous travelers lingered. They traversed divides in blinding summer storms, endured the relentless glare of the midnight sun on exposed ridges, and descended into valleys teeming with insects. Even amidst the shadows of glaciers, they paused to gather berries as vibrant as those of the southern hemisphere. By autumn, they entered a desolate lake country, a graveyard of wildlife where only the chilling wind and the rhythmic lapping of ice-forming waters testified to life's former presence.

Winter followed, erasing their tracks as they wandered paths laid by ancestors. At one point, they discovered an ancient blaze leading toward the Lost Cabin, yet the trail began and ended without logical connection, preserving its mystery alongside the cabin's. Later, they stumbled upon the skeletal remains of a hunting lodge. Among the rotting debris, Thornton unearthed a long-barreled flintlock—a Hudson's Bay Company firearm from the fur-trade era, valued then at its height in beaver pelts. No other clue remained regarding the lone settler who had abandoned the gun centuries prior.

Finally, spring returned with a revelation. They bypassed the mythic cabin entirely, discovering instead a shallow placer deposit in a broad valley. Gold glittered across the pan-bottom like melted butter. They settled in, working ceaselessly. Days blurred into nights as they sacked the earth in moose-hide bags, piling fifty-pound loads of wealth beside their spruce-bough lodges. For the dogs, the routine shifted. Beyond hauling occasional kills, Buck spent long hours brooding by the fire. The image of the short-legged, hairy man—the ghost of his past—haunted him with renewed frequency.

In these visions, fear was the dominant atmosphere. The man slept restlessly, jerking awake to scan the darkness, tossing fuel onto the flames. Whether gathering shellfish on a shore or stalking through the woods, the man moved with predatory alertness, ears twitching, nostrils flaring. He climbed trees with supernatural agility, swinging from branch to branch with a fluid grace that suggested he belonged equally to the canopy and the forest floor. Buck recalled nights spent beneath these very trees, watching the man roost and guard against unseen threats.

Closely intertwined with these memories was the Call. It resonated from the deep recesses of the forest, a vibration that induced a profound, restless longing within Buck. It promised a vague, sweet ecstasy, stirring primal yearnings he could not name. Driven by forces beyond comprehension, he would pursue the sound, barking defiantly or pressing his nose into the cool moss and rich, loamy soil. He would crouch motionless behind fallen logs, wide-eyed and attentive, hoping to intercept the source of the summons.

Impulses seized him with irresistible force. Dozing in the midday heat, he would abruptly snap upright, ears pricked, and launch himself into a sprint that lasted for hours. He relished the thrill of running down dry creek beds, creeping silently to observe the avian inhabitants. He would spend entire days lying in the underbrush, watching partridges strut and drum. But most captivating was the dim twilight of the summer midnights. In the subdued murmur of the forest, he became a student of the wild, reading signs and sounds with the precision of a scholar deciphering a text, driven solely to locate the mysterious entity calling him.

One night, he woke with a jolt, nostrils quivering, mane bristling in rhythmic waves. From the tree line emerged the Call—or rather, a singular, piercing note of it. It was distinct, definitive, unlike any bark of a Husky, yet undeniably familiar. Recognizing it instantly, Buck leaped from the sleeping camp and surged into the woods. As he neared the source, his pace slowed, movements cautious and deliberate. He emerged into a clearing to find a long, lean timber wolf standing erect on its haunches, nose pointed skyward.

The wolf sensed his approach and halted its howling. Buck stalked forward, body coiled tight, tail rigid, feet placing themselves with unnatural care. His posture broadcast a complex message: a mix of threat and tentative friendship, the necessary truce between predators. Yet, the wolf bolted. Buck gave chase, driving the animal into a narrow channel blocked by a timber jam. The wolf spun, pivoting on its hind legs in a defensive stance reminiscent of cornered dogs, snapping teeth in a rapid, furious rhythm.

Buck did not strike. Instead, he circled, closing the gap with friendly gestures. Suspicion and terror radiated from the wolf; Buck outweighed it significantly, his head reaching the beast's shoulder. Seizing an opening, the wolf darted away, and the pursuit resumed. Repeatedly, the wolf was trapped, only to escape at the slightest chance. Eventually, however, Buck's persistence prevailed. Realizing no malice was intended, the wolf lowered its guard and sniffed noses with him.

Friendship blossomed instantly. They engaged in a playful, nervous frolic typical of fierce beasts masking their aggression. Soon, the wolf broke into a leisurely lope, signaling a destination. He beckoned Buck to follow, and they raced side-by-side through the gloaming, ascending the creek bed, climbing the gorge, and crossing the bleak divide where the water originated.

On the opposite slope, the landscape opened into a vast expanse of forest and rushing streams. They ran relentlessly, hour after hour, as the sun climbed higher and the air warmed. Buck felt a surge of wild joy. He knew he was answering the Call, running alongside his wooden brother toward the origin of the song. Ancient memories flooded back, stirring the dormant instincts of his other life. He had done this before, in that forgotten realm, and now he was repeating it: running free, earth unpaved beneath his paws, sky infinite above.

They paused at a flowing stream to drink. Buck sat, and the memory of John Thornton surfaced. The wolf moved on toward the Call's source, then returned, nudging Buck encouragingly. But Buck hesitated, turning slowly to retrace his steps. For nearly an hour, the wild companion trotted beside him, whining softly. Finally, the wolf sat, nose to the sky, and unleashed a mournful howl. As Buck persisted on his backward journey, the sound faded into the distance, swallowed by the vastness.

Back at camp, John Thornton dined when Buck burst in, launching himself atop his master in a frenzy of affection. He overturned the man, scrambled over him, licking faces and biting hands—a chaotic display Thornton dismissed as "playing the general tom-fool," shaking the dog back and forth while cursing him with love.

Restlessness clawed at him, haunted by visions of the wild brother and the smiling lands beyond the divide. He ventured into the woods again, but the wolf did not appear, and the mournful howl remained silent.
