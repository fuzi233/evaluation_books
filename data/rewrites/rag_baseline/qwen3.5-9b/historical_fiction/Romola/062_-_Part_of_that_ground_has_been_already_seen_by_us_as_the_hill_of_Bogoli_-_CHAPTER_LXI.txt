On the eighth day following that catastrophic night, Romola stood upon the precipice of the Mediterranean, gazing at the gentle, rhythmic pulse of the summer sea just beyond the modest fishing hamlet of Viareggio. She had fled Florence once more, and this time, no authoritative voice halted her retreat. Clad in the grey habit of a religious sister, she no longer viewed the garb as a mask; rather, it served as armor against a heart-sickness that rendered the distinction between identity and disguise irrelevant. A fresh rebellion had ignited within her, fueled by a profound despair. Why cling to one label over another, or to the hollow echo of her own name, when she despairs of finding any coherent duty attached to it? What force remains capable of forging that sacred motive men term "duty," unless it is anchored in a specific, believing love? Every bond of strong affection had shattered. In her marriage—the highest covenant of all—she had ceased to perceive the mystical union that guarantees indissolubility, nor even the obligation of a voluntary pledge. Had she not proven that the objects of her vows were inherently impossible? The impulse to liberate herself surged with overwhelming force, yet this freedom promised only an exchange of calamities. There is no recompense for a woman who realizes her life's primary relationship was merely a mistake. She had lost her crown. The deepest secret of human blessedness had whispered its truth to her, only to slip past her forever. Now, her greatest support in this supreme sorrow had vanished. The vision of any grand purpose, any end to existence capable of ennobling endurance and elevating the mundane toil of daily life with divine ardor, was utterly eclipsed by a sense of cosmic confusion. All effort felt like dragging at tangled threads; every fellowship, whether for resistance or advocacy, appeared as mere unfairness and exclusion.

Who, after all, was the man who had embodied for her the highest heroism—not the stoic endurance of the isolated warrior, but the willing, self-offering love of the martyr? Who was the cause he struggled to defend? Romola had lost her trust in Savonarola, extinguishing the fervent admiration that had previously blinded her to his aberrations, allowing her to focus solely on the grand trajectory of his ambition. Now that her devotion to her godfather had cast her into direct antagonism with the Friar, she perceived the repulsive and contradictory details of his teachings with a painful, hyper-lucid clarity that distorted their scale. In the bitterness of her disillusionment, she declared his striving to renovate the Church and the world to be a pursuit of a mere name—a title that signified nothing more than the measures designed to fortify his own position in Florence. Indeed, these were often questionable deeds and words, undertaken solely to shield his influence from the consequences of his own errors. The political reform that once sparked a new passion in her life now reduced itself to narrow stratagems for the safety of Florence, a contemptible contradiction to his alternating professions of blind trust in Divine providence.

It was inevitable that she should judge the Friar unjustly regarding questions of individual suffering, viewing them through the lens of personal tenderness, while he viewed them through the rigid eyes of theoretical conviction. In his assertion that his party's cause was synonymous with the Kingdom of God, she heard only the ring of egoism. Perhaps such words are rarely spoken without that baser undertone; yet, they constitute the implicit formula of all energetic belief. If such belief, pursuing a grand and distant goal, risks descending into demon-worship—where the votary sends his children through the fire with a readiness that hardly resembles sacrifice—then tender fellow-feeling for the near and dear possesses its own dangers, tending toward timidity and skepticism regarding the larger aims without which life cannot ascend to religion. Thus, poor Romola was being blinded by her tears. No one who has ever experienced the loss of faith in a fellow-man whom they have profoundly loved and revered will lightly claim that such a shock leaves the faith in the Invisible Goodness unshaken. As the trust in high humanity sank, so too did the dignity of life; we cease to believe in our own better selves, for that too is part of the common nature degraded by our thoughts. All the finer impulses of the soul grew dull. Romola felt even the springs of her once-active pity drying up, leaving her to barren, egoistic complaint. Had not *she* suffered her sorrows? And few had cared for her while she had cared for many. She had done enough; she had striven for the impossible and was weary of this stifling, crowded existence. She longed for that repose in pure sensation she had sometimes dreamed of during the sultry afternoons of her youth, when she imagined herself floating naively in the waters. The clear waves seemed to beckon; she wished she could lie down upon them and drift from sleep into death. Yet Romola could not directly seek death; the fullness of young life within her forbade such a direct act. She could only wish that death would arrive.

At the point where she paused, the shoreline curved sharply inward, forming a deep bay where a small sailing boat was moored. In her longing to glide across the waters turning golden under the level rays of the setting sun, she recalled a tale from Boccaccio, one she had loved to linger over when her father slept and she moved from her stool to the floor to read the *Decameron*. It was the story of Gostanza, a woman in lovesick despair who desired no further life but lacked the courage to end it. She had placed herself in a boat, pushed off to sea, and lain down, wrapping her mantle around her head, hoping to be wrecked so that her fear would be powerless to flee from death. This memory had remained a mere thought in Romola's mind until now. As she paced back and forth, she observed another vessel gliding black against the red-gold horizon, carrying a single man toward the bend where the first boat lay. Continuing her walk, she eventually saw the man land, haul his craft onto the sand, and begin unloading cargo. He was likely the owner of the smaller boat as well; he would depart soon, and her opportunity would vanish with him—her chance to purchase that smaller vessel. She had not yet consciously admitted her intent to use it, but she felt a sudden, sharp eagerness to secure the possibility of its use, signaling the unconscious growth of a thought into a desire.

"Is that little boat yours also?" she asked the fisherman, who had looked up, startled by the tall grey figure, and offered a reverence to this holy sister wandering mysteriously in the evening solitude. It was indeed his boat—an old craft, barely seaworthy, yet valuable to any man willing to repair it. By the blessing of Saint Anthony, whose chapel stood in the village nearby, his fishing had prospered, and he now possessed a better vessel, formerly owned by Gianni, who had died. However, he had not yet sold the older one. Romola inquired of its value, and while the man was occupied, she thrust the sum into a small satchel lying on the ground, containing the remnants of his dinner. Afterwards, she watched him furled his sail and asked him how to set it for a voyage to sea. Pacing up and down again, she waited for his departure. The image of herself gliding away in that boat upon the darkening waters expanded into a consuming longing, much like the thought of a cool stream in oppressive heat becomes a painful thirst. To be freed from the burden of choice when all motives were bruised, to commit herself, in a state of sleep, to a destiny that would either bring death or new necessities capable of awakening a new life—it was a thought that beckoned her the more because the soft evening air made her crave rest in the still solitude, rather than returning to the noise and heat of the village.

Finally, the slow-moving fisherman gathered his belongings and walked away. Soon, the gold of the sunset faded into dusk, and there was no living creature in sight, no sound but the lulling monotony of lapping waves. In this sea, there was no tide strong enough to carry her away if she waited for its ebb; however, Romola sensed the land breeze rising slightly. She entered the boat, unfurled the sail, and secured it as she had learned in that first brief lesson. She noted that it caught the light breeze, and that was all she required. Loosening the boat from its moorings, she attempted to urge it forward with an oar until she was far from the land, until the sea turned dark even to the west, and the stars began to disclose themselves like a palpitating life over the vast heavens.

Resting finally, she threw back her cowl and removed the kerchief beneath, which had confined her hair. She folded both under her head for a pillow against one of the boat's ribs. Her fair head was still very young and could endure a hard cushion. Thus she lay, letting the soft night air breathe upon her as she drifted on the water, watching the deepening quiet of the sky. She was alone now; she had freed herself from all claims, freeing herself even from that crushing burden of choice which grows heavier as claims loosen their guiding hold. Had she found anything resembling the dream of her girlhood? No. Memories clung to her like the weight of broken wings that could never be lifted—memories of human sympathy which, even in its pain, leaves a thirst that the Great Mother has no milk to still. Romola felt orphaned amidst those wide expanses of sea and sky. She read no message of love for her in that distant, symbolic writing of the heavens.
