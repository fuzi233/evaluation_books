After the day’s loud rejoicings—after that olive-branch which chance had put into his hand and the people had pressed to their lips as if it were a consecrated herb—Tito Melema found the rest of his errands of a colder, studied sort. He halted at the Palazzo Vecchio and waited beneath its stone gaze for the return of the Ten, who held the threads of war and foreign speech; and when they came, he delivered, with a smooth fulness, what he had gathered in Pisa—those half-opened doors, those measured answers, the scent of fear and bargaining that hangs in the corridors where men decide whether cities shall be eaten like bread or spared.

He spoke as one who sets dishes in order: first the clean facts, then the seasonings—names, times, plausible intentions. Bernardo del Nero, sitting among the Ten, felt admiration forced from him as one may swallow a draught too bitter for choice but too wholesome to refuse. He would have spat it out if he could. The old dislike that had once lain dormant had risen again, sharper since the selling of Bardo’s library; and though Romola had said nothing—her silence like a locked chest that forbade prying—Bernardo’s long-used eyes read other signs. A woman’s face can keep counsel, yet it cannot hide the wasting that comes when the daily bread is made of grief. He had seen Romola move through Florence not with a girl’s step but with a pale steadfastness, and he had heard where her feet were going: to Fra Girolamo’s sermons, to the Piagnoni, to those places where the very air seems thin with fasting.

“In truth,” he said to himself, “if they are unhappy and childless, they must needs seize on some madness or some overstrained holiness. And my poor Romola—Latin and Greek have not made her less woman; they have only given her a larger pain to feel with. This Greek husband—so useful, so smooth-handled—may cut the fingers that use him. Yet—solco torto, sacco dritto—crooked furrows do fill sacks.”

With that old Florentine belief that politics must take its agents as the market takes its eels—slippery, dubious, still fit for supper—he held his peace. For Romola’s sake there were things he would not name; and beyond those he had nothing to allege except what everyone in Florence alleged and forgot: that Tito was a Greek, and that he smiled.

When his formal duty was done and the Ten dismissed him, Tito felt the day’s official labour fall from him as a cloak. He was tired, dust-dried from riding; sweat and road-grit clung to him, and his mouth tasted of leather and stale wine. Yet he did not go home. There were certain things in his scarsella—and certain thoughts in his mind—that he longed to be rid of; but the unburdening must seem accidental, never hunted after.

He walked away from the Palace with the saunter of one who has nowhere to be. The procession had ended, yet the bells still shook the air. Florence was like a kitchen after a feast where the pots are emptied but the steam and clatter linger; the streets were full of restless bodies seeking another course to the meal of joy. Since the new discipline had declared Christ the special King of the Florentines and would have all pleasures taste of piety, there was still, in many young throats shouting “Viva Gesù!”, a hunger for rougher seasoning—for stones flung in honest exuberance, for the old street-fury that made gratitude audible.

Tito could not pass without being known. Hands caught at his sleeve; mouths, hot with wine and shouting, asked questions that breathed on him like a crowd in a bakehouse. He escaped by giving them a better quarry: he told them Meo di Sasso, the true messenger from Leghorn, must be nearing the Porta San Frediano. Meo, he said, would tell more than Tito knew. So he slipped away, turning their attention as one turns boiling milk from spilling by lifting the pot at the right moment.

At the Piazza del Duomo he let his long eyes wander over the space with the look of pure idleness, though he was searching as carefully as a buyer in the market who must find a particular fish before it spoils. The day’s gathering at the Duomo had left more loungers and talkers than common about the Baptistery rails and around Nello’s shop. Tito’s glance found what he wanted—so precisely that he made his face more indifferent than ever.

A group leaned there in expensive ease. Tito seemed to notice the tallest only by chance as he half passed, turning his head with a slight greeting and tossing the end of his becchetto over his shoulder.

The man thus greeted was one who wore his claims like embroidery. He had on a tunic rich with stitched ornament and an ostentation of linen after the newest French mode; a sword and poniard hung at his belt, fine as jewellery but meant for flesh. His hat carried a red plume—a defiant flare against the new Florentine gravity, which under the Piagnoni had grown severe as Lent. His youth made his breadth—the wide face, the heavy waist—seem less the promise of strength than the stamp of coarseness; and his eyes had that rude desecrating stare which is like a bad smell in a narrow room.

He and his companions—young men too, armed and costly dressed—were laughing with a loudness that was itself a protest, as if laughter could beat down suspicion by mere noise. For there were reasons why Messer Dolfo Spini, leader of the Compagnacci—the Evil Companions—might need to prove he was not mortified. Dolfo, heir of the great house by the loggia over Santa Trinita bridge, had drawn together the dissolute youth of the old aristocratic party, enemies to Medici, enemies to the popular government, and bitterest enemies of Savonarola. They had made themselves sworn champions of extravagant suppers and the pleasant sins of the flesh, as if a well-roasted capon and a flagon of strong wine were holy relics to be defended against reformers who threatened to make the world chaste and temperate until life should taste like water without salt.

Up to this very morning Dolfo had been crying that Florence was given up to famine and ruin through her obedience to the Frate, and that salvation lay only in joining the League and driving Fra Girolamo out—sending him to Rome where, he said, he had long since been summoned by the Pope. It was not likely such a man felt pure joy now that succour had come as if to justify Savonarola’s prediction; and indeed, as Tito joined the group by Nello’s door, the laughter that broke out again rang hollow.

For at the centre of the group, leaning against the door-post, stood a close-shaven, keen-eyed young man who listened to men as others taste sauces, discerning the hidden bitterness at once. Niccolò Macchiavelli had already learned the small secrets of egoism as one learns the notes of a street: by walking it daily.

“Messer Dolfo’s head,” he was saying, “is more pumpkin than I thought. I measure men’s dulness by the devices they trust in for deceiving others. The dullest beast is he who grins and says he does not mind, just after he has had his shins kicked.” He let the circle widen as Tito came near, and his smile sharpened like a knife laid flat on the palm. “If I were a trifle duller, I should pretend to be fond of this Melema, who has got a secretaryship that would exactly suit me—as if Latin ill-paid could love better Latin that is better paid! Melema, you are a pestiferously clever fellow, in my way, and I am sorry to hear you have had another piece of good-luck to-day.”

“Questionable luck, Niccolò,” Tito answered, touching his shoulder with friendly ease. “I have got nothing by it yet but being laid hold of and breathed upon by wool-beaters, when I am as soiled and battered with riding as a tabellario from Bologna.”

At the sound of Tito’s voice, Nello the barber came forward, nimble as one who lives by keeping his hands clean while all Florence brings him its dust.

“Ah, you want a touch of my art, Messer Oratore,” he cried. “Your chin has yesterday’s crop upon it. Come, come—consign yourself to the priest of all the Muses. Sandro—quick with the lather!”

“In truth, Nello, that is what I most desire at this moment,” Tito said, seating himself. “That was why I turned hither instead of going home at once when I had done my business at the Palazzo.”

The shop smelled of warm soap and sharpened steel, of damp cloth and the faint sweetness of some herb-water that Nello used to finish a gentleman’s hair—the scent of rosemary and something citrine, like peel bruised under the thumb. Outside, the bells still shook the air; inside, the shears made a smaller music, the rasp of strop on blade a steady whisper. In that mingled sound—public thunder and domestic scraping—Florence seemed to breathe.

“Yes, indeed,” said Nello, spreading the lather with the practised stroke of one who knows how to soften what is rough. “It is not fitting that you should present yourself to Madonna Romola with a rusty chin and a tangled zazzera. Nothing that is not dainty ought to approach the Florentine lily; though I see her going about like a sunbeam among the rags that line our corners—if indeed she is not more like a moonbeam now, for I thought yesterday, when I met her, she looked as pale and worn as that fainting Madonna of Fra Giovanni’s. You must see to it, my bel erudito: she keeps too many fasts and vigils in your absence.”

Tito’s shoulders lifted in a melancholy shrug under the barber’s hands. “It is too true, Nello. She has been depriving herself of half her proper food every day during this famine. But what can I do? Her mind has been set all aflame. A husband’s influence is powerless against the Frate’s.”

“As every other influence is likely to be, that of the Holy Father included,” said Domenico Cennini, who had followed Tito in and now stood among the door-group that crowded nearer, drawn as much by politics as by the pleasant sight of a man being made handsome. “I don’t know whether you have gathered anything at Pisa about how the wind sits at Rome, Melema?”

“Secrets of the council-chamber, Messer Domenico,” Tito returned with a smile, opening his palms as if to show they held nothing. “An envoy must be as dumb as a father confessor.”

“Certainly, certainly. I ask no breach of that rule. But my belief is: if his Holiness drives Fra Girolamo to extremity, the Frate will move heaven and earth to get a General Council of the Church—ay, and would get it too; and I, for one, should not be sorry, though I am no Piagnone.”

“With leave of your greater experience, Messer Domenico,” Macchiavelli said, his thumbs hooked into his belt, his tone enjoying its own clearness as a cook enjoys a knife that cuts true, “I must differ from you—not in your wish to see a General Council that might reform the Church, but in your belief that the Frate will checkmate his Holiness. The Frate’s game is impossible.

“If he had contented himself with preaching against Rome’s vices and prophesying, in some undefined way, that Italy would be scourged, Pope Alexander would have let him spend his breath thus as long as he could find hearers. Such spiritual blasts knock down no walls. But the Frate wants to be more than a trumpet: he wants to be a lever—and what is more, he is a lever. He would spread the doctrine of Christ by maintaining a popular government in Florence; and the Pope, as I know on the best authority, has private views to the contrary.”

“Then Florence will stand by the Frate,” Cennini broke in, heat rising in him like quick-fermenting dough. “I might prefer he would let his prophesying alone, but if our freedom to choose our own government is attacked—I am an obedient son of the Church, yet I would vote for resisting Pope Alexander the Sixth, as our forefathers resisted Pope Gregory the Eleventh.”

“But pardon me,” Macchiavelli said, unruffled, letting the argument unfold in his hands like a well-laid map. “Have you correctly seized the Frate’s position? How is it he has become a lever worth attacking by so acute a man as his Holiness? Because he has got the ear of the people: because he gives them threats and promises they believe come straight from God—not only about hell, purgatory, paradise, but about Pisa and our Great Council. Let events go against him so as to shake that faith, and the cause of his power will be the cause of his fall.

“He is piling three sorts of hatred upon his head: the hatred of average mankind against anyone who would lay a strict yoke of virtue on them; the hatred of stronger powers in Italy who want to farm Florence for their own profit; and the hatred of the people, to whom he has ventured to promise good in this world instead of confining his promises to the next. If a prophet is to keep power, he must be a prophet like Mahomet, with an army at his back—so that when faith faints it may be frightened into life again.”

“Rather sum up the three sorts in one,” said Francesco Cei, impetuous, his poet’s blood quick to boil, “and say he has won the hatred of all men who have sense and honesty by inventing hypocritical lies. His place is among the false prophets in the Inferno, who walk with their heads turned hind-foremost.”

“You are too angry, my Francesco,” Macchiavelli said, smiling as one who has heard many pots boil over. “You poets are apt to cut the clouds in your wrath. I am no votary of the Frate’s, and would not lay down my little finger for his veracity. But veracity is a plant of paradise; the seeds have never flourished beyond the walls. You, yourself, tell poetical lies only—partly compelled by fervour, partly to please your audience—and you object to lies in prose. The Frate differs from you only as to the boundary of poetry. When he gets into the pulpit of the Duomo, he has fervour within and an audience without. Ecco!”

“You are somewhat lax there, Niccolò,” said Cennini gravely. “I believe in the Frate’s integrity, though I do not believe in his prophecies; and as long as integrity is not disproved, we have a popular party strong enough to protect him and resist foreign interference.”

“A party that seems strong enough,” Macchiavelli returned, with a shrug and an almost imperceptible glance towards Tito, who meanwhile abandoned himself to Nello’s combing and scenting with a pleasure that was nearly voluptuous—each tooth of the comb drawing through hair like a rake through fine flour. “But how many Mediceans are there among you? How many who will not be turned by a private grudge?”

“As to the Mediceans,” said Cennini, “I believe there is very little genuine feeling left for them. Who would risk much for Piero de’ Medici? A few old staunch friends, perhaps, like Bernardo del Nero. But even some most connected with the family are hearty friends of the popular government and would exert themselves for the Frate. I was talking with Giannozzo Pucci only a little while ago, and I am convinced there is nothing he would set his face against more than against any attempt to alter the new order of things.”

“You are right there, Messer Domenico,” Tito said, laughing, the meaning in his eyes like spice one tastes after swallowing. He rose from the chair as Nello wiped away the last foam with a cloth warm from hot water. “And I fancy the tender passion came in aid of hard theory. I am persuaded jealousy lay at the bottom of Giannozzo’s alienation from Piero de’ Medici; else so amiable a creature would never feel the bitterness he sometimes lets escape him in that quarter. He was in the procession with you, I suppose?”

“No,” Cennini answered. “He is at his villa—went there three days ago.”

Tito adjusted his cap, glanced down at his splashed hose as if the words were no more than street dust on his ears. In truth, he had gained what he sought. In his scarsella lay a crushed gold ring, engaged to be delivered to Giannozzo Pucci—a sign rather than a gift, like a seal that calls a man back to town. Tito had received it from an envoy of Piero de’ Medici, whom he had ridden out of his way to meet at Certaldo on the Siena road. Since Pucci was out of Florence, the ring must go by Fra Michele, a Carthusian lay-brother in Medicean service; and the sight of that gold circle would draw Pucci home to hear the unspoken part of Tito’s mission.

“Behold him!” Nello cried, flourishing his comb as if it were a sceptre and pointing it at Tito. “The handsomest scholar in the world or in the wolds—del mondo o di maremma—now he has passed through my hands! A trifle thinner in the face, though, than when he came first in his bloom to Florence—eh? And, I vow, there are lines faintly hinting themselves about your mouth, Messer Oratore! Ah, mind is an enemy to beauty. I myself was thought beautiful by women once—when I was in swaddling-bands. But now—oimè! I carry my unwritten poems in cipher on my face!”

The company laughed. Nello held up the hand-mirror and looked at himself with tragic vanity; the shop rang with voices, with the clink of metal, with the far-off bell-surge that poured through the open door like a river of sound. Tito, smiling with them, made his farewell sign and went out into the Piazza’s brightness.

“I am of our old Piero di Cosimo’s mind,” Francesco Cei said when the door had closed. “I do not half like Melema. That trick of smiling grows stronger—no wonder he has lines about the mouth.”

“He is too successful,” Macchiavelli answered lightly. “I am sure something is wrong with him, else he would not have that secretaryship.”

“He is an able man,” Cennini said, judicial as one weighing grain. “My brother and I have found him useful with our Greek sheets, and he gives satisfaction to the Ten. I like to see a young man work his way upward by merit. The secretary Scala, who befriended him from the first, thinks highly of him still, I know.”

“Doubtless,” said a notary in the background. “He writes Scala’s official letters for him—or corrects them—and gets well paid for it.”

“I wish Messer Bartolommeo would pay me to doctor his gouty Latin,” Macchiavelli said, with a shrug. “Did he tell you about the pay, Ser Ceccone, or was it Melema himself?” He made his face innocently ironic, as if asking whether a sauce had been salted by the cook or by the diner.

“Melema? no indeed,” Ser Ceccone replied. “He is as close as a nut. He never brags. That is why he is employed everywhere. They say he is getting rich by doing all sorts of underhand work.”

“It is a little too bad,” Macchiavelli murmured, “and so many able notaries out of employment!”

“Well,” Cei persisted, “I thought it was a nasty story a year or two ago about the man who said Melema had stolen jewels. It got hushed up somehow; but I remember Piero di Cosimo said he believed there was something in it, for he saw Melema’s face when the man laid hold of him, and never saw a visage so ‘painted with fear,’ as our sour old Dante says.”

Nello’s easy humour turned sharp as a razor edge. “Come, spit no more of that venom, Francesco, else I shall deem it my public duty to cut your hair awry next time I get you under my scissors. That story was a lie. Bernardo Rucellai and the Magnificent Eight knew all about it. The man was a dangerous madman, and was properly kept from mischief in prison. As for our Piero di Cosimo, his wits chase the wind of Mongibello: he has such extravagant fancy he would take a lizard for a crocodile. No—let that tale lie. It has been dead and buried too long—our noses object to it.”

“It is true,” Macchiavelli said, with the calm of one who prefers law to gossip. “You forget the danger of the precedent, Francesco. The next mad beggarman may accuse you of stealing his verses—or me, God help me! of stealing his coppers.” He turned his head towards the Piazza. “Ah! Dolfo Spini has carried his red feather out. That captain of swaggerers would like the Republic to lose Pisa, merely for the chance of seeing the people tear the frock off the Frate’s back. With your pardon, Francesco—I know he is a friend of yours—there are few things I should like better than to see him play Capo d’Oca, who went to the tournament blowing his trumpets and came back with them in a bag.”
