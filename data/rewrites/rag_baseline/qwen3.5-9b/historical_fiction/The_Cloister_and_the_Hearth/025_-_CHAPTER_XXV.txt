The momentum of Gerard's fall was arrested only by Denys's desperate grasp, yet the physics of gravity remained unforgiving; without the interposition of the dying bear, whose hairy carcass served as a final, tragic buffer, Gerard's neck or limbs surely would have shattered. Denys tore the animal from the boy, a futile gesture born of instinct rather than necessity. The bear panted, her limbs trembling in the twilight, but she was already beyond redemption. Within moments, her breathing ceased, leaving the forest floor littered with the evidence of a failed hunt. Denys, displaying a pragmatic cruelty suited to survival, propped Gerard against the soft, cooling fur and fanned him with frantic energy. Consciousness returned to the boy in fragments, clouded by confusion and the phantom sensation of the bear's weight, until he rolled away in a scream of terror.

"Courage," Denys cried, his voice cutting through the panic. "Le diable est mort."

"Is it dead? Quite dead?" Gerard stammered, hiding behind the trunk of a nearby oak. His courage was a feverish delusion, and the chill of the coming night had already settled deep in his bones.

"Behold," Denys said, pulling the bear's ear with playful insolence. He prised open her jaw and stuffed Gerard's head inside, performing other acts of grotesque mockery. In the midst of this absurdity, Gerard vomited violently.

Denys laughed, a harsh, braying sound. "What ails you now? And why did you tumble from your perch just as we secured victory?"

"I swooned, I trow," Gerard gasped.

"But why?"

Receiving no answer, Denys pressed on. "Green girls faint merely at the sight of you, yet they possess the wisdom to choose their time and place. What woman in her right mind ever faints while scaling a tree?"

"She sent her filthy blood all over me," Gerard retched. "I believe the scent alone was enough to overpower me! Faugh! I loathe blood."

"I daresay it was potent."

"Look at the mess she has made of me."

"But with her blood, not yours. I pity the enemy who sought to satisfy you."

"You need not boast, Maitre Denys," Gerard muttered, wiping his face. "I saw you beneath the tree; your shirt was stained."

"Let us distinguish," Denys said, flushing slightly. "It is permissible to tremble for a friend."

In response, Gerard flung his arms around Denys's neck in a silent embrace.

"Look here," whined the stout soldier, visibly moved by this sudden eruption of youthful affection. "Was anything in creation ever so reminiscent of a woman? I love thee, little milksop—go on. Good heavens, behold him on his knees now. What fresh caprice is this?"

"Oh, Denys, ought we not to return thanks to Him who has spared both our lives against such fearful odds?" Gerard knelt, raising his voice in prayer. Soon, he found Denys kneeling quietly beside him, his hands crossed over his chest in the manner of his homeland, his face drawn long and thin. When they rose, Gerard's countenance was radiant.

"Good Denys," he said, "Heaven will reward thy piety."

"Ah, bah! I did it out of politeness," the Frenchman dismissed, waving a hand. "It was to please thee, little one. *C'est égal*, it was prayed well and orderly, and it edified me to the core while it lasted. A bishop could scarcely have managed the affair better. Now that our vespers are sung and the saints enlisted on our side—*marchons*."

Before taking two steps, Denys halted. "By-the-by, the cub!"

"Oh, no, no!" Gerard protested.

"You are right. It is late. We have squandered precious time climbing trees, tumbling from them, swooning, vomiting, and praying. Furthermore, the brute is heavy to carry. And now I recall, we shall likely have Papa hunting for it next; these bears make such a racket over an odd cub. What is this? You are wounded! You are wounded!"

"Not I."

"He is wounded; miserable that I am!"

"Be calm, Denys. I am not touched; I feel no pain anywhere."

"You? You only feel when another is hurt," Denys cried, his voice thick with emotion. Throwing himself to his knees, he examined Gerard's leg with glistening eyes.

"Quick! Quick! Before it stiffens!" he urged, dragging the boy forward.

"Who makes the fuss about nothing now?" Gerard asked, his composure returning.

Denys's reply was indirect. "Be pleased to note," he said, "that I have a bad heart. You were a man enough to save my life, yet I must sneer at you, a novice in war. Was I not a novice once myself? Then you fainted from a wound, and I assumed you swooned from fear, calling you a milksop. Briefly, I have a bad tongue and a bad heart."

"Denys!"

"Plait-il?"

"You lie."

"You are very kind to say so, little one, and I am eternally obliged to you," mumbled the remorseful Denys.

As they walked many furlongs, the muscles of the wounded leg contracted and stiffened until Gerard could barely lift his toe, doing so with excruciating agony. Finally, he could endure it no longer.

"Let me lie down and die," he groaned. "This is intolerable."

Denys argued that it was afternoon and the nights were growing frosty; that cold and hunger were ill companions, and that it would be unreasonable to lose heart, especially since a certain great personage was notoriously deceased. Thus, Gerard leaned upon his axe and hobbled on, but soon he gave in completely, collapsing helplessly into the road.

Denys dragged him aside into the cover of the woods. To Gerard's astonishment, the soldier handed him his crossbow and bolts, commanding him strictly to remain motionless. "If any ill-looking fellows discover you and approach," Denys instructed, "bid them keep aloof. Should they refuse, shoot them dead at twenty paces. Honest men keep the path; knaves in a wood are fools to parley." With this, he snatched up Gerard's axe and set off running—not, as Gerard expected, toward Düsseldorf, but back along the road they had traveled.

Gerard lay aching and smarting. Rome, which had seemed so imminent at the start, now appeared impossibly distant, despite him being two hundred miles closer. Yet, soon his thoughts drifted toward Sevenbergen. How sweet it would be one day to hold Margaret's hand and recount every trial he had endured for her! The mere thought of her soothed him; amidst the pain and the irritation of his nerves, he lay resigned, offering a sweet, faint smile.

He had lain thus for more than two hours when suddenly shouts erupted. The next instant, something struck a tree hard by and vibrated violently.

Gerard looked up. It was an arrow.

He sprang to his feet. Several missiles rattled among the branches, and the wood echoed with battle cries. Their source was indeterminate; noises in these vast forests reverberate so wildly that a stranger is perpetually at fault regarding their origin, yet they seemed to fill the entire air. Presently, there was a lull, followed by the fierce gallop of hooves. Louder shouts and cries arose, mingled with shrieks and groans, accompanied by strange, terrible sounds like fierce claps of thunder, bellowing loudly before dying off in cracking echoes. Red tongues of flame shot intermittently among the trees, and clouds of sulfurous smoke drifted overhead. Then, all was still.

Gerard was struck with awe. "What will become of Denys?" he cried. "Oh, why did you leave me? Oh, Denys, my friend! My friend!"

Just before sunset, Denys returned, nearly collapsing under a hairy bundle—the bear's skin.

Gerard welcomed him with a burst of joy that startled him. "I thought never to see you again, dear Denys. Were you in the battle?"

"No. What battle?"

"The bloody conflict of men, or fiends, that raged in the wood a while ago," Gerard explained, describing it vividly, more fully than history might record.

Denys patted him indulgently on the back. "It is well," he said. "Thou art a good painter; and fever is a great spur to the imagination. Once, I lay in a cart-shed with a cracked skull and watched two hosts maneuver and fight for a good hour on eight feet of space, which I fairly described to my comrade in proper order, only not so gorgeously as thou, for lack of book learning."

"What, then, you believe me not? When I tell you the arrows whizzed over my head, and the combatants shouted, and—"

"May the foul fiends fly away with me if I believe a word of it."

Gerard took his arm and quietly pointed to a tree close by. "Why, it looks like—it is—a broad arrow, as I live!" He approached and looked up.

"It came from the battle. I heard it, and saw it."

"An English arrow."

"How do you know that?"
