“A spade! a rake! a hoe!
A pickaxe or a bill!
A hook to reap, or a scythe to mow,
A flail or what ye will—
And here’s a ready hand
To ply the needful tool,
And skill’d enough, by lessons rough,
In Labour’s rugged school.”

When the Hales set out to call on the widow Boucher the next day, they found Nicholas Higgins’ cottage door locked fast. A garrulous, over-involved neighbour hurried to set their old assumption straight: this time, Higgins was truly out and about, not hiding away from well-meaning callers. He had popped in to see the grieving widow before dawn that morning, before vanishing off to whatever errand held him for the rest of the day.

Their visit to Mrs. Boucher was little more than disheartening. She nursed a bitter, half-justified conviction that the world had treated her poor husband cruelly, a grievance stoked by enough real hardship to be impossible to reason away. Worse still, every thought she held circled back entirely to her own suffering: she saw her children not as a comfort, but as a heavy burden, even in the midst of her dull, animal love for them. While Mr. Hale strove to lift her muddled, sullen thoughts above the fog of helpless self-pity, Margaret knelt to speak to the children one by one, and found them far truer, simpler mourners than their mother. Their father had been soft and gentle with them, each stammering out eager little stories of the treats he had brought home, the games he had played with them after long shifts at the mill.

“Is that thing upstairs really him?” the oldest boy whispered, eyes wide with dread. “It doesn’t look a bit like daddy. I’m feared of it, and I never was feared of daddy before.”

Margaret’s heart ached to the core. In her selfish, desperate hunger for sympathy, Mrs. Boucher had dragged all the children up to see their father’s disfigured, water-swollen corpse, weaving raw, harsh horror into the natural softness of their grief. She turned their attention gently to small, useful tasks: what they could do to ease their mother’s burden, what kind daddy would have wanted them to do for her now. The children, eager to act, soon fell to tidying the slovenly, soot-streaked rooms, but Mr. Hale’s lofty, abstract appeals fell entirely flat on the widow. Torpid, bitter, wrapped in her own hurt, she could not summon a single thought for her husband’s final hours of despair: she could only see his suicide as a slight against her, not a tragedy born of unbearable suffering. She raged against everyone she could name as the cause of her ruin: the mill masters, Mr. Thornton most of all (even though he had withdrawn the arrest warrant for Boucher after the riot), the Union that Higgins represented, her swarm of hungry, noisy children — all of them a vast army of personal enemies who had conspired to leave her a helpless widow.

Margaret left the cottage heavy-hearted, and could not rouse her father out of his quiet dejection as they walked home. “It is the town life, you see,” she said. “Their nerves are frayed thin by the rush and bustle, the unceasing speed of everything around them, not to mention the cramped, airless little houses they are pent up in, enough on their own to breed constant depression and worry. In the country, people spend so much more of their time outdoors, even the children, even in the depth of winter.”

“But stagnant country life breeds its own fatalism, too,” Mr. Hale sighed. “People there get stuck in ruts just as deep as any mill worker’s.”

“I do not deny that,” Margaret replied. “Every way of living carries its own trials. Town dwellers struggle to be patient, the way country folk struggle to adapt to sudden, unaccustomed change. Both find it hard to imagine a future far beyond the present: the townsman, because the rush of the world around him crowds every other thought out, and the countryman, because he is lulled into nothing more than quiet, animal contentment, no urge to strive or plan or hope for anything more.”

And so they moved on, turning their steps home, neither able to shake the weight of how little they could do for the broken Boucher household. Soon their conversation drifted to brighter memories: Frederick’s recent, joyfully secret visit, the snatched hours they had all shared together. But the thought of her brother’s name pulled Margaret up short. She had lied to protect him, lied straight to Mr. Thornton’s face, and he had seen through her, clear as day. She had always despised cowardice and petty deceit more than any other flaw, and now she was guilty of the very thing she had once scorned.

The shame of it burned hot in her cheeks. She could bear almost anyone knowing of her lie: her aunt Shaw, Edith, even Frederick, the very brother she had lied to save. But the thought of facing Mr. Thornton again, of looking him in the eye and knowing he knew she had stooped to falsehood, made her shrink back. She remembered, all at once, the early days of their acquaintance, when she had foolishly dismissed trade as a profession riddled with deceit, where merchants passed off shoddy goods as fine and pretended to wealth they did not have. She remembered his quiet, calm disdain as he told her that every dishonest act in commerce always unraveled in the long run, that even measured by the low standard of mere success, deceit was folly, not cleverness. Back then, she had been so proud of her own untested, unbroken honesty, she had lectured him that buying cheap and selling dear lacked the chivalric virtue that true justice required, and her father had corrected her, gently, to say it was Christian virtue they ought to strive for. No more pride for her. No more sneering at the trade he had built his life around. Now she could only feel humiliated, disgraced in his sight.

Yet she found herself longing for him to come, to get the meeting over, to know exactly how low she had fallen in his esteem. Her heart leapt at every single ring of the doorbell, only to sink back into dull, disappointed sadness when it was not him. Mr. Hale, who had been expecting Mr. Thornton to call for days to continue their unfinished conversation about mill work and labour, was equally confused by his absence. He had not yet resumed tutoring his pupils, a duty he had set aside during his wife’s final illness, and he was restless, eager to pick up their discussion where they had left off.

At last, a heavy step sounded on the stairs. It was not Mr. Thornton: it was Dixon, announcing that Nicholas Higgins was at the door, acting very strange, asking to see first Margaret, then Mr. Hale. They bade him come up, and Dixon huffed off, complaining that his muddy work boots would dirty the carpet. But when Higgins stepped into the hall, he paused, sat down on the lowest stair, and pulled his scuffed, mud-caked shoes off in silence, walking up the steps in his stocking feet.

“Your servant, sir,” he said, slicking his matted hair down when he stepped into the parlour, nodding at Margaret in apology for his bare feet. “Tramping all day, and Milton’s streets are none too clean.” He was unusually quiet, subdued, almost shy, as if he did not know how to say the thing he had come all this way to tell them. Mr. Hale pressed him to stay for tea, and Margaret slipped out to hurry the meal along, much to Dixon’s irritated surprise — the housekeeper had never gotten used to the Hales inviting working men up into their parlour, no matter how much Margaret softened her irritation with gentle, kind words.

Once the door was shut behind Margaret, Higgins leaned in close to Mr. Hale. “Master,” he said, “you’d never guess what I’ve been tramping after all day, not after all the angry speeches I gave you yesterday. I’ve been looking for work. I swore I’d bite my own tongue clean through before I spoke a hasty, angry word to any master who’d give me a job. I’m doing it for him, you see.” He jerked his thumb vaguely towards the direction of the Boucher cottage.

“For Boucher,” he clarified, when Mr. Hale looked confused. “Him that drowned himself. I never thought he’d have it in him to lie still and let the water creep over him. I know I led him astray, set him off the right path, so it’s my job to look after his wife and his little ones.”

Mr. Hale grabbed his hand and shook it hard, too moved to speak. Higgins shifted awkwardly, abashed. “Aye, well, there’s not a man among us that wouldn’t do the same. But not a single master will take me on. I told Hamper I’d be the hardest worker in his whole mill, that I wouldn’t even ask for union benefits — but I couldn’t sign that no-union pledge, not even for them. He turned me away, same as all the rest. I’m a useless, blacklisted sheep. The children’ll starve if I can’t find work. Parson, can you help me?”

Margaret stepped back into the room then, and he turned to her. “You’ve talked many a time about the South, the countryside down there. I don’t know how far off it is, but I thought if we could all move down there, where food’s cheap and wages are steady, where master and man get along friendly like, I could find work. I’m not even forty-five, I’ve got plenty of strength left in me.”

Margaret stepped forward, gentle but firm, to disabuse him of the dream. “Higgins, the hard farm labour you’d do down south would pay you at most ten shillings a week. Food costs nearly as much as it does here, and you’d be out in the rain and the frost and the blistering sun all day long. It would cripple you with rheumatism before the year was out. The life there is dull — stagnant, like standing water. The farm workers, they labour day after day in the steaming fields, too tired to speak to each other after their shifts, too worn down to argue or debate or laugh the way Milton’s men do. The peace that feels like rest to them would eat at you, fret at you, drive you half mad. You’d miss the company, the talk, too much. And besides, you could never afford to move the widow and all those children hundreds of miles south, even if you wanted to.”

Higgins sighed, the wild hope he had clung to all day draining out of him. “Aye, you’re right. North and South, both of them have their own miseries. Work’s steady down south but pays starvation wages. Here, we’ve got a flood of money one quarter, not a farthing the next. The whole world’s all mixed up, and no man alive knows how to set it right.”

He ate in silence, barely noticing the food on his plate, lost in gloom, until Margaret spoke up, the words sticking in her throat for a long while before she could say them. “Higgins — have you gone to Marlborough Mills to ask for work? To Mr. Thornton’s mill?”

“I went there,” he grunted. “The overlooker told me to go to hell. That’s all the welcome I got.”

“Would you go back tomorrow?” Margaret asked, her soft, grave eyes fixed on his, impossible to resist. “I know it’s a great deal to ask, but would you go there and try to speak directly to Mr. Thornton?”

Higgins heaved a great, heavy sigh. “Pride would chafe me raw to do it. I’d sooner knock him senseless than beg a favour from him. But you’re no common girl, Miss Hale. You’ve never treated any of us like we’re beneath you. I’ll do it. I’ll stand outside his lodge gate at six in the morning and wait till he comes out. But don’t you hold out much hope. That man’s stubborn as flint. I’ll never get a job out of him. But I’ll try, for your sake. No woman’s ever got me to back down from anything before. Not even my wife, not even Bessy.”

Mr. Hale offered to write him a note of introduction, but Higgins refused. “I don’t want no one meddling between master and man. It’s like meddling between a husband and wife — takes more wisdom than most folks have to do any good. I’ll stand on my own two feet.” He said his good nights, and Margaret pointed him to the kitchen fire, where she had set his wet shoes out to dry. He paused at the door, looked at her steadily for a long moment, then brushed a calloused, lean hand across his eyes and tramped off down the path.

“Pride is running strong in that man,” Mr. Hale said, a little amused at Higgins’ refusal of his help.

“There’s granite in all these northern folk, isn’t there?” Margaret replied, softly. “Even poor Higgins, with all his stubbornness, he’s a fine, strong man at the core.”

Her father pinched her ear, smiling. “You’re finally doing Mr. Thornton justice at last, my dear.”

Margaret’s throat tightened, and she could not speak. All she could think, in the quiet hush of the parlour, was how she wished he would come. She wished she was brave enough to face him, to tell him openly that she knew she had been wicked and cowardly, that she deserved all his disapprobation. It felt cruel to lose him as a friend, just when she had finally come to value how good and true he was. She thought of how gentle he had been with her mother, in her final days, and she ached to see him, just to know exactly how far she had fallen in his eyes.
