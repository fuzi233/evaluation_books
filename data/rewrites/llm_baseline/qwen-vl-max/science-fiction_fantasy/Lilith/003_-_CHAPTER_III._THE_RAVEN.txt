I turned, and behind me—nothing was certain. The world blurred at the edges, like a dream seen through fog or mist. I could not tell where sky ended and earth began, nor whether the shapes before me were clouds or mountains. Only one thing was clear: I saw nothing I recognized.

Believing my eyes deceived me, I reached out, feeling blindly around, stepping forward and back, hoping that touch might anchor what sight had failed to define. But my hands found only air. No wall, no tree, no solid form. Nothing.

Then, from a distance, I noticed the raven. He stood still, watching me with an expression that seemed both curious and amused. A strange instinct made me turn to him—perhaps because he was the only living thing in sight. But then it struck me: how absurd to seek guidance from a bird?

I looked away, overwhelmed. Was I lost? Not just in space, but in reality itself? Had I crossed some threshold where the laws of nature, even the rules of thought, no longer applied? Could a man simply step beyond order and fall into chaos?

Yet I heard wind rustling through low plants. I felt the ground beneath my feet. And there—standing still—was the raven.

“How did I get here?” I whispered—then realized I’d spoken aloud.

“You came through the door,” replied a voice—dry, rough, yet strangely familiar.

I spun around, scanning the emptiness. No one. No shape. My heart sank. Was this madness? Had my mind begun to unravel?

But then I saw it—the raven, looking up at me as if waiting for my answer. The sun didn’t shine, yet he cast a shadow—and the shadow seemed part of him, as if it were not separate, but woven into his being.

I must ask you to bear with me. I am trying to make sense of something that may not be sensible. I was in a place—no, not a place, perhaps, but a condition—where time, matter, identity… everything I knew… ceased to hold meaning. The words I use are too small, too shaped by ordinary life, to carry what I mean. Each phrase feels like a sketch of a truth too vast to draw.

I fear I cannot explain it. I try to write, and already I see how inadequate my language is. The moment I attempt to describe it, the experience slips away—like a dream fading upon waking. What once seemed real shifts into something else, until even its essence becomes unrecognizable.

Still, I must go on.

The raven spoke again. And though he was a bird, I felt he deserved an answer—perhaps more than a man might.

“I did not come through any door,” I said.

“You did,” he insisted. “I saw you with my own ancient eyes!”

“But I saw no door!” I argued.

“Of course not,” he replied. “All the doors you’ve known were doors *in*. This one was a door *out*.”

He paused, thoughtful. “And the stranger thing for you will be—each time you walk out, you go deeper in.”

I stared. “Then tell me—where am I?”

“That,” he said, “is impossible. You don’t know what ‘where’ means. The only way to learn where you are is to begin making yourself at home.”

“But how can I do that when everything is strange?”

“By doing something.”

“What?”

“Anything. And soon. Because until you’re at home, you’ll find it as hard to get out as it was to get in.”

“I’ve found it far too easy to get in,” I muttered. “Once I’m out, I won’t return.”

“You stumbled in,” he said gently. “You may stumble out again. Whether it was unfortunate… remains to be seen.”

“Do you never go out?” I asked.

“When I please,” he answered. “But not often. Your world is half-baked—childish, proud, underdeveloped. It’s not fit for an old raven—at your service.”

I frowned. “So I’m wrong to think a man is superior to a bird?”

“Not necessarily. We don’t waste our minds on generalizations. We take each creature as we find them. Now—it’s my turn to ask you a question.”

“You have every right,” I said. “Because you *can* speak.”

“Good answer,” he nodded. “Now—tell me: who are you? If you know.”

I hesitated. “How should I not know? I am myself. I must know.”

“If you know you are yourself,” he said, “you know you are not someone else. But do you truly know you are yourself? Are you sure you’re not your father? Or—excuse me—your own fool? Who are you, pray?”

I froze.

I realized then—I didn’t know. I couldn’t say. The name I used in my world—what was it? I couldn’t recall. And even if I could, it meant nothing here. Names were just labels, and here, labels fell away.

I had almost forgotten—once, everyone had a name. But now, I was nameless. Unnamed. Unknown.

So I said nothing. It was wiser.

For this raven—this creature—saw beyond appearances. He saw into being.

“Look at me,” he said. “And tell me who I am.”

As he spoke, he turned his back.

And suddenly, I knew.

He was no longer a bird.

He was a man—tall, thin, stooped, wearing a long black tailcoat. Then, in a breath, he turned again—and I saw the raven.

“I’ve seen you before,” I said, feeling foolish.

“How can you say that?” he asked. “You saw me from behind.”

“Have you ever seen yourself from behind?” I countered. “You’ve never seen yourself at all.”

“Then tell me now—who am I?”

“I beg your pardon,” I said. “I believe you were once the librarian of our house. But beyond that… I don’t know.”

“Why apologize?”

“Because I called you a raven.”

“You did no harm,” he said. “To call me a raven—or think me one—is to acknowledge my existence. That is all any being can demand of another.”

He paused.

“Let me give you a lesson: No one can say they are themselves—until first they know that they *are*, and then what *themselves* means. In fact—nobody is himself. And himself is nobody. There’s more in it than you see now—but not more than you need to see.”

He turned to leave. As he walked, I saw the librarian again—not changed, but as if he had simply taken up his shadow.

It sounds mad. But I cannot deny it.

I watched him go—until he vanished. Not into distance, perhaps, but into the heather. Or maybe into silence.

Was I dead? Did I not know it?

Was this the afterlife? The world beyond death?

And how was I to find myself at home?

The raven had said: *Do something.*

But what could I do here? And would that make me someone?

Because now—alas—I was nobody.

I followed the path he took. Slowly, I moved forward.

Soon, I saw a forest—tall, slender pines, standing like sentinels. The scent of pine filled the air. I hurried toward it, eager to disappear into its shadows.

At last, deep within the twilight, I saw something between two trees—a shimmering shape, without color, like heat rising from sunbaked ground, or the vibration of a struck string.

I approached. But as I drew near, it faded. When I stood beside it, I saw nothing—only the trees beyond, their forms uncertain, wavering.

I tried to pass between the trunks.

A jolt—then I stumbled, fell.

When I rose, I stood before the wooden wall of the garret chamber.

I turned.

There was the mirror.

And perched upon it—the black eagle—just as if it had landed moments ago.

Terror seized me.

I ran.

Outside the chamber, the garret spaces looked uncanny. They seemed to have been waiting—waiting for something. It had come. And now, they waited again.

On the winding stair, a shudder ran through me. The house had changed. It felt alive. Watching. Something would leap from behind!

I darted down, hit the wall, fell. Rose. Ran.

On the next floor, I lost my way. I passed the same corridors twice before finding the stairwell.

At the top of the great staircase, I steadied myself. I sat, breathing hard.

No. Never again. I would never climb that final, terrible stair.

The garret above—its presence weighed on the whole house. It sat upon it, heavy, threatening to crush me out.

It was the brain of the building—brooding, full of unseen dwellers. One might appear at any moment in the library where I sat.

I was nowhere safe.

I would sell this place. Let it go.

I would buy a crag in Switzerland—build a simple wooden house, one story high, no garret, no hidden rooms. Guarded by ancient peaks that would send down only rocks, not spirits.

I knew my thoughts were foolish. I even sensed a quiet mockery in them—like a whisper from the raven.

But then—his croak echoed again in my mind.

*If I don’t know my own garret,* I thought, *how can I trust my own brain?*

What is it generating now? What thought will it offer tomorrow? Next month? A year from now?

What lies at the core of my mind?

What is behind my *thinking*?

Am I even there?

Who—what—am I?

I could not answer. Not then. Not now.

I stood, trembling.

I crossed the room to the masked door—the one with the book protruding, half-hidden among soulless, faceless volumes.

It seemed to beckon.

I knelt. Opened it as far as possible.

Nothing.

I lit a taper.

Peered in—like gazing into the jaws of something reluctant.

The manuscript was verse.

Lines broken—beginnings on one page, endings on the other. I could not read a single complete line.

Yet the words—some fragments, some half-sentences—stirred something deep inside.

Not just emotion. Something deeper. Like the scent of an idea. A longing—unfamiliar, intense—to understand what the poem might hold—even in ruin.

I copied a few lines, tried to reconstruct them.

Failed.

Only weariness remained.

That night, I slept deeply—without dreams.

In the morning, the horror of the garret was gone.

But I knew—something had changed.

And I was not the same.
