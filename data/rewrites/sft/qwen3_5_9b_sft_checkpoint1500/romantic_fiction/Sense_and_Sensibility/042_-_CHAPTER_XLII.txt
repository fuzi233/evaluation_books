A final brief encounter on Harley Street concluded the sisters' time in town. There, Elinor accepted her brother's cheerful congratulations regarding their progress toward Barton—a journey accomplished thus far without financial strain—and learned that Colonel Brandon would accompany them to Cleveland within the next day or two. This exchange, paired with a tentative, likely futile invitation from Fanny to visit Norland, and a fervent, private promise from John to pay her a speedy visit at Delaford, constituted all future plans for rural reunion.

It struck Elinor as amusing how resolutely her circle conspired to exile her to Delaford; a destination she now dreaded above all others. To her brother and Mrs. Jennings, it appeared as her destined home, and even Lucy, upon their parting, pressed her with urgent entreaties to visit. Yet, as the date approached, the reality of leaving London, however painful, began to take shape.

Early in April, the travelers departed from their respective lodgings in Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, converging by appointment on the roads. Their journey, prolonged slightly for Charlotte and her infant, promised to span several days, with Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon joining them near Cleveland shortly after their arrival.

For Marianne, whose time in London had offered scant comfort yet held so many hopes for Willoughby, the prospect of departure was a torment. She lingered over memories of a house where she had last clung to illusions now shattered forever, weeping as she imagined leaving it to a man engaged in new schemes from which she was eternally excluded. Conversely, Elinor felt a clear, unburdened relief. She possessed no lingering attachments that would cause regret, no creature from whom separation would be a grief. Freed from the subtle persecution of Lucy's "friendship" and grateful to whisk her sister away unseen by the newly married Willoughby, Elinor looked forward with genuine hope to the tranquility of Barton, confident it would heal Marianne's mind and fortify her own.

The journey proceeded smoothly. By the third morning, they crossed into Somersetshire—the land of cherished memories for Marianne and prohibition for others—and drove up to Cleveland.

The estate was a spacious, modern residence perched upon a sloping lawn, lacking a park but boasting extensive pleasure grounds. A winding gravel path, bordered by a mix of firs, mountain ashes, acacias, and towering Lombardy poplars, concealed the offices from view. Marianne entered with a heart swollen with emotion, barely eighty miles from Barton and a mere thirty from Combe Magna. Before five minutes had elapsed, having helped show off the accommodations to the housekeeper, she fled again, slipping through the awakening shrubberies to climb a distant hill. From a Grecian temple atop the rise, her gaze wandered southeast, resting fondly on the farthest ridges of the horizon, imagining Combe Magna visible from these summits.

In moments of exquisite, agonizing misery, she rejoiced in being at Cleveland. As she returned, savoring the luxury of country liberty and solitary wandering, she resolved to dedicate nearly every hour of her stay to such rambling excursions. Just in time to join the others for a tour of the immediate grounds, she spent the remainder of the morning lounging around the kitchen garden, admiring the blooms, and listening to the gardener's woes about blight, or dawdling through the greenhouse where lost favorites elicited Charlotte's laughter, before inspecting the poultry yard, where the dairy-maid's disappointments regarding hens, foxes, and dwindling broods provided further amusement.

The weather remained fine and dry initially, leading Marianne to neglect calculating for a change. However, a sudden, settled rain thwarted her plans after dinner. While a cool twilight might have tempted her, the heavy downpour convinced even her that outdoor exercise was impossible. The party was small; Mrs. Palmer tended to her child, Mrs. Jennings to her carpet work, and they discussed old acquaintances and Lady Middleton's affairs, speculating on whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would travel past Reading. Elinor, though outwardly indifferent, contributed to the chat, while Marianne, possessing an uncanny knack for locating libraries, secured a book despite the family's general avoidance of the room.

Mrs. Palmer displayed a constant, friendly goodwill that made them feel welcome, her openness and hearty nature atoning for her occasional lack of polish. Her kindness, framed by a lovely countenance, was engaging, and her follies, though evident, were not conceited. Elinor could forgive everything save her laugh.

The following day saw the arrival of the two gentlemen, enlarging the party and invigorating the conversation, which had languished under the persistent rains. Elinor observed Mr. Palmer closely. In her limited interactions, he had shown varied addresses toward her and Marianne; here, he proved a perfect gentleman to visitors, though occasionally rough with his wife and mother-in-law. Capable of pleasant company, he was hindered by a self-importance that blinded him to the equality of others, particularly regarding Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte. His habits were marked by nicety in eating, irregularity in hours, an affectionate yet dismissive attitude toward his child, and idle mornings devoted to billiards rather than business. Overall, Elinor found him far more agreeable than anticipated, and inwardly relieved that she could like him no better, preferring the memory of Edward's generosity, simplicity, and humility over this man's epicurean selfishness.

Intelligence concerning Edward flowed from Colonel Brandon, who had recently visited Dorsetshire. Treating Elinor both as the impartial friend of Mr. Ferrars and a trusted confidante, Brandon spoke extensively of the parsonage at Delaford, detailing its deficiencies and outlining his intentions to remedy them. His behavior—marked by open pleasure in seeing her after ten days, a readiness to converse, and a respectful deference for her opinion—seemed to validate Mrs. Jennings's suspicions of his attachment. Yet, Elinor remained skeptical. Having long believed Marianne to be Colonel Brandon's true favorite, she could not accept the notion that he loved her. She prided herself on being the sharper observer of the pair. Where Mrs. Jennings saw only polite manners, Elinor read the stories behind the eyes. She noticed his look of anxious solicitude when Marianne, suffering from an unspoken heavy cold involving headaches and sore throats, failed to express her distress. While the older lady missed the signs, Elinor discerned the quick, needless alarms of a lover.

On the third and fourth evenings, two delightful twilight walks took them across the grounds, venturing deep into the wilder sections where ancient trees grew amidst long, wet grass. These excursions, compounded by Marianne's imprudence in remaining in wet shoes and stockings, resulted in a violent chill. Though initially dismissed or treated lightly, the ailment quickly escalated, drawing the concern of everyone present and forcing Marianne's attention to herself. Prescriptions poured in from all directions, which she, as was her custom, declined. Heavy, feverish, plagued by limb pains, cough, and a sore throat, she was told a single night's rest would suffice, yet it required Elinor's firm persuasion to attempt even the simplest remedies before retiring.
