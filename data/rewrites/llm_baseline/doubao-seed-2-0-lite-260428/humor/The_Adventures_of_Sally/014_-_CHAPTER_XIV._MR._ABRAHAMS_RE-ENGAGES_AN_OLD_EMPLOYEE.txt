Wise old sages will tell you the only genuine joy a human can get comes from spreading joy to their fellow man. By that metric, Bugs Butler should have been floating on a cloud of serene contentment the morning after the fight in Jersey City, when he blinked awake from a nice little coma mid-ring to realize Lew Lucas had flattened him stone cold with a left hook followed by a jaw-smashing right in round three. He’d failed to block a punch so thoroughly it had brightened the day of roughly two dozen people, minimum.

Lew Lucas himself was grinning so wide his cheeks ached. His second was so overjoyed he planted a big sloppy kiss right on his cheek, and 90% of the crowd that had bet their week’s wages on Lucas were whooping so loud the rafters shook. But Bugs? He was in a filthy temper, slouching out of the ring under a thundercloud of a frown, singlehandedly killing the vibe of the entire post-fight party.

But the good vibes he’d accidentally manufactured kept spreading long after he’d stormed off to ice his jaw. The two local wise guys who couldn’t score ringside seats got the fight results off their ticker tape and whooped so loud their neighbor banged on the wall, $500 richer each. The pimply teen office boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises got so excited reading the results on his morning commute he yelled so loud in the subway that a guy with a violin case nearly dropped his lunch. And Sally, tucking into her toast and coffee at her little apartment, cackled out loud at the sports page when she saw the play-by-play of Bugs getting absolutely demolished. She wasn’t the type to hold a grudge against anyone, but Bugs Butler had earned himself a spot on her very short “people I want to trip on a banana peel” list.

Lew Lucas, she decided, was her new favorite person. If he’d been Ginger’s blood brother he couldn’t have avenged that public humiliation more perfectly in three short rounds. First round: he’d decorated Bugs’ left eye into a lovely mottled Easter egg. Second round: he’d pounded his midsection so hard Bugs was still going to be feeling it at Christmas. Third round: lights out. What more could you ask for? Sally raised her coffee mug in a toast, hoped Lew Lucas’ mom was hanging a prize ribbon on the wall for him, and the phone rang.

“Hello?”
“Oh, hi,” said a voice.
“GINGER!” Sally yelped, so delighted she nearly spilled her coffee all over the sports page. “I say, I’m chuffed you’re back! Only got your letter this morning, found it at my old boarding house when I popped by to pick up my leftover socks and —”
“Ginger, your voice is better than a whole gramophone of opera, get over here right now. Where even are you?”
“At the chemist’s across the street, actually, I was just wondering if —”
“Come up this second!”
“Wait, really? I was just about to ask permission —”
“You terrible creature, why haven’t you come to see me the second you got back?”
“Well, y’see, I haven’t exactly been out and about much the last day or so. I —”
“Oh right, of course!” Sally’s face softened immediately, and she shot a grateful glance at the giant photo of a grinning Lew Lucas staring up at her from the newspaper. “You poor thing! How are you feeling?”
“Oh, all right, nothing to complain about.”
“Then hurry over. I’ve got extra pastries.”

There was a little awkward pause on the other end of the line.
“Er — Sally? I should warn you. I’m not much to look at right now.”
“You never were. Stop stalling and run up.”
“No, I mean —”

Sally hung up the receiver. She bounced over to the door, waiting, and when the bell rang she wrenched it open and recoiled so fast she nearly tripped over her own rug.
“Oh, Ginger!”

He hadn’t been exaggerating. His right eye was bright and clear as a sunny day, but the left one was completely buried under a puffy purple bruise you could have mistaken for a plum someone stuck to his face. His left cheekbone was streaked black and blue, and his lips were so swollen he spoke like he’d stuffed two marshmallows in his mouth.
“Not bad, really,” he mumbled. “Most of the swelling’s already gone down.”
“It’s horrifying! You poor darling!” Sally clenched her fists. “I wish Lucas had knocked him into next week, the brute!”
“Eh? What? Bugs? Oh, he’s not that bad, y’know.” Ginger’s innate sense of fair play kicked in, even though half his face was still screaming in protest. “Look at it from his shoes, right? A sparring partner he’s paying to take easy hits suddenly decides to go all Rocky on him mid-exhibition, of course he got a bit spooked. Totally reasonable reaction. It was all my fault, anyway. I saw you watching, and he was being a total ass, and I just sort of lost my head. Shouldn’t have done it. A sparring partner’s supposed to soak hits, not dish ‘em out.”

“Sit down,” Sally said, pushing him onto her couch. “Ginger, you’re too nice to survive in this world. If someone sandbagged you and stole your wallet you’d probably turn around and say he probably had a good reason for it. I, meanwhile, am a terrible person, and I wish Bugs Butler had tripped over the ring ropes and fallen into a vat of custard. I’d go dance at his funeral. Now — why on earth did you take that sparring partner job anyway?”
“Well, it was the only gig going at the time! I’ve boxed since I was a kid, I was in great shape, the pay was way better than waiting tables, and it seemed fun —”
“Fun? Getting smacked in the jaw every five minutes?”
“Oh, you don’t really notice after the first few hits. Plus, after your brother gave me the sack —”

Sally yelped so loud Ginger jumped.
“Wait a second! I drove all the way out to White Plains that exact afternoon to yell at Fillmore for sacking you, and then I got distracted and completely forgot to bring it up, and I haven’t been able to track him down since! Why did he fire you, anyway?”
“Ah, that. Just a silly mix-up. Misunderstanding, y’know.”
“What kind of misunderstanding? Spit it out. I sent you to him, I have a right to know what went wrong.”

Ginger squirmed, his already purple face turning an even darker shade of purple. “Well, Gladys — that’s Fillmore’s wife, right? — popped into the office one morning, and we started messing around —”
“Messing around?”
“Chasing each other, sort of. She grabbed something I was holding and wouldn’t give it back, so I chased her around the desk, and I just caught her by the wrists when Fillmore walked in. He’d driven her to the office, hung back to chat with the doorman, walked in right at the exact worst possible moment. Thought I was attacking his wife, obviously. Acted totally normal, didn’t say a word, then called me into his office an hour later and handed me my walking papers.”
“Wait, what did she steal from you?” Sally said, leaning forward.
“Ah. Just a thing.”
“Ginger, I will get the corkscrew out of the kitchen if you don’t tell me.”

“Fine! It was a photograph. Of you.”
Sally stared. “But I never gave you a photo of mine!”
“Er. No. I sort of stole it. Remember that day I helped you tear the whole apartment apart looking for that missing photo you thought you lost? I had it in my pocket the whole time. Didn’t have the guts to ask you for it.”
Sally blinked, then started cackling. “You absolute fraud! You humbug! I thought you were being so helpful! I had no idea you were such a devious little sneak. That’s the best news I’ve heard all week.”

Ginger scratched the back of his neck, sheepish. “You’re not mad, right? I just really liked it.”
“Mad? That’s the best compliment anyone’s ever given me. So I’ve ruined your career twice over, huh? First you got chased out of your rich uncle’s house for my sake, then you got fired from Fillmore’s office because of a photo of me. I’m your very own personal bad luck charm. That means it’s 100% my job to find you a new, proper job that doesn’t involve people smashing your face in.”
“Nah, don’t bother, I’ll find something —”
“No, no, we’re brainstorming right now. What are you good at? You’ve tried waiting tables, sparring, being Fillmore’s assistant, none of those stuck. We need something you’re actually built for. Not street cleaning, not delivering parcels, something where you end up sitting in a big fancy office chair as the president of something, right? Think.”
“Er. Well. If I had a little bit of startup capital…”
“Startup capital! Ooh, big business tycoon moves. What would you do with your little startup capital, Mr. Carnegie?”
“Run a dog thingamajig.”

Sally blinked. “A dog what?”
“Y’know. A place for dogs. Breed ‘em, sell ‘em, win dog shows, all that stuff. Makes great money. I talked to a guy who ran one out in Long Island when I was working for Fillmore, he was looking to sell the whole place off cheap.”
Sally stared at him, then burst out laughing. “A kennels! You couldn’t just say kennels, could you? Had to call it a dog thingamajig. But wait — that’s perfect! Remember that day in Roville, when a whole pack of stray dogs started a riot in the street? You strolled in, whispered something to them, and they all sat down like perfect little angels? You’re basically a dog whisperer. That’s the job for you.”
“Apparently it pays 100% returns on your initial investment, too,” Ginger said, brightening up.
“100%? That sounds like the kind of scam Fillmore pitches to producers. Let’s say 99% to be safe. You’re going to be the Dog King of Long Island. But what’s the price tag on that place you mentioned?”
“Ah. The catch. He wants five thousand bucks. Total.”

“Done. I’ll lend you the five grand.”
“Absolutely not,” Ginger said, so firm he almost split his swollen lip.
Sally groaned. “Ginger, don’t be ridiculous. This is a business deal! I’m not handing you charity money, I’m investing in a very sure thing. Queen Isabella didn’t make Columbus feel weird about taking her money to sail to America, did she? You have to pay me interest, obviously.”
“25% interest a year,” Ginger said immediately.
“Absolutely not. 3%.”
“Three percent? That’s pocket change! 20% minimum.”
“5%. That’s my offer.”
“10%.”
“6%!” Sally yelled, holding up six fingers. “That’s final. No haggling, no negotiating. 6% a year.”
Ginger did the math in his head. 6% of five grand was $300 a year. “That’s way too little —”
“Take it or leave it,” Sally said, grinning. “Now go call that guy right now, before some other dog lover snaps the whole place up. Go go go!”
Ginger paused at the door, still looking uncertain, but Sally shooed him out.

Sally carried her paper over to the window after he left, to re-read the fight report for the third time and people-watch the quiet street below her apartment building. It was a perfect sunny day, everyone looked happy, and she was half-convinced this whole dog kennel plan was going to make Ginger a millionaire before the year was out.

Then a taxi pulled up outside, and out stumbled her brother Fillmore. She was about to wave and holler at him through the open window, when his behavior got so weird she froze. He looked like he was rehearsing a new dance craze no one had heard of yet. First he stormed purposefully towards the front door, then skidded to a halt and ran back to the sidewalk. He did that three whole times, stood there frowning like he was arguing with a ghost, made one last mad dash for the door, then turned around and sprinted off down the street like someone had lit his coat on fire.

Sally was so confused she almost forgot to breathe. Why take a taxi all the way over just to run away at the front step? Then the phone rang, and Fillmore’s voice came over the line, hoarse like he’d been eating sawdust.
“Sally? You saw that, didn’t you.”
“Hi Fill! What do you call that new dance you invented? The Doorstep Shuffle? I was very impressed.”
“Ugh. I came over to talk to you, but — er — I couldn’t work up the nerve. Sally, Gladys is coming over to see you right now. I told her to head straight over. Will you be home?”
“Sure, I’ll stay in. Is everything okay? You sound like you’ve seen a ghost.”
“Everything’s — well. Not great. She’s got bad news to tell you. I’ll — I’ll leave you two to it.” And he hung up before Sally could ask any more questions.

Ten minutes later the doorbell rang, and Gladys was there, hugging Sally so tight her ribs creaked. She still had that same no-nonsense, warm personality she’d had before marrying Fillmore, but there was a little frown on her face that Sally had never seen before.
After a minute of casual chit-chat about Sally’s trip to England, Gladys leaned forward, sighed, and said the thing Sally had been dreading. “Sally, who’s this rich guy Carmyle over in England that Fillmore says proposed to you? You couldn’t have changed your mind about saying no, right? Because —”

And that’s when Sally realized the bad news wasn’t about her. It was about Elsa.
