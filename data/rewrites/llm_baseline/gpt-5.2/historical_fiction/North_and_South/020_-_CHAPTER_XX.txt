Margaret went home as one carries a dish too full through a crowded room: every step a peril, every jostle a spill. What she had seen and heard clung to her, not as an idea but as a taste—metallic and bitter at the back of the tongue—so that when she crossed the threshold and met her mother’s expectant look, she had to gather herself, as if to set a table for cheerfulness while her hands still smelt of another house’s grief.

Mrs. Hale, immured now, made of Margaret’s smallest errand a messenger of the world.

“And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?” she asked, with that half-pleading eagerness of an invalid who lives by reports and descriptions, as by spoonfuls.

“She was so ill I never thought of asking her,” said Margaret; and her voice had the flatness of sorrow that has been held too long.

“Dear! Everybody is ill now, I think,” returned Mrs. Hale, with the quick, sharp jealousy sick people sometimes turn upon other sufferers, before their natural kindness resumes its place. “But it must be very sad to be ill in one of those little back streets.” The Helstone habit of imagining cottages with honeysuckle and a clean doorstep came back upon her. “It’s bad enough here. What could you do for her, Margaret? Mr. Thornton has sent me some of his old port wine since you went out. Would a bottle of that do her good, think you?”

“No, mamma! I don’t believe they are very poor,—at least, they don’t speak as if they were; and, at any rate, Bessy’s illness is consumption—she won’t want wine. Perhaps I might take her a little preserve, made of our dear Helstone fruit.”

As she spoke, she saw in fancy the glass jar, the clear amber set thick with soft pieces of plum and apricot, as if sunshine had been sealed for winter. The very thought brought the orchard-scent—warm skins, crushed leaves, the green sharpness of stalks—up against the city’s soot and steam. But even that comfort turned, for another family rose before her; and then the day’s sights broke their bounds.

“No! there’s another family to whom I should like to give—Oh mamma, mamma! how am I to dress up in my finery, and go off and away to smart parties, after the sorrow I’ve seen to-day?” she cried, and told her mother all—Higgins’s close room, the sick girl’s breath, the talk of hunger and pride, and the story of the man who had been driven to desperation.

It distressed Mrs. Hale as if Margaret had brought the back street into the drawing-room. She could not sit still with knowledge that demanded action; she must do something, at once, to turn pain into a visible, handleable remedy.

“Pack up a basket,” she said, and would not hear of delay. The drawing-room became a kind of improvised pantry and store. Margaret brought out what could be spared—the loaf with its firm crust, slices of cold meat, a little tea, sugar wrapped carefully against damp; the jar of preserve she had named, its lid tied down, as if the very sweetness might escape. The linen smelt faintly of lavender, that domestic scent of old order. Mrs. Hale watched and directed, restless, her cheeks a little flushed with the agitation of charity.

“It will do if it goes in the morning,” Margaret ventured, because she had left money with Bessy and knew Higgins had not let them go wholly unfed for the moment.

Mrs. Hale’s eyes flashed. “Unfeeling!” she said, and would not breathe freely until Dixon had been summoned and the basket sent out that instant, as if the speed itself were a salve.

Only when the door had closed upon it, and the house had resumed its quiet, did doubt creep in like a draught.

“After all,” Mrs. Hale said, “we may have been doing wrong. It was only the last time Mr. Thornton was here that he said, those were no true friends who helped to prolong the struggle by assisting the turn-outs. And this Boucher-man was a turn-out, was he not?”

Mr. Hale came up then, as he often did, after a lesson with Mr. Thornton that had slipped into their accustomed talk. The question was put to him, and he, trying to hold the scales evenly, repeated what had sounded so clear half an hour before, when it had been spoken in Mr. Thornton’s steady voice. Yet when he looked at his wife and daughter, and at Margaret’s set, pale face, logic softened into human compromise.

“You have done right—quite right—in this instance,” he said. “I do not see how you could have done otherwise.” Then, as if he must save the principle though he spared the particular case, he added, “Still, as a general rule, what Mr. Thornton says has truth. If a strike is prolonged, it ends—often as it has ended before—in masters bringing hands from a distance; or else in the invention of some machine that lessens the need of hands altogether. Then refusing help is, in a certain view, the kindest thing; it will not bolster men up in folly.”

But he would go himself in the morning to see Boucher, to learn the man’s condition, and to judge what might be done without feeding the quarrel. Margaret hardly heard the policy; her mind stayed with the children’s faces, the thinness of the wife’s arms, the way a teacup had seemed in that house as precious as a jewel.

Mr. Hale went as he had said, early. Boucher was out; but the wife, worn into a kind of quiet endurance, talked with him. He promised to ask for an Infirmary order for her; and seeing what Mrs. Hale had sent—good things laid out, already attacked with the eager fingers of children who had learned by want to snatch at plenty while it lasted—he came back with a more consoling account than Margaret had dared to hope for. Indeed, she had prepared him the night before for a misery so complete, that, by the rebound of imagination, he described all as brighter than the truth.

“But I will go again, and see the man himself,” he said. “I hardly know, as yet, how to compare one of these houses with our Helstone cottages. I see furniture here which our labourers would never have thought of buying, and food commonly used which they would consider luxuries; yet for these very families there seems no other resource now that their weekly wages are stopped, but the pawn-shop. One had need to learn a different standard, up here in Milton.”

Bessy, too, was rather better that day; so weak, however, that she seemed to have forgotten the feverish longing to see Margaret dressed, if ever it had been more than a wandering fancy. And now Thursday came, with its strange demand that Margaret should put on splendour while her heart felt hung with lead.

The toilette, once a girlish pleasure shared with Edith—laughing over ribbons and the fall of a sleeve—was now a duty, performed like laying out best linen for a guest one does not wish to entertain. Still, there was one sweetness in it: her mother’s delight. Mrs. Hale watched every touch, as if the sight fed her.

Dixon, flinging the drawing-room door open with ceremony, cried out to her mistress, “Miss Hale looks well, ma’am,—doesn’t she? Mrs. Shaw’s coral couldn’t have come in better. It just gives the right touch of colour, ma’am. Otherwise, Miss Margaret, you would have been too pale.”

Margaret’s black hair, too thick for plaiting, had been twisted and compressed into heavy coils that circled her head like a crown, then gathered into a great spiral knot behind, the glossy strands catching light as oil catches flame. Two long coral pins—small arrows—held the weight in place. Her white silk sleeves were looped with white strings, and at her throat lay a heavy necklace of coral beads, the colour of crushed berries, or the inner flesh of a shell—warm against her skin’s milk-white coolness.

“Oh, Margaret!” Mrs. Hale said, and the old life in her broke out in a little burst of maternal vanity. “How I should like to be going with you to one of the old Barrington assemblies,—taking you as Lady Beresford used to take me.”

Margaret kissed her, and tried to answer with a smile, but it was faint. “I would rather stay at home with you,—much rather, mamma.”

“Nonsense, darling! Be sure you notice the dinner well. I shall like to hear how they manage these things in Milton. Particularly the second course, dear. Look what they have instead of game.”

Margaret promised, as one promises a child, though she thought it bitter that, in the midst of talk of courses and second courses, men and women were measuring out hunger by the day. Yet she carried her mother’s wish with her like a small task, and perhaps it gave her a way of turning away from the heavier thoughts.

At the Thorntons’ the dinner-table, when she saw it, was less a meal than a display of conquest. The linen lay white and hard as if ironed into pride; silver flashed; cut glass cut the candlelight into sharp stars. Dishes came up in a succession that seemed meant to prove there was no limit: soups rich with long-boiled bones and herbs, fish dressed to whiteness, joints that had been basted till the outer fat turned to a crisp brown shell. Sauces—thick, glossy, carefully strained—clung to spoon and tongue. There were small dainties too—jellies quivering like captured light, creams whipped to air, fruits out of their season, as if the very climate had been purchased. Margaret, with her cultivated taste, felt the abundance oppressive. Half would have been enough; less would have been more elegant. Yet it was one of Mrs. Thornton’s rigorous laws that of each separate dainty there should be enough for every guest to choose, as if hospitality meant not offering, but supplying.

Mrs. Thornton herself, careless to abstemiousness in daily life, set feasts as a point of honour; and her son shared the same feeling, though he was denying himself, privately, the expenditure of an unnecessary sixpence. He had more than once regretted sending out the invitations at such a time; and yet, since the thing was done, he was glad of the old magnificence, as if it proved that nothing—not even this strike—could disorder his household.

Margaret and her father were the first to arrive, Mr. Hale anxiously punctual. Upstairs, in the drawing-room, only Mrs. Thornton and Fanny waited. Every cover had been taken off; the room blazed with yellow silk damask and a carpet thick with brilliant flowers. Ornaments filled every corner until the eye grew weary of being asked to admire. And through the windows the great mill-yard stared back—wide folding gates open for carriages, and the factory itself looming on the left, high and many-storied, throwing a shadow that darkened the summer evening before its time.

“My son was engaged up to the last moment on business. He will be here directly, Mr. Hale. May I beg you to take a seat?” said Mrs. Thornton.

Mr. Hale had been looking out. He turned, and spoke with the delicate discomfort of a man new to Milton. “Don’t you find such close neighbourhood to the mill rather unpleasant at times?”

Mrs. Thornton drew herself up, as if he had spoken ill of a member of the family. “Never. I am not become so fine as to desire to forget the source of my son’s wealth and power. Besides, there is not such another factory in Milton. One room alone is two hundred and twenty square yards.”

“I meant that the smoke and the noise—the constant going out and coming in of the workpeople—might be annoying!”

“I agree with you, Mr. Hale!” cried Fanny. “There is a continual smell of steam and oily machinery—and the noise is perfectly deafening.”

Mrs. Thornton’s sternness hardened into scorn. “I have heard noise that was called music far more deafening. The engine-room is at the street-end of the factory; we hardly hear it, except in summer weather, when all the windows are open; and as for the continual murmur of the workpeople, it disturbs me no more than the humming of a hive of bees. If I think of it at all, I connect it with my son, and feel how all belongs to him, and that his is the head that directs it. Just now, there are no sounds to come from the mill; the hands have been ungrateful enough to turn out, as perhaps you have heard. But the very business”—and she bit the words as if they had grit in them—“had reference to the steps he is going to take to make them learn their place.”

The room, with its heavy fabrics and crowded ornaments, seemed to hold in sound. Outside, Milton’s accustomed iron breathing was muted by the strike: no clatter of clogs, no ceaseless mechanical throb. That absence had a quality of its own, like a pause in a harsh song. Margaret, later, would feel it as keenly as any noise—silence, not peace.

Mrs. Thornton’s dark anger did not clear away when Mr. Thornton entered. She saw, at once, the weight of care he could not shake off, though he greeted his guests with a cheerfulness that looked cordial.

He shook hands with Margaret. He knew—though she did not—that it was the first time their hands had met; the touch was brief, as if manners were a glove. He inquired after Mrs. Hale, and listened to Mr. Hale’s sanguine account; then glanced at Margaret to see if she contradicted it by expression. Her face gave no dissent.

And in that glance he was struck anew by her beauty. He had not seen her thus dressed. Now it seemed to him that such elegance belonged to her, as if she ought always to move in white silk and coral, her head crowned with that massive, glossy hair. She was talking to Fanny; he could not hear the words. He watched his sister’s fidgeting—hands tugging at a ribbon, eyes wandering without aim—and compared it, uneasily, to Margaret’s steady composure. Her large soft eyes rested on one object at a time, as if their look could quiet whatever they touched. Her red lips curved and parted in listening; her head bent a little forward, making a long sweeping line from the light on her raven hair down to the smooth ivory shoulder. Her round white arms and taper hands lay lightly across each other, perfectly still.

He sighed at the sight, then, with an effort, turned away and threw himself into conversation with Mr. Hale, as if business of the mind were safer ground than the sight of a woman who could stir him into unrest.

More people arrived. Fanny went to help her mother receive them. In the influx, Mr. Thornton became conscious—almost irritably—of one thing: no one was speaking to Margaret. It was like a discord in the room’s hum. Yet he did not go to her; he did not look at her. Still he knew, with an odd exactness, what she did and did not do, more certainly than he knew any other movement in the crowded room.

Margaret, for her part, was unconscious of herself. She watched, quietly amused, the stir and show, as if observing a play whose rules she did not quite believe in. Somebody took her down to dinner—she did not catch the name—and he did not trouble himself to talk much.

At table, the gentlemen’s talk rose and fell with animation; the ladies, for the most part, were silent, busy noting dishes, criticising dress, measuring one another by lace and the fall of a sleeve. Margaret’s mind, starved for earnest speech, caught the gentlemen’s discussion like a hand closing over warmth. Mr. Horsfall, a stranger—the visit that had been the germ of the party—asked questions about the trade and manufactures; the Milton men answered, eager, technical, proud. A dispute sprang up, contested warmly; it was referred to Mr. Thornton, who had scarcely spoken. Now he gave his opinion, the grounds laid out as plainly as if he were setting down accounts. Even opponents yielded.

Margaret’s attention fixed on him. Here, as master of his house and among his equals, his manner was straightforward, simple, modest—thoroughly dignified. She thought she had never seen him to such advantage. When he had come to their home, there had always been something of over-eagerness, or of vexed annoyance, as if he pre-supposed himself unjustly judged and yet was too proud to explain. But here there was no uncertainty of place. These men regarded him as force, as power. He did not struggle for their respect; he had it, and he knew it. Security gave him a grand quietness of voice and gesture that she had missed before.

He spoke little to ladies; what he did say was formal. To Margaret he hardly spoke at all.

To her surprise she enjoyed the dinner. She understood now many local interests; she even caught technical words. She took, silently, a decided part in the question, aligning herself with arguments, rejecting others. At least these men talked as if what they said mattered; there was none of the used-up wit and languid phrases that had wearied her in London drawing-rooms. And she wondered, with a sort of puzzled relief, that no one alluded to the strike. She did not yet know how coolly such things were taken by masters, as having only one possible end. The men, they thought, were hurting themselves as they had done before. If they chose to be fools and put their fate into the hands of paid delegates, they must take consequences. Thornton would lose, of course; it was an accident that might visit any of them. But Thornton was iron; he would manage a strike as well as any. The hands had mistaken their man.

After dinner, when the ladies were left together, the talk shrank to smallness again. Margaret grew sleepy with it, and glad when the gentlemen returned—not only for the comfort of catching her father’s eye, which always seemed to steady her, but because she could listen once more to something larger than petty rivalries.

She liked, too, the exultation in power these Milton men displayed. It could be rampant; it could savour of boasting. Yet there was something admirable in their intoxication, not with wine but with achievement—with the memory of what had been wrested from matter, and the anticipation of what would yet be done. Their imagination leaped forward beyond their own lifetimes. They spoke of changes none of them would live to see, and forgot themselves in that far triumph. In her cooler moments she might not approve the spirit in all things; but she could not deny the vigour.

She started a little when Mr. Thornton spoke suddenly close at her elbow.

“I could see you were on our side in our discussion at dinner,—were you not, Miss Hale?”

“Certainly. But then I know so little about it. I was surprised, however, to find from what Mr. Horsfall said, that there were others who thought in so diametrically opposite a manner, as the Mr. Morison he spoke about. He cannot be a gentleman—is he?”

He looked at her, and there was a brief hardness in his mouth, as if the word pricked him.

“I am not quite the person to decide on another’s gentlemanliness, Miss Hale. I mean, I don’t quite understand your application of the word. But I should say that this Morison is no true man. I don’t know who he is; I merely judge him from Mr. Horsfall’s account.”

“I suspect my ‘gentleman’ includes your ‘true man.’”

“And a great deal more, you would imply. I differ from you. A man is to me a higher and a completer being than a gentleman.”

“What do you mean?” asked Margaret. “We must understand the words differently.”

“I take it that ‘gentleman’ is a term that only describes a person in his relation to others; but when we speak of him as ‘a man,’ we consider him not merely with regard to his fellow-men, but in relation to himself,—to life—to time—to eternity. A cast-away lonely as Robinson Crusoe—a prisoner immured in a dungeon for life—nay, even a saint in Patmos, has his endurance, his strength, his faith, best described by being spoken of as ‘a man.’ I am rather weary of this word ‘gentlemanly,’ which seems to me to be often inappropriately used, and often, too, with such exaggerated distortion of meaning, while the full simplicity of the noun ‘man,’ and the adjective ‘manly’ are acknowledged—that I am induced to class it with the cant of the day.”

Margaret paused, feeling a slow conviction shaping itself, as if she were trying to translate one dialect into another and finding no exact equivalence. Before she could answer, he was called away by the eager manufacturers.

She could not hear their speeches, but the meaning came through in scraps, as sound carries through walls: anxiety, counsel, risk. Mr. Thornton’s replies came back short, clear, steady—like the boom of a distant minute-gun marking time with inevitability.

“That has been done,” she heard him say.

A hurried murmur, more than one voice.

“All those arrangements have been made.”

Mr. Slickson, anxious and insinuating, took hold of Mr. Thornton’s arm as if to anchor him. Doubts were urged, difficulties named. Mr. Thornton moved slightly away—only slightly—and lifted his eyebrows a fraction.

“I take the risk,” he said. “You need not join in it unless you choose.”

Still more fears: hints of what men might do in anger when they are hungry, and when the mills stand silent but for resentment.

“I’m not afraid of anything so dastardly as incendiarism,” Mr. Thornton answered, with a contempt that rang through his composure. “We are open enemies; and I can protect myself from any violence that I apprehend. And I will assuredly protect all others who come to me for work. They know my determination by this time, as well and as fully as you do.”

Mr. Horsfall drew him a little aside; Margaret supposed it was to ask more about the strike. In truth, it was to inquire who she was—so quiet, so stately, so beautiful.

“A Milton lady?” asked he.

“No! from the south of England—Hampshire, I believe,” said Mr. Thornton, and the cold indifference of his tone was like a shutter closing.

Elsewhere in the room, Mrs. Slickson, with the cheerful curiosity of those who love to place people upon a social shelf, was catechising Fanny.

“Who is that fine distinguished-looking girl? a sister of Mr. Horsfall’s?”

“Oh dear, no! That is Mr. Hale, her father, talking now to Mr. Stephens. He gives lessons; that is to say, he reads with young men. My brother John goes to him twice a week, and so he begged mamma to ask them here, in hopes of getting him known. I believe we have some of their prospectuses, if you would like to have one.”

“Mr. Thornton! Does he really find time to read with a tutor, in the midst of all his business,—and this abominable strike in hand as well?”

Fanny coloured, uncertain whether pride or shame was expected of her, as if her feelings must take their rule from another’s “ought.” But the company began to break up; shawls were drawn on, farewells said, the flow of voices thinned. The night air outside received them, and with it the faint, far-off stir of Milton—its mixed breath of smoke and summer, the hush where the mill’s usual roar should have been, and beneath that hush the uneasy sense of men waiting, as if the whole town were listening for the next sound it would be forced to learn.
