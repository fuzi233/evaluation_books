The hidden passage had been cunningly sealed from outside, but it was no mere man-made tunnel: it was one of the ancient lava veins where Earth’s fiery core once ran red hot eons prior. The molten rock had long since cooled, but the air still hung thick, warm, and acrid with sulphur, burning the back of my throat the second I stepped inside. I fished a tallow lamp from its carved niche in the stone wall and marched fast, coughing so hard my ribs ached, cursing the fact my entire pack of healing herbs, the only treasure I’d brought back from the Yucatan vice-royalty, had gone to the bottom of the sea during that naval fight against Dason’s raiders. Since I’d landed back in Atlantis, there’d been no spare time to restock simple remedies for a wracking catarrh.

The network of old lava veins branched like a madman’s map. Any soul who stumbled in by accident would have gotten hopelessly lost, or plunged straight down one of the unmarked pits that dropped miles to the still-boiling magma below. But the 20-year-old initiation rite I’d taken when I was named to the Seven Priests had seared the correct path into my memory, no map needed. The floor jutted with jagged cooled rock, the air grew fouler the further I went, and I knew full well the sun would be high overhead long before I crawled out on the other side of the city walls, inside Atlantis’ inner precincts.

The vow of the Seven slowed me to a crawl, though: we were strictly forbidden from stepping out of this secret passage in front of any mortal eye, no matter how urgent the emergency. One exit after another, I found crowds loitering, chattering too loud to slip past unseen. I’d planned to emerge inside the royal pyramid, but the place hummed like a kicked beehive, no chance to sneak out unobserved. The next five outlets were all the same: the whole city was roiling with some wild, unnameable excitement.

At last I found an exit behind the crumbling statue of a minor, all-but-forgotten god, and paused to listen. No sound. The floors, the worshipper’s stone pews, were thick with undisturbed dust—this temple had sat abandoned for months. I shifted the stone covering the exit, stepped out, slid it back into place, then grabbed the frayed broom propped against a pew and swept away every trace of my footprints. But when I got to the main temple door, it was sealed tight. Even as a high priest, I didn’t know the secret mechanism for the massive stone slab that blocked the way. Trapped.

No one but this temple’s own attendant priests could open that door. If every last one of them had run off to gawk at whatever spectacle was whipping the city into a frenzy, I’d be stuck here forever. I followed the dark corridor back to the priests’ quarters built into the temple roof, half expecting every room to be empty. I already knew the old order had been shattered, but I was only just starting to grasp how ruthlessly the old traditions had been torn down to make way for the new.

I rapped the sacred call on the first door I found, and it swung open. The man peering at me through the dimness was Ro, my old classmate from the sacred college. Everyone had called him the dullard back then: he could barely memorize the god rites, knew nothing of statecraft, was useless with a weapon. The only reason he’d climbed to second custodian of this forgotten temple was that every single one of his superiors had abandoned their posts. His beard was badly curled in the latest fashion, his robes unbrushed and ill-fitting, just as slovenly as I remembered.

“Phorenice, that impious tyrant, has declared herself a living goddess,” he mumbled, when I asked what had the whole city so riled up. “She says she’ll light the public sacrifice with her own divine fire, and she’ll do it too. She gets everything she wants. The old cult of the true gods is bleeding dry. No one brings offerings anymore. And yesterday? The high priest here tried to do the weekly sun sacrifice at the great stone circle. One of those giant featherless man-eating birds from the northern mountains swooped down right in the middle of the rite. The priests were unarmed, per the rubric, trusting the Sun God to guard them. The bird tore the sacrifice to pieces, mauled my superior so bad he’s lying half-dead on my bed right now. The crowd screamed that the Sun God was weak, that no old god was left to protect them. Then someone shouted Phorenice was the new goddess, and the whole circle took up the cry. Today she’s leading the sacrifice herself to prove it.”

I tensed. The city gates were under siege by rebel forces, I could hear the faint, constant crash of rams against stone even in this remote temple, and no one was paying it a single mind. The law had dissolved completely, Ro added: people were murdering each other in the streets for scraps of food, no one buried the dead, five months of no offerings had left him surviving on his own hidden jars of corn.

“Open the doors,” I told him. “I have to see this for myself.”

He hesitated, fumbled with the levers, and the massive stone slab rumbled ajar. I stepped out onto the street, and the surging, weapon-carrying crowd swept me along with them, all of us heading straight for the ancient Sun God’s stone circle. The once-clean paved streets were ankle-deep in mud, scattered with the bones of people who’d starved and been left to rot where they fell. Savage chaos lapped at the feet of Atlantis’ grand pyramids and gilded palaces, a sick, jarring contrast.

When we reached the sun circle, I stared up at the 49 massive uncut stones, set in a perfect ring, lintel stones balancing across their tops so heavy no modern Atlantean engineer could have moved them even with a hundred teams of men. The crowd packed around the outer perimeter, no common soul allowed past the line on pain of death, and we waited for hours in the brutal midday sun. Dozens fainted from heat and hunger, several died where they stood, no one paused to help the fallen. The crash of the rebel siege engines boomed louder and louder from the walls, and no one turned their head.

At last the drums blared. The procession rounded the corner, glittering with gold, the lords and new court priests draped in silk while the crowd starved at their feet. Then Phorenice rode into view, perched in the golden howdah I’d once sat in myself, on the back of her giant shaggy mammoth. The sullen, hungry faces of the crowd lit up like they’d all been handed a full meal, and they roared her name unbidden, no guards forcing them to cheer. Even from a distance I could see Ylga, the fan girl, Zaemon’s own daughter, standing stiff and pained in her place beside Phorenice, her face heavy with dread. She knew what sacrilege was about to unfold.

Phorenice stepped down the ivory stair from the mammoth’s back, and strode straight across the sacred sun circle, no old high priest leading her. The men trailing behind her, a mix of her loyal laymen and traitor priests from our own sacred clan, dragged a live cloven-hoofed plains horse to the altar stone, bound it, and laid it on the stacked pyre. They stepped back, leaving Phorenice standing alone before the carved altar, the image of the snake and outstretched hand of the Sun God’s life glowing in the sun on its face.

She called out in the common tongue, so every last person could hear her, raising her arms to the clear, bright blue sky. No one noticed the tiny, whirling ball of mist that appeared out of nowhere, growing darker and denser by the second, until it cast a dark shadow over the whole circle, full of tiny stabs of silent, dancing flame. She cried out to the Sun, claiming she was his daughter, that she was the new goddess of Atlantis, that he send down divine fire to light the sacrifice as proof.

A blinding flash spat down from the cloud. The horse neighed once, convulsed, and fell dead. Fire crackled across the pyre, the smell of burning meat curling through the air. The dark cloud dissolved instantly, the flames burned bright against the clear blue sky, and a thin spiral of blue smoke drifted straight upward.

Phorenice stood before the altar, flushed, exultant, radiantly beautiful. The priests inside the circle, the thousands of people outside, all threw themselves to the ground, foreheads pressed to the stone. “Phorenice! Goddess!” they screamed, the roar echoing off the great standing stones.

I did not kneel. I refused to bow to this apostasy. I stood straight, in the middle of the screaming, prostrate crowd, and waited for whatever fate would come for me next.
