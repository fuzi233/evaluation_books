"Where are the true men?"

"Here we stand. God bless you all!"

A rush surged toward the stairs, where half a dozen hands, hard yet warm with brotherhood, reached out to grasp the rescuers.

"You have saved our lives, lads," cried Denys, his voice thick with relief. "You have saved us this very night."

The scene that greeted the rescued pair was wild and stark. Torches flared, casting dancing shadows against the glittering breastplates of the archers, their faces bronzed by the fire and the fight. Bound and silent lay the thieves, their white cheeks a pale contrast to the violence around them. In the center of the carnage lay the bleeding giant, his life extinguished, left to rest in his own gore.

Gerard moved among the soldiers, taking each by the hand, his eyes glistening with unshed tears. In return, they all kissed him. Turning to a handsome archer of his own age, Gerard spoke softly, "Prithee, good soldier, keep watch over me. A strange drowsiness overtakes me. Let no one cut my throat while I sleep—for pity's sake."

The archer laughed, thinking Gerard jesting, and promised faithfully. Almost immediately, Gerard sank into a deep, heavy sleep.

Denys watched, surprised but unmoved, for it served his immediate purpose. Elsewhere, two archers were turning the Abbot's body over with their boots, asking which of the two had thrown the enormous rogue from the upper storey. They sought the trick of his arm.

At first, Denys scoffed, unwilling to play the braggart, fearing the young vagabond would claim it was the finger of Heaven and make him look a fool. But seeing Gerard unconscious, he suddenly supplied the required tale.

"Well, then, comrades," Denys declared, "I ran my sword through this one to the hilt. Others came buzzing about me; I needed my sword or I must die. So I planted my foot against his stomach, gave a tug with my hand, a spring with my foot, and sent him flying to kingdom come! He died in the air, and his carrion rolled down among you without ceremony—made you jump, I warrant. But pikes and pillage! What avail prattling of trifles once they are gone? Drink, comrades, drink!"

The archers noted it was easy to say "drink" where no liquor existed, but harder to do it.

"Nay, I'll soon find you liquor," Denys insisted. "My nose has a natural alacrity for scenting wine. Follow me, and bring a torch!"

They descended a short flight of stone steps into a large, low, damp cellar. The air was close and dank; the walls were encrusted with what looked like cobwebs but proved to be saltpetre oozing from the damp stones.

"Oh! the fine mouldy smell," Denys murmured. "In such places still lurks the good wine. Advance thy torch. Diable! What is that in the corner? A pile of rags? No—it is a man."

They gathered round with the torch. There, crouched on a heap in the corner, pale as ashes and shivering, was a figure.

"Why, it is the landlord," Denys said.

"Get up, thou craven heart!" shouted an archer.

"Why, man, the thieves are bound, and we are dry that bound them. Up! Show us thy wine; for no bottles see here."

"What, be the rascals bound?" stammered the pale landlord. "Good news. Wine? That will I, honest sirs."

He rose with unsure joints to lead the way, but Denys interposed. "You are all in the dark, comrades. He is in league with the thieves."

"Alack, good soldier, me in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?"

"The girl said so anyway."

"The girl! What girl? Ah! Curse her, traitress!"

"Well," interposed the other archer, "the girl is not here, but gone to the bailiff. So let the burghers settle whether this craven be guilty or no; for we caught him not in the act. Let him draw us our wine."

"One moment," said Denys shrewdly. "Why cursed he the girl? If he be a true man, he should bless her as we do."

"Alas, sir!" said the landlord. "I have but my good name to live by, and I cursed her to you because you said she had belied me."

"Humph! I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief that cannot lie with a smooth face? Therefore hold him, comrades: a prisoner can draw wine an if his hands be not bound."

The landlord offered no objection, hoping they would pay for what they drank, noting his rent was two months overdue. The archers smiled grimly at his simplicity. One laid a quiet, firm hand on his shoulder; the other led on with the torch.

They reached the threshold when Denys cried, "Halt!"

"What is't?"

"Here be bottles in this corner; advance thy light."

The torch-bearer moved forward, having just removed his scabbard to probe the heap the landlord had crouched upon.

"Nay, nay," cried the landlord. "The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing there."

"Nothing is mighty hard, then," said Denys, drawing a hand from the heap.

It proved to be only a bone.

Denys threw it on the floor; it rattled.

"There is nought there but the bones of the house," said the landlord.

"Just now 'twas nothing. Now that we have found something, 'tis nothing but bones. Here's another. Humph? Look at this one, comrade; and you come too, and bring your smooth knave along."

The archer with the torch, Philippe, held the bone to the light, turning it round and round.

"Well?" said Denys.

"Well, if this was a field of battle, I should say 'twas the shankbone of a man; no more, no less. But 'tisn't a battlefield, nor a churchyard; 'tis an inn."

"True, mate; but yon knave's ashy face is as good a light to me as a field of battle. I read the bone by it. Bring yon face nearer, I say. When the chine is amissing, and the house dog can't look at you without his tail creeping between his legs, who was the thief? Good brothers mine, my mind it doth misgive me. The deeper I thrust, the more there be. Mayhap if these bones could tell their tale, they would make true men's flesh creep that heard it."

"Alas! young man, what hideous fancies are these! The bones are bones of beeves, sheep, and kids, not as you think, of men and women. Holy saints preserve us!"

"Hold thy peace! Thy words are air. Thou hast not got burghers by the ear, that know not a veal knuckle from their grandsire's ribs; but soldiers-men that have gone to look for their dear comrades, and found their bones picked as clean by the crows as these I doubt have been by thee and thy mates. Men and women, saidst thou? And prithee, when spake I a word of women's bones? Wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour ago? Drag him close to me, let me read his face: now then, what is this, thou knave?"

He thrust a small object suddenly into the landlord's face.

"Alas! I know not."

"Well, I would not swear neither: but it is too like the thumb bone of a man's hand. Mates, my flesh it creeps. Churchyard! How know I this is not one?"

He drew his sword and began to rake the heap of earth, broken crockery, and bones onto the floor.

The landlord assured him he but wasted his time. "We poor innkeepers are sinners," he pleaded. "We give short measure and baptize the wine; we are fain to do these things; the laws are so unjust to us. But we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven's lightning strike me dead if there be any bones there but such as have been used for meat. 'Tis the kitchen wench that flings them here. I swear by God's holy mother, by holy Paul, by holy Dominic, and Denys my patron saint—ah!"

Denys held out a bone under his eye in dead silence. It was a bone no man, however ignorant or lying, could confound with those of sheep or oxen. The sight of it shut the lying lips and palsied the heartless heart.

The landlord's hair rose visibly on his head like spikes, and his knees gave way as if his limbs had been struck from under him. The archers dragged him fiercely up, keeping him erect under the torch, staring fascinated at the dead skull which, white as the living cheek opposed, but no whiter, glared back at its murderer. The pale lip opened and opened, but could utter no sound.

"Ah!" said Denys solemnly, trembling now with rage. "Look on the sockets out of which thou hast picked the eyes, and let them blast thine eyes, that crows shall pick them out ere this week shall end. Now, hold thou that while I search on. Hold it, I say, or here I rob the gallows—" and he threatened the quaking wretch with his naked sword until, with a groan, he took the skull and held it, almost fainting.

Oh! that every murderer and contriver of murder could see him, sick and staggering with terror, hair on end, holding the cold skull, feeling that his own head would soon be like it. Soon the heap was scattered, and alas! not one nor two, but many skulls were brought to light, the culprit moaning at each discovery.

Suddenly Denys uttered a strange cry of distress from so bold and hard a man. He held up to the torch a mass of human hair. It was long, glossy, and golden—a woman's beautiful hair. At the sight of it, the archers instinctively shook the craven wretch in their hands, and he whined.

"I have a little sister with hair just so fair and shining as this," gulped Denys. "Jesu! if it should be hers! There, quick, take my sword and dagger, and keep them from my hand, lest I strike him dead and wrong the gibbet. And thou, poor innocent victim, on whose head this most lovely hair did grow, hear me swear this, on bended knee, never to leave this man till I see him broken to pieces on the wheel even for thy sake."

He rose from his knee. "Ay, had he as many lives as here be hairs, I'd have them all, by God," and he put the hair into his bosom. Then, in sudden fury, he seized the landlord fiercely by the neck, forced him to his knees, and ground his face savagely among the bones of his victims where they lay thickest. The assassin first yelled, then whined and whimpered, just as a dog first yells, then whines, when his nose is forced into some leveret he has killed.

"Now lend me thy bowstring, Philippe!" He passed it through the eyes of a skull alternately and hung the ghastly relic of mortality and crime round the man's neck. Then he pulled him up and kicked him industriously into the kitchen, where one of the aldermen of the burgh had arrived with constables, taking an archer's deposition.

The grave burgher was much startled at the sight of the landlord driven in, bleeding from a dozen scratches inflicted by the bones of his own victims, carrying his horrible collar. But Denys came panting after, and in a few fiery words soon made all clear.

"Bind him like the rest," said the alderman sternly. "I count him the blackest of them all."

While his hands were being bound, the poor wretch begged piteously that the skull be taken from him.

"Humph!" said the alderman. "Certes I had not ordered such a thing to be put on mortal man. Yet being there, I will not lift voice nor finger to doff it. Methinks it fits thee truly, thou bloody dog. 'Tis thy ensign, and hangs well above a heart so foul as thine."

He then inquired if Denys thought they had secured the whole gang or but a part.

"Your worship," said Denys, "there are but seven of them, and this landlord. One we slew upstairs, one we trundled down dead, the rest are bound before you."

"Good! Go fetch the dead one from upstairs, and lay him beside him I caused to be removed."

Here a voice like a guinea-fowl's broke peevishly in. "Now, now, now, where is the hand? That is what I want to see." The speaker was a little pettifogging clerk.

"You will find it above, nailed to the door-post by a crossbow bolt."

"Good!" said the clerk. He whispered to his master, "What a goodly show will the 'pieces de conviction' make!" and with this he wrote them down, enumerating them in separate squeaks as he penned them: Skulls—Bones—a woman's hair—a thief's hands, an axe, two carcasses, one crossbow bolt. This done, he itched to search the cellar himself; there might be other invaluable morsels of evidence, an ear, or even an earring. The alderman assenting, he caught up a torch and hurried thither, when an accident stopped him, indeed carrying him a step or two in the opposite direction.
