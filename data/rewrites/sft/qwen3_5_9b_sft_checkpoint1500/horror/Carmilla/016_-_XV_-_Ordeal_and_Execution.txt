Ordeal and Execution

A figure emerged from the shadows of the chapel's threshold, a man so grotesque and strange in appearance that he seemed plucked from a nightmare rather than reality. He towered above the others, yet his chest was unnaturally narrow, his shoulders hunched with a bird-like stoop. Clad in somber black, his skin was the color of old earth, etched with deep, cruel furrows. Upon his head sat an ill-fitting hat adorned with a broad, unnatural leaf. Long, grizzled hair cascaded down his back like tangled roots. A pair of heavy gold spectacles perched on his nose, magnifying eyes that darted restlessly—sometimes fixed upward toward the vaulted ceiling, sometimes bowed low toward the dust. A perpetual, rictus grin stretched across his face, revealing nothing of joy. His long, spindly arms swung with a jerky, shambling rhythm, clad in black gloves far too wide for his gaunt hands, waving in a frantic, abstract dance.

"The very man!" cried the General, stepping forward with a radiant, almost feverish delight. "My dear Baron, how fortuitous! I dared not hope to encounter you so swiftly." He signaled to my father, who had just returned, gesturing for the eccentric visitor—the Baron—to approach. Introductions were exchanged with stiff formality, yet immediately the air grew thick with urgent business. The stranger produced a rolled parchment from his coat and unspooled it across the weathered stone of a nearby tomb. From a leather case, he drew a pencil, tracing invisible geometries onto the paper. As he worked, his gaze flickered constantly between the sketch and specific points within the chapel, suggesting to me that he was drafting a blueprint of the sanctuary itself. Occasionally, he would mutter passages from a grimy, yellow-leaved booklet, reading aloud as if chanting a spell.

Together, the General and the Baron wandered down the side aisle, past the silent rows of tombs, debating fervently. They paused to measure distances by pacing, their steps rhythmic and precise. Finally, they halted before a section of the sidewall, examining it with microscopic scrutiny. With practiced hands, they peeled back clinging ivy and tapped the plaster with wooden sticks, listening intently to the hollow echoes beneath the surface. Scrape after scrape, knock after knock, until at last, hidden behind layers of decay, they revealed a broad marble slab inscribed with relief lettering.

It was the lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

Though the General was not a man known for piety, he stopped abruptly. Raising his hands and eyes to the heavens, he offered a silent prayer of thanks, his face bathed in a sudden, holy light.

"Tomorrow," I heard him declare, his voice trembling with authority, "the Imperial Commissioner shall arrive. The Inquisition will proceed strictly according to the laws of the land."

Turning to the old man with the gold spectacles, the General seized both of his hands in a warm, vigorous grip. "Baron, words fail me. How shall we repay your service? By driving this plague from our soil, ending a scourge that has haunted these people for over a century, you have saved us all. Thank God, the monster is finally found."

My father stepped aside, ushering the strangers away. I watched them retreat, aware that they intended to speak in private. Their glances back at me were quick, furtive, filled with a mixture of triumph and caution.

When my father returned, he embraced me repeatedly, his face wet with tears. Leading me from the gloom of the chapel, he whispered, "Time to return. But first, we must summon the local priest, who resides nearby. We need him to join us at the castle."

Our search for the cleric was swift and successful. Though I was utterly exhausted, a profound sense of relief washed over me. Yet, as we walked home, a cold dread settled in my stomach. There was no word from Carmilla. No explanation was offered regarding the chaotic scene in the ruined chapel. My father had decided, for now, to shroud the truth in silence.

Her sinister absence rendered the memory of the chapel's interior infinitely more terrifying. That night, the arrangements were peculiar and foreboding. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my chamber, while the priest and my father kept vigil in the adjacent dressing room. I saw the priest perform obscure, solemn rites that night, rituals whose meaning I could not grasp, nor could I comprehend the necessity of such extreme precautions for my safety.

Only days later did the pieces fall into place. With Carmilla gone, the nightly terrors ceased.

You have likely heard tales of the superstitious dread that grips the lands of Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkey, Poland, and Russia—the terror of the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with judicial solemnity before countless commissions of learned and upright men, holds any weight, then one cannot doubt the existence of this phenomenon. For my part, having witnessed horrors that defy rational explanation, I accept only the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my homeland.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced in the Chapel of Karnstein.

The grave of the Countess Mircalla was broken open. Both the General and my father stared in horror and recognition at the visage now exposed to the air. Though one hundred and fifty years had passed since her burial, her features burned with the warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no stench of decomposition rose from the coffin. Two medical experts, one official and one representing the prosecutor, confirmed the miraculous facts: a faint but distinct breath, a beating heart, limbs perfectly supple, and flesh that remained elastic. The leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, submerging her body to a depth of seven inches.

There, laid bare before the world, were all the undeniable signs of vampirism. In accordance with ancient custom, the body was lifted. A sharpened stake was driven violently through her heart. She let out a piercing shriek—a sound of agony indistinguishable from that of a living soul in its final torment. Then, her head was severed from her neck, and a torrent of dark blood gushed forth. Her body and head were cast upon a pyre of wood, consumed by flames, and reduced to ash. These remains were scattered into the river, carried away by the current, and the land has never since suffered the visitation of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission's report, bearing the signatures of every witness, verifying this account. From this official document, I have drawn the narrative of this final, shocking ordeal.
