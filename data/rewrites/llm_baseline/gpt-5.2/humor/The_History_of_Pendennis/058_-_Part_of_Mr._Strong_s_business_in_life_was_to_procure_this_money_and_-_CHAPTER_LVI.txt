Could Helen have guessed that recovery is a kind of witchcraft—one which not only knits flesh back to bone, but stitches up old follies and sets them walking again? As Arthur’s pulse steadied and his appetite came creeping back (a spoonful of broth one day, a decent slice of toast the next, and presently that dangerous return of opinion about what the cook “ought” to have done), his unfortunate tenderness for little Fanny began to stir too, like yeast warming in a bowl.

Mrs. Pendennis never once pronounced the girl’s name. If you had offered her “Fanny” as a word upon a platter, she would have pushed it away as if it smelt of garlic in a drawing-room. Yet she watched her son with a vigilance that would have shamed a barnyard goose guarding her goslings: always looking down into her teacup, always turning a page, always somehow aware—by motherly instinct, by fear, by that quiet domestic clairvoyance—of every letter that came to the table.

Arthur, who had been forbidden (on the plea of “health,” which in families is the grand despotism) to fatigue himself with correspondence, still could not help that twitch of the heart which accompanies the sound of paper slid from an envelope. Each post brought a little tremor with it, like the first tap of a cook’s knife on the chopping-board before the onions begin to weep. He expected, daily, to see a small hand—one he would have known without ever having seen it—lying there among the bills and invitations. He half dreaded it; he half desired it; he could not have told you which. When it did not come, day after day, he was relieved in a way that vexed him, and vexed in a way that shamed him.

For there is a particular sort of pride in sentimental bankruptcy. A man does not like to shut up his shop with no announcement, no decent inventory of ruins. We loiter before we pull down the shutters; we hold mournful meetings of partners; we pawn a jewel—an excuse, a reminiscence, a last little lie—to keep the concern going another week. On the whole, Arthur was almost angry with Fanny for making the separation so clean. What! could she go down in that little storm of hers without even stretching out a hand and crying, “Help, Arthur?” Why, one expects a drowning person to splash and clutch: it flatters one’s sense of importance. Yet not everybody who ventures upon that voyage sinks; many are only ducked, and, sputtering, crawl ashore. The reader, who has observed Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s habits of self-preservation, may form an opinion as to which of these he was most likely to prove.

He was still too weak to walk far; he was treated like a choice soufflé that might collapse if the door were slammed. A drive alone was forbidden. But Helen could not keep watch on Mr. Warrington as well, nor had she authority over that gentleman’s legs when they chose to carry him to London.

Indeed, when business called him to town, she could not say “stay,” though a secret, selfish part of her may have wished him gone and kept gone. She checked the wish as soon as she recognised it (her conscience was a creature that never slept), and she received Warrington with the same melancholy kindness she gave to all who served her boy. Yet on the morning he set off, she divined—by some domestic omen, perhaps by the pitch of his voice, perhaps by the way he fastened his glove—that he was going to learn news of Fanny for Arthur.

Arthur himself had spoken with George. He had told him, more gravely than he meant, how the business began, how it ended, how it had frightened him. He was thankful—he said so—he had escaped. Warrington heartily said “Amen” to that, as a man says it over any hot pan he has not spilled into his lap. Arthur had no great wrong, he believed, to reproach himself with; yet if they parted (and part they must), he wished, at least, to breathe a God bless her, and hope she might remember him kindly.

He spoke with so much feeling that George—who had been the stern preacher of separation—began to suspect his friend’s cure was not as complete as his boasts. If the two met again, all the temptation would have to be fought over—like a sauce which separates the moment you turn your back, and must be coaxed together again at the very edge of boiling.

“It is hard to struggle, Arthur,” Warrington said, “and easy to fall. The best courage for us poor wretches is to run away from danger. I would not have been what I am now, had I practised what I preach.”

“And what did you practise, George?” Arthur cried, eager as a child prying into the locked spice-box. “I knew there was something. Tell us.”

“There was something that can’t be mended,” Warrington answered, and his voice, which could jest so readily, went dull. “It broke my fortunes early. I said I’d tell you some day—and I will—but not now. Take the moral without the fable, Pen. If you want to see a man wrecked against a rock he struck when a boy—look at me. So I warn you.”

While this sober counsel was being given in a quiet house, another kind of counsel—wetter, noisier, and delivered in brandy-and-water—was being taken nightly in London at a place with the amiable name of the Back Kitchen.

Mr. Huxter, the young surgeon from Saint Bartholomew’s, had written home to Clavering with vast importance about a fashionable club he attended, and about an Irish officer of distinction he met there, who had told him—among other news—the intelligence regarding Pendennis that Huxter had forwarded with such patriotic speed. The Back Kitchen, indeed, was one of those haunts where youth supposes it is tasting Life, chiefly by swallowing it in half-pints.

There, Captain Costigan—General Costigan, if you asked himself—was a standing dish. His brogue, his stories, his readiness to be drawn out, were the very oysters on which the young gentlemen fattened their mirth. Huxter, who possessed a natural genius for mimicking everything—actors, animals, corkscrews, and any human creature who offered a weakness to the stage—took especial delight in studying the honest old fellow. Sixpennyworth of brandy-and-water was the bait; Costigan swallowed it with the trusting simplicity of a trout; and then, under its warming influence, who so happy as he to sing his own triumphs? He could be produced, as if on command, fighting a jewel in the Phaynix; bowing to the Juke of York; feasting at his son-in-law’s teeble with the nobilitee; or—best of all, to the cruel audience—crying drunk, confidentially lamenting his daughter’s ingratichewd, and explaining how his grey hairs were hastening to a praymachure greeve. The landlord pardoned many foibles, seeing they brought many customers; and though it is not the proudest station for an old man, it must be recorded that Costigan had no notion his station was low. In his whiskied blood there was no black drop of malice, nor in his muddled brains any bitter resentment. Even his “cruel Emily” he would have forgiven with tears, if she would only have come. And what more can one say of a man’s charity than that he is ready to forgive the person who has done him every kindness—when he himself has been wrong the whole time?

Among the Back Kitchen wags there was a legend that Costigan made a mystery of his lodgings—either to escape duns or to cultivate romance. The landlord, being a gentleman of principle where other people’s privacy was concerned, refused to answer impertinent curiosity, saying he knew gentlemen only in his room, and once they paid their scores and walked out, his acquaintance with them ceased.

Costigan too, when “confidential,” was astonishingly evasive. Not because there was any grand secret—we, who have been more than once admitted to his apartments, know better—but because in the vicissitudes of his life he had often lodged in places where privacy was a comfort, and certain visitors would have been pain. Hence the legends multiplied. He slept in a watch-box, in a cab, in the Duke of York’s Column—Huxter inventing the wildest with the joyful cruelty of an imaginative young man.

One fine September morning—pink light on chimney-pots, shutters still asleep, Covent Garden smelling of damp vegetables and yesterday’s crushed herbs—Huxter, with the sticky palate of a night’s dancing at Vauxhall, stood at a coffee-stall, treating himself to a cup that was more soot than Mocha. He had paid his twopence—indeed, he had only eightpence left, or he would have ridden home in a cab like a gentleman of fortune—and as he sipped, he spied the General reeling down Henrietta Street, with a troop of hooting boys at his heels.

These were the city’s early birds: creatures who had slept under arches and rose betimes for breakfast and mischief. Cabmen and watermen at the stand knew the old man and made their comments; policemen looked after him with scorn and pity, warning off the boys. But scorn and pity are poor missiles against a skull lined with whisky. Costigan reeled along with glazed eyes, with just sense enough to know his beat homewards. How often had he gone to bed not knowing how he reached it? He woke and found himself there and asked no questions, as if each day’s disgrace were a dish cleared away before morning.

Huxter’s curiosity leapt like flame under fat. He followed.

Costigan dived down the alleys by Drury Lane Theatre, where gin-shops and oyster-shops and theatrical wardrobes lurk—places that in daylight look like kitchens after a feast, littered with the crumbs of glamour. The shopkeepers were asleep behind shutters; the dawn made a mockery of their chimneys. Through courts and passages Huxter tracked his prey, until they reached Oldcastle Street, where the gate of Shepherd’s Inn stands like a patient, ancient jaw waiting to swallow the needy.

Just as Costigan was within sight of home, a slice of orange-peel—London’s own little banana-skin comedy, though less genteel—slid beneath his heel. The General went down backwards with a thump that seemed to ring on the morning air, and for a moment even the street had the good manners to pause.

Huxter ran up, and while the old veteran gathered what dizzy sense he could from the pavement and the whisky, the young surgeon lifted him, examined him with that professional hand which is so brisk and so coldly kind, and offered to conduct him home.

Costigan refused at first, thickly insisting his lodgings were “hard by,” that he could reach them without difficulty. He shook off Huxter’s arm and made a rush, like a ship trying to tack alone. But he lurched so wildly that Huxter insisted, soothed him with cheerful doctorly lies, and got the old man’s dirty hand under what he called his “fin,” and guided him across the street.

At the ancient gate, decorated with the armorial bearings of the venerable Shepherd, Costigan stopped.

“Here ’tis,” he said, and tugged the bell. Old Mr. Bolton the porter came out scowling, grumbling as he did each morning when it was his turn to let in that disreputable early bird.

Costigan attempted a moment of genteel conversation. Bolton would not be baited.

“Don’t bother me,” said he. “Go to your hown bed, Capting, and don’t keep honest men out of theirs.”

So the Captain tacked across the square and reached his staircase, Huxter close behind like a conscientious shadow. Costigan had a key; Huxter, being steadier, inserted it; and there was no need to wake little Mr. Bows—the old musician—who had only lately fallen asleep.

Huxter helped disrobe the tipsy patient, ascertained no bones were broken, and got him to bed. He applied compresses and water to the knee and shin that had suffered; and with the trousers, which had encased the limbs, the General had torn himself pretty badly. At that age, with that habit of body, such wounds do not heal briskly. Inflammation followed; pain and fever sat down like unpleasant guests who would not be turned out. For some days Costigan lay ill.

Mr. Huxter undertook the case with tremendous confidence—the kind young men always have when the world has not yet punished them for it—and treated it with becoming skill. He came day after day; he consoled the old fellow with lively rattle and conversation for the lack of society without which Costigan withered. And he gave special instructions to the nurse about the quantity of whisky the patient was to take—instructions which, as the poor man could not reach the bottle himself, could not easily be broken.

At the bedside waited Bows; waited Mrs. Bolton; and when she was able, waited little Fanny. They made the old warrior as comfortable as their narrow means and broader good nature could manage.

Thus Huxter—whose affable manners made him quickly familiar wherever he fell, and whose “refinement” was not of that sort which shrinks from garrets—became intimate in Shepherd’s Inn with the garrets and the porter’s lodge alike. He thought he had seen Fanny somewhere; he felt sure he had; but it is no wonder he could not place her. The poor little thing never chose to tell him where they had met; and he had seen her at a time when his own judgment of faces and morals was clouded with drink and music. Besides, Fanny was altered. Three weeks of fever, agitation, passion, and despair had worn her as a knife wears down a wooden spoon. Her head was bowed now; her face pale and wan; and many a time those sad eyes had searched the postman’s as he came into the Inn, while her heart sank as he passed away.

When her father’s accident happened, she was almost glad—yes, glad—to have something to do besides ache. Duty is a kind of gruel for sorrow: thin fare, perhaps, but it keeps one alive. She stirred her father’s gruel, and if a tear dropped in, she stirred again and swallowed it down as part of the portion. Courage, little Fanny: if everybody died outright of that complaint, undertakers would have the merriest year of all.

Whether from compassion or from delight in Costigan’s talk, Huxter now found occasion to visit two or three times a day at least. And if none of the porter’s lodge family were at the bedside, the young doctor always had “particular directions” to deliver at their own door. He was kind in that bustling, boyish way: he brought toys for the children; apples and brandy-balls; he brought a mask and frightened them, until even pale Fanny smiled for a moment. He called Mrs. Bolton “Mrs. B.” and was so familiar, facetious, and impudent with her that she liked him the better for it—quite different, she declared, from that “aughty, artless beast,” Mr. Pendennis, whom she now vowed she never could abear.

It was from Mrs. Bolton, who was free with her opinions as with her tongue, that Huxter soon learned what illness was preying upon little Fan, and what Mr. Pendennis had done. Her account, it may be imagined, was not entirely impartial. From her story, one would think Arthur had used the most persevering and flagitious artifices, made solemn promises by the dozen like a pastrycook turning out pies, and then broken them all with the ease of snapping a biscuit.

Huxter, already sore under Arthur’s contempt, was quite ready to believe everything unfavourable. Yet—oddly—he did not write home to Clavering as he had before, with this delicious scandal. He announced in a letter to his brother-in-law that that “nice young man,” Mr. Pendennis, had nearly died of fever and would no doubt delight Clavering by recovering. He mentioned an “interesting case of compound fracture”—an officer of distinction—that kept him in town. But as for Fanny Bolton, he wrote not a word. No more than Pendennis himself had written a word.

O mothers at home, with your quiet parlours and your belief in the wholesome candour of your boys—how much do you think you know?

With Bows, however, Huxter had no reason to keep silent; and a short time after talking with Mrs. Bolton, he spoke his mind to the musician. He described Pendennis as a confounded conceited blackguard, and declared he would punch his impudent head the moment the fellow was well enough to stand like a man.

Then Bows, on his side, told his version: how the meeting of Arthur and Fanny had been no contrivance of Arthur’s but a blunder of the old Irishman now in bed with a broken shin; how Pendennis had acted with manliness and self-control; how Mrs. Bolton was an idiot; and he repeated the conversation he himself had had with Arthur, and the sentiments the young man had uttered.

Perhaps this narrative pricked Huxter with a twinge of conscience. He owned frankly he had been wrong in his judgment, and withdrew his project of punching Mr. Pendennis’s head—an act of mercy, no doubt, to Pendennis, but also to Huxter’s own knuckles.

Yet the cessation of hostility did not diminish Huxter’s attentions to Fanny; and here unlucky Mr. Bows began to mark them with his habitual jealousy and bitterness.

“I have but to like anybody,” he thought, “and somebody is sure to come and be preferred to me. It has been so since I was a lad—until now, that I am sixty. What can such a man expect but to be laughed at? It is for the young to succeed and be happy, not for old fools like me. I’ve played second fiddle through life,” he said, with a bitter laugh. “How can I suppose the luck is to change after it has gone against me so long?”

Few would have seen reason for jealousy in that pale, grief-stricken child; she received Huxter’s good-natured efforts kindly enough, laughed now and again at his antics with her little sisters, then sank back quickly into her dejection—a dejection that ought to have proved to Bows there was no room in her heart for any new preference, had he been able to see clearly.

But he could not. Fanny, by a miserable twist of pain, attributed Pendennis’s silence to Bows’s interference. She hated him for it. She treated him with constant cruelty and injustice; turned from him when he spoke; loathed his clumsy consolations. A hard life had Mr. Bows—hard, and with a cruel return for his regard.

When Warrington came to Shepherd’s Inn as Arthur’s ambassador, he asked for Mr. Bows’s apartments—as if the matter had been arranged beforehand between principal and agent in this delicate negotiation. He did not even catch sight of Miss Fanny when he stopped at the gate and made his inquiry. Directed upstairs, he found Bows tending the patient; the musician came out from the sick-room to receive him. They had known one another previously, and shook hands with sufficient cordiality.

After some preliminary talk, Warrington said he had come from Arthur Pendennis and his family to thank Bows for his attendance at the beginning of Arthur’s illness, and for his kindness in hastening into the country to fetch the Major.

Bows answered it was his duty; he had scarcely expected to see the young gentleman alive again when he went; he was glad Mr. Pendennis had recovered and had his friends around him.

“Lucky are they who have friends, Mr. Warrington,” said he. “I might be up in this garret and nobody would care for me—nobody mind whether I was alive or dead.”

“What! not the General, Mr. Bows?” Warrington asked.

“The General likes his whisky-bottle more than anything in life,” Bows replied. “We live together from habit and convenience; and he cares for me no more than you do. What is it you want to ask me, Mr. Warrington? You ain’t come to visit me, I know. Nobody comes to visit me. It is about Fanny, the porter’s daughter, you are come—I see that—very well. Is Mr. Pendennis, now he has got well, anxious to see her again? Does his lordship the Sultan propose to throw his ’andkerchief to her?

“She has been very ill, sir, ever since the day when Mrs. Pendennis turned her out of doors—kind of a lady, wasn’t it? The poor girl and myself found the young gentleman raving in a fever, knowing nobody, with nobody to tend him but his drunken laundress. She watched day and night by him. I set off to fetch his uncle. Mamma comes and turns Fanny to the right-about. Uncle comes and leaves me to pay the cab. Carry my compliments to the ladies and gentleman, and say we are both very thankful—very.

“Why, a countess couldn’t have behaved better. And for an apothecary’s lady, as I’m given to understand Mrs. Pendennis was—I’m sure her behaviour is most uncommon aristocratic and genteel. She ought to have a double-gilt pestle and mortar to her coach.”
