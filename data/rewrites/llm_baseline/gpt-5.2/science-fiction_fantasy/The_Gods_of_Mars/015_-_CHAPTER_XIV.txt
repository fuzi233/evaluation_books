My son.

For an instant the words were only sound—vibration in the thin Martian air, a note struck somewhere between the bones of my ear and the deeper chambers of memory. Then I rose, slowly, as though gravity itself had changed its mind, and looked full upon the youth who had stepped from shadow into the scant light.

Now that my eyes had leisure to read him, I understood the pull he had had upon me from the first. In the clean planes of his cheeks and the proud set of his head there lived the old, unforgotten beauty of his mother—yet tempered, hardened into a masculine mould; and in the grey eyes that met mine there was no mistaking the mirror. Their glance held the same quick fire, the same stubborn refusal to bow to the universe, that I had known all my life.

He stood very still, as if fear of disappointment were a physical weight upon his shoulders—half hope, half uncertainty.

“Tell me of your mother,” I said, and my voice did not sound like my own. “Tell me all you can of the years that a relentless fate has stolen from me.”

At that he broke. With a cry that was joy and ache together he sprang forward and flung his arms about my neck. I held him—my boy—and in that brief, fierce clasp I felt tears gather, hot and foolish, threatening to choke me. Let them. A man may be iron in battle and yet melt where women and children are concerned; it is no weakness, only proof that he has something worth fighting for.

“Your stature, your manner,” he said against my shoulder, “the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship—my mother has told me of them a thousand times. Yet even so, I could scarce believe. Do you know what convinced me more than all else?”

“What, my boy?” I asked.

“Your first words to me. They were of her. No other man—no other—would have thought first of my mother unless he loved her as she has always said my father did.”

“For long years,” I said, and it hurt to say it aloud, “there has not been a moment when the radiant vision of her face was not before me. Tell me of her.”

“Those who have known her longest say she has not changed—unless to grow more beautiful, were that possible. Only… when she thinks I do not watch, her face grows very sad and wistful. She thinks ever of you. All Helium mourns with her and for her. Her grandfather’s people love her; they loved you also, and worship your memory as the saviour of Barsoom.

“Each year the anniversary is held—the day you raced across a near-dead world to unlock the secret of that awful portal that guarded the power of life for millions. They feast and give thanks; but there are tears in the celebration, because the giver of the gift is not there to share it. Upon all Barsoom there is no greater name than John Carter.”

“And by what name has your mother called you?” I asked, clinging to particulars as a starving man clings to crumbs.

“The people of Helium wished me named for my father,” he said, “but my mother would not. She said that you and she had chosen a name together, and that your wish must stand before all others. So she called me Carthoris—hers and yours joined.”

It was then that Xodar, who had remained at the wheel while my world remade itself, called out with the clipped urgency of a man who hears disaster in the ship’s own voice.

“She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter,” he said. “While we rose it was not to be seen, but on a level course—look. The wound in her bow has opened one of the forward ray tanks.”

I went forward and examined the rent. It was worse than I had believed. Not only did the forced angle at which we must hold the bow to keep a horizontal flight drag against our speed, but the forward repulsive rays were bleeding away as surely as blood from an opened vein. Within an hour—perhaps less—we would hang stern-up and helpless in the sky like a butchered bird on a hook.

I took the helm again and drove the engine wide open. The little craft answered bravely; she shrieked through the thin air northward at a pace that made the dead world beneath blur into ochre and shadow. Behind me, in the cramped bow, Carthoris and Xodar crouched with tools, their hands busy, their brows drawn—patching, propping, attempting the hopeless: to stem the invisible tide of escaping force.

Night still lay on Barsoom when we passed the northern boundary of the ice cap and slid beneath the last of the cloud banks. Below us unrolled a typical Martian vista—rolling sea bottoms of ancient, long-vanished oceans, low hills like the ribs of a sleeping beast, and here and there the hulks of dead cities: grim architectures piled in silence, tenanted only by memories and by the great white apes that haunt old grandeur with old ferocity.

The bow sagged lower, lower. It became impossible to keep her level without turning our flight into a murderous dive. At last I shut down the engine rather than risk driving us nose-first to destruction.

With sunrise came the final failure. As the new light swept the dark away, our craft gave a spasmodic plunge, rolled half upon her side, and began to swing in a slow, drunken circle—deck tilting, bow dropping, stern rising with every heartbeat.

We clung to hand-rails and stanchions until there was no longer any “down” that could be trusted; then we snapped our harness buckles to the rings along the sides. A moment later the deck reared to a full ninety degrees, and we hung in our leather, feet dangling a thousand yards above the ground, the wind tugging at us like a hungry thing.

The controlling levers were close enough to my swing that I could reach them. I stretched, fingers straining, and found the lever that directed the rays of repulsion. The boat responded, as though grateful for instruction, and very gently we began to descend.

It took fully half an hour to touch ground.

North of us rose a range of hills, not mountains, but lofty enough to break sight-lines and shelter us from any aerial scouts that might hunt this way. We set off toward them, and within an hour we were in their time-rounded gullies, among the hardy flowering plants that somehow make beauty in Barsoom’s waste places.

There, too, grew the huge milk-giving shrubs—those strange, swollen-stemmed plants that are food and drink to the roaming green hordes. To men who had lived on danger and haste, it was a blessing more immediate than any prayer. We broke the thick skins and drew out the pale, nourishing milk. It had a faintly mineral sweetness and a coolness that slid down the throat like mercy. Even the sound of it—soft gurgle into our improvised cups, a steady, sane domestic noise—seemed to quiet the nervous ringing in my skull after days of engines, battle cries, and the dead hush of Dor.

Beneath a clustered growth that hid us perfectly from above, we lay down to sleep. For me it was the first true sleep in many hours—the beginning of my fifth day upon Barsoom since I had been snatched from my cottage on the Hudson and flung into Dor: the valley beautiful, the valley hideous. In all that time I had slept but twice, and once only by the ticking mercy of a clock within the thern storehouse.

It was mid-afternoon when a hand seized mine—warm fingers, urgent—and covered it with kisses. I started awake to find Thuvia bending over me, her beautiful face lit as though the sun had risen inside her.

“My Prince! My Prince!” she cried. “It is you. I mourned you as dead. My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain.”

Her voice woke Xodar and Carthoris. My son stared at her, astonishment plain upon him; but Thuvia saw no one but me. She would have flung her arms about my neck, smothering me with gratitude and relief, had I not disengaged myself gently but firmly.

“Come, come, Thuvia,” I said, striving to steady her. “You are overwrought by danger and hardship. You forget yourself—and you forget I am the husband of the Princess of Helium.”

“I forget nothing, my Prince,” she replied, and if she spoke calmly now it was only because she had willed her heart into obedience. “You have spoken no word of love to me, nor do I expect you ever shall. But nothing can prevent me from loving you. I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you forever—as your slave. No greater boon could I ask.”

Her words made me uneasy in a way no sword-point ever had. I knew the Martian custom—how high honour guards every woman in a man’s household even when she bears the name of slave. Yet I had never chosen women as my body servants; I had never wished to complicate the clean lines of duty with the tender entanglements of the heart.

“And if ever I return to Helium, Thuvia,” I said, “you shall go with me—but as an honoured equal, not as a slave. In Helium you will find handsome young nobles enough who would face Issus herself for a smile from you; and we shall see you married quickly to one of the best. Forget this gratitude-born infatuation. I value your friendship more.”

“You are my master,” she said simply. “It shall be as you say.” Yet there was sadness in the cadence, as though a song had lost a note that could not be replaced.

“How came you here?” I asked. “And where is Tars Tarkas?”

At the name, her face changed.

“The great Thark,” she said slowly, “I fear is dead. He fought like a god, but a multitude of green warriors—of a horde not his own—overwhelmed him. The last I saw they bore him wounded and bleeding to the deserted city from which they had sallied to attack us.”

“You are not sure he is dead, then?” I demanded. “Where is this city?”

“Just beyond this range of hills,” she answered. “The vessel in which you resigned your place—so nobly—defied our skill in navigation. We drifted aimlessly for two days. Then we abandoned it and attempted to go on foot to the nearest waterway. Yesterday we crossed these hills and came upon the dead city beyond.

“We had passed within its streets, walking toward the central portion, when at an intersecting avenue we saw green warriors approaching. Tars Tarkas was in advance. They saw him, but they did not see me. He sprang back and forced me into a doorway and told me to remain hidden until I could escape, making my way to Helium if possible.

“‘There will be no escape for me now,’ he said. ‘These are the Warhoon of the South. When they have seen my metal, it will be to the death.’

“Then he stepped out to meet them.” Her voice trembled despite her effort. “Ah, my Prince—such fighting. For an hour they swarmed about him until the Warhoon dead formed a hill where he had stood. At last they overwhelmed him—those behind pushing the foremost upon him until there was no room to swing his great sword. Then he stumbled. He went down. They rolled over him like a wave. When they carried him away toward the heart of the city, I think he was dead, for I did not see him move.”

“Before we go farther we must be sure,” I said. “I cannot leave Tars Tarkas alive among the Warhoons. To-night I shall enter the city and make certain.”

“And I shall go with you,” Carthoris said at once, stepping forward as though his blood had leapt to answer mine.

“And I,” said Xodar.

“Neither of you,” I replied. “This is work for stealth and strategy, not force. One man alone may succeed where more would invite disaster. I shall go alone. If I need help, I will return.”

They did not like it, but both were soldiers; and it had been agreed that command rested with me. The sun already leaned low, and Barsoom’s night falls suddenly, like a curtain dropped. We waited only long enough for the last light to drain from the sky.

I gave them parting instructions in case I did not return, and then I left them—Thuvia’s eyes following me like a hand I could feel between my shoulders—and trotted toward the city at a rapid dogtrot.

When I emerged from the hills, the nearer moon was winging its wild flight overhead, throwing bright beams that turned the barbaric splendour of the ancient metropolis to burnished silver. The city had been built upon gentle foothills that, in some dim past, had sloped down to meet a living sea. Because of that I could enter from the hillside streets without being seen, while the Warhoons—when they come and go—usually cross the dead sea bottoms that face the city.

Within the streets I kept to dense wall-shadows. At each intersection I halted, listening not only with my ears but with my skin, feeling for the slightest change in the city’s breathing. Then I sprang across to the opposite shadow, and so advanced, block by block, without detection.

As I neared the inhabited portion, I knew it before I saw it. The sonic world changed. There came the squealing and grunting of thoats and zitidars corralled in hollow courtyards, the occasional clank of metal, the low, clipped monosyllables of green men—sounds as characteristic of green Martian life as the scent of their rancid oils and the dust of their streets. Those animal voices, crude as they were, struck something in me like the remembered crackle of a hearth: a domestic noise, a lived-in noise, as opposed to the dead, commercial hush of temples and false gods. I had first courted Dejah Thoris to such accompaniment, in the marble halls of Korad, while beasts complained softly in courtyards below and the city itself listened.

At the far corner of the first square occupied by the horde, I saw warriors emerging from several buildings. All moved in one direction, toward a great structure in the centre of the plaza. That, by custom, could only be the quarters of their principal chieftain or the audience chamber where the Jeddak met his jeds and lesser chiefs. In either case something was afoot—something that might concern the capture of Tars Tarkas.

To reach that building I must traverse the full length of a square, cross a broad avenue, and cover a portion of the plaza itself. From the animal noise rising from every courtyard, I knew there were many people in the surrounding piles—likely several communities of the Warhoons of the South.

I stood at the corner where the avenue I had entered met the first intersecting avenue south of the plaza. The buildings along the south side of the square appeared uninhabited—no lights, no movement—so I chose one and slipped in to gain an inner courtyard, hoping to move unseen along the rear.

Nothing interrupted me. I passed through a deserted pile and came into the court, where a great herd of thoats and zitidars moved restlessly about, cropping the moss-like ochre growth that overcovers uncultivated Mars. A light breeze came from the northwest; it would not carry my scent to them. Had it done so, their grunting and squealing would have risen to a storm that would draw warriors like flies to blood.

Close to the east wall, beneath overhanging second-floor balconies, I crept in dense shadow along the full length of the court to the north buildings. Here there were lights—about three floors up. Above that, darkness.

To pass through lighted rooms was impossible; they swarmed with green Martian men and women. My only path lay through the upper floors. To gain them I must scale the wall.

The balcony of the second floor was easy—an agile leap, hands grasping stone rail, a swift pull, and I was up. Through open windows I saw green folk squatting upon sleeping silks and furs, grunting an occasional monosyllable—speech made sparse because their minds carry so much unspoken between them. Their language, pared to bone, always struck me as both powerful and perilous; so much can be commanded without being said, so much can be erased without a word to witness the loss.

A warrior entered from the hall.

“Come, Tan Gama,” he cried. “We are to take the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring another with you.”

The one addressed rose. He beckoned to a fellow near him. The three left the apartment together.

If I could follow them, I might find Tars Tarkas at once—perhaps free him. At the least I would learn where they held him.

To my right was a door from the balcony into an unlighted hall. On impulse I stepped within. The corridor was broad, running straight to the front of the building, with doorways opening on either side.

I had barely entered when I saw the three warriors at the far end. Then they turned right and vanished. I hurried after them, reckless now, because Fate had tossed me a thin chance and I could not afford to let it fall.

At the corridor’s end I found a spiral stair leading both above and below. They had taken it. From my knowledge of these buildings and of Warhoon habit, I was sure they had gone down.

I had once been a prisoner of the northern Warhoons; I remembered their underground pits, remembered the smell of stone that never sees sun, remembered the way sound is swallowed there so that a man begins to doubt his own breathing. It seemed certain that Tars Tarkas lay in such darkness beneath some nearby structure.

I descended.

At the lower landing the shaft continued into deeper pits, and a flickering torch below revealed the three I hunted. Down they went, and behind them I followed, far enough back to keep beyond the outer rim of their light.

The way led through a maze of corridors, unlighted except for that wavering flame. After perhaps a hundred yards they turned abruptly through a doorway on the right. I followed as quickly as I dared, and at the doorway I saw into the chamber.

There they were, removing chains that secured a massive figure to the wall—Tars Tarkas, the great Thark. Even in that poor light his bulk was unmistakable; even in captivity his posture held contempt for it.

They hustled him roughly out, so quickly that I came within a breath of discovery. I sprang down the corridor in the direction I had been moving, far enough to slip outside their torch’s radius. I assumed they would return the way they came, carrying him away from me.

To my chagrin, they turned directly toward me.

There was nothing for it but to keep ahead of them, always skirting the edge of the light, never letting its thin blade touch me. I dared not duck into a side passage at random; I did not know this underworld well enough, and chance might throw me directly into their path.

I ran through blackness that felt thick as cloth. The faint torch-glow behind gave just enough radiance to show the passage directly before me, to keep me from smashing into walls at turns. Yet every step carried the knowledge that beneath these ancient cities are pits, wells, breaks in the stone—and things that live where sound and light do not belong.

At last I came to a place where five corridors diverged from a common point. I chose one and hurried along it for some distance when, suddenly, the faint light behind me vanished.

I stopped, listening. Silence lay down like a hand across my mouth. The green men’s naked feet gave no echo.

They had taken another corridor.

Relief came sharp and immediate; it is safer to trail than to be trailed. I turned back, but now the darkness was complete, and I could not run. I had to feel my way with one hand on the wall, inching along, counting turns by touch. Time stretched until it seemed I had been underground all my life.

At last I found the meeting point again—recognized it by groping across openings until I counted five. Yet in none of them did I see even a thread of light.

I listened. Very faintly—so faint I doubted it at first—I thought I heard the clank of side arms, far away, from the middle corridor. I took it, moving quickly now, stopping now and again to listen for repetition. But only darkness and silence answered me, and soon I had to admit I had followed a blind lead.

I retraced my steps—only to find myself abruptly at the mouth of three diverging corridors, where there should have been one. In my haste after the false clue I had crossed some branching without knowing it.

Here, indeed, was a pretty fix. If I could regain the place of the five ways, I might wait there and catch the warriors upon their return; my knowledge of their customs suggested they had taken Tars Tarkas to the audience chamber for sentence, saving him for the Great Games. But if I could not find my way back, I might wander these black passages for days until thirst and hunger ended it—or until something else did.

A faint shuffling sounded behind me.

I glanced over my shoulder, and my blood froze—not only from present danger, but from the memory it dragged up: a corpse in a Warhoon dungeon, my mind teetering toward madness, and then those eyes—blazing eyes—coming out of darkness to claim their feast, scraping their prize over stone.

Now I saw them again.

Two fiery points burned in the void behind me, unblinking, revealing nothing of the creature that bore them. That is their most terrible trait: they are not seen. Only the eyes exist, floating in blackness like hatred made visible.

I gripped my long-sword and backed slowly away. As I retreated, the eyes advanced. No sound came—no breath, no pad of feet—only an occasional soft shuffle, as of something dragging a dead limb.

On and on I backed, the corridor seeming endless. Then, from an intersecting passage to my right, came that same faint shuffle, and another pair of eyes appeared, approaching.

I started to renew my retreat—heard the shuffling behind again—and before I could turn I heard it to my left as well.
