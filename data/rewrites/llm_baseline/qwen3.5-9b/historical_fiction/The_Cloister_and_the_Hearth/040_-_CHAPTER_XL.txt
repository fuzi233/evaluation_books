Some grow old in heart at forty, others remain young at eighty. Margaret Van Eyck's heart was an evergreen tree, blooming perpetually. She loved her young namesake, Margaret Brandt, with a fervor that defied her age. Yet, this affection was no fleeting caprice; Margaret possessed a sharp insight into character, recognizing in the younger woman a genuineness rare among women of her own sex. Beyond this sterling quality, Margaret Brandt held a powerful ally in the bosom of the elder artist: human nature itself.

It is strange, perhaps, to the unobservant, that our hearts warm more readily toward those whom we have benefited than toward our benefactors. Ancient Greek philosophers noted this tendency, but the British bard Homer has etched it into immortal verse:

> "I heard, and thought how side by side
> We two had stemmed the battle's tide
> In many a well-debated field,
> Where Bertram's breast was Philip's shield.
> I thought on Darien's deserts pale,
> Where Death bestrides the evening gale,
> How o'er my friend my cloak I threw,
> And fenceless faced the deadly dew.
> I thought on Quariana's cliff,
> Where, rescued from our foundering skiff,
> Through the white breakers' wrath I bore
> Exhausted Bertram to the shore:
> And when his side an arrow found,
> I sucked the Indian's venom'd wound.
> These thoughts like torrents rushed along
> To sweep away my purpose strong."

Mark this! An assassin's hand is stayed not by memories of favors received, but by recollections of kindnesses conferred.

Margaret Van Eyck had shown wonderful kindness to Margaret Brandt. She had broken her own rigid habits to visit the sickbed, nursing, soothing, and petting the young woman until she was cured more effectively than any physician could have hoped. Consequently, her heart opened fully to the recipient of her goodness. She now loved Margaret with a tenderness surpassing even her love for Gerard, though initially, her visits had been motivated solely by concern for Gerard's welfare.

Thus, when she beheld the roses blooming on Margaret's cheek and read the fragment of parchment that caused such color, she surrendered her own plans without a murmur.

"Sweetheart," she said gently, "I had wished for him to remain in Italy for five or six years, returning wealthy and, above all, a finished artist. But your happiness comes first. Seeing you cannot live without him, we must hasten his return home."

"Oh, madam! You see my very thoughts!" the young woman replied, hanging her head and blushing. "But how shall I let him know? That exceeds my skill. He has departed for Italy, though I know not which city he frequents. Wait! He named the places he must visit: Florence and Rome." Yet hesitation clouded her resolve. Being a sensible girl, she feared a letter addressed simply "My Gerard—Italy" might miscarry. She looked imploringly at her friend for counsel.

"You have come to the right place, and at the right time," declared the old lady. "Hans Memling was with me today; he departs for Italy no later than next week, 'to improve his hand,' he claims. Not before necessity demanded it, I assure you."

"But how shall he find my Gerard?"

"Why, he knows your Gerard, child. They have dined here more than once, fitting like hand and glove. Since their trades are identical, they will traverse the same lands, and sooner or later, they must meet. Therefore, compose a long letter, copying this pardon into it, and I vouch for the messenger. Within six months at the latest, Gerard shall receive it. When he does, he will kiss it, press it to his bosom, and fly home. What are you smiling at? And why do your cheeks burn? What are you stifling? I cannot guess. Yes! Happy days approach for my little pearl."

Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Martin sat before the black-jack while Reicht Heynes spun beside him. Alas, she pumped him that night with relentless questions.

Hans Memling was indeed an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister. Though Margaret had sneered at his profession, he was a painter nonetheless—a good soul with one distinct flaw. He loved the "nipperkin, canakin, and the brown bowl" more than they deserved. This peculiar penchant prevented him from accumulating wealth, causing him frequently to seek meals and occasionally a groat from Margaret Van Eyck. Yet, this dependence created a bond; she knew he would not slight any commission she entrusted to him.

The letter was duly written and left with Margaret Van Eyck. The following week, as promised, Hans Memling returned from Flanders. Margaret handed him the letter and a piece of gold for his travel expenses. He seemed eager to depart.

"All the better," remarked the old artist; "he will reach Italy the sooner."

However, just as some horses burn with rage to start, only to require a whip after the first few yards, so too did Hans's haste cool into inertia once he reached the principal inn of Tergou and spotted two of his boon companions in the bay window. He entered for a farewell drink. When he offered to pay, they refused; he was embarking on a long journey, and they insisted on treating him—the landlord included.

The result was that his tongue loosened as if the wine were oil. Confiding in his jovial circle, he boasted of showing the Italians how to paint, recounted his exploits in battle having wielded a pike, and detailed his amorous conquests with women present to contradict their versions. In short, *plenus rimarum erat*; he flowed hither and thither with tales. Among the miscellaneous matters leaking forth, he blurted that he carried a letter to a townsman of theirs, one Gerard, a fine fellow. Adding, "You are all good fellows," he slapped Sybrandt on the back so heartily that he nearly drove the air from the man's lungs.

Sybrandt circled the table to evade this muscular greeting but listened intently, learning for the first time that Gerard had gone to Italy. Determined to verify the news, he feigned skepticism.

"My brother Gerard is never in Italy."

"Ye lie, ye cur!" roared Hans, instantly turning irascible and failing to recognize that the man sitting opposite was the very individual he had just praised and punched. "If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What proof do you offer? Read this inscription!" He flung the letter onto the table.

Sybrandt picked it up, examined it gravely, and finally set it down, claiming he could not read. However, by some immense fortuity, one of the company could. Proud of this rare skill, he seized the document and read aloud:

"To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed."

"'Tis excellently penned," observed the reader, scrutinizing every letter.

"Ay!" declared Hans bombastically. "No wonder: it is written by a famous hand—that of Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honored be her memory! She is an old friend of mine, Margaret Van Eyck."

Afterward, Miscellaneous Hans diverged into forty different topics.

Sybrandt slipped out of the gathering and sought Cornelis. Together, they pondered the news. Italy was a vast distance. Could they keep him there?

"Keep him there? Nothing would hold him long from his Margaret."

"Curse her!" spat Sybrandt. "Why didn't she die when she was about it?"

"She die? She would outlive the plague to vex us." Cornelis raged at her selfishness in refusing to die and thereby obliging them.

These two black sheep continually plotted, poisoning each other's minds until their corrupt hearts devised a scheme to keep Gerard in Italy forever, thus securing his share of their father's estate. Yet, planning was easy; execution required talent, and iniquity stalled. Suddenly, as if Satan whispered simultaneously into their ears, they both shouted:

They approached Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them immediately. The man tortured by suspense clutches eagerly at any knowledge. Certainty is often painful, but rarely, like suspense, is it intolerable.

"You have news of Gerard?" he asked eagerly.

They recounted the letter and Hans Memling. He listened with restless eyes. "Who wrote the letter?"

"Margaret Van Eyck," they replied, assuming the content matched the signature.

"Are you sure?" Ghysbrecht moved to a drawer and produced a paper written by Margaret during negotiations for her house. "Was it written thus?"

"Yes. 'Tis the same hand," Sybrandt affirmed boldly.

"Good. Now, what do you desire of me?" Ghysbrecht asked, his heart pounding beneath a mask of feigned carelessness so convincing it staggered them. They fumbled with their bonnets, stammered, hesitated, and beat about the bush, gradually revealing their intent: they wanted a letter drafted to keep Gerard in Italy, proposing to substitute it for the one in Hans Memling's wallet. As they fumbled with their hats and their wicked schemes, vacillating between respect for the burgomaster and suspicion that he was as rogue as themselves, conflicting thoughts raced through the old man's mind.
