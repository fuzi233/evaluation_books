Miss Butterworth was not a woman prone to fidgeting. Raised in the strictest school of manners, she prided herself on her composure—whether sitting in stillness or moving with brisk precision. Yet now, alone after her unsettling interview with Mr. Gryce, she found her fingers twitching against the armrest of her chair, her foot tapping an impatient rhythm. The case had gotten under her skin.

When Gryce finally returned, his expression was almost cheerful. "Well, that’s settled," he announced. "Now we wait. Unless, of course, you’ve had another stroke of insight?"

She arched a brow. "You’d like another, would you? I’d prefer to see the first one proven before venturing a second. Let’s compare notes instead. For instance—why are we both so certain Bartow isn’t the killer?"

"I thought we’d exhausted that," Gryce mused. "Your suspicions lie with the fleeing couple, while mine… well, the mute’s pantomime suggests a witness, not an actor. He’s reenacting what he saw, not what he did."

"Precisely. And insanity is more often the result of witnessing horror than committing it."

"Unless the victim was dear to him. Bartow shows no grief for Mr. Adams."

"His mind is shattered. He may not remember grief."

"True. But if we trust his gestures as truth, we must accept all of them—including the unholy satisfaction on his face afterward. That’s not the look of a horrified witness. It’s triumph."

"Or mimicry. The killer might have rejoiced."

"The young man you described didn’t seem the rejoicing sort."

Gryce exhaled. "A puzzle, then. Let’s move forward. His first gesture—reaching behind him while leaning forward. What does that signify?"

Miss Butterworth frowned. "I didn’t see that. When I observed him, he was thrusting left-handed across his body—a vicious strike. Odd, given the doctors confirm Adams was killed by a left-handed blow."

Gryce’s eyes sharpened. "You see the contradiction?"

"Of course. Bartow mirrors what he saw. If the killer struck left-handed, Bartow would mimic with his right—like a reflection. Try it yourself." She raised her right hand; instinctively, Gryce mirrored with his left.

He chuckled. "Point taken. So either the killer wasn’t left-handed, or he wasn’t standing where we assume."

"Or the doctors are wrong," she countered. "But I doubt that. What we need is to observe Bartow in that study, free to move as he pleases. We’d learn more in minutes than we will in hours of speculation."

Gryce considered. "After the funeral, perhaps. The house is swarming now."

She nodded, then hesitated. "Mr. Gryce… might my presence at the scene draw the coroner’s attention?"

He gave her a sidelong glance. "If we find those young people first, the coroner may *overlook* your involvement."

The emphasis was deliberate, and she stiffened. But there was more to confess. "There was another person in the house. An old gentleman. I didn’t mention him earlier—he seemed irrelevant, but now…"

Gryce leaned forward. "An old man? Who knew Adams?"

"Apparently. He arrived just as I was leaving—expectant, almost jovial, until he saw the body. Then…" She hesitated. "His reaction wasn’t just shock. There was hatred. Terror. And something worse—a kind of glee. It vanished when the bird screeched *‘Remember Evelyn!’* He recoiled, muttered, *‘Amos’s son, not Amos…’* then left laughing. A cruel, mocking laugh."

Gryce’s fingers drummed the table. "‘Amos’s son.’ Adams’s full name was Felix Adams. Felix, son of Amos? That’s a thread to pull. This man must be found."

She rose. "Then I leave it to you. Though I fear my boa may have left traces on the study table—bloodstains, if I leaned too close."

Gryce’s smile was thin. "A detail, but a telling one. Goodnight, Miss Butterworth."

As she departed, he didn’t dwell on the ethics of extracting so much while revealing so little. Detectives, after all, were pragmatic creatures.
