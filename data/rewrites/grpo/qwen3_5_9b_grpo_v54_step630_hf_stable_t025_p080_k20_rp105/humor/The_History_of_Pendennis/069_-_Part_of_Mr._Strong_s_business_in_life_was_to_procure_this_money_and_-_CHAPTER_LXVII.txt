While Pen was busy weaving his own selfish parliamentary tapestry in his county, news arrived that Lady Rockminster had descended upon Baymouth, bringing with her the radiant figure of Laura. The announcement struck Pen with a sudden, stinging guilt. His deepest desire was to stand higher in her esteem than in anyone else's; she was his mother's legacy, the living embodiment of his inheritance. He felt a paralyzing dread at the prospect of facing her. How would she react to the news he carried? How could he possibly explain the ambitious, worldly schemes he was hatching to a soul so spotless and pure? He imagined that neither he nor Blanche could withstand Laura's calm, scrutinizing gaze, nor dare to expose their muddy ambitions to such a perfect judge.

Overwhelmed by panic, Pen penned a letter filled with flowery phrases, feigned affection, and easy satire, all while secretly feeling like a rogue caught in the act of hypocrisy. How could a simple country girl, untouched by the corruption of the world, terrify such an accomplished gentleman? Pen realized that his diplomatic skills and cynical knowledge of human nature crumbled before her purity. His affairs were in such a precarious state that he simply could not tell the truth to that honest soul. Riding from Clavering to Baymouth, he felt as guilty as a schoolboy about to face his teacher without knowing his lesson. Truth was the master, and she held the book.

Meanwhile, Laura, under the watchful, somewhat imperious care of Lady Rockminster, had spent the last year gathering the armor of society. While many girls, bred in excessive tenderness, would have withered under the harsh realities of the world, Laura had blossomed. Though Helen, her mother, had worshipped her with a suffocating affection that had shielded her from the world, Laura had stepped out into the great, careless expanse alone. She had faced rude servants, indifferent relatives, and the sting of exclusion, yet she met it all with a cheerful spirit. She viewed her situation not as a burden, but as a privilege compared to the countless unprotected girls who suffered in silence.

Laura possessed a talisman far more valuable than any diamond: a heart of such innocent, trusting goodness that it disarmed everyone around her. From the irritable old lady to the grumbling housekeeper who had endured forty years of Lady Rockminster's temper, everyone was won over. When Laura fell ill in Paris, the housekeeper, usually hostile, nursed her with fierce devotion, fighting with the maid for medicine. The chef wept over her food; the Swiss major-domo praised her in broken languages; the footmen celebrated her recovery with wild hilarity. Even Lady Diana Pynsent, who had previously disliked her, declared Laura a "trouvaille"—a wonderful find. Laura achieved this not through artifice, but through the simple, magnetic force of being pleased and making others feel pleased.

When Pen finally arrived, he found Laura waiting. Instead of the cold reception he feared, she ran downstairs and embraced him so warmly that the stoic footmen, Calverley and Coldstream, would have watched a head-snipping disaster with impassive indifference. Laura had improved immensely; her eyes beamed with health, her cheeks flushed with beauty. As Pen looked at her artless grace, he felt a profound touch of innocence that moved him deeply.

"How good you are to me, Laura—sister!" Pen exclaimed, feeling unworthy of her kindness.

"Mamma left you to me," Laura replied, brushing his forehead with her lips. "You promised to come to me in trouble or share your joy. Are you happy now, or in trouble?"

Pen confessed his parliamentary plans. Laura's reaction was immediate and devastatingly casual. "All the world knows!" she laughed. "Even the doctor talked about it today. It will be in the papers tomorrow. Sir Francis Clavering is retiring for Mr. Arthur Pendennis, and the beautiful Miss Blanche Amory is—"

"What! That too?" Pen asked, wincing.

"That, too, dear Arthur," Laura continued, her tone light and accepting. "I've heard Blanche speak warmly of you. When is it to be? May I come and live with you?"

"My home is yours," Pen said, trying to deflect the weight of her acceptance. "But nothing is decided. No words have passed between us. I have no heart, I think; only a sober attachment. I want my wife on one side of the fire and my sister on the other."

As he spoke, a strange, jealous anger rose in him. Why did he feel angry at the thought of an invisible "somebody" with the right to take Laura away? He was hurt that she received his news so easily, taking his happiness for granted.

"I will stay at home and be Aunt Laura," she laughed, performing a perfect Parisian curtsey. "I've learned to sing and dance with your money. When you become a minister, Blanche shall present me!"

Just then, Lady Rockminster entered, shaking Pen's finger with a commanding air. "So you are going to marry, sir," she declared bluntly. "And go into Parliament. I wanted my grandson to have the seat! I hope you have a great deal of money with Miss Amory. I wouldn't take her without it."

Pen managed a polite smile, but the old lady was relentless. She criticized Blanche as an "affected little creature" and a "crooked lump of affectation," contrasting her unfavorably with Laura. She ranted about the selfishness and cowardice of modern young men, lamenting that they married only for money.

"You are very glad, and you are very sorry," she chided Pen. "A young man who prefers Miss Amory to Miss Bell has no business to be sorry or glad. Where is your friend Bluebeard? Why doesn't he marry Laura? They all marry for money. You are all selfish and cowards."

Pen defended himself with some heat, asking if she had consulted Laura before offering her to the embassy attaches. Laura, sensing the tension, intervened with a kind smile. "He is going to marry somebody else; and I intend to be very fond of her," she said, taking Pen's hand. "Provided he doesn't ask every bachelor why he doesn't marry me."

With his conscience temporarily appeased, Pen found himself drawn constantly to Baymouth. Lady Rockminster, surprisingly, invited him to dine regularly, claiming she could cure his selfishness. "Come here," she ordered, "and let us teach you to behave properly. You will not have to carve; Hecker will give you as much wine as is good for you."

During these walks and rides, Pen began to truly appreciate Laura's frankness and the purity of her heart. Their conversations often turned to their late mother, Helen. Laura told stories of her mother's silent, absorbing devotion, a love that had followed Pen through life. One day, as they rode past Fairoaks, Pen reflected bitterly on the vanity of human existence. He saw how people kicked away happiness for trivial gains, how the world was selfish and cruel.

"I have been round to the cottages where my mother used to visit," Pen said, his voice hard. "It is scarcely a year since she is gone, and the people she benefited care no more for her death than for Queen Anne's. We are all selfish. There are but a few exceptions, like you, to shine like good deeds in a naughty world."

Laura looked down at the honeysuckle on her breast. "When you gave that to the little boy, you were not selfish," she said gently. "Your heart was kind. If you think humbly of yourself, that kindness is diminished, is it not?"

She challenged his cynicism, suggesting that if the world was wicked, he should leave it to study and write good books. "I would have you bring your wife to Fairoaks," she pleaded. "I would like to see your children playing on the lawn, and us praying in our mother's church again."

Pen scoffed at the idea of Blanche becoming a country wife, fearing he would become a petty gentleman. Then, the topic shifted. Laura blushed, and Pen noticed the fervor in her eyes before she pulled her veil down.

"What does Mr. Warrington say?" she asked softly.

The name brought a sharp pang to Pen. He had never heard George speak of his past with Laura since the day of his misfortune. Now, driven by a morbid curiosity and a sense of guilt, he stopped his horse close to hers.

"Tell me something, Laura," he said. "Would you... would you have given him what you refused me?"

"Yes, Pen," she burst out, tears streaming down her face.

"He deserved you better than I did," Pen groaned, wrapping his arm around her as she sobbed on his shoulder.

In that moment, the truth of Laura's heart was laid bare. During her mother's final journey, she had confessed her lingering feelings for George. It was only when Warrington revealed his own hopeless situation that Laura realized her affections had shifted. She had admired and respected him, but the tender feeling had died, leaving only humility and remorse. She felt guilty for having withdrawn her love from Pen, whom she had secretly cherished, and for entertaining thoughts of another. She judged herself a criminal for a trifling fault, deciding that she must be gentle in judging others' greater temptations.

When the news of Pen's engagement to Blanche finally hit her, the humbled girl accepted it meekly. "He knows the world better than I do," she reasoned. "My duty is to aid him and Blanche." She immediately began calculating how to give Blanche gifts from her small treasury to secure their friendship.

Later, a slim letter awaited Laura in the hall. Trembling slightly, she opened it to find it was from Blanche. The letter detailed a trip to London and a visit to Shepherd's Inn to see Fanny, ending with a request to know when the "horrid canvassing" would be over.

After dinner, while the doctor read the *Times*, he announced the death of John Henry Foker, Esq., of Logwood Hall, at Pau. The news hung in the air, a stark reminder of the fragility of life and the relentless march of fortune, as Pen and Laura sat in the quiet aftermath of their revelations.
