The evening had the audacity to begin creeping in, as if apologizing for interrupting such a flawless summer day. A reluctant blush still lingered in the west, and a smug new moon hung over the barn like a freshly polished scythe. Sally stepped outside, bowed three times (for luck, obviously), and inhaled the kind of air that makes you want to write poetry—or at least sigh dramatically.

Darkness, having initially hesitated, now committed fully, like a guest who starts with one drink and ends up dancing on the table. Stars blinked into existence, the road vanished like a bad memory, and across the meadow, house windows lit up like fireflies who’d finally remembered their cue.

From the kennels, a single, half-hearted bark sounded. Toto, the small white dog who had trotted after Sally, let out a squeak of outrage—less a challenge, more a vocal shrug.

Ginger’s footsteps pounded down the road, returning from the village where he’d presumably gone to acquire essentials: newspapers, wool for Sally’s eternally unfinished sweater, and—oh yes—dinner. (He’d forgotten the chops. Naturally.)

“Ginger!” Sally called.

“What ho!”

Toto scampered toward the sound, tail wagging with the enthusiasm of a creature who had never been snubbed in his life. Ginger, alas, remained unimpressed. He had tolerated the dog’s presence for a month—ever since Mrs. Meecher had begged them to take the lethargic puffball for some "country air." Privately, Ginger suspected Toto was just lazy.

“Look at him now!” Sally said proudly. “A whole new dog!”

“A whole new nuisance,” Ginger corrected. “He used to waddle. Now he sprints—usually toward food he’s not supposed to have.”

“You’re just jealous because he’s living his best life.”

Ginger shuddered. “This morning I caught him on the kitchen table, inhaling bacon like a vacuum cleaner. I nearly threw a shoe at him.”

“My brutish caveman,” Sally sighed, patting his arm. “What an evening, though.”

Ginger stopped dead, squinting at her in the kitchen light. “Hold on. You look even prettier than you did when I left.”

Sally hugged his arm. “Flatterer. Now, about those chops you didn’t get…”

Ginger winced. “Ah. Yes. Wool, though! Very… knit-worthy.”

“I’ll knit you a consolation sweater.”

They settled for vegetables (Sally insisted they were "spiritual" dining) and mail—two letters inquiring about Airedale puppies (business was booming) and one from Fillmore, who had apparently remembered he had a sister.

“It’s from Fillmore,” Sally announced, slicing the envelope open. “And it’s about time.”

Ginger perched on the table, pretending to read the paper while actually watching Sally with the dazed contentment of a man who still couldn’t believe his luck. Marriage, he’d discovered, was the one thing in life that didn’t lose its shine after the first week.

Sally snorted. “Ginger, look at this.” She handed him a slip of paper bearing the bold proclamation:

Ginger frowned. “Is this a ransom note?”

“It’s Fillmore’s latest venture. He and Gladys have opened a restaurant. In *Pittsburg*.”

Ginger gasped, horrified. The great Fillmore Nicholas, former theatrical impresario, reduced to peddling pork pies? It was like hearing Napoleon had taken up knitting.

Sally, however, was delighted. “It’s brilliant! Gladys is a genius in the kitchen, and Fillmore’s actually selling things instead of just talking about ‘Big Ideas.’ Listen—” She read aloud: *“‘Our success has been so instantaneous that I am now planning branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit… soon, the world!’*”

Ginger’s stomach growled. “I’d settle for one pork pie right now.”

Sally rolled her eyes. “You’re hopeless. Come on—let’s walk to the village and eat like normal people. We’re practically millionaires anyway.”

And with that, they set off into the night, Toto trotting behind, still hoping for bacon.
