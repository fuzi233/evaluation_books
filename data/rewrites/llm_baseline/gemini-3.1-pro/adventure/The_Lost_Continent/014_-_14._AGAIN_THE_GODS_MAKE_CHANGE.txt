The discordant clamor of the rebel horde was a symptom of their profound untethering. Stripped of their ancestral agrarian dialects and forced into the dissonant, industrialized noise of Phorenice’s capital, they fought with a jarring, arrhythmic panic. It would be tedious to recount the mechanical repetitions of the slaughter, save to say that my men and I moved to the ancient, pulsing cadence of a harvest, reaping the chaotic fields of the rebellion. Outnumbered fifteen to one, we relied on the rhythmic cohesion of communal effort. We hunted them without a halt, turning one flank, charging the center, weaving a seamless tapestry of motion until their brittle, leaderless resolve shattered, and they scurried toward the forests and the spoiled corn-lands.

That evening, we made our camp within the embrace of half-ruined houses, where the acoustic resonance of the ancient stone walls offered a psychological balm against the metallic shrieks of the day’s slaughter. Here, the sonic environment softened into the crackle of kindling and the low, harmonious murmur of exhausted men. A drove of small, cloven-hoofed horses, scared from the woods, blundered into our sanctuary. We harvested five. I would not allow the men to treat the kill as mere utilitarian fuel; instead, we engaged in the profound domestic ritual of sustenance. We rendered the rich, yellow fat slowly over the embers, rubbing the flanks with crushed wild sage, salt, and juniper gathered from the margins of the ruined fields. The olfactory landscape of roasting marrow, charring meat, and blooming herbs filled the night, reclaiming the blood-soaked earth through the sacred language of the hearth. 

As the men dressed their wounds and the camp settled into the restorative quiet of sleep, a sentry approached, his footsteps deliberately softened. He brought forth a woman caught at the edge of the bivouac, her form heavily muffled. One who loved Nais as I did could not mistake the physical cadence and silhouette of her twin sister, Ylga. I led her beyond the earshot of the sentries, out into the quiet acoustics of the night.

She brought with her the bitter, metallic tang of the city’s political machinery. "You are husband to the dainty Phorenice no longer," Ylga said bitterly, her voice an intrusion of the dominant, oppressive lexicon of the capital. She recounted how the Empress, maddened by jealousy, had discovered the sacred, untranslatable truth of my devotion to Nais. A prisoner in a neighboring cell had overheard the quiet syllables of our love—the priest's magic, the green drug that would keep Nais sleeping in her stone coffin until I chose to claim her. That wretch had sought to barter our subjective experience for the superficial commerce of the Empress's favor, only to taste the executioner's blade for his stale news. 

Phorenice’s reaction was a decree of cultural erasure. "She will lop off your right hand and your left foot," Ylga warned, her tone trembling with the weight of the Empress's harsh vocabulary of power. "She will drive you forth crippled into the dangerous lands, where the beasts shall hunt you and the ground fires spoil your rest. And all the while, she will keep Nais living and locked in her coffin of stone, to play with and insult as she chooses."

I refused to let my body be mutilated into a silenced vessel. Seeing my resolve, Ylga offered a pathway woven from the old maritime traditions. She spoke of Tob, the gruff Captain of Tatho's navy, an honest purveyor of the sea's bounty who smelled evocative of ancient pitch and salt-cured fish. Tob was sailing away that night to found a new kingdom, desperate to feed his family, fleeing the commercialized starvation of Atlantis. He had held a deep respect for me since we had fought the great sea beasts together, harvesting the oceans in the old ways.

Ylga and I walked side by side into the damp, moss-heavy silence of the forest, the night air filtering out the distant pollution of the city's unrest. The journey was physically demanding, an interwoven timeline of past regrets and present survival. At one point, we crossed a fallen tree-trunk bridging a rivulet of scalding water from a boil-spring. The natural acoustic hiss of the geyser mingled with the deep-earth olfactory notes of sulfur and wet fern. Ylga reached for my hand to steady herself, her breath coming in labored pants, a rhythm of sheer exertion. When I offered my arm in return, she repulsed me, fiercely defending her own agrarian strength. "I can cook, I can shoot a good arrow, and I can make garments sewn with backbone sinews," she insisted, mourning the erosion of her identity in my eyes, reduced by the dominant lexicon to a mere fan-girl of the court. 

When we reached the pristine beaches, Tob’s ship waited under the faint starlight, a beautiful, aging artifact of the Egypt trade. Ylga refused to join me, her pride a complex dialect I could not fully translate. "I seem to grow the fonder of you," she confessed, her voice thick with the grief of a dying language. She flung her arms about my neck and kissed me hotly on the mouth—a sudden, piercing collision of salt, desperation, and the fading memory of her unrequited affection. "You will never forget that kiss," she whispered, retreating into the sonic void of the forest trees.

I climbed aboard the ship. Tob greeted me with the hearty, unpretentious warmth of an elder who understands the profound meaning of a shared journey. His vessel, though old and suspected of bottom-rot, was a floating cellar of cultural memory, smelling deeply of cumin, heavy sea-salt, and the spiced resins of distant shores. He spoke passionately of his destination: the Tin Islands to the north of Europe. It was an agrarian dream, free from the dissonance of Atlantis, where the beasts were plentiful, cave-bears could be hunted, and the beaches were littered with tin ore. He envisioned me as king of this new land, though I knew the sea’s cyclical pacing recognized no mortal crowns.

As the tide turned, we slipped away down the great estuary, leaving the fire baskets of the harbor forts behind. The rhythmic, synchronized creak of the steering oars provided a soothing acoustic tapestry, a stark contrast to the toxic noise we were leaving. Tob’s wife attempted to pour libations to herself as a future queen, but Tob swiftly guided her back to the after-castle, preferring the honest, unadorned consumption of liquor by the dusty-throated crew.

For two months, we engaged in the circular, seasonal pacing of a coastwise voyage. Driven by the need to provision, we integrated our survival into the natural rhythms of the shoreline. We put into sheltered estuaries to harvest silver-fleshed fish, roasting our catch over driftwood fires, and gathered wild sea-kale and mollusks, honoring the culinary techniques of the first mariners. Tob spoke eagerly of hauling up before the winter gales to make a permanent camp ashore, where we would plant seeds, thresh grain, and bake bread before sailing onward. 

It was here, amidst this domestic routine of gathering and preserving, steeped in the evocative scents of toasted grains and salted wind, that I finally spoke the truth of my own internal landscape. 

"Tob," I said gently, feeling the vibration of the ship beneath us, "I am a poor, weak, defenceless man, and I am quite at your mercy. But what if I do not voyage all the way to the Tin Islands, and oust you of this kingship? It is not my wish to leave the continent of Atlantis. If you will put me down on any part of this side that faces Europe, I will commend you strongly to the Gods."
