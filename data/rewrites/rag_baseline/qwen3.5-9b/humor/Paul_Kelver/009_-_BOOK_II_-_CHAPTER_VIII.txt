The eighteen months that followed—the end arriving sooner than our collective anticipation—constituted, I believe, the zenith of my parents' existence. If "happy" fails to capture the nuance, allow me to posit "beautiful" and "nearly perfect." To them, it appeared as though Divine Providence, in a gesture of tender mercy, had dispatched Death to tap gently upon the threshold, murmuring, *"Not yet. You possess a measure of time remaining. A little while longer."* During these final days, they discarded all pretense and the trivialities of the mundane. Nothing held weight save the mutual adoration, the solace, and the profound comprehension exchanged between them. We reverted to poverty, yet the exhausting pantomime of maintaining appearances vanished, along with the paralyzing dread of neighborly judgment. The petty anxieties regarding inconsequential matters, the base desires and fears that mar the soul, simply evaporated. In their stead arose a broader horizon of thought, a wider charity, a deeper pity. Their love expanded beyond their immediate necessities, spilling outward to embrace all creation. Occasionally, reflecting upon these months, I suspect we err in our fervent attempt to banish Death, God's intermediary, from our consciousness. Is it not akin to shutting the door upon a benefactor who would assist us if we allowed him entry? For who understands life better than He, whispering softly, *"In a little while. Only a brief extension of your union. Is it truly worth consuming so much mental energy on the self? Is it worthwhile to remain unkind?"*

From this domestic transformation radiated a grace that permeated our entire sphere. Even Aunt Fan, deciding for the second time in her illustrious career to experiment with amiability, made her intentions public. This declaration occurred one afternoon shortly after our return from Devonshire.

"I am a beast of an old woman," Aunt Fan stated abruptly.

"Do not say that, Fan," my mother implored.

"What utility lies in commanding silence when the words have already escaped?" Aunt Fan snapped back.

"It is your manner," my mother elucidated. "People occasionally perceive you as disagreeable."

"They would be foolish not to," Aunt Fan interjected. "Naturally, you do not mean it," my mother countered.

"Nonsense," Aunt Fan scoffed. "Does she imagine I am a fool? I delight in being disagreeable. I enjoy watching them squirm."

My mother laughed.

"I possess the capacity for agreeableness," Aunt Fan continued, "whenever I choose. No one does so better."

"Then why not exercise that choice?" my mother suggested.

"I attempted it once," Aunt Fan revealed. "It failed catastrophically. Nothing could have fared worse."

"It may not have garnered significant attention," my mother replied with a smile, "yet one ought not pursue agreeableness merely to attract notice."

"That was not the sole issue," Aunt Fan returned. "It yielded no satisfaction to anyone. It did not suit me. A disagreeable individual reaches their apex precisely when they are disagreeable."

"I struggle to concur with that assessment," my mother answered.

"I could replicate it again," Aunt Fan muttered to herself, a hint of vindictiveness coloring her tone. "It requires minimal effort. Observe the sort of fools who cultivate agreeableness."

"I am certain you could manage it if you applied yourself," my mother urged.

"Leave them to it," Aunt Fan continued, addressing the empty air. "That is the method to instill sense. Leave them to it."

Strangely, my aunt proved correct, and my mother entirely mistaken. My father was the first to detect the shift.

"Is there nothing troubling poor old Fan?" he inquired one evening, a day or two after Aunt Fan enacted her threat. "Has nothing transpired?"

"No," my mother responded, "nothing to my knowledge."

"Her demeanor is so peculiar," my father explained. "So... so bizarre."

My mother smiled. "Say nothing to her. She is attempting to be agreeable."

My father chuckled, then gazed wistfully. "I almost wish she would not," he remarked. "We were accustomed to it, and she was rather amusing."

Yet, being a woman of iron will, Aunt Fan persisted. Coinciding with this determination was the introduction of James Wellington Gadley into our modest circle. Technically, his name should have been Wellington James, the order in which he was baptized in 1815. However, over time, and particularly during his schooling, he realized that "Wellington" was a burdensome moniker for an ordinary mortal. With great wisdom, he inverted the sequence. He was a slightly pompous yet simple-minded elderly gentleman, fiercely proud of his role as head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father served as assistant. Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal traced its lineage back to the Georges, a firm entwined with the history—sometimes dubious—of aristocratic England. Indeed, in recent years, its luster had faded. Old Mr. Stillwood, the firm's sole survivor, refused new partnerships, frankly stating in response to every inquiry that the business was moribund and that revitalizing it would amount to pouring new wine into worn skins. Although its client list shrank annually, substantial business remained, largely of high caliber, for its name still signified solid respectability. My father considered himself exceptionally fortunate to secure such an appointment. James Gadley had joined as an office boy during the firm's prime and never grasped that it was no longer the preeminent legal entity within a half-mile radius of Lombard Street. Nothing pleased him more than recounting repeatedly the myriad extraordinary cases handled by Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal, all of which he knew by heart. Given an audience, he would rehash them endlessly, employing professional discretion to transform names into "Mr. Y" and "Lady X," and locations into "the capital of a foreign nation" or "a specific town not a thousand miles from here." Most of his friends, adopting somewhat forensic methods, sought to dissuade him, but my aunt was an inexhaustible listener, particularly when the case involved whispers of mystery and crime.

When, during their inaugural conversation, he exclaimed, "Now, why—why, after absconding from his wife for nearly eighteen years, never informing her of his living or dead status, does this sudden resolve to return arise? That demands explanation!" he paused, as was his habit, awaiting sympathetic commentary. My aunt, unlike others who might have yawned, "Oh, I presume he felt a desire to see her," replied with prompt intelligence:

"To murder her—with slow poison."

"To murder her! But why?"

"In order to wed the other woman."

"The other woman?"

"The woman he had just encountered and fallen in love with. Prior to that, her fate was immaterial to him. This woman declared to him: 'Return to me a free man, or never behold my face again.'"

"Good heavens! How curious."

"Not in the slightest. Plain common sense."

"I mean, it is curious because, as a matter of fact, his wife died shortly thereafter, and he did remarry."

"Told you so," my aunt observed.

Thus, every case in the Stillwood annals was scrutinized, illuminated by my aunt's penetrating insight into the hidden springs of human action. Fortunately, the actors remained abstract figures, mere Mr. X and Lady Y, for into the most innocuous behavior my aunt projected dark criminal intent.

"I believe you are somewhat too severe," Mr. Gadley would occasionally plead.

"We are all miserable sinners," my aunt would cheerfully affirm. "Only we do not all receive identical opportunities."

She was convinced that an elderly spinster, Miss Z., residing in "a western town once renowned as a fashionable resort, though we shall omit its name," had burned down a house containing a will and forged another, ensuring that her hypothetical future children—should she ever marry and be blessed with offspring—would inherit a fortune of seven hundred pounds upon reaching maturity.

The freshness of her perspectives on this, his favorite topic, invariably captivated Mr. Gadley.

"I owe you, madam," he would remark upon rising, "a most delightful conversation. While I may not align with your conclusions, they provide ample fodder for reflection."

To which my aunt would retort, "I detest conversing with anyone who agrees with me. It resembles walking to view one's own reflection in a mirror. I prefer discussing with someone who disagrees, even if they are a fool," which, for her, constituted graciousness.

He was a stout little gentleman with a stomach protruding approximately a foot forward, of which he seemed entirely oblivious. Nor would this have mattered had it not been for his compulsion, when speaking, to approach his listener as closely as possible. Gradually, during the course of conversation, his stomach acting as a gentle battering ram, he would drive you backward around the room. Unless one was artful, he would pin you hopelessly into a corner, only to express surprise that despite his efforts, he never succeeded in drawing nearer. On his first evening at our home, he spoke to my aunt from the corner of his chair. As interest mounted, he drew his chair closer and closer until, having retreated inch by inch to evade his encroachments, my aunt sat on the extreme edge of her seat. His subsequent move propelled her onto the floor. She said nothing, which surprised me; however, on his next visit, she was busy darning stockings—an unusual occupation for her.
