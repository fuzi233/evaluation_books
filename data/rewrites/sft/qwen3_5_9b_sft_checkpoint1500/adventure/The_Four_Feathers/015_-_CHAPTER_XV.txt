"I will not interrupt you," said Ethne, as Willoughby took his seat beside her. He had barely spoken twenty words before she broke her promise.

"I am Deputy-Governor of Suakin," he began, his tone shifting instantly from polite deference to the grim reality of the Sudan. "My chief was on leave in May. You are fortunate enough not to know Suakin, Miss Eustace, particularly in May. No white woman can live in that town. It possesses a sodden, intolerable heat peculiar to itself; the air hangs heavy with brine, and one cannot sleep at night for its sheer oppression."

He paused, describing the scene of his vigil. "Well, I was sitting on the verandah of the first-floor palace about ten o'clock at night, looking out over the harbor and the distillation works, wondering if it was worth sleeping at all, when a servant told me a man wished to see me who refused to give his name. It was Feversham."

At once Ethne interrupted. "How did he look?"

Willoughby wrinkled his brow, opening his eyes wide in genuine confusion. "Really, I do not know. Much like other men who have been a year or two in the Soudan, a trifle overstrained, I suppose."

"Never mind," said Ethne, with a sigh of profound disappointment. For five years she had heard no word of Harry Feversham. She hungered for news of him, for the sound of his habitual phrases, for the description of his familiar gestures. She possessed the woman's anxious need to know if he had altered in face or figure, and if so, how. But glancing at the obtuse, unobservant countenance of Captain Willoughby, she realized that however much she craved these particulars, she must go without them.

"I beg your pardon," she said gently. "Will you go on?"

"I asked him what he wanted," Willoughby resumed, "and why he had not sent in his name. 'You would not have seen me if I had,' he replied, drawing a packet of letters from his pocket. Now, those letters, Miss Eustace, were written a long while ago by General Gordon in Khartum. They were carried down the Nile as far as Berber. But the day after they reached Berber, that town surrendered to the Mahdists. Abou Fatma, the messenger who carried them, hid them in the wall of the house of an Arab called Yusef, who sold rock-salt in the market-place. Abou was thrown into prison on suspicion and escaped to Suakin. The letters remained hidden in that wall until Feversham recovered them."

Willoughby recounted how Feversham had told his story quietly on the verandah, standing before the quiet pool of water. He had detailed falling in with Abou Fatma, planning the recovery, traveling together to Obak, and then, since Abou dared not proceed further, driving his grey donkey alone to Berber. Feversham had not even concealed the access of panic which had loosened his joints when first he saw the low brown walls of the town and the towering date palms behind them on the Nile bank; it had set him running and leaping across the empty desert in the sunlight, a marrowless thing of fear. However, he made one significant omission. He said nothing of the hours spent crouching on the hot sand, with his coat drawn over his head, while he drew a woman's face toward him across continents and seas, nerving himself to endure by the look of sorrow it wore.

"He went down into Berber at the setting of the sun," said Captain Willoughby, and that was all he had to say. It was enough, however, for Ethne Eustace. She drew a deep breath of relief, her face softening, a light entering her grey eyes, a smile upon her lips.

"He went down into Berber," she repeated softly.

"And found that the old town had been destroyed by the orders of the Emir," continued Willoughby. "A new town was being built upon its southern confines. All the landmarks by which Feversham was to know the house in which the letters were hidden had gone. The roofs had been torn off, the houses dismantled, the front walls carried away. Narrow alleys of crumbling five-courts crossed this way and that, gaping to the stars. Here and there perhaps a broken tower rose up, the remnant of a rich man's house. But of any sign telling where the hut of Yusef, the rock-salt seller, had stood, there was no hope in those acres of crumbling mud. The foxes had already made their burrows there."

The smile faded from Ethne's face, but she looked again at the white feather lying in her palm, and she laughed with a great contentment. It was yellow with desert dust, a proof that in this story there was to be no word of failure.

"Go on," she said.

Willoughby related the dispatch of the negro with the donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak.

"Feversham stayed for a fortnight in Berber," Willoughby continued. "A week during which he came every morning to the well and waited for the return of his negro from Obak, and a week during which that negro searched for Yusef. I doubt, Miss Eustace, if you can realize what that fortnight must have meant to Feversham—the anxiety, the danger, the continued expectation that a voice would bid him halt and a hand fall upon his shoulder, the urgent knowledge that if the hand fell, death would be the least part of his penalty."

Willoughby painted the town: low houses, broad streets of sand dug into pits for mud, overhead the blistering sun and a hot, shadowless sky. In no corner was there darkness or concealment. All day a crowd jostled and shouted up and down these streets—for that was the Mahdist policy to crowd the towns so that all might be watched and every man his neighbor's spy. Feversham dared not seek the shelter of a roof at night, for he dared not trust his tongue. He bought his food daily at booths but feared any conversation. He slept in corners of the deserted town, in the acres of ruined five-courts. By day he could not slink in by-ways lest anyone question him, nor listen in public places lest loiterers draw him into talk, nor prowl the crumbled ruins. From sunrise to sunset he had to move quickly up and down the streets like a man bent upon urgent business permitting no delay.

"That continued for a fortnight, Miss Eustace!" Willoughby exclaimed. "A weary, trying life, don't you think? I wish I could tell you of it as vividly as he told me that night upon the balcony of the palace at Suakin."

Ethne wished it too with all her heart. Harry Feversham had made his story very real to Captain Willoughby, so that even after fifteen months, this unimaginative creature sensed a contrast and a deficiency in his manner of narration.

"In front of us was the quiet harbor and the Red Sea, above us the African stars. Feversham spoke in the quietest manner possible, but with a peculiar deliberation, his eyes fixed upon my face as though forcing me to feel with him and understand. Even when he lit his cigar he did not avert his eyes. By this time I had given him a cigar and offered him a chair. I had really, I assure you, Miss Eustace. It was the first time in four years that he had sat with one of his equals, or indeed with any of his countrymen on a footing of equality. He told me so. I wish I could remember all that he told me." Willoughby stopped and cudgelled his brains helplessly, giving up the effort in the end.

"Well," he resumed, "after Feversham had skulked for a fortnight in Berber, the negro discovered Yusef, no longer selling salt, but tending a small plantation of dhurra on the river's edge. From Yusef, Feversham obtained particulars enough to guide him to the house where the letters were concealed in the inner wall. But Yusef was no longer to be trusted. Possibly Feversham's accent betrayed him. The more likely conjecture is that Yusef took Feversham for a spy and thought it wise to confess to Mohammed-el-Kheir, the Emir, his own share in the concealment. That, however, is a mere conjecture. The important fact is this: On the same night Feversham went alone to old Berber."

"Alone!" said Ethne. "Yes?"

"He found the house fronting a narrow alley, the sixth of the row. The front wall was destroyed, but the two side walls and the back wall still stood. Three feet from the floor and two feet from the right-hand corner the letters were hidden in that inner wall. Feversham dug into the mud bricks with his knife; he made a hole wherein he could slip his hand. The wall was thick; he dug deep, stopping now and again to feel for the packet. At last his fingers clasped and drew it out; as he hid it in a fold of his jibbeh, the light of a lantern shone upon him from behind."
